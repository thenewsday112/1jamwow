Data centre debate heats up ahead of US midterms - here's what you need to know

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Data centre debate heats up ahead of US midterms - here's what you need to know

Until a few years ago many of us may have been blissfully unaware of what a data centre was.

These giant, warehouse-like buildings power virtually all our internet activity, from our web searches and emails to movie downloads and online banking.

They are at the heart of the AI boom, housing the advanced computer chips and servers needed to run services like ChatGPT and Claude.

But the speed at which they're growing, the enormous amount of electricity they consume and concerns about their environmental impact are fuelling a public backlash around the world.

In the US, they've become a huge issue ahead of the midterm elections on 3 November, with both Republicans and opposition Democrats coming out against their development.

It's putting opponents of data centre expansion on a collision course with US President Donald Trump, who has dismissed the pushback as a "sick conspiracy", even as public anger grows.

Here's what you need to know about the data centre boom, and why opposition is growing.

There are about 12,250 data centres around the world, according to Data Center Map, external, a company that tracks both operational and pre-operational sites.

That's up sharply from 2021, when there were around 8,000, external operational sites.

As the chart below shows, data centres are most densely clustered in the US which has 4,767 sites. It is followed by Europe, where the UK and Germany lead.

Data centres have typically been located near to cities where it's easier to access high voltage power lines or water resources for cooling. But as they get bigger, more are being built in rural areas.

But when it comes to actual capacity, the US is ahead by a much larger margin, with China in second place and Europe lagging far behind.

This gap is set to widen in the coming years, leading some to warn Europe risks missing out on the potential economic benefits of the AI boom, external.

Data centres vary wildly in size, ranging from ones that fit inside a garden shed to others covering vast expanses of land.

The world's largest operational data centre is widely reported to be one run by China Telecom in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia, covering an area of approximately one million square metres (10.7 million sq ft). That is equivalent to about 140 Fifa football pitches.

However, much larger sites are under construction as the graphic below shows.

All these sites are being built exclusively to provide the immense computing power needed to run AI services.

For instance, Colossus 2, which is part of the wider Colossus campus, will house hundreds of thousands of advanced AI chips made by tech giant Nvidia.

It will also be used to train xAI's chatbot Grok. Elon Musk's company will use 41 methane gas turbines to help power the site, which activists say could "significantly increase" local air pollution, external. Local regulators say the turbines will meet air quality standards.

The pressure data centres put on local resources has sparked increasing concern. These facilities can use significant amounts of water to cool their systems, which could lead to water shortages, some experts say.

Data centres worldwide are also thought to consume more electricity than the whole of France, as the chart below shows.

This growing hunger for power is driving up carbon emissions, analysis shows, while putting pressure on local grids, which some fear could push up energy prices.

With others worried about the noise data centres make and the space they take up, a public backlash is gathering steam.

Some 16 US states have proposed laws to ban or temporarily block new facilities, and it could be a key issue in November's midterm elections.

Outside the US, opposition to data centres has emerged in countries like Mexico, France, Indonesia and the UK.

But supporters say data centres are critical to national security, and the development of AI.

They also say they are good for the economy, external, attracting investment, generating tax revenue and creating jobs in areas like construction.

A recent report by the Brookings Center, external suggests data centres do create local jobs, although fewer than industry advocates claim.

The type of data centre matters too. So-called hyperscale facilities - massive sites owned and run by tech giants like Amazon and Microsoft - have a bigger positive effect on jobs, it found.

But "colocation" facilities, which lease space to remote tenants without a local presence, brought fewer benefits.

As the debate over their merits intensifies, the data centre rollout continues.

This year, the tech industry is expected to spend a combined $765bn, external ($544bn) on AI infrastructure in 2026, with this topping $1tn next year.

But as concerns about the environmental impact grow, the tech industry is exploring alternatives.

One idea is to put data centres in space, external or on the Moon, where they could harness abundant solar energy.

Others predict that more AI processing could eventually be carried out by the hardware already inside our computers and smartphones, reducing the need for vast data centres altogether.

Ein bundesweiter Demoaufruf für den 31. Oktober löst Unbehagen aus. Linksextreme, Migranten und Kriminelle könnten die Anliegen der Jugendlichen kapern, warnen Politiker von SPD und Union. Auch die Schülerkonferenz geht auf Abstand, während Linke und Grüne widersprechen.

Nach Massenprotesten in mehreren europäischen Ländern warnen Innenpolitiker von Union und SPD vor Ausschreitungen in Deutschland und der Unterwanderung durch extremistische Kräfte. "Wir müssen die Gefahr sehr ernst nehmen, dass die angekündigten friedlichen Jugendproteste auch in Deutschland durch linksextreme Kräfte, Teile des migrantischen Milieus und Kriminelle vereinnahmt werden können", sagte der innenpolitische Sprecher der Unionsfraktion, Alexander Throm, der "Rheinischen Post". Entsprechende Aufrufe in den sozialen Medien seien "besorgniserregend". Die Jugend müsse friedlich für ihre Anliegen wie bessere Bildung und Generationengerechtigkeit demonstrieren können. "Aber Gewalt, Angriffe auf Polizisten oder öffentliche Einrichtungen überschreiten eine Grenze und sind nicht zu tolerieren", betonte der CDU-Politiker.

Der innenpolitische Sprecher der SPD-Fraktion, Sebastian Fiedler, sagte der "Rheinischen Post": "Für gewalttätige Proteste gibt es zu keinem Zeitpunkt und an keinem Ort irgendeine Legitimation." Er habe den Eindruck, "dass unsere Sicherheitsbehörden sich frühzeitig und außerordentlich professionell auf mögliche Proteste einstellen und die Lage analysieren". Dazu gehöre, dass man sich sehr genau anschauen müsse, wer diejenigen seien, die "im digitalen Raum die Lage anheizen", sagte der frühere Vorsitzende des Bundes deutscher Kriminalbeamter weiter.

Im Netz ruft eine Initiative, die sich "GenZ regelt" beziehungsweise "GenZ steht auf" nennt, für den 31. Oktober zu Protesten in 23 Städten auf. Sie setzt sich unter anderem für mehr Klimaschutz ein, Maßnahmen gegen steigende Lebenshaltungskosten und gegen die Wehrpflicht. Dabei lehnt sie Gewalt ausdrücklich ab. Bei Protesten in Frankreich und anderen europäischen Ländern war es zu Krawallen gekommen.

Die Bundesschülerkonferenz hatte sich am Wochenende von den Protesten am 31. Oktober distanziert. "Wir sehen aufgrund undurchsichtiger Organisationsstrukturen die Gefahr, dass die ernstzunehmenden Anliegen junger Menschen zum Spielball politischer Instrumentalisierung und Polarisierung werden können. Das wird diesen Sorgen und Forderungen nach einer guten Bildung und sicheren Zukunft nicht gerecht", hieß es einer Erklärung.

Politiker von Grünen und Linken warnten davor, junge Leute unter Generalverdacht zu stellen und deren Anliegen zu diskreditieren. "Junge Menschen haben ein Recht darauf, gehört zu werden und friedlich für ihre Zukunft auf die Straße zu gehen", sagte Grünen-Fraktionschefin Britta Haßelmann der "Rheinischen Post". Grünen-Parteichef Felix Banaszak sagte in der ZDF-Sendung "Berlin direkt", bevor man über mögliche Gewalt spekuliere, sollte man ernst nehmen, was die Menschen in ihrem Aufruf zu den Demonstrationen schreiben.

Gewalt sei nicht akzeptabel, stellte er klar. Er kritisierte aber: "Der Generationenvertrag geht nicht mehr auf. Die Älteren haben das Klima vor die Wand gefahren." Schulden würden nicht für Zukunftsinvestitionen gemacht. Die Sozialversicherungssysteme funktionierten nicht mehr, Mieten seien unbezahlbar geworden. Das einzige, womit der Staat auf die jungen Menschen zukomme, seien die Briefe an 18-Jährige für den neuen Wehrdienst.

Die Linke-Innenexpertin Clara Bünger sagte der "Rheinischen Post", konkrete Gefahren bei den Protesten müssten "selbstverständlich ernst genommen werden". Sie betonte zugleich: "Aber wer vor extremistischer Unterwanderung oder ausländischer Einflussnahme warnt, sollte dafür belastbare Anhaltspunkte vorlegen. Statt junge Menschen zu kriminalisieren, sollte die Bundesregierung endlich ihre Anliegen ernst nehmen."

Serbian leader Aleksandar Vučić ousted by student movement in seismic election

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Vučić’s 12-year reign looks to have been ended by record turnout of voters in election provoked by student-led protests

The long-serving leader of Serbia, Aleksandar Vučić, has conceded defeat, ushering in a seismic political shift after a historic election that saw ​the increasingly authoritarian leader pitted against a student-led movement that had spent two years protesting against his government.

Vučić pointed to preliminary results that suggested voters had overwhelmingly backed the students, potentially giving them a majority in the country’s 250-seat parliament.

“We still do not have final ⁠results,” Vučić told supporters at his party headquarters in Belgrade. “On the basis of a sample … it is clear ⁠that we have lost these ​elections, the Student ‌List has ‌won, they will have between 135 ‌and 140 seats and we will have 105.”

A representative sample by the ​CeSID-IPSPS pollsters suggested that the ​Student List’s candidate list will have ​138 deputies, while Vučić’s rightwing Serbian Progressive party (SNS) garnered 104 seats. The remaining seats ‌were likely to be shared ​between ethnic ​national minority parties, said the pollsters.

As news broke that Vučić’s 12 years in power had come to an end, celebrations erupted on the streets of Belgrade and other cities.

In the capital, Ivana, 43, was ecstatic. “This feels like New Year’s Eve to me, like three New Year’s Eves all at once, really,” she said. “We can finally breathe again.” She added that she was hoping for swift accountability for top members of the ousted regime, a key campaign pledge from the Student List.

Others offered handshakes, high fives and hugs to police officers in the streets, while a chorus of honking horns rang out from passing cars.

At the headquarters of the Student List, supporters chanted “Victory, victory!” as they applauded, cried and traded hugs. “Dear Serbia, tonight the voice of the people is being heard,” said Radomir Saičić, a professor at Belgrade University, speaking on behalf of the students at their headquarters. “Tonight we celebrate victory, tomorrow we build the future.”

The scenes of joy came hours after a record number of voters turned up to cast their ballot on Sunday. Across the country, long lines snaked out of polling stations, with some reporting wait times of up to two hours. By 7pm local time pollsters and monitors CeSID and Ipsos put the turnout at 67.6%, the highest since the breakup of Yugoslavia in 1992.

The high turnout, along with the estimated 150,000 people who flocked to the students’ final pre-election rally last week in Belgrade, fuelled early speculation that the students may have rallied enough support to launch a new political era in the Balkan country.

In the lead-up to the vote, opinion polls had varied wildly, with analysts pointing to the high degree of uncertainty at play as the candidates representing the grassroots student movement went up against Vučić’s SNS.

The results were watched across the world as part of 10 pivotal days of elections across the globe in which the authoritarian right is facing off against pro-democracy forces. The victory for the student-led movement in Serbia is also likely to reverberate across Europe, adding momentum to the youth and student protests that have rocked cities across the continent.

As people waited for the election results to come in, speculation had begun to swirl online about whether Vučić would seek to hold on to power regardless. His swift admission of defeat, however, was reminiscent of his ally, Viktor Orbán, in neighbouring Hungary six months ago, fuelling hopes of a democratic resurgence to central Europe.

The high-stakes election in the EU-candidate country traces back to November 2024, when a concrete canopy collapsed at the Novi Sad railway station, killing 16 people. The tragedy set off protests among students, snowballing into a broad anti-government movement as evidence suggested that slipshod construction, fraud and corruption were to blame for the roof collapse.

As Vučić, the increasingly authoritarian leader who has dominated Serbian politics for more than a decade, grappled with mounting pressure from the students, he resigned the presidency last month to gamble on snap elections.

The students seized on the chance to transform the movement into political power. They announced a slate of 250 candidates for the 250-seat parliament, ranging from academics to athletes and farmers, as they called on voters to back their push to rebuild Serbia’s economy and restore its democracy.

The campaign pitted them against the political monolith of Vučić’s SNS party, leaving them in an uphill battle against a ruling party that had taken over almost all of the broadcast channels and which wielded ministries and other state institutions as political tools.

Vučić had repeatedly sought to undermine the students by accusing them of being stooges of foreign powers, a charge that the students have dismissed. As he pledged to increase pensions and public sector salaries, Vučić sought to present himself as a leader capable of walking the diplomatic tightrope between the country’s ties with the west, and Russia and China to steer Serbia through global uncertainties.

Undaunted, the students took to the streets, staging rallies, turning up in the rural areas that had long been Vučić strongholds and going door-to-door as they sought to pitch themselves as the anti-establishment candidates and to rally voters behind their slogan of “Serbia or Mafia?”

The message was reiterated on Sunday as Dejan Bodiroga, a candidate on the Student List and the Euroleague Basketball president, cast his ballot in Belgrade.

“Today, above all, Serbia is fighting for freedom and for the return of a country that has been hijacked,” Bodiroga told reporters. “Serbia is voting today to decide which direction it will take – towards the future or the past.”

In the lead-up to Sunday’s vote, both international and local election monitors expressed concerns about detentions and reports of surveillance and cyber-attacks against activists.

On Sunday, independent monitoring groups warned of recorded cases of photographing of ballots that suggested pressure on voters, attempted vote-buying and carousel voting.

Among them was Raša Nedeljkov of the election monitory CRTA, who said that “a large number of irregularities continue to be recorded, while violent incidents outside polling stations are also becoming increasingly frequent”.

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

While still a teenager, Yamal could surpass Brazilian legend Ronaldo Nazario, who won the Ballon d’Or at the age of 21 in 1997.

A once-in-a-generation talent. A superstar in the making. Equal parts audacious and ambitious.

Those are just a few words that best encapsulate Lamine Yamal’s appeal.

Since making his first-team debut three years ago, Yamal has taken world football by storm, compelling fans and players alike to take note of his talent.

His flair on the football field and flamboyant personality off it have made Yamal one of the sport’s best showmen in recent years.

Now, the 19-year-old has set his sights on another big prize to add to his glittering cabinet – the Ballon d’Or, football’s most prestigious individual honour.

No teenager has won the Ballon d’Or in the award’s 70-year history – Yamal is campaigning to become the youngest winner.

So, how does his bid compare with the other leading contenders? Al Jazeera breaks it down:

Very few players can boast such an impressive season as Yamal’s.

In 2025-26, the winger won the FIFA 2026 World Cup with Spain, only days after his 19th birthday. His success in North America came on the back of his La Liga title triumph with Barcelona.

While an injury-marred campaign meant Yamal did not dazzle viewers on his World Cup debut, grabbing just one goal and an assist, the youngster enjoyed far more success on club duty.

Yamal racked up 22 goals and 15 assists in 38 league appearances, playing a major role in Barcelona’s successful title defence. He was also part of the Catalan club’s Spanish Super Cup-winning squad.

The major double of a world title and a domestic title has strengthened Yamal’s claim for the Ballon d’Or.

According to bookmakers, he is among the top three contenders, alongside Bayern Munich’s Harry Kane and Real Madrid’s Kylian Mbappe.

The 2026 Ballon d’Or race is the closest in decades. But Yamal is quite confident of winning the award, singling out Mbappe as his only threat.

“For me, Mbappe and me, we’re the two best players in the world,” the winger said in an interview on Movistar Plus.

“I honestly think that, as the best in the world, maybe there are two of us, and this year I would deserve it because of what I’ve won,” he added.

This self-promotion by footballers is a relatively recent development in a team sport where, until a few years ago, the top individual prize went largely unnoticed until it was time to present it.

But now, the Ballon d’Or dominates football conversations during the following season, and the award, despite carrying no cash prize, brings greater status and worth.

Barca manager Hansi Flick said although he would have preferred Yamal not to have spoken about it, he accepts it as part of the winger’s self-belief.

“He’s exceptional, and it’s a part of this … I would not prefer this, but he’s doing this. He has self-confidence; he wants to win the Ballon d’Or.”

Nominated for the third time, Yamal finished runner-up to Ousmane Dembele of PSG in the 2025 Ballon d’Or race.

Monday’s ceremony in London is likely to revolve around a three-way battle between Yamal, Mbappe and Kane. It is an interesting contest between three players in three different stages of their careers: one in his teens, one in his 20s and one in his 30s.

While Yamal has publicly pitted himself against Mbappe in the battle, he is confident that the trophies in his portfolio will help him beat the Frenchman, who has had no luck with silverware.

However, purely on an individual basis, Mbappe’s statistics are far better than Yamal’s.

Yamal had 25 goals and 20 assists for club and country. Mbappe, on the other hand, had a staggering 58 goals and 13 assists. The 27-year-old also finished the season as La Liga and Champions League top scorer, lifted the World Cup’s Golden Boot and broke the all-time goals record with 22.

Then there is the unstoppable force of Kane, who was in a league of his own. His 73 goals last season were the best haul by a footballer since Lionel Messi’s 82 in 2011-12.

So while Yamal has the shiny trophies to vouch for his Ballon d’Or bid, his solo displays pale in comparison with his seniors, Mbappe and Kane.

Kane’s incredible goal-scoring prowess – even at the age of 33 – is hard to ignore, but Yamal believes simply registering the maximum goals cannot be the criterion for the prize.

“The Ballon d’Or should go to the best player in the world, full stop,” he said. “Not to a player who has scored 80 goals, because if you score 80 goals, you get the top scorer award for that,” Yamal added, seemingly taking a dig at Kane.

“Those are two completely different things that people do not understand.”

Yamal’s fate now lies in the hands of a jury of journalists from 100 countries whose votes will decide whether he can become the youngest Ballon d’Or winner, surpassing Brazilian legend Ronaldo Nazario, who was 21 at the time of his triumph in 1997.

After lifting the Kopa Trophy for the best Young Talent of the year twice, Yamal will hope the third time is the charm, this time stepping up to the biggest stage to bring home the ultimate individual trophy.

Words often matter as much as actions and amid the civil unrest that France has felt in recent days, some French people are apparently angry and offended over the altering of their national anthem before the NFL game between the Pittsburgh Steelers and New Orleans Saints.

And, yes, this has to do with immigrants in France.

Before the game, singer Nina Tonji performed La Marseillaise at the Stade de France. Although she received a warm round of applause afterward, there has been significant online backlash because she modified the lyrics of the song.

Tonji changed "Qu'un sang impur abreuve nos sillons!" to "Qu'un sang uni abreuve nos sillons!," and that was clearly done on purpose because it has political and societal ramifications.

"Qu’un sang impur abreuve nos sillons" means "May impure blood water our furrows/fields." In the revolutionary context of La Marseillaise, "impure blood" referred to enemies and traitors – particularly French immigrants fighting alongside foreign armies.

Replacing impur ("impure") with uni ("united") changes the meaning of the passage from from a call for enemy blood to nourish France’s soil into an appeal for national unity and solidarity that includes immigrants to nourish France's soil.
