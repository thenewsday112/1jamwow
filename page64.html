The TikTok hate preacher: Terrorist's 'highly toxic' social media rants 'target 11 to 15-year-olds online'

By RICHARD MARSDEN, GENERAL REPORTER and DOMINIK LEMANSKI

Hate preacher Anjem Choudary's former right-hand man has been telling thousands of social media users that most imprisoned extremists are jailed for 'reading magazines and holding placards'.

Mizanur Rahman, 43, who is a convicted terrorist, also raged about Britain being part of an 'axis of evil' with the USA and Israel and labelled Muslim cops 'greedy sinners' in a series of online diatribes.

Experts fear these rants could be aimed at 'sweet talking impressionable Muslims into committing acts of violence against non-Muslims by insisting that Islamist jihadists are not terrorists or killers'.

A longtime associate of Choudary – labelled Britain's most prolific Islamist terror radicaliser – Rahman is an internet expert who created private online chatrooms for the hate preacher's followers.

In 2016 he was jailed for five-and-a-half years for inviting support for Isis alongside Choudary.

But the Daily Mail can now reveal north London-based Rahman, who was freed in October 2018, has been using TikTok to broadcast angry anti-British rants to his near 2,000 followers, earning himself almost 10,000 likes in the process.

Amid fears Rahman is using TikTok to target impressionable young Muslims, anti-extremism commentators last night said the 'highly toxic nonsense' could have 'horrific repercussions'.

Security expert Professor Anthony Glees, of the University of Buckingham, said: 'Mizanur Rahman prides himself on being clever with words but how he uses them could have horrific repercussions.

Mizanur Rahman featured on earlier videos praising Islamist militants including Boko Haram

'His mission is to sweet talk impressionable Muslims into committing acts of violence against non-Muslims by insisting that Islamist jihadists are not terrorists or killers.

'It's highly toxic nonsense. Yet Rahman exploits a barmy logic that can all too easily appeal to the TikTok generation.

'Last year almost one thousand young British Muslims were referred to the Prevent counter-terror platform, with 36 per cent of them being very young, 11 to 15 years old – TikTok targets.

'Dame Sara Khan's compelling report published earlier this month found that 29 per cent of 18- to 34-year-olds think it's ok to use violence for political purposes, and that 15 per cent of these are 'violence fixated' individuals like Axel Rudakubana.

'But the glorification of terrorism which is what Rahman seems to be engaged upon is a serious criminal offence under the Terrorism Act 2006. Let us hope the counter-terror cops act to stop Rahman now before it is too late.'

The Daily Mail's discovery comes just days after it was revealed Rahman was also using TikTok to sell and promote Islamic books, including ones instructing women to seek their husband's permission to leave the house.

A newspaper reported that Rahman has written eight books under the alias Abu Baraa with content including discouraging Muslims from holding elected political office and promoting interpretations of Islamic law that call for homosexuals to be killed.

Rahman's incendiary videos were reposted just days ago on the Chinese social media platform by supporters on a new account after a prior report prompted TikTok chiefs to delete Rahman's personal account.

One video posted by Rahman last month was captioned 'The Truth about Terrorism convictions'.

In the footage, he told followers: 'They [terrorists] don't mean violence. I would say 99 per cent of the people convicted of terrorism – their cases have nothing to do with violence.

Mizanur Rahman, right, with hate preacher Anjem Choudary in picture issued by police in 2016

'I would say even 100 per cent, but I am leaving that room just in case there is one case I do not know about.

'I can't think of a single terrorism case that involves a person who has killed another person.

'I said this to a bunch of journalists. Initially, they all started laughing and saying, "what are you talking about?" I said, "well, show me, tell me?"

'One said, "what about Michael Adebolajo?"

'I said, "he is not convicted of terrorism he was convicted of murder."

'The people convicted of terrorism they are convicted of reading a magazine, the majority of them, others for protesting, hundreds were arrested in the protests against the genocide in Gaza, men, women, the elderly people, grey-haired, the disabled, because they put a placard up, saying 'from the river to the sea', something like that.

In a second post also made last month Rahman slammed Muslims who sign up to work as police officers.

Mizanur Rahman pictured outside the Old Bailey, where he went on trial in 2007

He explained to an associate, who claimed to have a friend who is 'obsessed with joining the Met Police': 'Sometimes people's greed and their hunger for money leads them to make compromises.

'And those compromises, sometimes they can make you a sinner, and sometimes they make you kafir (non-believer or infidel).

'If you are in a state where you don't care what you do as long as you get the money then that's what's going to happen.'

He also blasted Britain for its role in the War on Terror in a video titled 'False narratives of Islam are nothing new – War on Terror Post September 11th'.

He argued: 'To borrow a term from (George) Bush there was this 'axis of evil,' this triangle of three major players, America, Britain, and Israel.

'These are the ones that have the most to benefit from keeping Islamophobia alive.

'They claim we are jealous of their way of life and that is why we are attacking them. It hasn't anything to do with the genocides that they are committing, the wars that they are doing, it is all to do with ideology.

'It is failed war propaganda that (has) failed over time.'

He went on to add that most of those convicted of terrorism in the UK were simply 'curious teenagers' who had 'downloaded a magazine' and ended up being 'labelled a terrorist'.

Rahman has previously used online sermons to denounce Israel's 'genocide' in Gaza and insist abortion is prohibited by Islam.

Choudary was jailed for 28 years in 2024 for directing banned terror group al-Muhajiroun.

Accountant Rahman, a father of four, was previously one of three men jailed for six years in 2007 after being convicted of soliciting murder of western troops in Iraq.

Rahman, then of Palmers Green, north London, had been filmed addressing a rally by loudspeaker, calling for UK soldiers to be brought back from Iraq in body bags.

He said: 'We want to see their blood running in the streets of Baghdad.

'We want to see the Mujahideen shoot down their planes the way we shoot down birds. We want to see their tanks burn in the way we burn their flags.'

In a rare Sunday hearing, a federal appeals court judge appointed by President Trump questioned how the Trump administration’s request to intervene in California’s ongoing midterm election in order to force state officials to set aside 20,000 ballots from voters whose citizenship it questions squares with the U.S. Constitution.

Judge Mark J. Bennett said that while the executive branch is “unhappy” with what California law requires for ensuring voters are citizens, the state has the right to determine the manner of its elections under the Elections Clause of the Constitution and Congress has not challenged that process, despite having the ability to do so.

“It seems to me that you want us to issue an injunction that is entirely inconsistent with the law the California Legislature has adopted pursuant to the Elections Clause,” Bennett said. “How can we do that?”

Matthew Donnelly, a Justice Department attorney, said the Trump administration has good reason to believe those ballots are from ineligible noncitizen voters, and has the legal right under separate federal criminal law to challenge their tabulation until state officials can prove otherwise.

Donnelly said both federal criminal law and state election law bar noncitizen voters from casting ballots, but “the problem is that California isn’t interested in enforcing that provision.”

The exchange came during an hour-long hearing before a three-judge panel of the U.S. 9th Circuit Court of Appeals, over the Justice Department’s unprecedented emergency request that the court order California Secretary of State Shirley Weber and Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk Dean Logan to immediately stop counting ballots from the 20,000 flagged voters until they can definitively prove those voters are U.S. citizens.

The appellate panel — on which Bennett was joined by circuit Judges Patrick J. Bumatay, another Trump appointee, and Gabriel P. Sanchez, an appointee of President Biden — did not issue a ruling, though a decision is expected soon given the emergency nature of the appeal.

The Justice Department sent letters to county elections offices statewide Friday demanding they segregate ballots from voters the Trump administration believes are not U.S. citizens.

How it will rule is unclear, though both Bennett and Sanchez hammered Donnelly with questions about how the Trump administration’s position squared with the law.

Rick Hasen, director of the Safeguarding Democracy Project at UCLA School of Law, wrote online Sunday that the court seemed “very likely to reject” the Trump administration’s appeal, with Bennett and Sanchez having “expressed great skepticism on both jurisdiction and the merits.”

In addition to questioning its constitutionality, Bennett also questioned how the appellate court even had jurisdiction over the case, given that the Trump administration appealed the matter to the appellate court before the lower district court had a chance to assess the facts in the case or issue an order.

“How do we have jurisdiction when the district court has said it’s going to rule expeditiously?” Bennett said. “I don’t see how we have jurisdiction.”

Bennett also questioned why the appellate court should ignore the claims of state elections officials that having to comply with the Trump administration’s demands in the few remaining days before the Nov. 3 election would be catastrophically disruptive to the election, given that the lower court has had no chance to assess those claims.

“Isn’t that sufficient to give us pause as to whether we should do exactly what [a state expert] avers is going to cause a catastrophe?” Bennett asked.

Bennett’s questions evoked many of the arguments made by the state in its own filings in the matter, which were repeated by California Deputy Solicitor Gen. Samuel P. Siegel during the hearing.

Siegel said there was “nothing” for the appellate court to review, given there has been no fact finding and no order out of the lower court, and that the administration’s demands were unconstitutional and illegal. He said the court did not have to wrestle with many of the arguments being put forward by the Trump administration, because it should simply reject the appeal for lack of jurisdiction and send the matter back down to the lower court.

Bumatay appeared most interested of the three judges in the damage done when noncitizens do vote, and the underlying question of how the state should handle objections to alleged noncitizen voters when they arise.

Siegel and Seth Waxman, an attorney for Logan, said there are existing processes for that — which require the challenger to present extraordinary proof — which they said the Trump administration has not done for the 20,000 voters it has flagged.

Sanchez repeatedly questioned the provenance of the administration’s list, asking which third parties were involved in providing the data. Donnelly could not answer, which Sanchez said raised questions about the reliability of the list.

The Justice Department appealed the case after a lower district court judge declined to immediately rule last week on its request for a temporary restraining order barring California officials from tabulating the flagged ballots.

In court filings, the administration argued that once mail ballots are removed from their envelopes in California, or a ballot has been cast in person, there is “no way to associate the voter with the ballot” or resegregate it from other cast ballots.

Attorneys for California, Weber and Logan have argued the demands are illegal, impossible to comply with before the Nov. 3 election and based on deeply flawed federal immigration and third-party data sets shown to be inaccurate and inclusive of legitimate voters.

The Department of Justice filed a lawsuit in federal court in Alaska demanding that the state be blocked from certifying Democrat Mary Peltola’s win if the other Dan Sullivan remains on the ballot.

In court filings, they have argued that the Trump administration’s argument “rests on mere suspicion, aroused by suspect data,” and that its lawsuit is at best “a reckless political stunt” and at worst “threatens to disenfranchise thousands of Californians this election.”

Independent elections experts have said the Justice Department’s efforts break with longstanding department policy of not wading into election-related issues within 90 days of an election, to avoid disenfranchising legitimate voters.

They also have said the Trump administration’s lists of alleged noncitizen voters and its authority to intervene are both questionable, including because the U.S. Constitution empowers states to run elections with oversight from Congress, not the executive branch.

The Justice Department sued California, Weber and Logan on Oct. 15, two days after Acting Deputy Atty. Gen. Trent McCotter wrote to Weber and Logan giving them 48 hours to set aside the ballots for the 20,000 flagged voters — whom McCotter claimed were “likely noncitizens.”

First Assistant U.S. Atty. Bill Essayli, the lead federal prosecutor in L.A., has claimed federal authorities have “overwhelming evidence” that tens of thousands of noncitizens are registered to vote in California, and has defended the methods used to identify those voters. He and other federal prosecutors in the state also recently charged 28 legal permanent residents in California with election-related crimes, including illegally registering to vote.

On Friday, the Justice Department sent letters to county elections offices statewide with demands that they segregate ballots from voters the Trump administration believes are noncitizens, drawing from the same list of 20,000 at issue in the case against Weber and Logan. In those letters, the Justice Department gave the county officials 48 hours to comply, and threatened them with criminal punishment if they refuse.

State officials have said neither Essayli nor other Trump administration officials have provided proof to back up their claims, and that their list of 20,000 voters is clearly inaccurate. Logan said a preliminary review of a subset of those voters found many were born in the U.S., weren’t registered and had never voted.

More than 3 million people have already cast ballots in California — and more than 500,000 have already done so in Los Angeles County.

The challenge to the 20,000 ballots in California is just the latest Trump administration attempt to intervene in state-run elections ahead of the midterms.

Federal judges in dozens of states have rejected Justice Department lawsuits demanding the states turn over their voter rolls to the Trump administration. Federal judges also have rejected multiple Trump executive orders trying to institute national voter ID and citizenship checks, and his administration’s attempt to empower the U.S. Postal Service to vet mail ballots.

Other cases — including over the Trump administration using unproven and inaccurate federal data sets to question the citizenship of state voters — are pending.

Democrats and voting rights groups are also bracing for fresh litigation from the Trump administration to challenge the outcome of pivotal election results the president doesn’t like — and are already defending against a lawsuit the Justice Department brought at Trump’s urging in Alaska, preemptively challenging a Democratic win in the Senate race there based on a third candidate sharing a similar name as the Republican incumbent.

La agenda del clima llega a las juntas de 17 grupos europeos

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Las resoluciones consultivas sobre los planes climáticos de las empresas siguen presentes en las últimas juntas de accionistas, pero baja el ritmo.

Las estrategias climáticas y de ESG (medioambientales, sociales y de gobernanza) siguen formando parte de los puntos del orden del día de juntas generales en Europa, pero descienden en proporción a años anteriores y el apoyo de los accionistas es inferior.

Diecisiete empresas cotizadas en Europa presentaron resoluciones consultivas relacionadas con el clima y sus estrategias climáticas ante sus juntas generales celebradas este año, según apunta el proxy solicitor Georgeson, que recoge datos de nueve grandes mercados del Viejo Continente, incluida España. Esta cifra es inferior a las 22 empresas del año anterior.

La mayoría de las votaciones tienen como escenario Francia (con ocho grupos, uno menos que el ejercicio anterior) y Reino Unido (siete). A ellas se suman el grupo español Aena y Ferrovial, que figura como empresa neerlandesa tras trasladar su sede a los Países Bajos en 2023.

El nivel de apoyo a las iniciativas 'Say on climate' retrocede por primera vez desde las juntas de 2022. La media de respaldo se ha situado este año en el 94,6% respecto al 95,3% de 2025. En 2024 fue del 92,6% y en 2023 y 2022 del 91% (cuando creció sobre el 97% de 2021).

El gestor aeroportuario español Aena presentó un año más a votación, con carácter consultivo, la actualización de su informe del plan de acción climática correspondiente a 2025. El 87,73% de los accionistas de la compañía que preside Maurici Lucena votaron a favor y el 5,93% en contra, mientras que cerca de otro 6,32% lo hizo en blanco. Este apoyo fue inferior al de la junta de accionistas del año pasado, cuando fue del 92,53%, que también retrocedió respecto al 96,1% de 2024.

El punto tercero del orden de día de la junta de Ferrovial sometía a votación consultiva el informe de estrategia climática. Tuvo el voto favorable del 95,06% de los accionistas, frente al 4,94% en contra. El porcentaje de aprobación cae ligeramente respecto al 96,28% de la junta de 2025, pero supera la media de los 17 grupos analizados.

Entre los puntos con mayor respaldo en las juntas europeas figura el informe anual de gestión del grupo británico de servicios de agua Severn Trent, que cotiza en la Bolsa de Londres. Este incluye el progreso de sus estrategia de sostenibilidad, que detalla su ruta hacia las cero emisiones operativas netas en 2030. Fue aprobado por el 99,99% de los accionistas.

Superaron un porcentaje de respaldo superior al 99% el voto consultivo a las estrategias climáticas de grupos como el francés OVH, uno de los grandes proveedores europeos de servicios de computación en la nube y alojamiento de infraestructuras digitales (99,85%); la energética británica SSE (99,77%); y la empresa francesa de inversión y promoción inmobiliaria Icade. Esta presento una propuesta Say on climate que recibió el voto afirmativo del 99,42% y otra Say on biodiversity, aprobada con un 99,45% a favor.

La multinacional francesa de gestión de la energía, automatización industrial y transformación digital Schneider Electric recibió el visto bueno a su estrategia climática del 98,03% por parte de los accionistas. Medieval Children Were Exposed to Shocking Levels of Lead, 1,000 Years Before the Industrial Revolution

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Children in medieval English mining communities faced lead exposure matching or exceeding that in Industrial Revolution cities, with social standing apparently shaping who bore the greatest burden.

Between the 16th and 19th centuries, lead producers in the Derbyshire Peak District of central England repeatedly remelted waste left by Roman and medieval workings. Reusing that material erased many of the industrial traces archaeologists would normally examine to establish when mining and smelting resumed after Roman Britain. A record survived, however, in the teeth of the people who lived there.

Lead preserved in tooth enamel has now provided the first definitive evidence that production was established by the middle of the seventh century AD. It also reveals the extent of childhood exposure. Even in the seventh century, lead concentrations in the mining communities matched or exceeded those found in people from industrial cities around 1750 to 1850.

Mining and smelting exposed communities to lead through contaminated dust, fumes, and materials. Tooth enamel forms during childhood and does not remodel later in life, preserving a record of the lead absorbed while a person was growing up. Measuring the metal locked within enamel allowed researchers to reconstruct childhood exposure from about 650 to 1500 and compare its extent across individuals and communities.

The research was led by the University of Nottingham in collaboration with the British Geological Survey, the University of Sheffield, York Archaeology, and Castleton Historical Society. Combining archaeology, history, chemistry, and environmental science, it is the first study to analyze lead contamination in people who mined and smelted the metal in western Europe’s largest medieval lead-producing region.

The samples came from three cemetery populations spanning the industry’s revival and expansion. Wigber Low, in the Derbyshire White Peak, dates to the seventh century. At Castleton, Joule Yard dates from the seventh to 11th centuries, while Spital Field dates from the 12th to 15th centuries. The research builds on decades of archaeological investigation, including earlier work by Colin Merrony at the University of Sheffield, who identified and excavated key medieval burial sites in the region.

To place the measurements in context, the researchers compared these communities with populations elsewhere in Britain, ranging from the Neolithic period through the Industrial Revolution. This comparison revealed how high childhood exposure already was during the early medieval period, long before the industry reached its greatest scale.

Lead author Christopher Loveluck, Professor of Medieval European Archaeology at the University of Nottingham’s Department of Classics and Archaeology, said, “The team is delighted to have finally confirmed definitively the date by which the early medieval lead industry was established in the Peak District, by the mid-7th century AD, through evidence provided by the people themselves, providing the foundations of western Europe’s largest lead-producing region between the late 12th and 16th centuries.”

By the middle of the eighth century, large-scale production had contaminated the wider environment around mining centers such as Castleton. Childhood lead exposure remained high throughout the medieval period, reaching its greatest levels in the late 12th and early 13th centuries. Historical records place the medieval peak of Peak District lead production in the same period.

That timing also connects the people’s teeth with evidence collected far beyond their communities. Previous studies of Alpine ice cores and sediment cores from river channels, lakes, and peat bogs across western Europe identified a rise in atmospheric lead pollution around 1200, attributed especially to British production. The enamel findings provide a direct link between those environmental records and the people living and working in one of Europe’s major lead-producing landscapes.

Within the mining communities, exposure varied with apparent social standing. People buried with high-status grave goods, including weapons, generally had much lower lead concentrations than those whose burials were less richly furnished. The pattern suggests that elites may have controlled or benefited from lead production while lower-status households worked more directly with ore processing and smelting. Children growing up in those households would consequently have faced greater exposure.

Isotopic analysis indicated that most of the individuals studied had grown up locally. This suggests that mining and smelting knowledge passed through generations within relatively stable communities, rather than the industry depending on large numbers of incoming workers.

Merrony said, “Our recent excavations in Castleton have provided a lot of new information about the people living in the Hope Valley during the medieval period. We now understand more about the size of the population in the valley, and we can see from the archaeological evidence that these working communities were involved in industries that required hard physical labor. However, this study has enabled us, for the first time, to see beyond this broader understanding and to look more deeply into the profound impact lead production had on individuals and the communities they lived in.”

Lead can damage the nervous system, kidneys, and reproductive system, making the childhood exposures a concern even for people who survived for many years afterward. Three women analyzed in the study had elevated childhood lead exposure and died between approximately 17 and 25 years of age. The relationship between childhood exposure and premature death is complex, so these findings suggest a possible association with increased health risks without establishing that lead caused their deaths. Pregnancy may have been a particularly vulnerable time because lead stored in the body can be released back into the bloodstream.

Dr. Anna Bloxam, co-director of the Castleton excavations, said, “We know from modern studies that lead can have profoundly harmful effects on the health and development of children: there is no safe level of exposure. All the individuals included in our study survived into adulthood, but they were exposed to lead while they were young and growing. We can tell from their skeletons that most of the men we studied lived until middle or old age, whereas the women primarily died much earlier, in young adulthood.

“Our results indicate that in these mining communities, girls and boys alike were raised in dangerously polluted home environments. This exposure will likely have caused negative health effects in life, and perhaps contributed to higher rates of premature death among women of childbearing age.”

The findings concern medieval mining and smelting between the seventh and 15th centuries and do not describe modern environmental conditions. The legacy of historic mining in the Peak District is now well understood and carefully monitored, with land management, conservation, and environmental regulation helping to minimize risks associated with former mining activity.

The results will contribute to the developing medieval archaeological research agenda for the Peak District National Park. A temporary display in the region during fall and winter 2026–27 will present the research alongside archaeological discoveries from the Castleton excavations.

The midterm races that could matter most to the U.S. economy aren't in Washington

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Data centers have emerged as a national issue during this midterm election cycle, and candidates for the House and Senate have seized on it in their campaigns.

But state and local officials actually have more sway over data center construction and energy infrastructure, so the winners of lower-profile races may hold the economy's future in their hands.

Hyperscalers have been spending hundreds of billions of dollars a year, with forecasts for 2027 topping $1 trillion. JPMorgan has estimated AI-related investment was responsible for about one-fifth of GDP growth over the past year. Analysts at ING believe AI will account for a third of growth in 2026.

With Election Day just over a week away and early voting already in full swing in many states, expectations for big Democratic gains could weigh on the stock market, which has sold off in recent days on fears the AI boom will go bust.

If there is a further stock slump, it will be because a "blue wave" at the state and local levels will usher in lawmakers opposed to data centers, Thierry Wizman, global FX and rates strategist at Macquarie, said in a note on Monday.

"For the direction of the U.S. economy, the composition and direction of governance at the state level may matter more than whether the Democrats 'win' the House or the Senate," he added.

That dynamic was on display on Tuesday, when Amazon canceled a plan to build data centers in Loudoun County, Virginia, after reaching an agreement with local officials amid widespread backlash from residents.

Amazon Web Services bought property for the project in February for $427 million, but the purchase reportedly blindsided county leaders who were already growing uneasy with the explosion of data centers in recent years.

Amazon vowed not to build data centers on the land and will repurpose most of it for mixed-use and residential development, while also donating 25 acres for a community arts center and recreational facility plus $5 million to a housing fund.

And in Texas, another AI industry hot spot, Republican Gov. Greg Abbott has turned from a data center booster to a skeptic as he faces a tough re-election race against Democratic state representative Gina Hinojosa.

Michael Pearce, chief U.S. economist at Oxford Economics, also observed that midterm races for state capitals and town halls represent a bigger risk to the data center buildout than what happens in Washington, D.C.

The Trump administration has already used regulations and executive orders to facilitate construction, and any attempt to pass comprehensive legislation in Congress will be slow, he added.

By contrast, local officials pose more immediate threats via moratoriums, environmental reviews, and bottlenecks in connecting new data centers to the power grid, he said in a note on Thursday.

The key midterm races for data centers are in Texas and the Carolinas as well as Midwest states like Ohio, Illinois, and Wisconsin, according to Pearce. And the outcome of the midterms could have implications for the presidential election cycle.

"If this year's elections show that an anti-data center stance wins political contests, it could put data center regulation at the heart of the 2028 campaign," he said.

Perhaps in anticipation of a more hostile political climate, data center construction shifted sharply higher in the middle of the year, Pearce wrote. Meanwhile, developers can soften local opposition if they can show that a data center will lower local tax bills or fund schools and roads.

Still, even if lawmakers manage to pass a moratorium on new data centers, it won't immediately slow the economy. Most of their economic value comes from the computing equipment inside, and that spending lags construction by six to eight quarters, Pearce estimated.

"This year's surge will keep supporting business investment over the next couple of years, whatever happens on the ground," he predicted.

Any slowdown in spending may actually be beneficial for the AI boom, according to Daniel Skelly, a portfolio manager at Morgan Stanley Wealth Management.

Despite the recent stock market volatility, the sector still enjoys solid growth and strong earnings, while the long-term AI investment cycle remains intact, he said in a note Wednesday.

"Also, ongoing power deficits, a shortage of people to build data centers, and political pushback against them may elongate the cycle and lower the odds of a blow-off top," Skelly added.
