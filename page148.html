JD Vance front and center during midterms -- dry run for White House bid?

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Vice-president’s visibility has risen as Trump’s popularity wanes, but path to inherit Maga throne is more complicated than it may seem

A day after JD Vance suggested Donald Trump would endorse him if he runs for president, the vice-president shifted awkwardly in the Oval Office as his boss was asked about the remark.

“He’s a very talented person, and he’s a very good person,” Trump said of the man standing behind him, with a forced smile this week. “But we’ve literally – I don’t think we’ve ever talked about,” the president claimed, leaving the assertion unconfirmed.

As the campaign for the congressional midterms has intensified, the vice-president’s visibility has risen. If Trump and Vance truly have yet to discuss his presidential prospects, they are firmly in the minority in Washington.

While many aspects of this political era remain extraordinary, the core question hanging over Vance is one that has been asked for decades: is the vice-presidency a platform, or a poisoned chalice, for those who dare to dream of higher office?

Having spent only two years as a US senator before Trump tapped him in 2024 as his running mate, Vance has hit the campaign trail in recent weeks like a seasoned veteran – making stump speeches on behalf of Republican candidates as the party struggles to retain its grip on the US Senate and House of Representatives amid Trump’s plunging approval ratings.

He has held rallies in states such as Iowa, Florida, Texas, Alaska, Georgia, Michigan and his native Ohio in a criss-crossing odyssey that compares to that of Trump, who has told voters to behave as though he were on the ballot.

With secretary of state Marco Rubio – widely seen as his main rival for the next Republican nomination – focused on fighting the fires sparked by Trump’s global agenda, analysts believe Vance’s campaigning roadshow is intended as a dry run for a 2028 presidential bid. Each stop enables him to build contacts and goodwill, which he can call on in the likely event of him seeking the nomination in less than two years’ time.

Matthew Dallek, a political historian at George Washington University, said Vance was trying to “build up chits” aimed at making Trump’s Make America Great Again (Maga) movement “remember that he was a good soldier”.

“He is trying to do what a lot of smart vice-presidents have done throughout history, and that’s to earn some credit out there with state and and congressional candidates,” said Rick Wilson, a former Republican strategist who now runs the anti-Trump Lincoln Project, “so that when he kicks up a campaign machine in two years, people owe him something”.

Time and again over the past two years Vance has slipped into “explainer-in-chief” mode, providing a more palatable narrative in the wake of Trump’s more extreme statements, impulses or policy decisions. Critics have branded the practice as “sane-washing”, whereby the verbally dextrous vice-president tries to use his facile language skills to normalize otherwise outlandish utterances or actions.

In recent months, Vance – after publishing a book on his conversion to Catholicism – has rationalized a meme posted by Trump on his Truth Social platform that seemed to depict him as Jesus Christ. He also tried to deny, or at least reinterpret, Trump’s words when he told journalists that he did not think about Americans’ financial situation in conducting the war with Iran.

And just last weekend, he defended the president against charges of corruption in a CBS town hall event, when told that many voters were concerned that Trump was making money from cryptocurrency. “You can make all kinds of criticisms of our policies and decisions, but I’ve not once seen evidence that Donald Trump has enriched himself,” Vance replied. (Trump’s personal fortune has surged from $2.3bn in 2024 to $6.5bn this year, according to Forbes.)

His slavishly supportive comments have naturally been welcomed by Trump, but they also highlight the problems Vance may face in trying to become the president’s heir as head of the Maga movement. While unstinting loyalty is the coin of the realm in the Trump-dominated Republicans, the president’s vice-like grip on the party presents a fundamental challenge to anyone hoping to step out from his shadow and assert an independent persona aimed at wooing the base.

The fate of Trump’s first vice-president, Mike Pence – who had to be evacuated to an underground shelter in the US Capitol on 6 January 2021, as pro-Trump rioters called for his execution following his refusal to overturn the 2020 presidential election result – serves as a salutary warning of the high price that autonomy may exact.

“Vance has two choices,” argued Kurt Bardella, a former Republican congressional staffer who left the party in 2017. “Either ride Trump all the way to the island of irrelevancy, or break with him and incur his wrath.”

“Neither are great options,” he added. “The political reality is that anyone who is tethered to Trump will face an electoral backlash. Trump will continue to erode democratic norms as his grip on power wanes.

“That will force people like Vance into untenable positions that they have proven unable to navigate. They’ll have to either double down on the crazy or break up with him and alienate the primary base he needs to win the nomination.”

Even if Vance were to attempt a clean break after securing the Republican nomination, the historical portents at not encouraging.

Al Gore attempted to put clear blue water between himself and Bill Clinton after becoming the Democrat nominee in 2000. “I stand here as my own man,” he told the party’s convention, in a bid to avoid an electoral backlash over the then president’s impeachment over his affair with Monica Lewinsky, a White House intern.

In the event, Gore lost the election to George W Bush – losing his own state of Tennessee, where he had been a US senator – despite excluding Clinton from the campaign trail.

“The Gore example is useful because it’s someone who tried to distance himself, didn’t do so very successfully – and he was doing so from a pretty popular president,” said Dallek of George Washington University. “When you’re vice-president, you can only get so much distance from the president, and if you try to distance yourself, you’re going to antagonize the people who supported the president, and create the impression that you’re not just disloyal, but two-faced.”

Vance, therefore, has limited options in how to pitch himself for the presidency.

“Even JD Vance doesn’t fully have a theory of the case yet of why he should be president,” said Wilson. “He is going to sort of play this out and say, ‘I’m going to see what the terrain looks like in mid-2027, then calibrate my message, my campaign and my personality to what I think will win.’

“But is not really something that works in the age of authenticity that we supposedly live in now. It’s harder for somebody to fake being anything anymore than it ever has been in politics.”

Underpinning such a wait-and-see approach is Vance’s reputation as a “shape-shifter”, capable of jettisoning previously held principles. Years before becoming a Trump supporter, he declared himself a “never Trumper”. He labelled Trump “cultural heroin” and voiced fears he could be “America’s Hitler” before forming an alliance with him after first presidency ended in 2021.

Vance’s self-anointed role as Trump’s “explainer-in-chief” positions him to sell himself as “all the things you love about Donald Trump without the craziness and the chaos”, Bardella suggested.

But Vance’s vitriolic personal attacks on Democratic candidates in the midterms, such as the Kansas Senate candidate, Adam Hamilton, and James Talarico, who is in a cliffhanger race with the Trump-endorsed Republican Ken Paxton for the Texas Senate race, could prefigure another weapon a Vance presidential campaign might use : demonizing opponents.

“One of the previews that we’ve had is that he is going to define the Democratic party as this radical leftwing communistic anti-American threat to the future of the country,” said Dallek.

Vance will need to sell himself to two audiences, according to Joe Walsh, a former Republican congressman who is now a Democrat: Trump’s Maga support base, and the broader electorate after winning the nomination.

“He cannot sell himself to the second audience if he doesn’t make the sale with the first audience, and that means sticking to Trump and going full Maga.”

But so well-honed has Vance’s rightwing identity become, argued Walsh, that expressing views that would appeal to Trump supporters would require little simulation.

“I don’t know what he actually believes, but he’s taken on a lot of these Maga beliefs,” he said. “JD Vance is more pro-Putin than people realize – more pro-Russia, more anti-Ukraine. JD Vance is very skeptical of Israel. Vance really does have a lot of the Maga beliefs.”

What may stymie his presidential aspirations, some observers believe, is not ideology but personality.

After being repeatedly mocked for his awkward persona on the campaign trail during the 2024 presidential election, Vance remains a grating presence on the stump, with attempts at humor often falling flat.

The trait appeared to be on display last week in the town of Tyler in Texas when a male supporter shouted: “I love you.”

“I love you too, sir. In a totally platonic way, to be clear. I’m the vice-president, not James Talarico,” he said, referring to the Democratic Senate candidate, whose sexuality Vance and other Republicans have repeatedly questioned, without evidence or obvious cause.

Tara Setmayer, a former Republican communications director on Capitol Hill, said Vance’s inability to project authenticity threatened his presumed frontrunner status. “JD Vance comes across as so manufactured. There’s nothing authentic about him, and people see right through it,” she said. “You can see that coming across when he goes out there and tells these jokes or tries to be funny and it lands like a lead balloon, because he’s not inherently secure with himself.

“As much as JD Vance thinks he is the Maga heir apparent, he’s not. There’s a lot of consternation about him. He’s not completely won the Maga base by any means.”

Wilson agrees that Vance was seen as an unsatisfactory substitute by many diehard Trump supporters, and said his ascension to the Republican nomination was not assured. “JD Vance is not Donald Trump, and he will never be,” he said. “The modern voter out there in the world is not interested in Diet Trump. They want the full experience.

“Vance is more facile than Trump. He can change his personality to adjust to the room. Trump, for all of his evils and corruption and craziness, is the genuine article. He didn’t change everything he believed overnight to achieve something political. He’s always been the asshole that he is. Vance is the asshole that you want him to be.”

The biggest encumbrance of all, argued Dallek, might be the tendency of the vice-presidency to become a poisoned chalice. Almost four decades have passed since the last serving vice-president won election as president.

“The vice-presidency historically was seen as a terrible job – very little power, and not that much of a stepping stone,” he said. “It’s tough to begin with for a vice-president to win essentially a third term, and there’s a reason why it’s only happened once in modern times [with George HW Bush in 1988].

“It’s not the best springboard for the presidency at any time, and especially when Americans hate the establishment, think Washington is corrupt, and distrust people in positions of power.

“JD Vance is still very much subject to the laws of vice-presidential politics – and in that, I think he is similar to a lot of his predecessors.”

In Lofoten, Norway, a Table Is Set Between Peaks and Fjords

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“Now you know what it’s like to be in the eye of a storm,” said our hiking guide, Audun Rasmussen, with a grin.

I tried to nod, but the wind whipped it into a wobble. We were nearly 2,000 feet up a steep, multi-edged mountain face, and simultaneously getting pelted by rain. If views over picturesque towns and freshwater lakes below are what is typically promised in this part of Northern Norway, today there’d be none. Instead, fog clung to every nook and crevice of the glacial hulks surrounding us.

A room at Holmen Lofoten framing the wild ocean and craggy shoreline

There was a sense of achievement, certainly. Rain had turned much of our trails into sinking mud, and rock faces to gripless stone. We had used our hands to push past birch and skidded on moss. And we would still have to make our way back down. But for now, we were in the clouds, hiding under rocky overhangs and finding reprieve in reindeer-meat sandwiches, dry fish bites, and chocolate-covered wafers called Kvikk Lunsj that translates to “a quick lunch."

It’s ascents like these, over rock foundations that vastly predate the Jurassic period, that draw close to a million visitors each year to Lofoten Islands. The tiny Norwegian archipelago, 68 degrees north of the Arctic Circle and two flights from Oslo, is a distinctive and dramatic landscape with spiky mountains rising from the Norwegian Sea and sandy beaches fringed by fjords. In summer, buoyed by endless daylight, adventurers arrive from Norway’s mainland to camp, climb, and swim.

But summer was past its twilight when I arrived in late September, and I had come to discover an entirely different side of Lofoten: one found not on its peaks, but on its plates.

Lofoten's rocky shorelines feature a nutrient-dense ecosystem and seabird colonies.

In a wind-whipped corner of Sørvågen, a village in the southwestern reaches of the archipelago, a cluster of mustard-colored cabins on stilts juts out into the sea. Once home to a cod factory, these buildings have been transformed into Holmen Lofoten, a unique culinary destination and hotel that has welcomed food lovers and adventurers from around the world since 2007. The traditional fishing cabins, known as rorbuer in Norwegian, are distinguished by their corrugated roofs, some of which are topped with insulating grass. Walkways connect the buildings to each other and to wooden decks facing the sea. Inside the guest rooms, large picture windows frame the shoreline. With its simple local furnishings and hand-hewn charm, everything about this place feels elemental.

A cluster of traditional fishing cabins make up Holmen Lofoten, a hotel and culinary destination.

Locally sourced furnishings at Holmen are kept deliberately simple so the views take centerstage.

During my four-day stay, Holmen would play host to Kitchen on the Edge of the World, an extraordinary culinary adventure launched in 2018 that welcomes acclaimed chefs into its kitchen four times a year, and brings guests around the table—and out into the surrounding wild. This, the 30th edition, would be helmed by Matt Orlando, a California-born, Copenhagen-based chef who has worked at some of the world’s most influential kitchens like The Fat Duck in London and Per Se in New York. For our part, we would feast and drink to our hearts’ content, with the landscape that nurtures every dish spread out just beyond the windows.

Ingunn Rasmussen, the warm, chatty matriarch behind Kitchen on the Edge (and hiking guide Audun's sister), took over the property in October 2001 and now runs it with her son, Håvar Holmen. “I wanted to build a space that felt like home, where we could all log off and reconnect with our natural roots,” she said when she picked me up from the airport. A fisherman's daughter, she grew up around this very site—in fact, one of the cabins still bears her sister’s childhood scribbles on the wall. After a period abroad, Ingunn returned determined to breathe life back into this corner of Lofoten. What followed was a meticulous restoration focused on preserving the original structures and historical details, and eventually, the addition of a couple of new buildings in the same vernacular to accommodate larger suites.

In Lofoten, there are often four seasons in an hour.

Traditional bunk style beds in a larger room at Holmen

On the first night of the multi-day event, we gathered in the living room above the kitchen. Like all of Holmen’s shared spaces, it is defined by rustic interiors, exposed beams, and raw, sculptural elements—foraged flora, taxidermy, found objects—that echo the weathered landscape outside. That evening’s dinner would be helmed by Holmen’s own chefs, a young and talented assemblage from as far as Sicily and Piauí, in the northeast of Brazil.

Trays circulated with shrimp croustades and cocktails that looked like Negronis but told a more local story: the vermouth was infused with foraged blackcurrant leaves, and the Campari steeped with dried berries and yarrow flowers. Each sip seemed to capture the autumnal palette I’d seen blanketing the hillside on our drive in. The nonalcoholic version was just as heady: an elderflower tonic with a syrup made from freshly picked wild hops from nearby. “Holmen was created because we want to show our guests the Lofoten Islands,” said Ingunn. “And food is a critical part of understanding it.”

The kitchen is the heart of Holmen, and guests are welcome to pull up a stool and chat with the chefs at any hour.

I wandered over to Mia Hjorth Hansen, co-owner of Gedulgt, a cocktail bar in Aarhus, Denmark, and resident mixologist at Kitchen on the Edge. Shelves around her workstation held glass jars filled with foraged and dried ingredients—cornflower, dandelion, and fireweed—that she was transforming into elixirs. While visiting chefs rarely return, Hansen is an exception: this is her twelfth Kitchen on the Edge. Still, there's been a through-line among the chefs. Whether Ana Roš, the renowned Slovenian chef behind the three-Michelin-starred Hiša Franko, or Simon Rogan, the UK pioneer of the farm-to-table movement, each has shared a commitment to land stewardship. “We take time picking them,” says Ingunn, “because we want [chefs] who will be considerate about the people and wildlife that make this [our] home.”

The first night of the event is always designed to be an icebreaker, and Ingunn warned us we’d be surprised by how quickly we’d grow comfortable sharing close quarters over the four days. We were a motley crew, including a geologist from Brunei, a committed adventurer from Dubai, and a Melbourne retiree who travels for intimate food events like this one. Our conversations followed us outside for dessert, onto a deck where we savored ice cream made with black currant leaves and lovage, topped with mascarpone made from Lofoten cream and finished with a meringue charred over binchotan, a Japanese white charcoal. A tracking app suggested the Northern Lights were tantalizingly close, and Holmen kept watch on the skies. It was a taste of what was to come: an intimate experience shaped by live-fire cooking and an embrace of the Arctic elements.

Chef Matt Orlando working his magic by the tradition wood-fired oven

The next morning, encouraged by Ingunn's invitation to explore every corner of the property, I wandered into the kitchen to watch Orlando and his sous chef, Tom Siebeler, prepare for their first dinner. “Anything that comes out of that wood-fired oven is going to taste amazing,” Orlando told me, working his way through a pile of reindeer ribs from a sustainable farm run by Sámi herders just north of Lofoten. Originally expecting chops, he was improvising garnishes to suit the ribs. Reindeer is one of many ingredients Holmen takes care to source locally, alongside cheeses, Lofotlam (a Geographical Indication-protected local lamb), and vegetables. “For us, knowing where things come from matters, even with the objects we use," Ingunn said. The exquisite tableware we were dining on, for instance, was made by local ceramicist Gunvor Tangrand, whose organic forms pay homage to the surroundings and textured surfaces incorporate old fishing yarn.

Orlando had arrived in Lofoten a few days prior with a case full of his favorite building blocks—barley, Danish yellow peas, several kinds of miso—but was most excited to work with what he could find in the immediate surroundings. He invited me to step outside to forage for his dinner menu, and we returned with chickweed, corn shoots, and sheep sorrel. “I love cooking in remote places,” he said. “Thinking on the fly and exploring the potential in things you’ve never worked with is where the coolest ideas come from.”

The writer foraging for herbs with chef Matt Orlando

Fishbone noodles reflect Matt Orlando's zero-waste philosophy.

Orlando, like Ingunn, is a bit of a visionary. Some years ago, he opened Amass, a circular restaurant in Copenhagen’s industrial Refshaleøen neighborhood, where friendly service and an approachable, low-waste tasting menu were paired with a harborside produce garden diners could wander through. Amass has since closed, but last year, Orlando opened Esse in a restored Nordhavn warehouse, and it received its first Michelin star just a few months later. Signature dishes, including his fermented potato bread, made the move, along with his commitment to environmental responsibility. At Holmen, that philosophy is a natural fit.

Later that night, as we gathered around communal tables adorned with wildflowers and candlelight, we squealed with delight when the potato bread arrived with allium butter, then again for the fishbone noodles, a masterful creation made by pressure-cooking fish bones and puréeing them until smooth. The collagen-rich mixture gives the noodles their slippery texture, which Orlando complements with smoked chili, sunflower-seed miso, and fermented chili cream. Served family-style, the meal was messy and fun, with utensils set aside so we could savor the main course, the reindeer, with our hands. As a pescatarian, I happily dug into my dish: hedgehog mushrooms grilled with chili-strawberry barbecue sauce and served with Lofoten chimichurri.

Foraging for Rowanberries in the hillsides around Holmen

The weather changes quickly in the North. Quiet and calm one minute, then wild and Baroque the next. “Four seasons in an hour,” joked Holmen. It’s this unique confluence of climatic conditions that makes Lofoten such a productive landscape: the interaction between the warmer Gulf Stream and cold Arctic waters supports marine life; the long hours of sunlight send plants into hyperdrive; and the sea spray nourishes lamb grazing pastures. “This land and sea have nourished us for generations,” says Ingunn, who leads guests on foraging walks, returning with fireweed flowers for immune-boosting tea, cloudberries for jam, or lovage for a vibrant, savory butter.

But to really understand this landscape is to understand its enduring connection to one particular food source: skrei, or Arctic cod. Here, tepee-style wooden drying racks known as hjeller are as much a visual fixture as Lofoten’s mountains; their distinctive frames have dotted the terrain for more than a thousand years. The racks are empty when I visit, but come January they will be stacked with skrei that have been caught at the end of their annual migration, from the Barents Sea to Lofoten’s milder waters where they spawn. Transformed into tørrfisk (wind-dried stockfish), this seasonal bounty has sustained the local economy for centuries. However, like many other coastal societies shaped by the migration and economics of Atlantic cod, Lofoten has been no stranger to ecosystem pressures over the decades, from shifting migration patterns to offshore fishing fleets.

Perhaps, it is no surprise then, that a more diversified agricultural movement is taking root. One rainy afternoon, I departed from the cozy confines of Holmen and drove an hour north to the fishing village of Napp, home to Lofoten Seaweed Company, whose products supply Holmen’s kitchens—and line our bathroom shelves. Co-founded by Angelita Eriksen and Tamara Singer, this entirely women-led team dons their wetsuits and hand-harvests seven types of local seaweed, including sugar kelp and nori, from the strong tidal currents of straits like Nappstraumen, transforming them into prized seasonings, even toiletries. Singer showed us around the processing facility inside a traditional rorbu, where Eriksen once cut out cod tongues—"a common way for kids in this region to make a bit of pocket money," Singer explained—and where they now sort, store, and age seaweed for top kitchens like Noma and Geranium in Copenhagen.

Holmen’s prep table, heaped with treats gathered from the wild landscape outside

Norwegian scallops with ocean truffle and shaved hazelnuts

They’re not the only ones. Across the islands, artisanal cheesemakers and farmers growing root vegetables and edible flowers are cultivating the region’s rich ecosystem. Back at Holmen, I spoke with local Bjørg-Elin Kärn, who specializes in food and agriculture systems at Love Food, an organization that connects small-scale producers with local restaurants. “Lamb, stockfish, and seaweed are all remarkably sustainable ways of producing food,” she said. “But conservation only works when the people living on the land and by the sea can make a good living. When a restaurant chooses these products, it helps keep that whole system alive.”

Matt Orlando with Holmen Lofoten's kitchen team

Kärn also works to steer tourism toward local food and agriculture, rather than simply encouraging visitors to “hitch tents and climb mountains.” We talked about the growing toll of [the wrong kind of] mountain tourism—a sore point among residents. Holmen’s own mountaineering guide Audun prefers not to share the names or locations of ascents anymore. He fears that overexposure on social media will draw the wrong kind of attention, not least from underprepared climbers who underestimate both the difficulty of the routes and the whims of the weather. A few years ago, Lofoten banned wild camping, restricting campers to designated grounds.

It is clear that tourism has come knocking on Lofoten’s door. In 2025, inbound visitor growth in Northern Norway as a whole, and Lofoten in particular, outpaced the rest of the country, bringing with it a familiar tension: how to welcome visitors while protecting what makes a place worth visiting in the first place. “Our hope is that people come to Lofoten for the right reasons,” Ingunn told me. “Ultimately, that will be what helps us protect the islands and our way of life.” Food, increasingly an anchor to how people travel, offers a way to bridge that gap, inviting visitors in while helping them understand the place they’ve come to experience. “Here, you can’t separate food production and landscape,” said Kärn. “Much of what visitors come to see, whether they know it or not, is shaped by food.”

In this way, Kitchen on the Edge hopes to sound a call for travelers to rethink why and how they visit places like Lofoten. Hospitality that takes guests beyond the plate and into the fields isn’t a new concept, but here it’s more than a perk: it’s a way of life.

One morning, when the weather cleared just enough, we piled into inflatable boats that cut through choppy waters over to an uninhabited island. Once a thriving fishing village, the island was abandoned when motorboats became commonplace in the mid-20th century, and fishing communities no longer needed to live close to spawning grounds. Today, nature has reclaimed this idyllic spot—where Ingunn spent childhood summer days picking berries and swimming—with waterfalls and carpets of reindeer moss.

While others traced the shore on foot, I sat by the jetty and took it all in: the mountains disappearing into the sea, the pair of white-tailed eagles circling overhead, the tiny berries and subarctic flora underfoot. Inside the Supermassive Debate Over the James Webb Telescope’s ‘Little Red Dots’

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The original version of this story appeared in Quanta Magazine.

Astronomers built the James Webb Space Telescope to pick up faint light from the first billion years after the Big Bang, a chaotic era when vast swaths of hydrogen and helium gas gathered into the chains of galaxies we see today. Even in the telescope’s first images, astronomers could see a whole zoo of mysterious smears of light.

One batch of objects proved especially difficult to interpret. They glowed blindingly bright, emitting red light with long wavelengths and shining as brilliantly as a whole galaxy. They were tiny, spanning just a pixel. And they were everywhere. A couple appear in almost every image Webb takes. In 2023, researchers started calling them “little red dots.” Astronomers have repeatedly pointed Webb toward the little red dots, wringing precious new information from these pixels of light.

Initially, researchers thought the dots looked kind of like galaxies. Later, they concluded that little red dots look more like the supermassive black holes that sit at the heart of most galaxies. These monstrous masses are themselves dark, but their formidable gravity violently vacuums up gas and other nearby matter, generating rings of hot, swirling detritus that completely outshine the stars around them.

Then, in the spring of 2025, two teams of astronomers simultaneously announced observations of a pair of little red dots that were unlike all the rest. In fact, they were unlike any object ever seen.

“In all the millions of [observations] we’ve taken with ground-based telescopes,” said Anna de Graaff, a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy in Heidelberg, Germany, and head of one group, “there’s nothing that looks like these sources.”

The two teams of astronomers propose that they are looking at a new astronomical object: a topsy-turvy lump of hydrogen that shines with the light of billions of suns while hiding a black hole deep in its core. They call it a “black hole star.”

In a paper posted in September, astronomers took this analysis a step further. They argued that the Webb telescope is witnessing the births of supermassive black holes inside the cores of colossal stars. “There is a fundamentally new phenomenon afoot,” said Rohan Naidu, an astronomer at the University of Hawai’i.

But not everyone agrees with this bold interpretation. It has sparked a flurry of follow-up research and reignited a fierce debate over the nature of these peculiar pinpricks of light. “The field has gotten very polarized,” said Anna-Christina Eilers, an astrophysicist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology who studies little red dots.

When all you can see is a speck, it’s hard to tell what you’re looking at. All you know about it is its color and brightness. Astronomers first argued that little red dots were distant galaxies on the cosmic horizon, mainly because of their brightness. But galaxies that bright would have to be huge—and there was no known way for them to grow so big in just hundreds of millions of years. Astronomers dubbed them “universe breakers” for the way they seemed to demolish the standard cosmic timeline.

Then they took a closer look. De Graaff led one survey, called Red Unknowns: Bright Infrared Extragalactic Survey (Rubies), and Naidu co-led another survey, called Mirage or Miracle (MOM). These were two of a wave of surveys that trained the Webb telescope on distant objects, including little red dots, for hours at a time. They tabulated precisely what shades of light were coming from each dot and how bright the shades were. This detailed color breakdown, known as a spectrum, told astronomers a far more detailed story than the initial observations had. Different atoms shine in subtly different hues, so the spectrum provided a sense of what the object’s particles were doing.

The bombshell discovery in the little-red-dot spectra was that the colors of hydrogen were smeared out across multiple shades. Usually, seeing such an effect means you’re looking straight at an exposed black hole. Black holes whip hydrogen clouds around them at furious rates, with the clouds emitting slightly different colors depending on their speed. The net effect is that instead of seeing just the hue of hydrogen, you see a range of colors called a broad line. The wider this range, the faster the fastest hydrogen clouds are flying—and the more massive the black hole.

Many astronomers concluded that big black holes dotted the universe, washing out the light of the stars in their host galaxies. As black holes, the little red dots would appear red because dust—grainy stuff much more complicated than gas—was blocking their blue light.

Yet they still seemed weird. Most supermassive black holes flicker as they gulp down chunky streams of gas around them. They also beam powerful x-rays across the universe. Most little red dots seemed to be doing neither of these things.

But that didn’t trouble astronomers much; they expected to see some strangeness during the pandemonium of the early universe. And at least the black holes weren’t breaking any cosmological theories.

Then, in the spring of 2025, de Graaff’s and Naidu’s teams unveiled the two strangest dots yet.

What made these two little red dots exceptional was how red they were. Webb picked up almost no light in the bluer hues of their spectra. And at a particular shade of red, the colors abruptly got much, much brighter. This feature, known as a Balmer break, is something you see when looking at a hot ball of hydrogen gas—typically, certain types of stars or galaxies, which are made of many stars. Deep in a star’s core, nuclear fusion pumps out heat and light, which slowly filters up to the star’s surface. There, hydrogen atoms can become energized in a way that blocks bluer light and lets through only redder light. These red colors have a hump-shaped spectrum that reveals the overall temperature of the star’s surface.

But the new little red dots couldn’t literally be stars—they were way too bright. And they didn’t look much like black holes either. Black holes have an assortment of ringlike structures of different temperatures. They don’t typically produce a Balmer break, or the red, hump-shaped curve indicative of a stellar surface burning at a uniform 5,000 or so degrees Kelvin.

Naidu and de Graaff concluded that they were looking at the first examples of something combining the vigor of a black hole with the outward appearance of a star: a black hole star.

From the outside, a black hole star would appear as a huge agglomeration of hydrogen gas. If our sun were replaced with a black hole star, it would extend a dozen times farther than the orbit of Pluto. Out toward the edge, the star would boil unstably, sloughing off outer layers and explosively ejecting mass. “It’s going to be a very messy system where stuff is being blown out and falling back in,” de Graaff said. “I wouldn’t want to come too close.”

Deep in the center, invisible to the outside world, the star would be powered by a black hole. This black hole would pull gas around it, dramatically heating it and pushing light and energy outward, which would keep the outer layers of hydrogen from collapsing inward. In this way, the black hole would form the “engine” of the star, analogous to the fusion-powered core of our sun. Moving outward, material swirling around the black hole would beam out a range of colors that would slowly make their way toward the surface.

And as with certain stars, the hydrogen near the surface would stop the bluer light while letting the redder light pass through. The end result would be a gassy surface shining as brightly as a more exposed black hole but with the Balmer break and smooth red hump of a 5,000-kelvin star, de Graaff and Naidu theorized. As a bonus, the gas “cocoon” would also block x-rays, and the gas wouldn’t flicker much—which would explain two mysteries surrounding other little red dots.

But what about the broad lines, supposedly caused by hydrogen swirling fast around a black hole? Another group provided a possible explanation.

That group, which included Vadim Rusakov, an astronomer at the University of Manchester, had been scrutinizing the broad lines of the best-observed little red dots. Broad lines take the shape of a sharp mountain peak. But Rusakov and collaborators noticed that in many cases, these mountains sloped slightly more gently than would be expected if they came from fast-moving gas around a black hole. So they suggested that instead of coming from rotating gas, much of the spread of the hydrogen colors could come from light scattering off electrons.

They digitally removed the effect of this electron-induced smudging from their data, Rusakov said. After that, the broad lines stopped looking quite so broad and started looking more like light passing through a sluggishly churning shell of hydrogen gas in a particular state, similar to what you’d expect to see from a black hole star. The three teams—de Graaff’s, Naidu’s, and Rusakov’s — posted their findings on March 20, 2025—“black hole star date,” as some of the researchers called it.

Black hole stars could represent a new stage in the development of a supermassive black hole: First, a black hole would form in the center of a shell of hydrogen, together with a baby galaxy of normal stars around it. Then, over time, the black hole would eat its way out of its cocoon, gaining mass as it cleared the hydrogen gas away.

“We are seeing the seed,” Naidu said. “This is the birth of potentially every massive black hole in the universe.”

The black hole star enthusiasts appeal to Occam’s razor, arguing that their theory gives the simplest accounting of these two little red dots, and perhaps of little red dots in general. But simple is subjective, and astronomers have spent the last year in a lively debate about what’s really going on.

Even years after the discovery of the first little red dot, not much about them is settled. Dale Kocevski, an astrophysicist at Colby College, recalls leading a discussion about them at an April 2026 conference in Aspen, Colorado. He started by recapping what he hoped would be an uncontroversial idea about their trace amounts of blue light. “The group erupted into argument, and we couldn’t even get past the first bullet point,” he said.

Many astronomers still argue that little red dots are traditional black holes—even the new duo. “The data is really compelling,” said Roberto Maiolino of the University of Cambridge. “I’m a little bit more dubious about the interpretation.”

For each point in favor of black hole stars, Maiolino fires off a quick rebuttal. The lack of flicker? In the early universe, black holes may have had a steadier food supply and may therefore have been tidier eaters. The lack of x-rays? Standard galactic black holes are ringed by a thick doughnut of gas and dust, which can block most x-rays. He sees no reason to suspect the little red dots of being anything other than standard supermassive black holes.

The redness of the new objects is striking, he said, and he agrees that it means there must be a ton of gas between the black hole and us. But that gas could come in the form of the doughnut, or as puffy clouds that fill in patches of the black hole’s sky, as opposed to the shell of gas around a black hole star. Maiolino agrees that electron scattering likely contributes to broadening the lines in the spectra of some of the little red dots. But electron scattering also smears hydrogen lines from supermassive black holes, he said.

Maiolino and his collaborator, Piero Madau of the University of California, Santa Cruz, argue that the redness of the dots comes mainly from the angle at which we see them. The reddest dots are those that we happen to see edge on, their gassy doughnuts blocking our view. Webb also sees some “little blue dots.” These could be the same exposed black holes, viewed top-down, Maiolino and Madau pointed out in spring 2026. They also appeal to Occam’s razor—in this case arguing that black holes are a simpler explanation for little dots of all colors.

At this stage either theory—black hole or black hole star—could match what the Webb telescope has seen. “I don’t think that there is a compelling reason to prefer one or the other,” said Mauro Giavalisco, an astronomer at the University of Massachusetts, Amherst, who has spent much of his career interpreting the spectra of distant galaxies.

To test their interpretations, astronomers need a clearer picture of how black hole stars might form and how exactly they expect them to look.

Over the last few years, Mitchell Begelman has been teaching a graduate student seminar on little red dots at the University of Colorado, Boulder. It’s kept him reading the firehose of papers coming out on the subject. In these mysterious objects that weren’t quite stars and weren’t quite black holes, he recognized a ghost from his past: the quasi-star. “Suddenly the switch flipped, and I realized that this is what quasi-stars should look like,” Begelman said.

Begelman had proposed the existence of quasi-stars back in 2006, along with Marta Volonteri and Martin Rees, to explain observations of what looked like impossibly massive black holes. Their quasi-star theory offers one way to make a black hole star: The core of a vast gas cloud collapses to directly form a black hole, gathering the remainder of the cloud around it.

In 2025, Begelman and his collaborator Jason Dexter applied the quasi-star model to the little red dots. They estimated that quasi-stars could quickly assemble themselves in a few million years before settling into a more mature form that would look just like little red dots. They would do this for tens of millions of years—lasting long enough for Webb to spot them.

In 2026, Giavalisco worked with a team to flesh out the quasi-star model as an origin for black hole stars, which he finds to be a natural way of explaining how little red dots could mask the signs of a feeding black hole. He points out that our sun performs the same trick, hiding its explosive fusion, just on a much smaller scale. “We have billions and billions of hydrogen bombs exploding every second, and yet we see none of them,” he said.

Giavalisco and his collaborators found that their new model of a quasi-star fit the spectra of de Graaf’s and Naidu’s objects even better than the initial models had. He thinks the quasi-star theory is a plausible explanation for the little red dots but remains open to other ideas. “I just want to know the truth,” he said.

In the meantime, researchers are starting to search for another distinguishing pattern: If little red dots are black hole stars formed in the early universe, then they should grow rarer over time as they each break free from their shells and reveal their inner black holes.

In an August 2026 census of both red and blue dots broken up into different eras, Kocevski of Colby College and his collaborators found exactly that pattern. In the data, as the universe approaches 2 billion to 3 billion years of age, the little red dots seem to vanish—preliminary evidence that little red dots, as black hole stars, might really be a puberty-like phase for many supermassive black holes.

Theorists are already working out what that puberty-like phase might be like. In another analysis, posted on September 8, Naidu, de Graaff, Eilers, and their collaborators tested out an assortment of techniques for deducing the mass of a black hole “seed” inside a black hole star. These hidden black holes seemed to be far less massive than standard, exposed black holes. The scientists propose that Webb is catching supermassive black hole stars—up to 1 million times the mass of the sun—in the act of incubating the universe’s first big black holes.

Some researchers, including Kocevski, still aren’t sure. Kocevski suspects that the universe is a messy place, and that some little red dots are truly as starlike as the black hole star camp is arguing. Others, he thinks, will be more like standard black holes, as the other camp argues. “I have a sneaking suspicion that we’re both right,” he said.

Fear and money: Financial lessons from horror villains Ghostface, Samara, and Candyman

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I was a card-carrying scaredy-cat through my childhood and early teen years, but the release of Wes Craven’s 1996 film Scream single-handedly turned me from nervous Nellie to horror aficionado overnight. Watching Neve Campbell’s Sidney Prescott fight back against Ghostface helped me understand the delights of feeling deliciously scared.

But watching horror films does more than just raise your heart rate. Scary movies reveal and reflect the cultural fears we collectively grapple with, making each era’s horror films a time capsule of society’s biggest concerns at the time.

Money has always been a common source of anxiety, so it should come as no surprise that some of the scariest villains in our favorite horror movies can also represent our financial worries. Here are the surprising money lessons you can learn from iconic movie monsters.

The villain: Death in Final Destination

Unlike most movie bad guys, we never actually see Death as a character in James Wong’s 2000 film Final Destination. In this movie, Alex Browning (Devon Sawa), a high school student, has a vision that the plane he and his classmates are taking to Paris will explode in midair. Alex, plus five other students and one teacher, are removed from the plane before takeoff—only for the plane to blow up just like in the premonition.

Unfortunately for Alex and the rest, by getting off the plane before it exploded they have cheated Death, which will now claim the lives of each of these accidental survivors, in the order of their intended deaths. And because Death is a bit of a diva, each victim is killed in increasingly unlikely ways.

The financial lesson: Understand real risk

While the Final Destination franchise as a whole makes it clear that there is no satisfactory way to avoid Death, we can still glean some important financial lessons from the film. Specifically, it’s vital to understand the real risks you face.

In the film (and in life), Death is inevitable and impossible to avoid. Alex tries to keep Death at bay by hiding in a remote cabin. He tries to avoid all risk and makes himself nuts thinking through the creative ways Death may come for him in the cabin. Similarly, risk-averse individuals might keep their money in cash rather than investing it. To avoid a possible loss of principal, they condemn their money to inevitably lose value to inflation.

At the other end of the risk spectrum is Carter (Kerr Smith), who intentionally stops his car on a train crossing to prove that the survivors have nothing to fear. Although Alex saves him from an oncoming train, the crash kills their friend Billy (Seann William Scott) in an especially nasty fashion. Carter reflects the kind of investor who doesn’t believe anything bad can ever happen to their money. Rather than risk-averse, they are simply risk-ignorant and may need to be rescued from their reckless choices.

The villain: Samara in The Ring

Samara (Daveigh Chase) from Gore Verbinski’s 2002 film The Ring is one of the creepier horror villains in recent memory. As the homicidal ghost of a little girl, Samara finds her victims via cursed VHS tape.

Once you’ve watched the strange video, you have seven days before she arrives to kill you—by crawling through your TV. The only way to save yourself from Samara is to make a copy of the tape and ensure someone else sees it, so the villain can continue killing without end.

The financial lesson: Just say no to MLMs

Just like multilevel marketing, Samara’s curse depends on constant recruitment of a downstream network. Rachel (Naomi Watts) is able to avoid death because she made a copy of the original VHS tape that she gave to her ex-husband, who Samara kills. The film ends with Rachel helping her 8-year-old son, who has seen the tape, make a new copy for someone else to find.

As with MLMs, the only way to stay afloat after Samara’s curse is to bring her new people. Each recruit will either be consumed by Samara or provide her with new victims to consume. Similarly, each new MLM “employee” will either lose money to the scheme or bring in more people to help their recruiter make more money.

No matter how curious you might be, just refuse any opportunity to watch a creepy videotape or join a friend for a “product party.”

The villain: Tony Todd’s Candyman in Candyman

The titular villain of Bernard Rose’s 1992 film Candyman was killed in a horrific lynching in the 19th century. After the lynch mob replaced his hand with a hook, they covered him with honeycomb and let a swarm of bees sting him to death. One hundred years later, Candyman continues to haunt and terrorize the residents of the spot where he died, which became the Cabrini-Green Homes public housing project in Chicago.

While the film offers a great deal of commentary about race, privilege, and class, the best financial lesson from the film was behind the scenes.

Candyman is associated with bees. They fill his ribcage and mouth, and he uses his bees to subdue Helen (Virginia Madsen), the main character. The late Tony Todd, who played Candyman, negotiated with Rose to receive an additional $1,000 bonus for every bee sting he got during filming. His 23 bee stings increased his salary by $23,000 ($55,236 in 2026 dollars).

The financial lesson: Create a win-win in difficult circumstances

Although the bee scene required Todd to have his mouth filled with the stinging insects—a process that reportedly took a half hour to complete—the actor recognized the significance of the bee symbolism for his character and the film. In a 2019 interview, Todd said, “Everything that’s worth making has to involve some sort of pain. Once I realized it was an important part of who Candyman was, I embraced it.”

But Todd didn’t just accept these truly difficult filming conditions. He negotiated a way to make each potential bee sting a personal win, while also giving the production a financial interest in keeping him as safe as possible.

It was inevitable that Todd would get stung during these scenes, so he found a way to make it less likely and worthwhile. When we are similarly faced with unavoidable difficulties, we can take a page from Todd and turn the inevitable negatives into a win-win scenario.

Scary and savvy

You may not expect to learn a thing or two about money when you sit down to watch a scary movie. But iconic horror villains can teach us better money management even while scaring us.

This Halloween, whether you revisit Death’s Rube Goldberg machines in Final Destination, Samara popping out of a television in The Ring, or Candyman bestowing a bee-filled kiss in Candyman, you can learn to better understand risks, ignore the siren song of multilevel marketing recruitment, and negotiate win-win scenarios in difficult situations.

But you may also want to be careful around stoves, televisions, and mirrors, too. Just in case.

Physicists Trapped a Single Antimatter Particle for Months. Now Comes the Hard Part

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For the first time, scientists can observe and control the quantum behavior of a single antimatter particle.

Physicists have trapped a single particle of antimatter for months and, for the first time, demonstrated that they can control its quantum behavior with extraordinary precision.

The particle is a positron, the antimatter counterpart of an electron. When the two meet, they annihilate each other, making positrons exceedingly difficult to study because, you know, electrons are everything.

Researchers at Northwestern University overcame this problem by suspending a single positron in a vacuum using electric and magnetic fields, then cooling it to nearly absolute zero.

“We actually coaxed this very fast positron to sit there and behave for months at a time,” said Gerald Gabrielse, the Northwestern physicist who led the research.

You hear the word ‘breakthrough’ thrown out a lot, but this really is one. It could allow scientists to compare matter and antimatter more precisely than ever before. Any unexpected difference could challenge our understanding of fundamental physics and offer clues to one of science’s biggest mysteries: why the universe is made almost entirely of matter rather than antimatter, even though the two were supposed to have been produced in equal quantities just moments after the Big Bang. The answer to this question may explain why anything exists at all.

Every familiar particle of matter has an antimatter counterpart. Electrons have positrons, protons have antiprotons, and neutrons have antineutrons. Their masses match those of their ordinary counterparts, but certain properties, including electric charge, are reversed.

Positrons are particularly difficult to study because electrons are everywhere. An encounter between the two usually ends in mutual annihilation, and there goes your experiment — and it’s not like you can just try the next moment again. Antimatter costs an estimated $62.5 trillion to $100 trillion per gram to produce, making it the most expensive substance on Earth.

“If you want to hold on to antimatter, you have to hold it in a container without walls,” Gabrielse told Scientific American.

To accomplish that, Gabrielse and his colleagues, including doctoral student and first author Thomas Myers, devised an elaborate procedure that begins with radioactive sodium-22.

When sodium-22 undergoes radioactive decay, it can release a positron traveling at tremendous speed. Capturing such a particle requires slowing it almost to a standstill without destroying it.

The researchers first passed the positrons through a cold tungsten crystal. Collisions inside the crystal robbed them of energy, although many particles annihilated along the way.

Some of the surviving positrons then picked up an electron, forming an unusual, loosely bound particle pair called positronium. In this highly excited state, the pair could travel together long enough to enter the trap but still far apart not to annihilate each other.

This seemingly counterintuitive step solved an important engineering problem. Because positronium is electrically neutral, it could slip through a tiny opening in the trapping apparatus without being deflected by the surrounding electric fields.

Once inside, those fields separated the electron from the positron. The electron escaped, leaving the positron safely confined.

The apparatus, known as a Penning trap, combines a powerful 5.6-tesla magnetic field with electric fields to hold the particle away from any physical surface.

Meanwhile, a specialized refrigerator cools the chamber to just 0.3 degrees above absolute zero, approximately minus 272.85 degrees Celsius. At this temperature, thermal radiation is almost completely eliminated, and the near-perfect vacuum minimizes the risk of collisions.

The entire process is excruciatingly painstaking. According to the authors, loading a single positron currently takes between eight and eleven hours.

But once captured, the particle can remain suspended for months.

Scientists have confined antiparticles and even entire antimatter atoms before, so that’s not the novel thing about this work.

What makes the Northwestern experiment different is the degree of control over a solitary positron’s quantum behavior.

Inside the trap, the particle circles in the magnetic field, behaving somewhat like a tiny artificial atom. Its motion can occupy only certain allowed energy levels, much as an electron in an atom can occupy distinct quantum states.

The researchers call this system a one-positron quantum cyclotron.

By delivering carefully tuned microwave pulses, they could make the positron jump from its lowest energy level to the next one. They could also flip its spin, an intrinsic quantum property that determines how the particle responds to a magnetic field.

Most importantly, they could detect these changes without disturbing the quantum states they wanted to measure.

“It’s a real kick to stand in my lab and watch a positron making transitions – ‘quantum jumps’ we call them – between its lowest energy states. How much more fundamental, how much neater can it get?” Gabrielse told New Scientist.

“This has the potential to become one of the most precise measurements of a fundamental constant in physics,” Stefan Ulmer, a physicist at the Japanese research institute RIKEN who was not involved in the experiment, told New Scientist.

All this effort is directed toward one of the most important questions in physics: Is an electron truly identical to its antimatter counterpart in every way that physics predicts?

One of the most important properties researchers want to compare is the particles’ magnetic moment, which describes the strength and orientation of their magnetism.

In 2023, Gabrielse and colleagues reported an extraordinarily precise measurement of the electron’s magnetic moment, achieving an uncertainty of just 0.13 parts per trillion.

Measuring the positron with comparable precision would allow the researchers to compare the two particles much more rigorously than before.

Historically, positron measurements have lagged behind electron measurements. The most precise direct comparison dates to 1987 and found agreement within 2.1 parts per trillion.

According to an independent assessment by physicist Paolo Crivelli of ETH Zurich, the Northwestern apparatus could make a comparison more than 30 times as precise as possible. However, the team must still complete the demanding measurements and account for potential experimental errors.

The stakes for theoretical physics are grand.

The Standard Model, physicists’ remarkably successful description of fundamental particles and three of nature’s four fundamental forces, incorporates a principle known as CPT symmetry.

The principle states that the laws of physics should remain unchanged if three transformations occur together: particles are exchanged for antiparticles, spatial directions are reversed as though viewed through a mirror, and time runs backward.

Among other things, CPT symmetry requires the electron and positron to have magnetic moments that are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign.

Even a tiny discrepancy would challenge some of the deepest foundations of modern particle physics.

And it could be relevant to an even older puzzle. According to our current understanding of the early universe, the Big Bang should have produced matter and antimatter in nearly equal quantities. As they encountered one another, most would have annihilated.

Yet somehow, a tiny excess of matter survived. That remnant eventually became every galaxy, star, planet and living thing we know.

Physicists have proposed mechanisms that might explain this imbalance, but none has been conclusively established by experiments and observations.

Experiments elsewhere are also on the lookout. At CERN, for example, the ALPHA collaboration has spent years studying antihydrogen, the antimatter version of hydrogen. In May 2026, researchers announced a hundredfold improvement in the precision of a key antihydrogen measurement. The results remained consistent with ordinary hydrogen.

So far, matter and antimatter have stubbornly obeyed the expected symmetries wherever these experiments have looked.

Nor has the Northwestern team found such a difference yet. The present achievement is the ability to trap, manipulate and observe a single positron’s quantum states. The decisive magnetic-moment comparison still lies ahead.

Nevertheless, the experiment has opened a new avenue for testing nature at an extraordinary level of precision.

“Current theories suggest that there will be no difference in the two measurements,” Gabrielse said.

“If it turns out they are not equal, that would be the biggest discovery in physics in the last century.”

The findings were reported in the journal Physical Review Letters.

Stop building digital employees. Start building intelligent companies

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The more news we read about the evolution of corporate AI offerings and implementations, the more I struggle with one thing: The industry is rapidly turning AI agents into some sort of simulated employees. 

There seems to be a rush to build the org chart in silicon: Microsoft gives agents identities, sponsors, access rights, and employment-like lifecycle management, and it explicitly governs them in a similar way as human identities. Salesforce even describes agents as “digital workers,” and promotes the idea of a digital workforce that tries to mimic human capabilities. ServiceNow has introduced individual AI passports specifying what each agent may do at runtime. 

Apparently, what we are trying to do is reproduce the human organization in software, instead of asking ourselves what a truly machine-native organization should look like. Now companies and managers brag about the number of agents they supervise, as if this were some sort of badge of honor. As usual, and as I say in all my innovation courses, learning is way more difficult than unlearning.

Is it smart to define our architecture with the employee metaphor? 

Why are we sticking to that metaphor? Human organizations have roles and hierarchies partly because we, humans, have characteristics such as limited attention, memory, and communication capabilities. However, I don’t think I need to explain to you that software does not have these restrictions, and therefore, does not necessarily need to inherit those constraints. 

You can do so if you want or if you don’t know how to do better: After all, identity and permissions are necessary. But bear in mind that such anthropomorphism is, at some point, going to become technical debt. A collection of increasingly autonomous agents can re-create silos, develop conflicting incentives, and even generate coordination overhead, but now at machine speed. 

Agents should be replaceable, not institutional 

Perhaps what we should do is treat agents as a temporary execution process: instantiated for a certain purpose, constrained, observed . . . and discarded. Among other reasons, because we should try to avoid at all costs letting knowledge, identity, or operational history become trapped inside particular agents. In the same way models should be replaceable components, agents should be too. 

I understand that AI agents are fairly new, and managers still look at them as if they’re some sort of “magic thing.” However, this sort of agent mystique should come to an end. An agent is a software process equipped with an objective, a set of tools, access to context and memory, and some capacity to decide what to do next, which ideally should be able to let us generate some sort of feedback or learning loops. But we should stop treating agents as if they’re magic, and even less as humanlike. They are not.

The employee metaphor is potentially useful for adoption and access management, but completely inadequate as the foundational architecture of an intelligent company. 

What must survive the agent

When we consider agents as replaceable components rather than allowing them to become new organizational silos, all the things that the company learns must exist independently of them. The company’s formal representation of itself—from its customers to its products, employees, suppliers, contracts, and processes, along with all the relationships among them—should persist. This is what I usually call “the nouns” and “the verbs,” and Palantir pompously calls “the ontology.” 

But the operational state of the company (not just what exists, but also what is happening, what has changed, and what remains unresolved) should be preserved, too, along with the objectives and trade-offs defined by leadership, the permissions governing every action, and the rules determining when a decision must be escalated. 

Exactly the same thing applies to institutional memory: Every relevant decision, action, and outcome should be recorded in a journal that no agent or model can silently rewrite, creating a reliable account of how the organization arrived at its present state. Maintaining rigorous control of the integrity of such a journal is essential for future accountability, and as we now know well, even for cybersecurity. 

That record must then feed back into future behavior, allowing the company to learn from successes, failures, and unintended consequences. Models may be upgraded or replaced, agents may be created or retired, and entire workflows may be redesigned, but the organization’s accumulated experience must remain. Otherwise, the company is not learning: It is repeatedly hiring intelligent amnesiacs.

Individual intelligence vs. organizational intelligence

In corporate AI, we need to understand that the important unit of analysis should be the organizational loop, not some sort of “synthetic worker.” Better models may improve individual cognitive performance, more agents may increase available activity and make their supervisors feel more important, but none of these automatically makes the company learn in a coherent way. 

The next enterprise platform will not look like a workforce or management system for bots. That’s clearly not the point. It will be the persistent substrate in which models and agents can be exchanged while the company’s accumulated intelligence remains safe and under control. 

The still missing product category is the system that preserves state, authority, evidence, and learning across models and agents. But don’t worry: It will not be missing forever.
