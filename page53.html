Flick saca del ostracismo a Frenkie de Jong, pero no le alcanza

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

El neerlandés no había disputado ni un minuto esta temporada en la Liga

Frenkie de Jong no había disputado ni un solo minuto en la Liga. Y cuando se preguntaba por él en la Ciudad Deportiva, su futuro se vislumbraba lejos del Camp Nou. En el Barça aseguraban haberse cansado del neerlandés, sobre todo después de la gestión de la lesión de rodilla en el último Mundial: según el club, no avisó de sus dolencias y se marchó de vacaciones. No era la primera vez que De Jong se enfrentaba a los despachos. Esta vez, en cambio, se presentaban dos inconvenientes: Hansi Flick se había alineado con el club, y la llegada de Rodri ponía en jaque su estatus. Contra el Madrid, sin embargo, cuando el alemán necesitaba remontar, lo mandó al campo como interior. Poco importó que durante la semana lo hubiera hecho entrenar como central.

Antes del duelo, a Flick le preocupaba cómo romper al Madrid. Esperaba un rival rácano, más ocupado en destruir que en construir, dispuesto a desquiciar a los jóvenes del Barcelona. El sábado, en la última sesión antes del clásico, el preparador azulgrana no alteró demasiado su plan habitual: rondos, ejercicios de presión, ataque en estático y un partidillo. Pero a la hora de enseñar los vídeos, el alemán insistió en cómo romper la defensa blanca. Repitió imágenes de partidos del Madrid en las que remarcaba cómo había que atacar para perforar un bloque replegado cerca de su área. De Jong, en el partidillo como central, en la pelota parada con los suplentes. Así lo explican distintos presentes en el último día de trabajo antes de la activación de este domingo por la mañana.

No esperaba Flick una presión adelantada del Madrid, y mucho menos que saltara al campo no solo a disputarle la tenencia del cuero, sino a tejer posesiones largas. La apuesta de Mourinho por un centro del campo más atrevido, con Bernardo Silva como compañero de baile de Tchouaméni, no solo pilló por sorpresa al Barcelona: también restó claridad a quienes más debían tenerla, Rodri y Pedri. Y eso que llegaban frescos. Con la mirada puesta en el clásico, Flick dejó al madrileño en el banquillo de inicio en el Parque de los Príncipes ante el PSG, y dosificó también al canario, que jugó algo más de una hora.

Resultó que no era una cuestión de energía, sino de fútbol. Al Barcelona no le funcionaba la presión y tampoco podía controlar el partido. Se repetían las pérdidas en la medular, con Pedri como primer señalado. Sucedía algo similar a lo que ya había pasado en París: cada balón perdido se convertía en una puñalada a la espalda de la defensa, con los velocistas del Madrid lanzados.

Entonces, con un Barcelona extraviado, la lesión de Xavi Espart agravó la crisis: Cancelo pasó a defender en el costado derecho, el mismo por el que ataca Lamine Yamal. Es decir, por momentos, Diomande y Cucurella dispusieron de tiempo y espacio para dominar la banda cuando el Barça no tenía el cuero.

Y Flick, que acostumbra a mover el banquillo cuando el equipo no funciona, aguardó. Eso sí, tocó el ego de sus jugadores. Bastó para despertar a Pedri. El hasta entonces errático canario afinó la tiza para encontrar a Raphinha, que volvió a parecerse al de antes del parón de selecciones. Es decir, al de antes de la lesión muscular.

Pero cuando parecía que el banquillo no podía mejorar lo que había en el campo, Flick sorprendió: Frenkie de Jong al campo. La presencia de De Jong activó al Barcelona. El neerlandés incluso tuvo su ocasión para marcar, precisamente tras un pase de otro de los olvidados por Flick que volvió a la palestra, Alejandro Balde. El lateral solo había disputado 12 minutos en la Liga.

Pero cuando el Barça ya olía la remontada, otra pérdida en la medular, esta vez de Gabriel Jesus, acabó en el segundo gol de Mbappé. No encontró Flick la clave del partido, ni siquiera cuando parecía haber dado con ella tras rescatar del ostracismo a De Jong. No le alcanzó.

Brazil election: Five economic challenges facing Brazil’s re-elected President Lula

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sign up now: Get ST's newsletters delivered to your inbox

Leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva’s (right) won Brazil’s run-off election on Oct 25 and is set to begin his fourth term in January.

BRASILIA – Brazil’s economy has beaten expectations in recent years, but mounting debt and entrenched structural problems could complicate leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva’s fourth term, which begins in January.

Here are the five main economic challenges he faces.

Brazil’s public debt has surged more than 10 percentage points during Lula’s current term to over 82% of gross domestic product, driving up borrowing costs.

The root cause is years of rising mandatory spending, leaving under 8% of the federal budget adjustable.

Lula has sent mixed fiscal signals, at times dismissing debt worries as “nonsense”, while arguing strong economic growth would resolve the problem.

His team has pledged to keep the government’s current budget rules, which cap spending growth and set primary balance targets – revenues minus expenditures, excluding interest payments on public debt – and hinted at tightening them.

Hitting those targets will be harder, given Lula’s promise to raise the minimum wage faster than inflation, which also lifts pension costs, after already raising welfare benefits and promising new spending.

Even with interest rates at 13.75%, Brazil’s annual inflation of 4.6% remains above the central bank’s tolerance range of 1.5% to 4.5%.

The central bank delivered its fifth straight rate cut in September on growing signs of an economic slowdown, but markets expect inflation to remain above the official 3% target until as late as 2029.

Lula’s platform promises lower interest rates through fiscal discipline that spares social programmes, but most economists say rates will only fall faster if Brazil cuts spending, and it is unclear how he will square the two.

A record nearly 84 million Brazilians and 9.3 million businesses are behind on debt payments, according to credit bureau Serasa, as high interest rates bite.

That has fuelled public dissatisfaction despite strong indicators such as record-low unemployment and wage growth outpacing inflation.

Lula plans a new round of debt relief, this time through discounted purchases of distressed household loans from banks, plus tax debt forgiveness for some small business owners. Analysts say such measures offer only temporary relief.

The government has also discussed with the central bank ways to rein in heavy use of easy but expensive credit, such as credit cards and unsecured consumer loans, though it has not detailed any proposals.

Lula will likely struggle to push his economic agenda through Congress after voters elected the most conservative legislature since Brazil’s return to democracy in the 1980s.

Although he defeated right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro in the run-off on Oct 25, Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party and its allies are set to control both chambers.

Lula’s aides have signalled he will keep pushing to curtail tax breaks, but lawmakers have repeatedly resisted measures that raise the effective tax burden.

During his current term, Lula has struggled to name nominees and win Senate approval for them at key institutions, including the Supreme Court, central bank, securities regulator and antitrust watchdog, and that is likely to be even more difficult ahead with the rightward shift of Congress.

Lula won congressional approval in 2023 for a sweeping overhaul of Brazil’s consumption taxes, and a new value-added tax is set to take effect in January.

With under three months to go, the government has still not disclosed the effective rate, which independent analysts estimate will be around 28%.

Fearing a backlash during the campaign, the administration has also held off on sending Congress a Bill to regulate a new tax on products considered harmful to health or the environment, such as cigarettes and alcohol.

That delay could push back revenue from the tax and has fuelled criticism of the government’s handling of tax policy. REUTERS

Trump critic Lula wins reelection in Brazil, securing fourth presidential term

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Brazil’s President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a vocal critic of U.S. President Donald Trump, has been reelected in a result that provides relief to critics of Trump and keeps some distance between Latin America’s biggest economy and Washington

RIO DE JANEIRO -- Brazil’s President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a vocal critic of U.S. President Donald Trump, was reelected Sunday in a result that keeps some distance between Latin America’s biggest economy and Washington.

With 100% of the vote counted, Brazil’s electoral court announced that the soon to be 81-year-old Lula held an unassailable lead. The announcement capped an evening of chaos at polling places across Brazil, following a last-minute decision to extend voting by two hours. That decision by a Supreme Court justice may trigger legal action by opponent Flávio Bolsonaro.

His triumph bucks a trend in Latin America where increasing numbers of right-of-center presidental candidates allied with Trump have been winning, further extending the United States’ influence in the region. Lula’s victory leaves Mexico and Brazil, Latin America’s two most populous nations, with left-of-center leaders. It also positions Latin America’s largest economy, with a GDP of $2.6 trillion per year, to remain independent of the U.S., despite the pressure of increased tariffs imposed by Trump.

The announcement kicked off a celebration of supporters in and out the Sao Paulo hotel where Lula watched the count with First Lady Rosângela da Silva, allies, and friends.

Lula’s victory, if confirmed by electoral authorities, will give him his non-consecutive fourth term as president. The next term begins on Jan. 5, 2027.

It is the first time since Brazil’s military dictatorship ended in 1985 that the second-highest vote getter in the first-round elections won the presidency. Bolsonaro had 47% of the vote in the first round to Lula’s 45%, a difference of 2.2 million votes.

Lula and Sen. Bolsonaro have yet to address their supporters.

Sunday’s victory will likely be the last of the veteran leader’s political career. Lula began his political career in the 1970s as a union leader. He then helped found the Workers’ Party, which he represented as a presidential candidate three times until he finally won it in 2002. He was reelected four years later and was succeeded by one of his cabinet ministers, Dilma Rousseff, in 2010. She was reelected in 2014.

Rousseff was impeached for accounting maneuvering in 2016 amid a sprawling corruption probe, which also ensnared Lula. He tried to run for president again in 2018 but was jailed in March after being convicted.

Lula was released from prison after 580 days and saw Brazil’s Supreme Court annul his sentence after police found messages that showed the judge who convicted him was biased. He quickly became one of former President Jair Bolsonaro’s biggest critics and narrowly beat him in 2022.

Lula admitted in 2024, two years into the job, that his administration had done little that far to earn the support of his voters.

Since he lost the first round, Lula reorganized his campaign, appealed to young voters, put millions of Brazilians on the streets to defend democratic values and his legacy, raised fears of another Bolsonaro administration and boosted several government initiatives.

Lula also defended Brazil’s sovereignty and insisted that a Bolsonaro presidency would allow the U.S. to exert control over Brazil.

He joins Trump and Nicaragua’s President Daniel Ortega — all age 80 — as the oldest presidents in the Americas.

Savarese reported from Sao Paulo. Brigida reported from Brasilia.

SpaceX closes $40 billion debt deal for AI chips, Elon Musk says

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Elon Musk said SpaceX has closed $40 billion in debt financing to purchase graphics processing units, or GPUs, used to power artificial intelligence systems.

In a post on X on Sunday, Musk said SpaceX had closed the financing, adding that the company was investment grade and generated revenue from its Starlink satellite internet and rocket launch businesses.

In a follow-up post, he added that the financing, primarily consisted of callable debt for graphics processing units, is expected to arrive in the first and second quarters of 2027.

Bloomberg reported earlier, citing people familiar with the matter, that SpaceX was in talks with banks and investors to raise $40 billion to purchase Nvidia chips.

The proposed financing would use a special purpose vehicle to keep the debt off SpaceX's balance sheet and preserve its investment-grade credit ratings, according to Bloomberg.

Goldman Sachs was leading talks with investors, including Apollo Global Management, over the financing structure, according to Bloomberg.

The financing underscores the enormous capital requirements of the AI boom, as technology companies pour billions of dollars into advanced chips and data centers to support increasingly powerful AI models.

SpaceX has expanded aggressively into artificial intelligence this year, referring to the business as "Super Intelligence," or SI.

The segment has become the company's largest business unit, with third-quarter revenue surging ninefold to $7.56 billion from $813 million a year earlier, according to its latest earnings release.

Musk also said that debt markets have largely shut off access to financing for companies without substantial revenue outside AI data centers.

He predicted that more than 200 so-called neocloud companies, which provide cloud computing infrastructure specifically designed for artificial intelligence and machine learning workloads, would face widespread failures and consolidation, with only a small number surviving.

Kopf-an-Kopf-Rennen in Brasilien: Lula gewinnt Stichwahl - Bolsonaro erkennt Niederlage nicht an

Der Sieger der Stichwahl um das Präsidentenamt in Brasilien steht fest: Nach einem knappen Rennen gewinnt Amtsinhaber Lula vor seinem Herausforderer Bolsonaro. Der will seine Niederlage anfechten.

Bei der Stichwahl um das Präsidentenamt in Brasilien hat sich Amtsinhaber Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva den Sieg gesichert. Wie die Wahlbehörde am Sonntag (Ortszeit) offiziell mitteilte, besiegte der linke Politiker seinen Kontrahenten, den rechten Senator Flavio Bolsonaro. Der 80-jährige Lula tritt mit dem Wahlsieg seine bereits vierte Amtszeit an. Der Wahlausgang gilt als Überraschung, da Lula im ersten Wahlgang noch hinter dem Sohn des inhaftierten früheren Präsidenten Jair Bolsonaro gelegen hatte. Sein Sieg bremst eine Welle rechter Wahlerfolge in Lateinamerika und durchkreuzt Hoffnungen von US-Präsident Donald Trump auf einen weiteren Verbündeten in der Region.

Nach Bekanntgabe der Wahlergebnisse weigerte sich Flavio Bolsonaro, seine Niederlage einzugestehen, und kündigte an, eine Annullierung der Abstimmung zu beantragen. Bolsonaro nannte die Entscheidung eines Richters am Obersten Gerichtshof, die Öffnungszeiten der Wahllokale für die Stichwahlen um zwei Stunden zu verlängern, inakzeptabel. Es sei unmöglich, das Wahlergebnis anzuerkennen, sagte er in der Hauptstadt Brasilia.

Lula betonte in seiner Siegesrede in São Paulo, er werde für alle 215 Millionen Brasilianer regieren. "Wir haben keinen Wahlgegner mehr", sagte der Präsident. Er kündigte an, mit dem vom Volk gewählten konservativen Kongress zusammenzuarbeiten und sich in den kommenden zwei Monaten mit Wirtschaftsvertretern treffen zu wollen. Zugleich forderte er eine Regulierung digitaler Plattformen. Man habe eine ausländische Einmischung über diese Kanäle abgewehrt. "Brasilien ist nicht der Hinterhof für andere", erklärte Lula. Die Wähler seien davon überzeugt worden, dass die Stimmabgabe zum Schutz der Demokratie in Brasilien extrem wichtig sei.

Meinungsforschern zufolge verhalf eine unerwartet hohe Wahlbeteiligung Lula zum Sieg. Die Wähler hätten sich trotz gewisser Bedenken für einen bekannten Weg entschieden, der bestimmte Rechte garantiere, erklärte Leonardo Barreto vom Beratungsunternehmen Think Policy. Für Lula markiert der Sieg ein weiteres politisches Comeback: Der frühere Gewerkschafter saß zwischen 2018 und 2019 wegen Korruptionsvorwürfen im Gefängnis, die später aufgehoben wurden. Im Wahlkampf hatte er auf sinkende Armut und Arbeitslosigkeit in seiner dritten Amtszeit verwiesen.

Zahlreiche Staats- und Regierungschefs aus Lateinamerika gratulierten Lula zum Sieg. Der chilenische Präsident José Antonio Kast erklärte, man werde weiter an einer gemeinsamen Agenda zur wirtschaftlichen Integration und zur Bekämpfung der organisierten Kriminalität arbeiten. Auch die mexikanische Präsidentin Claudia Sheinbaum sowie die Staatschefs von Paraguay, Uruguay, Bolivien, Kuba und Panama übermittelten ihre Glückwünsche.

Trotz des Wahlsiegs steht Präsident Lula nun vor massiven Herausforderungen. Hohe Zinsen und eine Staatsverschuldung von über 82 Prozent der Wirtschaftsleistung belasten das Land. Zudem sieht er sich dem konservativsten Kongress gegenüber, in dem Bolsonaros Liberale Partei (PL) und ihre Verbündeten in beiden Kammern über Mehrheiten verfügen.

Brazil election: Lula, iconic working class president, wins historic fourth term in dramatic comeback

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva won by more than four million votes.

SAO PAULO – Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva won an unprecedented fourth term on Oct 25, the country’s electoral court said, in a shock result that defied polls, precedent and global anti-incumbent sentiment to put the brakes on a wave of right-wing election victories across Latin America.

Lula, an icon of the global left and the most dominant force in Brazilian politics in this century, defeated Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the eldest son of former president Jair Bolsonaro.

The senator had finished first in the election’s first round but lost in the runoff by over four million votes, said he would ask for the result to be annulled due to a Supreme Court ruling on Oct 25 that extended voting for two hours.

Bolsonaro’s refusal to concede defeat has echoes of his father’s reaction after losing to Lula in 2022. Former president Jair Bolsonaro never accepted his loss and was later given a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup to remain in power.

The scale of Flavio Bolsonaro’s loss to Lula – by more than twice his father’s losing margin – and the fact his campaign also asked for an extension due to rains in the south, make any annulment unlikely, analysts said.

Mario Braga, a Latin America analyst at the risk consultancy RANE, said there could be right-wing protests in coming days, but “it’s very improbable that it will lead to the reversal of the result”.

For Lula, his victory marks another comeback in a career full of them. The 80-year-old was jailed from 2018 to 2019 on corruption charges that were later overturned, paving the way for him to beat Jair Bolsonaro in the 2022 election.

“He is a statesman, a giant,” leftist federal lawmaker Erika Hilton, who was with Lula in Sao Paulo during the vote count, told Reuters.

At times, Lula’s re-election looked like a long shot, as voters soured on him and his Workers’ Party after years of corruption scandals and rising prices.

Sweeping societal changes also left the former metalworker and union leader looking out of touch with an increasingly app-based economy and its growing ranks of informal workers.

A Tokyo-listed exchange-traded fund tracking Brazil’s Bovespa index fell nearly 15% in Asia trade on news of Lula’s victory.

Pollsters said better-than-expected turnout had powered Lula to victory. Leonardo Barreto, the co-founder of consultancy Think Policy, said voters had chosen Lula’s familiar embrace over Bolsonaro’s novelty.

“People today are choosing between change, with some apprehension, and a known path, which guarantees certain rights,” he said.

During the campaign, Lula pointed to a track record of falling poverty and inequality, while unemployment, illiteracy and deforestation hit record lows during his third term.

He warned that Bolsonaro would cut welfare spending, backslide on social advances and hand over Brazil’s sovereignty to US President Donald Trump, whose State Department led a campaign to get the Bolsonaros back to power.

Lula’s win deprives Trump of a major addition to what has been a growing bloc of friendly right-wing Latin American governments that have embraced Washington’s agenda on security and migration.

Celso Amorim, a former Lula foreign minister, told Reuters the leftist “would be very important for establishing a point of balance in Latin America. Lula is that balance”.

Lula becomes the first candidate since Brazil’s return to democracy in the 1980s to be elected after finishing second in the first round.

Lula, who was president between 2003 and 2011, campaigned on a potent mix of pragmatism and populism that has shaped his career in national politics for half a century, rolling out a slew of reforms aimed at improving the lives of workers and poor Brazilians.

These gained steam with late-campaign pledges that ranged from free nationwide weekend public ​transport, which has to be formally submitted to Congress, to a ‌15% increase in his trademark welfare programme that is already being implemented.

Lula also championed a constitutional amendment that became Brazil’s biggest labour reform in decades, which passed with wide support in Congress.

Still, Lula’s fourth term could be his most difficult yet, after Brazil elected its most conservative Congress since returning to democracy in the 1980s.

Combined, Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party (PL) and allied parties could control over 280 seats in the Lower House and up to 50 seats in the Senate, enough for comfortable opposition majorities in both chambers.

That may deepen recent trends of presidents ceding power to Congress on the budget, major legislation and key nominations.

Lula faces an economy that his government forecasts will grow a meager 2.3% in 2027, adding to fiscal concerns tied to high interest rates and public debt that has risen past 82% of GDP, the highest since the pandemic.

The President’s critics blame spending, though borrowing costs have driven most of the increase in debt.

Goldman Sachs said it expects Lula to make modest progress at most to contain spending in a fourth term, as he stays committed to a state-led growth model.

Surging household debt also concerns economists. Trying to address the issue, Lula rolled out a provisional ban on online betting and unveiled a new debt relief program during the campaign.

The incoming right-wing Congress will try to pass structural reforms that favor businesses, experts say, prompting spending cuts that could trigger popular backlash.

Departments water down human decision making standards as AI takes hold

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Government agencies are quietly reducing human oversight of artificial intelligence (AI) and are regularly altering public guidance on internal AI uses, making it harder for outsiders to verify if it is being used safely and for valid reasons, tech experts say.

The government agency in charge of AI implementation has admitted it is not tracking changes agencies make to their AI transparency statements, the principal public documents explaining how individual government services are using AI and what ethical and safety standards are in place.

A data scientist has meanwhile tracked dozens of changes to the documents across some of the government's most powerful departments, one of which - the Department of Social Services (DSS) - The Canberra Times learned has changed its statement seven times since February 2025.

The Digital Transformation Agency (DTA) which oversees implementation and compliance with AI policy disavowed responsibility for the changes, with an executive saying it was "not a regulator," even as scientists warned it is becoming unclear who has oversight of government AI use and called for new legislation to enshrine protections and unions warned that public servants may be left carrying the risks.

A submission to the parliamentary joint select committee on AI by Dr Gabrielle Josling warned tracking government AI use under current rules was opaque and inconsistent, requiring scientists in some cases to search "procurement records, vendor publications, speeches and FOI [freedom of information] releases" to form an accurate understanding.

Changes to AI transparency statements tracked by Dr Josling and verified by this masthead varied from updates to names and simple semantic shifts as agencies rolled out more AI uses, to the removal of bans on AI use for decision-making and language guaranteeing human oversight.

One example showed the DSS scrubbing an entire paragraph committing the department to track outcomes of AI use and measure their effectiveness.

The Treasury cut a pledge not to use AI in situations where the public could be "significantly impacted" and shifted its language from "human intervention" in all AI use to "human oversight".

A provision for a human to check the accuracy of AI outputs was cut by the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing, with its updated statement instead saying "human officials remain fully accountable".

The Department of Finance deleted a commitment to only deploy AI in "low-risk use cases" where it would not "directly interact with, or significantly impact the public without human intervention".

The Department of Employment and Workplace Relations flipped a statement banning AI from "service delivery", updating its statement in March to say AI could now support human decision-making and administrative action "by guiding, assessing, or making a recommendation to a human decision-maker."

The Australian Taxation Office likewise ditched limits on AI use for "administrative action".

The Australian Electoral Commission cut a total ban on AI use for public interactions, saying instead it should not be used to "provide authoritative advice or make decisions in public-facing interactions".

Spokespersons from the DSS, Treasury and the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing said human oversight remained a requirement of AI use within the departments.

The DSS spokesperson said AI was not used for decision-making and could only be deployed in approved use cases.

"The department will continue to update its public transparency statement as approved uses and governance arrangements evolve," the spokesperson said.

The DSS last updated its AI transparency statement in September.

The DTA's deputy chief executive of strategy, planning and performance Lucy Poole said changes to agencies' AI transparency statements were "not a focus of monitoring", but defended the many changes being made, describing the statements as "living documents, not static records".

The statements were "intended to give members of the public an accessible, high-level account of how Australian government agencies are currently approaching and using AI," said Ms Poole, and just one part of a broader government framework managing its adoption and responsible use.

"Agencies remain responsible for the content, accuracy and currency of their own statements. The DTA is not a regulator and does not validate or endorse individual statements," said Ms Poole.

Current government AI rules require the DTA to advise agencies about AI implementation and policy compliance, but do not empower it to assess or change how AI is being used.

Dr Josling in her submission to the joint select committee warned of a general lack of oversight of AI use by government, with current standards leaving it "unclear who, if anyone, is responsible for ensuring that the policy intent [for responsible AI use] is delivered".

"Agencies' public accounts of their AI use and governance remain largely self-attested and unverified," said Dr Josling.

The Department of Industry, Science and Resources, which contains the AI Safety Institute - launched last November and tasked with supporting safe AI deployment and governance - did not comment.

The national Office of AI - which sits within Prime Minister Anthony Albanese's department - is tasked exclusively with designing and legislating national data centre standards and copyright reform.

The DTA should provide agencies with guidance to ensure a consistent, whole-of-government approach to AI use, keep published records of past AI transparency statements, and those statements independently checked against actual department uses, Dr Josling told the committee.

Transparency standards should also be expanded to cover corporate Commonwealth entities "where AI use materially affects individuals, influences administrative decision-making, or involves sensitive information".

Intelligence agencies including the Australian Security Intelligence Organisation, the Australian Signals Directorate, the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission and the Defence Intelligence Organisation are exempt from current AI regulations.

ARC Centre of Excellence on Automated Decision-Making and Society research fellow Dr Alexandra Sinclair said Australia had fallen behind international best practice on transparent AI use, with governments in Canada, the UK and the US now publishing detailed registers of AI use cases.

"A lot of the information in the public domain about [Australian] government use of AI is high level and doesn't provide information about specific use cases," Dr Sinclair said.

"This makes it more difficult for the public or civil society to hold the government to account for potentially problematic uses of AI."

Dr Sinclair said rule changes that take effect in December requiring agencies to maintain an AI use case register should also require they be published and rules governing AI use, so far written by departments internally, developed instead by Parliament.

"The provision of AI regulation in legislation rather than soft law and policy would provide additional protections for the public," Dr Sinclair said.

The Community and Public Sector Union's (CPSU) national secretary Rebecca Fawcett said public servants should not be left to carry the risks of AI, renewing the union's calls for a pause on all complex automated decision-making inside government until stronger safeguards were in place.

"Public sector workers are best placed to identify the practical risks and unintended consequences of AI before they become serious problems for the community," said Ms Fawcett.

The CPSU has also called for a "standalone common condition on AI" in public servants' enterprise agreements to offer workplace protections, including a ban on AI use for surveillance, recruitment, performance assessment or promotion and "clear human oversight and accountability" for its use, which government negotiators in the current bargaining round have so far resisted.

Minister for the Public Service Katy Gallagher said the government continued to "listen and negotiate in good faith".

The joint select committee on AI will report to Parliament on November 30.
