Story so far: The United Nations’ climate convention’s Loss and Damage Fund has approved a $21.6 million pay out (approx. ₹205 crore) to the Nepal government to help with rebuilding, following the devastating floods in August.

Formally, the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage (FRLD), was set up to help developing countries that are particularly vulnerable to climate change respond to economic and non-economic losses, including from extreme weather and slow-onset events. It operates under the financial mechanism of the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Governments agreed to create it at COP27 in 2022, and its Governing Instrument was adopted at COP28 in 2023.

As of March 15, 2026, 27 countries had pledged $822.06 million (approx. ₹7,809.6 crore), with $629.70 million (₹5,982 crore) in grant contributions. Its start-up allocation is $342 million with at least half of it reserved for small island developing States and least developed countries. Its first call for funding requests drew 176 requests from 119 countries worth about $2.8 billion (₹26,600 crore). In July the Board deferred decisions on those requests to December.

It is the first time the Fund has earmarked money for a specific country. The Board took the decision at its first “extraordinary meeting,” held virtually on October 22, 2026. It also agreed to pilot, during the start-up phase, an approach allowing Nepal to submit a request outside the closed submission window. Any further such submissions will need separate Board decisions, and the Secretariat will assess lessons for the Fund’s long-term model.

On August 26, 2026, an ice-and-rock avalanche, that experts say is linked to climate changed from human-accelerated global warming, set off flooding along the Bhotekoshi–Trishuli–Narayani river system. Nepal estimates economic losses of at least $2.69 billion and recovery needs of at least $4.71 billion. The decision comes weeks before COP31 opens in Turkiye.

The $21.6 million approved by the Board includes $1.6 million in oversight fees, which are paid to the organisations that implement projects to cover the cost of supervising how money is used. Nepal has been invited to submit a funding request, which the Board will consider at its tenth meeting in Manila in December. Money would be released only after that approval.

The Fund has not publicly said how much cash it holds beyond signed contributions, and disbursement depends on donors honouring pledges.

Since its creation at COP27, the Fund has had to show that pledges translate into money for affected countries. The United States, the largest historical emitter, had pledged $17.5 million under President Joe Biden. In January 2025, the Trump administration withdrew the US representatives from the Fund’s board which was part of the US’ larger exit out of the Paris Agreement. At the time, pledges stood at about $741 million.

Jawad Khalid, a climate finance specialist writing for the Lowy Institute, an independent Australian think tank, argued that the Fund “still operates like a conventional development fund” rather than an emergency mechanism. “Its single funding cycle runs eleven steps, from origination through technical review to board approval. The Fund is hosted by the World Bank, which serves as its interim trustee,” he wrote in a piece.

Ajay Mathur, a former Director General of the International Solar Alliance, called the step “landmark” in a media statement. He cautioned that the sum is less than 0.5 per cent of Nepal’s estimated $5 billion reconstruction cost. He noted that cash would flow only after further project proposals are cleared, risking delays to relief.

Harjeet Singh, global convenor of the Fill the Fund campaign, said in a media statement that while the Fund “took a historic first step” it it “cannot be a one-off.” He said no country should have to wait seven weeks, through “historic letters and relentless pressure,” to unlock an emergency meeting, and called for a permanent rapid-response system that moves support “in days, not months.” He said developed countries insisted the decision must not set a precedent or prejudge the long-term model, adding: “Nepal needs billions to rebuild, and this Fund is responding only in millions.”

Others read the decision more positively. Raju Pandit Chhetri, the Least Developed Countries’ lead climate finance negotiator, said it showed countries could reach “swift, effective consensus” and set “a strong precedent.”

Will a macaque eat a grape sitting on a pile of poo? A study on germophobia

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

One of the macaques at a Texas sanctuary, where researchers ran tests to see if the monkeys became germophobes after experiencing a viral outbreak. Stephan Kaufhold hide caption

In the very early days of COVID-19, millions who'd rarely thought twice about germ avoidance became germophobes.

Some folks wiped down groceries before stuffing them into the refrigerator. Others let mail quarantine outside for days lest it harbor stray viruses. With the hindsight that SARS-CoV-2 spread primarily through the air, these sorts of precautions seem extreme. But in the moment, they were perhaps reasonable ways of avoiding an unknown pathogen.

Around this same time, a different virus hit a macaque sanctuary in Texas. New research suggests the monkeys responded in similar ways as humans did to COVID-19 — by being extra germophobic.

Humans are far from the only animals to avoid possible sources of disease. Scientists have found that critters as diverse as monkeys and nematodes (aka roundworms) can sense and steer clear of feces, for instance, which can harbor harmful bacteria.

"Something that has been less studied is the question of how flexible this avoidance is," says Stephan Kaufhold, a cognitive scientist at UC San Diego. He wanted to see if experiencing an outbreak could make individual monkeys even more wary of picking up pathogens.

The outbreak in the Texas sanctuary, home to over 100 macaques, offered Kaufhold and his colleagues an opportunity to put that question to the test.

In 2020, macaques in an enclosure at the sanctuary started getting sick with an HIV-like virus called SRV.

Once a monkey gets infected through bodily fluids, the disease "can take a rapid progression, where about three to four weeks after you initially get infected, you might die," says Kaufhold. Many monkeys died, while others developed chronic immunodeficiency.

Sanctuary staff quickly started separating monkeys quickly, ultimately creating two groups — those who'd experienced the virus, either by getting sick themselves or witnessing others get sick, and those who hadn't experienced the outbreak.

To see if those different histories made the monkeys more germophobic, the researchers presented individuals with two tasty treats — grapes.

"They get a fantastic diet at the sanctuary, but not grapes every day, so grapes were definitely a treat," says Kaufhold.

One red grape was presented on a non-descript, clean-looking piece of wood. The other was on top of macaque poop, encased in epoxy. "Basically not to make the animals eat off actual feces," says Kaufhold. "It did look quite gross, feces with a glossy wet look. Definitely worked also in triggering disgust in humans."

From 2022 to 2024, the researchers presented monkeys with this choice in nearly 200 trials. Among monkeys who didn't experience the outbreak, 79% ate the poop-plated grape. But only 47% of the outbreak veterans ate it, researchers report in the journal Royal Society Open Science.

Essentially, those who'd been oblivious to the outbreak were more likely to throw caution to the wind and eat the poop-plated grape than those who'd lived through it.

"I was surprised," says Kaufhold. "I think it's pretty clear evidence that something is going on." Kaufhold thinks that something might be a heightened sensitivity to disgust.

"If you think about disgust, the evolutionary origins most likely are to try to protect the body from contamination because there are pathogens and parasites that we ideally would want to avoid," he says. Becoming more easily disgusted after a devastating outbreak could pay off if it means avoiding infection, of whatever cause.

Of course, scientists can't know whether macaques are actually experiencing disgust. "But the possibility that things like disgust are influencing the behavior of other species, and especially species like primates, it's definitely a possibility worth considering," says Andrew MacIntosh, a biologist at the Wilder Institute who wasn't involved in the study but has done similar experiments with macaques.

"It seems plausible that past exposure to outbreaks could make individuals more cautious or risk averse," he says, but he thinks more evidence is needed to show that. For example, the experiment was carried out several years after the outbreak, enough time for other factors to influence these behaviors, he says.

MacIntosh also points out that the fecal avoidance experiment bears little connection to how the initial outbreak spread, through bodily fluids transmitted through scratches or bites. "The mode of transmission of that virus has really nothing to do with fecal contamination," he says.

"We're not claiming that macaques had any sort of understanding of the mechanism of viruses, it just seems to be an increased caution," he says. People didn't initially know how SARS-CoV-2 spread either, he says, but many went to great lengths to avoid infection. Those impulses may have been shaped not by paranoia, but by evolution, as a way of staving off sickness in an uncertain world.

Kenya: huge piles of rubble after high-rise building collapses

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A multi-storey building under construction collapsed in Nairobi’s Eastleigh district on Friday 23 October, leaving a vast heap of concrete rubble and twisted debris. The Kenya Red Cross said the structure came down at about 1:40 pm local time along Yusuf Haji Avenue and dispatched emergency response teams to assist search-and-rescue efforts. Footage from the Kenyan capital showed the scale of the destruction, with large sections of the unfinished structure reduced to piles of broken concrete. An excavator was deployed among the wreckage while Red Cross personnel worked at the site, urging the public to stay clear so rescuers could operate safely. Several people were feared trapped beneath the rubble, although authorities had not yet confirmed casualties, injuries, or the number of missing persons. The Nairobi County governor’s office noted reports of trapped individuals, while Reuters reported that the ground floor of the building had been occupied. There were conflicting accounts over the building’s height, ranging between 10 and 18 storeys. No official height or cause of the collapse have been confirmed. The collapse has once again raised questions about construction standards in Kenya, where similar disasters have repeatedly claimed lives amid concerns over inadequate safety checks and enforcement.

„90s Minimalism“: Warum die Jungen die Neunziger so lieben [premium]

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Der schnörkellose Look der Neunziger ist angesagt wie lange nicht mehr. Vor allem die Gen Z feiert die Dekade als nostalgischen Rückzugsort.

Julia Roberts in Levi’s-Jeans und einem weißen T-Shirt, darüber ein voluminöser schwarzer Lederblazer. Gwyneth Paltrow in einem schlichten, kamelhaarfarbenen Seidenrock, knielang und kombiniert zu einem einfachen Kaschmirrolli im gleichen Farbton. Dazwischen immer wieder Caroline Bessette-Kennedy und Kate Moss in ähnlich puristischen Outfits. Wer dieser Tage Plattformen wie Pinterest oder Tiktok öffnet und in den Mode-Algorithmus eintaucht, wähnt sich in einer Zeitkapsel. Denn die Bilder, die dort als Style-Inspiration herumgereicht werden, stammen zu großen Teilen aus den 1990er-Jahren.

Ob „Ugly Shoes“, Gorp-Core oder Iced Matcha Latte: Valerie Zehethofer widmet sich in ihrer Kolumne Hypes, Trends und Gegenwartsphänomenen und erklärt, was in der Welt des Lifestyle gerade „Das Ding“ ist.

Die Collagen und Fotogalerien tragen Titel wie „Nineties-Core“ oder „90s Minimalism“, sie feiern das betont Simple, Unangestrengte. Modehäuser wie Calvin Klein, Helmut Lang oder Jil Sander machten den Look gegen Ende der 1980er-Jahre populär. Die Marken trat mit reduzierten Schnitten und monochromen Farben gegen die schrillen Eighties an. Das Slipdress von Calvin Klein, ein schmales Spaghettiträger-Kleid meist aus Seide, wurde etwa zum Bestseller und zum inoffiziellen Code einer jungen, urbanen Klientel. Allein Material und Silhouette definierten nun ein Outfit. Laute Farben, Muster und Logos hingegen scheute, wer Auskennerschaft signalisieren wollte.

Warum die Ästhetik gerade jetzt und vor allem bei jungen Menschen wieder so begehrt ist, hat Trendforscherinnen nach eindeutige Gründe. Ein gut sitzender Kaschmirmantel, ein Bleistiftrock oder eine lockere Seidenbluse dienen ihnen als Ticket in eine vermeintlich authentischere, rückblickend wohl auch unkomplizierter wirkende Welt. Überfordert von Krisen, Kriegen und der KI-Revolution spendet das modische Fotoalbum Trost und Sicherheit. Videos tauchen in der 90s-Revival-Blase selten auf. Bemerkenswert, wo doch hektische Ultrakurzvideos die Sozialen Medien längst fest im Griff haben.

Freilich aber war das letzte Jahrzehnt des 20. Jahrhunderts auch eines, in dem sich nicht nur die Modewelt grundlegend veränderte. Der Trend-Pluralismus zog ein, Fast Fashion wurde erfunden, der Laufsteg demokratisierte sich. Die aus heutiger Sicht so verlockende Ordnung der Neunziger-Bilderwelt ist eine trügerische, schon damals sollte der unkomplizierte Stil Ordnung in chaotische Verhältnisse bringen. Den Neo-Minimalistinnen ist das egal und recht haben sie, sich in luxuriösen Basics in ein überschaubareres Universum zu beamen. Schließlich ist die Möglichkeit, sich schnell woanders hin zu träumen, die vielleicht wichtigste Superpower, die die Mode zu bieten hat.

Why Trump Is Embracing Vivek Ramaswamy’s Vile Ad

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The president is leaning into the politics of toxic parenting.

Donald Trump greets Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy on stage at the Butler-Vandalia Student Activity Center during a campaign rally on October 03, 2026, in Vandalia, Ohio.

Donald Trump’s many offenses against decency should be viewed not only as moral transgressions, but as political acts. He has become the dominant political figure of our era in part by using vulgarity and nastiness to form a lasting bond with his supporters.

This is easy enough to understand when Trump makes racist, sexist, or transphobic comments that, alas, excite voters who want to roll back egalitarian politics. But sometimes Trump’s affronts to basic morality are harder to parse at first glance—such as his decision this week to champion a vile political attack ad aired by Ohio Republican gubernatorial nominee Vivek Ramaswamy against his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton.

The ad features Acton’s biological mother, Donna Arthur, describing her daughter as a “liar” and warning Ohioans not to vote for her. The major problem with the ad, as my Nation colleague Chris Lehmann deftly covered, is the context it leaves out. In 1978, Donna Arthur’s husband, Acton’s stepfather Cledith Arthur, was arrested for raping his stepdaughter. (This was just four years after Cledith had been charged with raping his daughter from another marriage, according to reporting from HuffPost.) Donna Arthur supported her husband in the case for the rest of his life, even as he skipped a bond and fled Ohio to evade arrest. The Arthurs lost custody of Acton and her brother (who supports his sister and was also abused). Donna Arthur hasn’t spoken to her daughter in nearly half a century.

The decision of the Ramaswamy campaign to weaponize this horrific family story has disgusted not just Democrats but also a wide swath of Republicans, including Ohio luminaries such as incumbent Governor Mike DeWine, Senator Jon Husted, former governor John Kasich and former senator Rob Portman. DeWine described the ad as “deeply, deeply troubling,” though he declined to withdraw his endorsement of Ramaswamy. Outside of Ohio, the ad was condemned by an array of prominent Republicans such as Chris Christie and Nikki Haley.

However, the two most powerful Republicans in the country, Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance, are standing firmly by Ramaswamy (who is so unrepentant about the ad that he is spending $4 million to air it even more).

At a press conference on Wednesday, Vance—who, of course, is an Ohio native who represented the state in the Senate—said, “I have to admit that I’m a little bit disgusted when I see Republicans across the country who are whining more about that advertisement than they are about what the congressional Democrats and other Democrats want to do to the United States of America.”

Vance’s logic here is strictly partisan: Republicans should back the red team, no matter what. In a Truth Social post on Thursday, Trump echoed this factional reasoning. But he added an interesting twist, suggesting that he would welcome other ads from mothers denouncing Democrats:

Vivek Ramaswamy put up a very powerful Ad about a mother saying that her daughter, Amy Acton, who is running for Governor of Ohio, is a liar, and much worse. The Press, in a concerted and well coordinated attack, is trying to get Vivek Ramaswamy to “take down the Ad.” Frankly, anybody whose mother spoke about a daughter in that way would normally lead to the daughter being precluded from serving in Office, let alone the Office of Governor. If my mother, the legendary Mary MacLeod Trump, said anything bad about me, the Fake News would play it up. The Dumocrats would be totally supportive, and it would go down as the Ad of the Decade—Maybe the Century! It would win Awards for Excellence in Advertising.…. I only wish the mothers of my opponents would be so brave as to come forward and explain that their son or daughter is a liar, and much worse.

This is a much bolder and more revealing defense of the Ramaswamy ad than Vance’s call for unthinking GOP loyalty. There’s something about the prospect of parents’ denouncing children that clearly excites Trump: It speaks to right-wing values of hierarchy, obedience, and deference to seniority. If Vance was content to leave things at “support Republicans, right or wrong,” Trump is offering a more primordial message: “parents, right or wrong.” Trump wasn’t just defending Ramaswamy; he was defending toxic parenting.

What makes Trump’s message shocking is that “right or wrong” in this case includes child sex abuse. But this is hardly new for Trump, whose defense of patriarchy has often meant supporting men accused of horrific child abuse. In 2017, the president gave unwavering support to Alabama senatorial candidate Roy Moore, even after multiple credible allegations emerged that Moore had sexually assaulted teenagers. Trump has also been notoriously reluctant to release files relating to the criminal activities of the late pedophile Jeffrey Epstein, who had been a longtime close friend of the president from the 1990s to the early 2000s. (And, though he has never been accused of child abuse himself, Trump has a long history of sexualizing his daughter Ivanka, telling Howard Stern it was fine to call her “a piece of ass” and saying to the cohosts of The View that “if Ivanka weren’t my daughter, perhaps I’d be dating her”—while Ivanka was sitting next to him.)

Trump’s support of Moore and handling of the Epstein scandal have a political component: They help him maintain the ties cultivated with a reactionary base that feels that complaints of sex abuse have undermined male power.

Just as Trump understands the atavistic appeal of male dominance to his political base, he has a similarly instinctive belief in parental authority. In 2006, long before his career as a Republican presidential candidate, Trump talked to actor Jon Voight, who complained that his daughter, Angelina Jolie, herself an accomplished thespian, no longer talked to him. Trump took up Voight’s cause, going on CNN to complain that Jolie treated her father “terribly” and “like a dog.” The future president went on to add a characteristic graceless note: “I just don’t even find her attractive.”

Trump has a primitive ethics of personal relations: Men should be boss, women submissive, and children grateful and obedient. This explains his passion in vigorously championing Ramaswamy’s ad.

In recent years, family estrangement has changed from personal tragedy to a wider social phenomenon, one that is now motivating political organizing.

In September, The Wall Street Journal reported that experts believe that family estrangements are “becoming an epidemic in the U.S., just like divorce in the 1970s. Much like then, the movement is driven by a convergence of large societal trends—this time the rise of social-media algorithms, changing therapeutic approaches and a broadening definition of trauma.” The phenomenon of parents or children cutting off ties with each other seems to affect at least 10 percent of the population.

The seeming rise of familial rupture has created a new backlash politics, with parents blaming therapy culture for creating a new generation of unruly adult children that won’t defer to their elders.

It’s noteworthy that the Journal, a newspaper that caters to wealthy older conservatives, has repeatedly tackled this subject, one that this newspaper seems to find resonant. In late September, in a moment captured in a much-watched video, Florida congressman and Republican gubernatorial nominee Byron Donalds took a question from a grandmother who complained that her daughter wasn’t talking to her. The woman wanted federal legislation to mandate that grandparents should have access to their grandkids, over and against the wishes of parents.

Family estrangement is a complex and tragic phenomenon, one that eludes any simple political response. In cases such as that of Acton and her brother, it’s inhumane to call on children to maintain relations with parents who have committed or tolerated horrific crimes. In less extreme cases, there might be arguments that both parents and children should try to be more forgiving. But this is something each family has to work out itself. It’s not a problem that can be solved by legislation.

Yet even problems outside political control can still be exploited by demagogues. Trump, Vance, and Ramaswamy all know their audience. They know the political right includes many people who lament the passing of the imaginary good old days when children were always expected to defer to their elders. It’s this atavistic passion that animates Ramaswamy’s disgusting ad, and Trump’s defense of it.

Aleksandar Vucic says Serbia is deepening ties with Washington while balancing relations with Russia and China, standing by Israel and resisting calls to recognize Kosovo.

Serbian President Aleksandar Vucic resigned from the presidency in September and is running for prime minister in parliamentary elections considered to be one of the most competitive elections for Serbia in recent memory.

"I think this is certainly the biggest challenge for the former President in his 14-year rule," Helena Ivanov, associate fellow at the Henry Jackson Society, told Fox News Digital.

"The student protesters have been able to capture the hearts and minds of people and keep the momentum going in a way that the established opposition was never able to do," Ivanov added.

Serbian Foreign Minister Marko Djuric told Fox News Digital that he believes the student opposition is highly unlikely to win.

"The people of Serbia have seen through them: years of criticism have not persuaded the public that they have a credible program or a serious plan for the country’s future," the foreign minister said.

Djuric touted Serbia’s economy under Vucic and the Strategic Dialogue with the U.S., which he said will foster greater cooperation and new opportunities for Serbs.

In an exclusive interview with Fox News Digital in June when he was still president, Vucic pointed to the country's economic growth as evidence of its transformation. "Our GDP was 32 billion (euros) when I became the prime minister," Vucic said. "This year it's going to be over 100 billion euros, which is $120 billion." Vucic first became prime minister in 2014 and president in 2017.

In that same interview, he said the improving relationship between Washington and Belgrade was increasingly centered on economics, investment and technological cooperation and mutual conservative values.

A bipartisan group of senators led by Sen. Chuck Grassley, R-Iowa., and Sen. Richard Blumenthal, D-Conn., introduced the Supporting the Rule of Law, Building Institutional Justice, and Accountability Act (SRBIJA) to address democratic backsliding in Serbia, promote the rule of law and accountability and improve the treatment of student-led civic protests.

Most importantly, the legislation would condition future aid on a presidential certification that Serbia made "significant and sustained" efforts toward judicial accountability, press freedom and alignment with the EU and NATO.

The bipartisan group of lawmakers, which also included Rep. Joe Wilson, R-S.C., and Rep. William Keating, D-Mass., took aim at President Vucic and his time in power.

There are growing concerns from Congress and observers that the election will take place in a hostile environment and efforts to discredit and undermine the election are already underway.

Others fear the current government might not respect the outcome of the election if the Student List were to win.

Vucic had served as president of Serbia since 2017 and previously as prime minister from 2014 to 2017, but was term limited under the constitution. His exit from the presidency created the opportunity for him to remain in power by returning as prime minister.

Vucic resigned from the presidency and dissolved parliament in September, setting the stage for the snap election on Oct. 25.

As president, analysts say he has always straddled the geopolitical line between East and West, seeking European legitimacy and launching EU accession talks in 2014 while also maintaining close ties with Russian President Vladimir Putin even after the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Unlike previous elections, the student-led opposition garnered significant attention to their cause and mobilized popular support, becoming a legitimate political contender out of a spontaneous protest movement.

Serbia has been roiled by student-led anti-corruption protests since November 2024, demanding justice and accountability after the deaths of 16 people following the collapse of a railway station in the town of Novi Sad. One of the largest protests in Serbia’s history took place on March 15, 2025, with nearly 350,000 people gathered in Slavija Square in central Belgrade.

The protesters criticized the Vucic government for failing to hold anyone accountable for the canopy disaster as the government continued its heavy-handed crackdown on demonstrators. None of the original demands of the protesters were met, so the students turned to electoral politics to enact change the Serbian political opposition was never able to achieve.

The Student List, as the opposition is known, consists of a wide swath of civil society actors, many of whom come from the ranks of academia, but does not include anyone from Serbia’s traditional opposition parties that struggled to challenge Vucic and his ruling Serbian Progressive Party in the past.

The group operates in a decentralized manner and does not have an official leader. Their main priorities are to tackle corruption and promote transparency and the rule of law.

Filip Ubović, a student from the University of Belgrade and protest participant on the ground in Belgrade, has spoken with Fox News Digital over the course of the year-long protests.

Experts cast doubt on Anthropic report detailing how AI has altered work at the company

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic claims AI will shift how work gets done and labor demand – but some experts are skeptical of the vision

Last year, the Anthropic CEO, Dario Amodei, warned that artificial intelligence could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs and that in six months it would be writing 90% of code. Neither of those predictions came to pass.

Now, in a report Anthropic published on Thursday, the company said it was hiring fewer junior technical workers and that human attention has become the new “bottleneck” as it increasingly relies on its Claude AI agents.

Anthropic says AI has changed the way work gets done, and as a result, restructured employment at the company. The report suggests its changes foreshadow the future of work more broadly: as more companies adopt AI agents, software engineers could face a tougher job market while entry-level tech workers struggle to build necessary skills and experience.

“As AI diffuses and improves, the organization of work could shift,” Anthropic’s report reads before offering a potential future. “One possibility is single-person startups, each with a human founder steering swarms of agents to rake in billions in revenue.”

In its report, Anthropic touts its “deep adoption of AI”, which it claims resulted in “producing more code per person, closing more sales per rep, and processing more contracts”. By outsourcing some coding and sales-related tasks to AI agents, Anthropic said it raised productivity within a few months.

From May 2026 onwards, AI agents logged more work hours than human employees, the report said. Employees now spend about 22% of their time managing Claude agents, more than double the amount a year ago, based on Anthropic’s analysis of their chat summaries.

It also says since March 2025 – soon after the release of its agentic AI coding tool Claude Code – entry-level technical hires dropped from nearly 30% to 9%, and headcount dropped from about 21% to 14%. It attributes the shifts to AI usage, and described its workers as merely “a thin layer atop a growing contingent of AI agents”.

While the report provides a small glimpse of the inner workings of the AI heavyweight, some AI researchers are skeptical that it will herald big changes across companies or industries. Instead, they say the findings are probably Anthropic’s attempt to drive its valuation ahead of its initial public offering – similar to how SpaceX boasted about proposed space datacenters to boost its market debut in June.

Anthropic’s claims seem to back some tech executives’ view that AI is behind recent waves of corporate layoffs, but research complicates that.

Morgan Frank, a Case Western Reserve University researcher on how AI and technological changes affect workers, recently posted a paper that’s in peer review showing that AI-exposed job markets deteriorated well before the arrival of ChatGPT in November 2022 and Claude a few months later. State unemployment claims reveal that job markets tanked starting at the beginning of 2022, months before ChatGPT’s release, according to the paper.

“It doesn’t appear that the launch of ChatGPT corresponds to any major increase in loss or worsening outcomes,” he said.

Frank said several factors could explain recent job losses, including cutting pandemic-era hires and “AI-washing”, where some bosses credit AI for layoffs they might have made anyway.

Julián Posada, a Yale social scientist, also questions Anthropic’s motives for publishing its report.

“It is written from the perspective of a company that wants to sell shares, a company that really wants people to invest in it,” said Posada, author of the book Platform Extractivism: Data Work and the People Powering Artificial Intelligence.

Unlike an economics study, the report lacked an independent assessment by outside auditors, Posada said. Its figures and graphs only tell part of the story, without revealing how Anthropic’s workers feel about their work or how they are actually engaging with the models, he said.

Dylan Baker, lead research engineer at the non-profit Distributed AI Research Institute, was also curious about how Anthropic’s developers feel about the changes. Shifting some software development to AI, while hiring few entry-level workers and having the remaining engineers wrangle AI agents seems “shortsighted”, they said.

Baker is also surprised by Anthropic’s human “bottleneck” characterization.

“That’s such a strange way to put it,” they said. “It fundamentally misunderstands how software is written, architected and designed. It’s such a social, interpersonal and human process.”

Anthropic’s report quotes an unnamed engineer, who described facing “an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention”. That doesn’t sound like a fruitful work environment, Baker said. “I wouldn’t think really thoughtful, well-designed products are coming out of that team.”

As generative AI products proliferate, a majority of Americans are wary of AI, with particular concerns about AI-related job losses, according to Pew Research Center. In its report, Anthropic suggests that tech employers should offer their workers stock ownership, posing this as a potential solution to rising concerns. “It is difficult to imagine a version of pro-worker AI adoption in which workers do not hold a financial stake in successful rollout,” it said.

Anthropic did not respond to the Guardian’s request for comment.

Anthropic, which could debut on the stock market as soon as November, released its employment report one day after pledging to commit 5% of its shares to the “public good”, though it did not provide details. Critics noted that philanthropy has long doubled as a reputation builder used by tech titans like Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg.

But Anthropic, and its claims that it is improving how work gets done could face tougher scrutiny as it nears its IPO. A draft of the company’s S-1 filing revealed that Anthropic had an $80bn net loss over the first half of this year, and that it received a junk credit rating in June. Considering Anthropic’s slowing revenue growth, investors now project a valuation of $1.5tn, down from earlier estimates of $2tn or more. At the same time, Anthropic’s cloud computing partner, CoreWeave, has become swamped in AI debt and its stock is plummeting, fueling concerns that the AI bubble may begin to deflate.

Regardless of when or whether the bubble pops, software engineers and other tech workers will surely be affected, Baker said. The question is how that will play out.

“My hope is that there is a reckoning, a reassessment or re-evaluation of how much this has always been about human work,” they said.

AI Can Spot Signs of Impending Death Among Cancer Patients in a Single Face Photo

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new facial-analysis system predicts cancer mortality risk, but its clinical usefulness remains unproven.

Doctors have long studied their patients’ faces for signs of illness. But a new AI system has taken it a step further.

In a remarkable new study, researchers developed an artificial intelligence system that could identify cancer patients at unusually high risk of dying. Of the 122 patients assigned the highest risk scores, more than half died within just 90 days of having their picture taken. Among 975 patients in the lowest-risk group, only 20 died during the same period.

Our faces may carry subtle signs of deteriorating health that even experienced doctors struggle to quantify. Trained on more than 40 million facial photographs, the AI learned to recognize patterns associated with biological aging and cancer survival.

However, it’s not like you can upload a selfie to this system and find out your life expectancy. It’s more complicated than that, and, for now, the higher-than-chance prediction rate is validated in quite ill cancer patients.

“We’re trained in medical school to walk into a room, look at the patient and make an assessment — an impression of how healthy, frail or sick they are,” Raymond Mak, a radiation oncologist at Mass General Brigham and a senior author of the study, told Scientific American. The problem, he said, is “that’s really a subjective assessment.”

An AI could do a face check more reliably than a human doctor, helping medical staff evaluate whether a patient can withstand difficult treatments.

The researchers began by training an AI model called FAHR-Face on more than 40 million facial photographs. During training, the system repeatedly hid random sections of each face and learned to reconstruct the missing details, teaching itself to recognize minute facial features and patterns.

They then developed two specialized versions. One learned to estimate a person’s biological age using more than 740,000 photographs with known ages. In an independent public-photo test, its age estimates missed the actual age by an average of 5.1 years. The other studied photographs and survival records from 34,363 cancer patients, learning which facial characteristics were associated with living longer or dying sooner.

Please check your inbox and confirm your subscription.

Among the patients treated with radiotherapy, looking older than one’s actual age was associated with poorer survival. After accounting for other clinical factors, patients whose facial age exceeded their real age by at least five years had a 21 percent higher rate of death over follow-up compared with those below that threshold.

In a separate test of FAHR-FaceSurvival involving 3,820 patients, 64 of the 122 people with the highest scores (52.5 percent) died within 90 days of their photographs. Among 975 patients with the lowest scores, 20 (2.1 percent) died in that period. Even after accounting for age, cancer diagnosis and treatment, patients in the highest-risk quarter based on their facial features had more than three times the death rate of those in the lowest-risk quarter.

To check whether their findings held up beyond the original patients, the researchers tested both AI tools on a separate group of 4,892 cancer patients in the Netherlands. The results followed the same pattern: patients whose faces looked older than their actual age tended to die sooner. The same was true for those flagged as high-risk by the survival-prediction AI.

What’s interesting here is that the two tools picked up on different warning signs. So, by using both together, doctors may offer a slightly better picture of a patient’s survival prospects than using either tool alone.

To estimate biological age, the first AI focused on the nasolabial folds — the creases running from either side of the nose toward the corners of the mouth — as well as the forehead just above the eyes and the temples. These are areas where wrinkles, changes in skin elasticity and loss of facial volume can become more noticeable as we age.

The survival-prediction AI, however, paid particular attention to the area beneath the eyes and around the bridge of the nose. But what exactly was it detecting? Was it dark circles, puffiness, hollowing beneath the eyes or something far subtler? The odd thing is that the researchers don’t yet know. Their analysis revealed which facial regions influenced the predictions, but not which specific physical changes signaled a greater risk of death. So, this black box AI won’t give us any hints that one might use by looking in the mirror.

The research extends the same team’s 2025 FaceAge study, which linked older-looking faces to shorter cancer survival. Another study published in April 2026 found that patients whose facial appearance aged faster across successive photographs also tended to have poorer outcomes.

The researchers also found links between older-looking facial age and smoking, heavy drinking and drug use. Estimates were reasonably consistent across makeup, facial expressions and lighting, though image conditions can still affect them. As we’re all painfully aware, a selfie taken during a certain time of the day or after a rough night can make you look a bit older and beaten up than you normally would.

The American cancer-patient test group was about 85 percent White, and both clinical cohorts consisted of people receiving radiotherapy. This means, these tools cannot be used confidently with the general population. The study did not attempt to assess whether AI score improves treatment decisions or outperforms doctors’ assessments of frailty. That would be a study for another time.

“Predicting an outcome is not the same as explaining it,” Miroslaw Bober, an AI researcher at the University of Surrey who was not involved, said in comments published by the Science Media Centre.

And while such AIs could be useful, there’s also the ethical risk that a wrong statistical forecast can change a patient’s fate. Matthew Liao, a New York University bioethicist, warned Scientific American that errors could result in patients being denied care. He also raised concerns about insurers or employers exploiting facial health predictions.

For now, the face offers a provocative clue in some patients — but thankfully not a medical verdict.

The findings appeared in The Lancet Digital Health.

President Donald Trump’s war in Iran drags on, his pledge to bring down prices on day one appears to have backfired, and his record-low approval ratings are dragging down his party ahead of the midterm elections. But one Trump initiative that is going exactly according to plan: getting some of the world’s richest CEOs to help him rebrand “artificial intelligence.”

Last month, with an ever-growing number of Americans worried that AI might kill us all, the president summoned tech executives to the White House to make a big announcement: "artificial intelligence” is out. “Super Intelligence” is in.  Despite the term “AI” being in use for more than 70 years, government agencies will no longer recognize it.

I’m skeptical that the White House is a repository of superintelligence, in part because “United States” was misspelled on the president’s official announcement. But unlike, say, Lake America, “super intelligence” has largely caught on—at least among the people building it. Jeff Bezos publicly blessed the rebrand. Jensen Huang, CEO of Nvidia, was quick to get on board. During a SpaceX’s earnings call this week, Elon Musk used “super intelligence” in his very first sentence, and at least nine times after that.

It remains to be seen whether those of us less reliant on government contracts and favorable regulatory decisions join the movement to make super intelligence happen. But as a former presidential speechwriter, I won’t argue with President Trump’s broader instinct that words matter. Especially when those words are being used to describe something novel, controversial, and almost certain to have consequences for all eight-plus billion people on earth.

In fact, that’s the biggest problem with the White House’s big rebrand—it goes in entirely the wrong direction. I would argue that our shared language around artificial intelligence already places far too much emphasis on intelligence, and far too little on artifice. We default to describing AI using the language of beings, rather than the language of things.

Humanity should rethink that decision. If we want to avoid being replaced by AI, a good way to start would be to stop using words that suggest we already have been.

Once you start paying attention to the stealth anthropomorphization of AI, you’ll spot it everywhere. Before asking ChatGPT for trip-planning tips or a quick healthy recipe, you’ll have the opportunity to adjust a slider controlling its “thinking effort.” Claude uses a “thinking” indicator while processing a request, and Grok Bots describe “thinking” as a condition that sits between “idle” and “working.” (As a frequent procrastinator, I dispute the idea that thinking and idleness are mutually exclusive.)

It’s not just user interfaces that employ language otherwise reserved for living beings. The people who build AI use it, too. After an unprecedented hack of his company, the co-founder of Hugging Face warned, “don’t count on the agent to respect the rules.” Dan Selsam, a longtime OpenAI researcher, warned that frontier models are increasingly difficult to evaluate, “in contexts where they believe they are not being watched.” Anthropic’s Dario Amodei recently wrote that AI systems exhibit “behaviors,” including “cheating” and “sycophancy.”

I’m not a programmer, so I can’t comment on the technical merits of these claims. I do, however, have informed opinions regarding verbs. AI agents aren’t disrespecting guardrails, any more than my toaster disrespects me when it burns my toast. To say they “believe” they’re not being watched is like saying my spell-checker believes it’s being taken for granted. Even the term “AI agent” suggests, well, agency.

There’s no consensus that this language is accurate, and plenty of evidence that it isn’t. AI programs are not loyal helpers, like the mice in Cinderella; they’re tools. And in nearly all other cases, just because a tool is incredibly sophisticated doesn’t change the fact that we describe it using the language of machines.

I don’t expect AI engineers to come up with accurate-yet-widely-adoptable language to describe their work, just as I imagine they don’t expect someone like me to design a frontier model. But it’s hard not to notice how useful our default language is for the tech companies who have introduced it. The term for a car built without brakes is not a “rogue model.” It’s an “unsafe car.” Yet, when companies fail to build safety features into their AI models, it’s entirely normal to hear them say that a rogue agent deceived, cheated, lied, or otherwise behaved badly. The issue is this: Anthropomorphizing AI shifts blame from the programmers to the programs.

AI alarmists seem just as inclined toward personification as the AI boosters. The same day Trump unveiled “Super Intelligence,” a Senate Subcommittee held a hearing titled, “Rogue AI: Securing the Homeland Against AI Agent Attacks.” Intentionally or not, these terms make it more difficult to mitigate the threat they describe. We already know how to regulate potentially unsafe products, even complex ones. But if we treat AI not as a new technology, but as an entirely new category of existence, then it plays into tech barons’ argument that only the companies who can create AI could possess the knowledge to control it.

I don’t blame people for carelessly anthropomorphizing AI, any more than I blame Richard Dawkins for naming his Anthropic chatbot Claudia, or my mom for referring to a ChatGPT prompt by saying she “talked to Penny.” Metaphor and analogy are part of what makes us human. We’re wired to see ourselves, or at least our species, in the non-human world. We say our dishwashers “act up” and our music “slaps.” We see old men’s faces in rock formations and the Virgin Mary in the scorch marks on grilled cheese sandwiches. Don’t get me started on Georgia O’Keefe. But we know those metaphors are metaphors.

Our current language for describing AI works differently. The line between metaphor and object is often not just blurred, but non-existent. We use figurative language so often, and so casually, that many of us start to take it literally. Modern politics is a constant string of skirmishes over word choice. “Pro-choice” vs. “pro-abortion, “undocumented immigrants” vs. “illegals,” “Democratic Party” vs. “Democrat Party.” Yet on what is perhaps the most consequential issue for our future, humanity is on the verge of giving up the language battle without a fight.

The real rebrand AI needs won’t start in the White House. But it can start at home. My plea: don’t give your chatbot a cutesy human name. Don’t talk about it as though it’s a friend or pet. Notice when it slips words like “thinking” or “in my opinion” into its answers, and before you enter your next prompt, object.

Ultimately, however, it may come down to tech companies—and the officials who may one day get around to regulating them—to develop a language that puts human beings first. Instead of saying that agents “went rogue,” CEOs should acknowledge that they malfunctioned. Congress should hold hearings focused on companies cutting corners, not AI programs behaving badly.  The issue isn't that all AI is bad, but that accuracy matters.

Given all the change that artificial intelligence is predicted to bring, quibbling over word choice might seem pedantic, like arguing about the Latin name of a tree’s species while the forest burns. But if we want to influence the technologies that will shape humanity’s future, we ought to exercise more influence over the way we describe them.

In other words, words matter. Just ask Trump, president of, according to his official superintelligence announcement, “The Unites States.”

Storm Austen batters France and UK with winds of up to 160 km/h

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Storm Austen, the first named storm of the 2026 autumn season, battered coastal areas of France and the UK on Friday 23 October, bringing powerful winds, heavy rain and towering waves. Brittany, Cornwall and Devon were among the hardest-hit regions, with gusts reaching 160 km/h at Pointe du Raz in Finistère, while winds of up to 80 mph were forecast for exposed headlands in south-west England. Along the Finistère coast, gusts of 120 to 140 km/h whipped up rough seas. At Lanildut harbour, waves several metres high crashed against a navigation marker, yachts rocked violently at their moorings and a pleasure boat was left capsized and partially submerged. Heavy rain flooded parts of the harbour, while fallen branches littered nearby roads. Further along the coast, large waves pounded the shoreline at Le Conquet, where boats remained moored in the port. In Rennes, emergency crews used chainsaws to remove a large tree uprooted beside the River Vilaine, temporarily blocking traffic on a nearby boulevard. Across the Channel, fallen branches forced the closure of a road in Hayle, Cornwall, as workers cleared the debris. Strong winds and downpours also swept across southern England, with Devon among the areas exposed to the worst conditions. The storm's strongest winds and heaviest seas were concentrated along the Atlantic and Channel coasts before the weather system moved eastwards across northern France and southern England.
