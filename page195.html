Just 4 minutes a day can dramatically improve strength after 65

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A daily strength training routine lasting just four minutes can significantly improve strength, balance, and mobility in older adults, according to new research led by Penn State College of Medicine. The findings suggest that maintaining physical independence with age may require much less exercise than many people assume.

Published in the journal PLOS One, the study found that adults aged 65 and older experienced meaningful improvements in physical performance after only 12 weeks. These gains are especially important because muscle strength and mobility influence the risk of falling, the ability to live independently, and overall health and longevity.

For older adults, being able to move comfortably and perform everyday activities is essential for maintaining quality of life. Simple tasks such as standing up from a chair, climbing stairs, and walking can become more difficult as strength and balance decline.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, unintentional injuries, including those caused by falls, are among the leading causes of death in adults aged 65 and older.

Despite the importance of physical fitness, many older adults believe that meaningful improvements require long, demanding workouts. Christopher Sciamanna, professor of medicine and public health at Penn State College of Medicine and lead author of the study, says the new findings challenge that assumption. Even a brief daily routine can improve important measures of mobility and physical function.

"The human body is designed to improve very quickly," Sciamanna said. "And just a few repetitions of an exercise performed regularly can lead to huge improvements. Exercise is about forward thinking -- think about what you want to be able to do and train for it."

Why Shorter Workouts Could Make Exercise Easier

Strength training, also known as resistance training, involves making muscles work against some form of resistance, such as body weight or elastic bands. Research has shown that it can substantially improve muscle strength within a few months.

However, fewer than one in five older adults meet the recommendation to perform muscle strengthening activities at least twice a week. Common obstacles include discomfort, limited time, and the physical demands of conventional exercise programs.

Another challenge is that planning an effective workout can feel overwhelming, particularly for people who are unfamiliar with exercise.

"Exercise is actually really complicated, because you have to decide how many repetitions, how far, how many sets, how much rest, and how many times per week," said co-author Smita Dandekar, associate professor of pediatrics at Penn State College of Medicine. "It's hard work, so there's huge problems with people wanting to do exercise. If we can make it short, we're part way there."

The researchers had already seen encouraging results from an earlier experiment called FAST (Functional Activity Strength Training)-1. In that smaller study, 24 older adults performed just 30 seconds of push-ups and squats each day. After six months, their ability to perform squats had improved.

Other research has similarly suggested that performing only a few sets of exercises each week can produce strength improvements approaching those achieved with considerably longer workouts.

These findings led Sciamanna and his colleagues to investigate whether an especially brief exercise program could produce measurable improvements in older adults.

Testing a Simple Four-Minute Exercise Routine

For the new study, the Penn State researchers developed a program called FAST-2 to evaluate whether a short daily workout could improve mobility and physical ability in adults older than 65.

The trial included 97 participants whose average age was 74. They were randomly divided into two groups, with one following the exercise program and the other receiving no intervention.

Before the study began, participants reported exercising for an average of only about 18 minutes per week. That was far below the general recommendation of at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity each week, Sciamanna explained.

Participants received four elastic resistance bands and an adjustable-height stepper, along with written instructions explaining how to perform each movement. The researchers also provided easier variations to accommodate different physical abilities.

For example, participants could perform push-ups against a wall or countertop rather than on the floor. Those who found chair stands challenging could use their hands on their knees for additional support.

As their strength improved, participants were encouraged to make the exercises progressively more difficult. Someone performing modified push-ups might advance to the standard movement, while another participant might increase the height of the stepper.

To evaluate the program, researchers measured how quickly and effectively participants could rise from a chair and how long they could balance on one leg. These assessments were conducted at the beginning, midpoint, and end of the 12-week study.

Such tests are valuable because they reflect movements people rely on throughout the day. Performance on these assessments can also help predict future mobility problems, fall risk, and the potential need for additional care.

Sciamanna emphasized that these simple physical tests can reveal important information about a person's future health and independence.

"These indicators predict your future ability to go into a nursing home, your future likelihood of falling and of developing difficulty walking," Sciamanna said. "They give you a sense of whether or not you're going to be able to be active in the future."

Although the study measured improvements in physical performance rather than directly testing whether participants experienced fewer falls or avoided nursing home care, the findings suggest that short workouts could help preserve abilities closely tied to independent living.

A Workout Older Adults Can Actually Stick With

Beyond the improvements in strength and mobility, the researchers identified another major advantage of the four-minute routine: participants were able to follow it consistently.

Throughout the study, they completed their exercises on 81% of days.

That level of participation suggests that a short, straightforward workout may be easier to incorporate into daily life than a longer exercise program. It could also help people overcome obstacles such as limited time, discomfort, or difficulty maintaining a regular fitness routine.

For older adults who are largely inactive, even a small amount of consistent resistance training could therefore provide a practical starting point for improving physical function.

The researchers say their results reinforce the idea that exercise sessions do not need to be lengthy to produce meaningful benefits. By focusing on movements that support everyday activities, a few minutes of daily effort may help older adults maintain strength, mobility, and independence.

For Sciamanna, the ultimate purpose of exercise extends beyond simply becoming physically stronger. It is about preserving the ability to participate in life and continue doing the things that matter most.

"Exercise is the key to freedom," he said. "Freedom is the ability to be able to do what you want to do, and I would say that if you can't do what you want to do, you're not free. I approach exercise by thinking of what I want to be able to do in 20 years, and then I train to do that."

Executives are more likely to trust AI over themselves

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Executives are twice as likely as rank-and-file workers to trust AI over their own judgment, per a survey out Wednesday morning.

Why it matters: It's a worrying development. Leaders are hired for their experience and knowledge.

  • Outsourcing that to AI raises the risk of making decisions that don't line up with reality, which then reverberate across companies and beyond.

By the numbers: 32.8% of executives said that they trust AI more than their own judgment, according to the survey from Charter, a workplace research and media company.

  • That's twice the rate for workers, or individual contributors. Just 16.3% of individual contributors said the same.
  • The divide shows up at the level below executives — 34.8% of managers said they trust AI more than their own judgment.

Zoom out: "Cognitive offloading" is a term for using tools like AI to reduce mental effort.

  • "Executives—despite having the most experience—are doing it more than anyone," the report's co-authors wrote.

The fine print: Charter in August surveyed 5,002 U.S. office workers, at companies with at least 100 employees, who said they use AI at work, including 2,952 individual contributors, 1,547 managers and 503 executives — including vice presidents, C-suite executives, owners and founders.

Between the lines: Most executives are under a lot of pressure to know the answers to a lot of questions really quickly — and AI can help with that at least in the short term, says report co-author Brian Elliott, a former Slack executive who is now executive-in-residence at Charter and co-wrote the report.

  • AI "lends itself very well to the 'seldom wrong, never in doubt' tendency that some executives have," he says.
  • "If you feel like you have to fake it until you make it as an executive, then AI is certainly going to feed those tendencies because you can appear to be really smart, really quick."

Zoom in: He described tech executives who use AI to build product prototypes over a weekend, then expect their teams to quickly turn those into functioning products — without appreciating the more complicated work involved.

  • "AI is really good at giving you the executive summary. It's not necessarily as good at giving you the very specific answer to the problem you've got today, which is more often what an individual contributor faces."

Reality check: The Charter survey measures executives' self-reported trust in AI, but not whether they actually follow its advice when making consequential decisions.

  • The report's findings were overall positive about AI's potential in the workplace. 42.7% of AI users said it improved both their work and personal lives.

Friction point: A key tension for workers is the threat of AI job loss, the researchers observed in separate qualitative interviews with 91 more workers.

  • The better workers become with AI, the more they realize how much can be done without them, the survey found.
  • There's a "persistent tension."

The bottom line: Executives are mostly free from that tension, says Elliott.

  • "The odds that an AI CEO shows up and takes over a company are excruciatingly low."

Can James Carville Please Just Be Quietly Wrong Somewhere Else?

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The omnipresent consultant-cum-tantrum-thrower has long outlived whatever relevance he once had, but cable producers, podcast hosts, and Democratic campaigns haven’t got the memo.

Democratic political consultant James Carville at last year’s New York Times DealBook summit.

He recently appeared with Bill Kristol on a podcast hosted by The Bulwark and stated that he’d rather Republicans keep the House by a five-member majority after the midterms than have Democrats win, because then “they’re hostage to the DSA.” The Daily Beast, long tethered to a clickbait-through-faux-outrage business model, ran an account of Carville’s comments under the sensationalistic headline “Top Dem Strategist Makes Shock Midterms Wish.” Unfortunately, though, the fact that Carville is bashing progressives and suggesting they’re worse than Republicans is not shocking at all: He’s been doing that for years now, at every opportunity.

This has put me in the uncomfortable position of trusting Carville less than Bill Kristol, one of the architects of the Iraq War and a Republican conservative turned Never Trumper. Unlike Carville, Kristol at least seems to understand what progressives stand for and occasionally concedes that they have good points. “I don’t agree with people saying ICE and CBP need ‘more training,’” Kristol wrote on Bluesky recently. “They’re doing exactly what this administration has trained them to—impose a reign of fear in blue cities. They don’t need more training. They need to be ripped up root and branch.” In the absurdist political world of 2026, in other words, we have Bill Kristol calling for ICE to be abolished, and James Carville rooting for ongoing Republican rule in Congress in lieu of having his political sensibilities unpleasantly challenged.

Carville loathes the left in a knee-jerk way that isn’t backed by any serious thoughtfulness about what progressives want—or even what voters at large want. A majority of Democrats want Medicare for All. Seventy-four percent of Democrats want to tax the rich more. Four in 10 Americans (not just Democrats!) think capitalism isn’t helping them and are open to socialist solutions to some problems. Sixty-four percent of Americans think we should give Israel less military and financial support. These are all things that Carville and his cohort of 1990s-era political power brokers like to characterize as views held by tiny, unrealistic, and leftist minorities.

Carville often distorts the positions of the Democratic Socialists in particular, intentionally reinforcing negative characterizations lofted by fellow pundits at one of his preferred outlets for bloviating, Fox News. In August he criticized a DSA candidate on the basis that he “has the attribute of every DSA member in the country, and that is he has a motor mouth.”

James Carville complaining that someone who is not James Carville has a motor mouth is like Donald Trump complaining that someone who is not Donald Trump has a reputation for bankrupting casinos. But self-awareness is not Carville’s strong suit generally. He also likes to mouth loyalty to the working classes, playing up his “ragin’ Cajun’” persona while being married to a former Republican consultant and from the comfort of one of his multiple multimillion-dollar homes.

Still, Carville’s greatest offense is continuing to live in the reality of Clinton-era politics while spouting advice to those of us who live here in 2026. Some of this might be chalked up to age and complacency. Carville wouldn’t be the first 81-year-old who doesn’t have his finger on the pulse of what anyone under the age of 40 is thinking or doing. He’s also not the first man to enjoy early success and dine out on it for decades—or to assume that the expertise founded on that achievement is stable over time and does not need to be refreshed or reassessed as circumstances change. He is also not the only Democrat to trot out a ’90s-era political playbook posited on the vital role played by s a nebulous center of reasonable moderates in the electorate who switch back and forth between parties. Nor is he the only aging centrist who believes, wrongly, that the far left is as dangerous as the far right.

But he is one of the people who can’t stop talking about it. And for some inexplicable reason, he’s the outraged centrist whom the media can’t stop platforming as an avatar for mainstream Democratic politics—even as he stubbornly refuses to educate himself about who progressives are and what they want, beyond crude ideological caricatures that wouldn’t be out of place in the editorial pages of The Wall Street Journal.

He’s also still leaning on the idea that elections are about one issue: the economy. I watched D.A. Pennebaker’s The War Room three years after it came out, in a political science class taught by a former Republican consultant. I don’t remember any specific lines from it except Carville’s “it’s the economy, stupid” mantra, which, more than anything, defined Carville’s brand as a strategist to the public. Like many early Carville-isms, there was truth to it, but it was also an oversimplification. Voters are always driven by economic concerns, but elections are never about one thing—even Bill Clinton’s victory over George H.W. Bush, in the midst of a deep recession, was driven by increasing disillusionment with the first Iraq War, Clinton’s personal charisma and media savviness, and a host of other things that had little to do with the real economy.

We’re in much the same position now, as voters are angry over the state of the economy, but also register strong sentiments against rampant corruption and threats to democracy. Nevertheless, Carville is relentlessly on his 1992 message, trotting out a variation of “it’s the economy, stupid” on the countless occasions he’s in front of a camera or podcast mic. In a recent Fox News appearance, he brayed that Democrats need to avoid culture-war issues and identity politics and just focus on the economy. “quit f—ing around with all this cultural stuff and start talking about people’s wages…. Start talking about what we can do to protect people who are trying to make it and particularly young people.”

By protecting young people who are trying to make it, he did not mean protecting immigrants as they seek to avoid violence and detention from ICE, young women who need abortions, or young Black men who don’t want to be killed or beaten by the police. Nor was he referring to young trans people who end up murdered because the right paints them as sexual deviants or pedophiles, and people in Carville’s cohort nod solemnly and say maybe they have a point about trans women in sports. He’s talking about wages and the ability of young people to find jobs.

All politics are identity politics on some level, but like many white straight men of a certain age, Carville doesn’t consider “white straight male” an identity. It’s simply the cultural default in his view of America. He assumes the priorities of the liberal or liberal-leaning white straight man are generally good for everybody else.

But in reality, you cannot separate culture from politics, or identity from politics. Politics is about power and its uses—and elections are about who should have it. Voter perception of the economy is heavily driven by vibes in part because the metrics of the real economy—GDP, productivity growth, even inflation—don’t necessarily reflect the situation on the ground for people. Feelings of financial security are often proxies for whether people feel safe and satisfied generally.

In other words, what constitutes a kitchen-table issue—to use the refrain favored by Carville and the centrist Democratic consultant class—depends on whose kitchen table you’re sitting at. If you’re a young woman being forced to carry a baby you don’t want and bankrupted by medical bills for complications or an unexpected C-section, your earnings potential limited because now you’re not going to college and there’s a lower ceiling on your earning potential, that’s an economic issue as well as a bodily autonomy issue.

And you cannot talk persuasively about affordability without talking about class mobility via education. That ideal is under sustained attack from Republicans who oppose DEI on the basis that they do not want women and minorities in power. Meanwhile, our political economy teems with other practical obstacles afflicting the life chances of members of less-resourced communities, from the administrative burdens of receiving public assistance (some of which, it must be said, were created by Bill Clinton’s welfare “reforms”) to the increasing cost of caring for an aging population. The country’s manifold healthcare crisis—a key driver of affordability—has been made far worse by things like caretakers’ being detained and deported and ob-gyns’ withholding care because they fear criminal prosecution for treating complications in pregnancies. It’s affordability, stupid—but not to the exclusion of the things that Carville dismisses as culture war issues. In many cases, it’s an affordability issue because of these things.

Much of Carville’s analysis is facile and blinkered because he does not understand that the electoral environment of 2026 is not the electoral environment of 1992. “It’s negative partisanship, stupid” may not be the pithiest slogan, but it applies much more directly to politics in the Trump era than Carville’s nostrums do. There is no persuading the GOP to make common cause with Democrats—which is why Republican candidates never attempt it or talk about it. But like many self-identified centrist Boomers, Carville spends more energy reaching across the aisle to hypothetical swing voters than he does attending to the real-world plight of core Democratic constituencies.

Progressives—including the DSA members and candidates Carville keeps insulting—overwhelmingly supported Kamala Harris in the last election, far more so than the weaker independent coalitions Carville seems to want to reach with his Fox News appearances. Progressives exhibit the highest levels of political engagement in the Democratic coalition, yet Carville keeps insisting that Democrats win elections in spite of them rather than because of them.

I won’t pretend to know whether he does this because he’s genuinely ignorant of these facts, or because he just sympathizes more with neoliberal interests and thinks that if he paints the left as fringe-y, impractical, or stupid, that voters will internalize that message and pull the core Democratic electorate rightward. If Carville keeps suggesting that any Democrat’s association with Hasan Piker—a streamer he’d never heard of before this election cycle—is a bigger problem for Democrats than the associations of congressional Republicans with assorted white supremacists, technofascist interests, and Donald Trump himself, I’ll assume that Carville actually wants a Democratic Party made up of MAGA collaborationists. Either way, he does Democrats no favors, because that’s not what voters have told us—repeatedly—they want.

When I ponder the stubborn tenacity of Carville’s pundit career, I keep thinking about a recent Wired article titled “Men Can’t Stop Faking How Much They Know About AI.” That headline is not exactly surprising to me, since I’ve had men explain AI incorrectly to me as recently as yesterday. (I’m a former tech equity analyst and cohost a financial news podcast, but I am also a girl, you understand.) But the phony claims to expertise from American men catalogued in the Wired story have strong correlations with Carville-style punditry: One Harvard study cited in the piece found that when quizzed about AI leaders, men were more likely to confidently assert that they knew who specific people were—even when the names presented to them were fictional. They were also less likely to admit that they didn’t know the answer to a question.

Carville fits the same dreary profile: He’s overconfident about what thinks he knows and will not admit to what he doesn’t. Democrats and media organizations do not need to keep giving him a megaphone. There are plenty of smart people in Democratic political circles who have worked on an actual campaign since the turn of the century, who have informed and helpful things to say, and who understand that the Trump-era GOP is not analogous to prior GOP regimes. Nor do they uncritically assume that today’s Democratic constituencies are analogous to the coalition that put Bill Clinton in office.

James Carville has plenty of money, social connections, and leisure pursuits to keep him busy—which is more than most Americans get to enjoy during their retirement years. If he really wants to help Democrats, he might put his energies there—or at the very least, step back, stop talking, and see if he might, at long last, learn how to listen.

Elizabeth Spiers is a digital media strategist and writer living in Brooklyn. She is the former editor in chief of The New York Observer.

NASA’s Perseverance Mars rover just shattered the all-time off-world driving record

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NASA's Perseverance rover has officially set a new driving record on another world, traveling farther across an extraterrestrial surface than any vehicle before it. On October 11, its odometer reached 28.10 miles (45.22 kilometers), surpassing the previous record of 28.06 miles (45.16 kilometers) held by NASA's Opportunity rover, which explored Mars from 2004 to 2018.

Even more impressive is how quickly Perseverance accomplished the feat. Opportunity needed nearly 15 years to cover its record distance, while Perseverance did it in just five and a half years. The milestone came on the rover's 2,006th Martian day, known as a sol.

The record was set as Perseverance made its way toward a location called "Idubi," traveling through an area beyond the western rim of Jezero Crater known as "Lac de Charmes." This region contains what may be some of the oldest terrain ever examined in the solar system.

The landscape preserves a remarkable glimpse into the distant past. Similar rocks on Earth have largely disappeared because of erosion and the continual movement of the planet's crust through plate tectonics (Mars doesn't have tectonic plates). At Idubi, scientists hope to uncover evidence of ancient hydrothermal systems, where heated water once circulated through rocks and may have created conditions suitable for life.

How NASA's Self-Driving Mars Rover Broke the Record

One of the biggest reasons Perseverance has been able to travel so far is its sophisticated autonomous driving system.

Known as AutoNav, the technology allows the rover to navigate much of the Martian landscape without waiting for detailed driving instructions from Earth. As Perseverance moves, it continuously captures images of its surroundings, constructs a three-dimensional map of potential obstacles, and selects a safe route between destinations chosen by mission controllers.

NASA engineers describe this process as "thinking while driving."

The system operates while Perseverance travels at or near its maximum speed, allowing the rover to cover as much as 1,351 feet (412 meters) in a single Martian day.

More than 90% of the distance Perseverance has traveled has involved AutoNav evaluating the terrain. By comparison, the earliest versions of autonomous navigation used by Opportunity and its twin rover, Spirit, were responsible for assessing only about 5% of their driving distances.

The technological progress is even clearer when compared with NASA's first Mars rover, Sojourner. That early robot evaluated just 20 measurement points to determine the safest route ahead. Perseverance now analyzes approximately 600,000 points each time it examines the surrounding terrain.

"It is an honor to be working on Perseverance at a moment when the technology has evolved to the point that drives like these are routine," said Steven Lee, the project manager of the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "The original mission called for no more than 12.4 miles of driving. We've blown well past that, and with all mobility systems fully operational and favorable extended-ground-life test results, we expect Perseverance to keep adding to this record for many years to come."

NASA has already approved Perseverance's first mission extension. Beginning in October, the extended mission will allow the rover to continue investigating the rim of Jezero Crater through approximately the end of 2028.

Nearly 30 Years of Mars Rover Exploration

Perseverance's achievement builds on almost three decades of robotic exploration on Mars. With each new generation, NASA's rovers have become more capable of traveling longer distances, navigating difficult terrain, and investigating locations that earlier vehicles could never have reached.

The journey began in 1997 with Sojourner, a small rover roughly the size of a microwave oven. During its 83-sol mission, it traveled approximately 330 feet (100 meters).

NASA took a major step forward in 2004 with the arrival of Spirit and Opportunity. These golf-cart-size rovers were originally designed to operate for just 90 Martian days, but both dramatically exceeded expectations.

Spirit covered 4.8 miles (7.7 kilometers) before its mission ended in 2010 after it failed to survive the harsh Martian winter.

Opportunity continued operating much longer, remaining active for an extraordinary 5,111 sols. In 2015, it became the first vehicle to complete a marathon distance on another world. Its total mileage eventually established the driving record that Perseverance has now broken.

Meanwhile, NASA's Curiosity rover, which landed in Gale Crater in 2012 and continues exploring today, has traveled more than 23.65 miles (38.07 kilometers).

Perseverance Is Becoming an Even Smarter Driver

Although Perseverance has already surpassed every previous extraterrestrial driving record, NASA engineers are continuing to improve its navigation capabilities.

"The rover that broke this record did so by making its own decisions about the path. Given the autonomy to just go, Perseverance is a better driver than the one that landed, and we're not done," Mark Maimone, deputy team chief for robotic operations on Perseverance, said. "Our newest software teaches Perseverance to steer less and roll more, and to check its own position against orbital maps mid-drive. The next frontier is sunlight itself. Glare in the navigation cameras is why we park by mid-afternoon if we're driving toward the Sun, and if we can teach the rover to see past those artifacts, every rover that follows gets a longer working day."

One challenge is that bright sunlight can interfere with the rover's navigation cameras, making it harder for its software to interpret the terrain. When Perseverance travels toward the Sun, mission planners currently stop driving by mid-afternoon to avoid these problems.

Finding ways to overcome that limitation could allow Perseverance and future Mars rovers to spend more time exploring each day.

The rover's record also extends beyond Mars. Earlier in its journey, Perseverance exceeded two historic driving distances achieved on the Moon.

In 1972, Apollo 17 astronauts Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt traveled 22.2 miles (35.7 kilometers) across the lunar surface. The following year, the Soviet Union's robotic Lunokhod 2 rover covered 24.2 miles (39 kilometers). Perseverance has now surpassed both of those distances.

Exploring 4-Billion-Year-Old Rocks for Signs of Ancient Life

For NASA, however, setting mileage records is not the primary objective. The rover's ability to travel farther and faster allows scientists to investigate more geological formations, examine a greater variety of rocks, and piece together Mars' environmental history.

Perseverance has already explored several dramatically different landscapes. Its journey began on an ancient lakebed inside Jezero Crater and continued through the remains of a river system that once carried water and sediment into the basin.

Along that route, the rover encountered a rock called "Cheyava Falls," where scientists identified a potential biosignature. A biosignature is a feature or chemical pattern that could provide evidence of past life, although nonbiological processes can sometimes produce similar clues.

The rover has since climbed toward the crater rim, where scientists are investigating a different type of ancient environment. Instead of the lake and river habitats examined earlier in the mission, researchers are now interested in the possibility that hydrothermal activity once occurred in this region.

Such systems form when water interacts with hot rocks, potentially creating chemical conditions that could support microbial life.

So far, Perseverance has gathered 29 samples of rock and regolith, the loose material that covers the Martian surface. These samples come from several distinct environments, including the crater floor, a fan-shaped deposit left by an ancient river, and the crater rim.

Some rocks in the rim region formed during major impacts approximately 4 billion years ago. They preserve evidence from an extremely early period of planetary history, much of which has been lost on Earth through geological activity.

After completing its investigation of Lac de Charmes, Perseverance is expected to continue traveling along the crater rim before heading south during its extended mission.

One future destination is Rainbow Falls, an enormous piece of ancient Martian crust measuring 328 feet (100-meter-wide). The formation was pushed upward by the powerful impact that originally created Jezero Crater.

As Perseverance continues its journey, each additional mile could bring scientists closer to understanding the ancient environments of Mars and whether the Red Planet once possessed the conditions necessary for life.

Hungary’s Rebirth

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The fall of Trump’s favorite dictator—and what comes next.

With the midterm elections underway and the MAGA movement primed to face a reckoning of its own making, the Democratic Party is beginning to wrestle with the question of how to rescue the country from authoritarianism. Hungary—where, in April, voters ousted the long-standing far-right strongman Viktor Orbán, along with most of the members of parliament from his Fidesz party—offers insights into the opportunities and challenges that lie ahead.

As a journalist and author who has covered Donald Trump’s mobster-style leadership in both its 1.0 version and its more extreme 2.0 form, I believe that Hungary’s example is apropos, especially given Trump’s admiration for Orbán and his personal endorsement of the autocrat’s failed reelection campaign. So this summer, I traveled to Budapest, the capital of Hungary and long the heart of resistance to Orbánism, to see how the new government, under Prime Minister Péter Magyar, is faring in its attempts at re-democratization after nearly two decades of autocratic rule.

My first stop was the radio station Klubrádió, one of the few Hungarian media outlets that remained independent during Orbán’s final term in office. Even as Orbán expanded his control over state media as part of his project to unravel the country’s democracy, Klubrádió bucked this assault and provided a vital platform to a new generation of political leaders.

The station is housed in an elegant and eminently Mitteleuropean stone-fronted building near Budapest’s Heroes Square. A vast open space, the square is home to several huge statues of military heroes from Hungarian history riding on horseback, including the country’s founding monarch and his successors in what became a 1,000-year kingdom, as well as towering figures in the Austro-Hungarian Empire, a dual monarchy that united Hungary and Austria into a huge, ethnically diverse Central European mega-state. At the end of World War I, that mega-state splintered into numerous smaller ones. Hungary veered toward fascism and, in the post–World War II era, became a Soviet satellite state. The country liberalized with the breakup of the Soviet Union in 1989, but this period of democracy would prove to be a brief interlude before Orbán and his Fidesz party won power in 1998 and, more durably, again in 2010.

During his five terms in office—four of them consecutive—Orbán succeeded in gerrymandering districts to lock in majorities for Fidesz. He also gained control of the judicial system, which then rubber-stamped Fidesz policies; stifled the free press; and handed the reins of the economy to Fidesz-affiliated oligarchs. Orbán’s actions made the words on the plaque in front of the grandiose, neoclassical Museum of Fine Arts especially poignant. They were from a sermon that Pope John Paul II had given in the country two years after the fall of the Berlin Wall: “Be  mindful of the blessings that freedom, which you have definitively secured, means for your future. Cherish it and make good use of your liberty.”

At Klubrádió’s airy, plant-filled third-floor offices, György Bolgár has just finished his live call-in radio show, Let’s Talk About It, which he hosts every weekday afternoon. It was on this show that, in 2024, a relatively unknown politician, then-43-year-old Péter Magyar, nourished his political ambitions. In the spring of that year, during the darkest days of Orbán’s iron rule, Bolgár invited Magyar for an interview. A former Fidesz insider, Magyar had recently begun criticizing the government in online comments, catching the attention of Hungarians desperate for the country to reclaim the democratic potential unleashed in 1989. Unlike Orbán, who had consistently refused to appear on the program, Magyar accepted the invitation and soon became a regular guest. He hammered home the need to end the culture of cronyism and corruption that had enabled Orbán’s inner circle to enrich themselves at the public’s expense, and to invest more in education, transportation, and healthcare. He explained that in order to do so, a new government led by Magyar and his upstart Tisza Party would need to access the €16 billion in European Union funds that Brussels had frozen as a response to Orbán’s autocratic rule.

Magyar’s anti-corruption, pro-democracy message arrived at exactly the right moment for Hungarians eager for a political reset. That he was youthful, telegenic, and supremely self-confident didn’t hurt either.

The host of Let’s Talk About It understood all too well that Magyar was far from left-wing. On some key issues, including immigration, Magyar’s policy proposals largely mirrored Orbán’s intolerant approach. That wasn’t a surprise, since Magyar had spent his early career as a Fidesz insider: For nearly a decade, he’d lived in Brussels as a diplomatic liaison to the EU, and for many years he was married to Judit Varga, Orbán’s pliant minister of justice. But talking with him week in and week out, Bolgár came to believe that Magyar had experienced a road-to-Damascus conversion regarding the need to scrub the political system clean of Fidesz’s corruption.

“It’s a people’s party” was the way that Bolgár described his guest’s vision for Tisza, which had been a small and powerless opposition party until Magyar joined it in 2024. Magyar was promising a genuine social compact with the people and a return of decency to politics. “His intention is to restore some kind of balance,” Bolgár tells me. Even the name of the party, an acronym in Hungarian for “Respect and Freedom,” signified a desire for a fresh start after Orbán.

To defeat the then-61-year-old autocrat, Magyar knew he needed to build an ideologically diverse coalition united around the popular disgust with Fidesz’s corruption and cronyism, as well as the economic malaise triggered by Orbán’s fight with the EU; a burgeoning scandal over the presidential pardon of someone who had attempted to cover up years of sexual abuse by the director of a children’s home in rural Hungary, which belied Fidesz’s claim to be the party of family values; and the regime’s escalating assault on the rule of law and freedom of the press. “He understood the political opening here,” Bolgár says. “He was convincing. He provided something no other opposition politician could provide in the first 14 years of the Orbán regime: He could provide hope.”

In the months that followed his initial interview, Magyar appeared regularly on the Klubrádió show, and in the spring of 2026, he pulled off what many regarded as something close to an election miracle. With 80 percent of the country’s voters flocking to the polls—enough to swamp all of Fidesz’s gerrymandering efforts—the new Tisza Party leader ousted Orbán and established himself as a model for anti-autocratic movements around the world. “It [was] really astonishing,” Bolgár says. “Nobody would have believed it.”

Bolgár knows firsthand the costs of living under an autocratic ruler bent on destroying civil society. After honing his English-language and broadcasting skills as a US correspondent for Hungarian state radio in the dying days of Communist rule, when he’d worked out of an office in Cliffside Park, New Jersey, Bolgár had returned to Hungary, where he brought some of Rush Limbaugh’s methods—albeit in the service of a more social-democratic message—to the newly liberalized airwaves in his homeland. But in 1994, during Orbán’s first administration, he was abruptly fired as part of a purge of hundreds of journalists who were critical of the Hungarian leader.

Bolgár was rehired in the interlude between Orbán’s first and subsequent terms in office, then fired once more as conservative attacks on the media picked up steam. In 2010, when Orbán returned as prime minister, Fidesz passed a law in parliament that placed the media under the control of a quiescent National Media and Communications Authority, allowing fines to be imposed on outlets that were deemed critical of the government. The new Orbán administration used state media outlets to push Fidesz propaganda; stripped hostile TV and radio stations of their broadcasting rights; encouraged pro-Orbán oligarchs to purchase newspapers, magazines, TV and radio stations, and even some Internet sites; and then heavily subsidized those pro-regime outlets by funneling huge sums of government money their way via the purchase of overpriced ads.

Ostracized by his former employers at the state radio outlets, Bolgár landed at the private Klubrádió station—but the attacks followed him there. In February 2021, the station was deprived of its broadcast frequency as the Fidesz government, by then positioning itself at the head of an international hard-right nationalist movement, turned the screws ever tighter on what remained of the free press. Bolgár, who is now 80, ended up being exiled along with many of his colleagues to the Internet, where, he says, his show now gets up to 80,000 listeners a day.

Despite the government’s ruthless crackdown on the print media, TV, and radio, it never succeeded in controlling the online space, perhaps because Orbán himself never fully appreciated how influential online media could be. Magyar, however, saw its promise early on: Much like Trump the insurgent in 2016, he cultivated an extremely loyal online following. Before he appeared on Let’s Talk About It, Magyar had begun fine-tuning ways on social media to expose the fissures within the Fidesz government and to argue that it was irredeemably rotten, building up a huge following on Facebook in particular. He then announced his opposition to the government on a popular Hungarian YouTube channel, Partizán, while talking with its founder, Márton Gulyás, who had built a career on YouTube during the final years of Fidesz rule by interviewing people and investigating stories that the government and its allies in the legacy media preferred to ignore. “Certain things have to be said now,” Magyar declared in that interview, before laying out the crisis of legitimacy faced by Orbán’s regime and describing how Fidesz’s propaganda was unraveling what was left of the country’s democratic institutions. “The prime minister is the sun in the Fidesz solar system,” he continued, detailing the cult of personality that surrounded Orbán—one that everyone knew existed but that no political insider had dared to articulate before.

Sitting in a fashionable bakery in the center of Budapest, Gulyás—who was born three years before the fall of the Berlin Wall—told me that Magyar’s style came across as a breath of fresh air. “When the interview was on-air, I saw the numbers of live viewers,” he recalls over a large croissant and a glass of orange juice. In the days that followed, their conversation was watched by more than 2.5 million people—an extraordinary number, given that Hungary’s population is slightly less than 10 million—and it catapulted Magyar overnight from relative obscurity to a national sensation.

It also marked the moment that Orbán’s public image began to crack.

Before finally losing the prime ministership this past April, Orbán had conducted, for more than a decade, a master class in state capture. The authoritarian strongman found ways to control and leverage the country’s political, academic, economic, and media ecosystems to consolidate his power and channel resources to himself and his inner circle. Everyone within these ecosystems—as well as many outside them—knew they had been corrupted, but enough people benefited from the corruption to keep Fidesz’s winning streak going.

Orbán turned civil society on its head, promoting a no-holds-barred form of political extremism that he labeled “illiberal democracy.” In reality, this meant a form of exclusionary hard-right populism characterized by the demonization of immigrants, the LGBTQ population, and other marginalized groups, wrapped up in the language of Christian nationalism and family values. In her book Tainted Democracy, the former liberal MP Zsuzsanna Szelényi wrote that because of Orbán’s efforts to amend the country’s Constitution and personalize its politics, “Hungary came to acquire a level of concentration of power never seen in European democracies.” This agenda was never popular in urban areas—Budapest’s city government remained liberal and pro-European throughout Orbán’s tenure—or with young people and intellectuals. But, as with Trump in the United States, it appealed to many rural residents, working-class voters who felt sidelined by the priorities of the previous governments, and elites who found ways to benefit financially from Fidesz’s policies.

It’s no accident that Trump and his henchmen—as well as America’s latter-day oligarchs, leading conservative media figures such as Tucker Carlson, and hard-right coordinating bodies like the Conservative Political Action Conference—have long lionized Orbán. In his multiple election victories, they saw a template for attacking what they decried as a decadent “woke” culture, for injecting Christian nationalism into public life, for locking out immigrants, and for dismantling the administrative state, as Steve Bannon has long advocated.

When, in the weeks leading up to the election, it became clear how politically vulnerable Orbán was, MAGA leaders launched an all-out effort to get him over the finish line, with Vice President JD Vance flying to Budapest to stump for the prime minister. Vance and Orbán drew parallels between the United States and Hungary in their speeches, with Orbán saying about Trump’s agenda, “Our solutions are similar for fighting liberals: God, family, homeland.”

For Trump, Orbán represented a beachhead from which to pursue his increasingly nativist, anti-EU agenda. Orbán’s status as a thorn in the side of EU bureaucrats delighted Trump—and thus his downfall was a painful setback for MAGA ideologues. It may also have been a warning sign. As David Koranyi of the nonprofit Action for Democracy told NPR, “You can rewrite the constitution, you can capture public media, you can gerrymander election districts, but as long as people still enjoy the freedom to vote and to vote for whomever they want to, these systems can be reversed under the right circumstances.”

For years, Orbán’s opponents had searched for a pathway to unity that would allow them to overcome all of the structural advantages that Fidesz had baked into the political process. In Magyar, the various opposition parties and their supporters saw a charismatic leader who could take the fight to Orbán. “He was capable of reenergizing people and giving them the hope that change is feasible,” Gulyás says.

Dávid Bedő, who was elected to parliament in 2022 at the age of 29, was also impressed, despite the fact that, as a member of the left-wing Momentum movement, he disagrees with Magyar on many issues. Magyar “surrounds himself with the right people, and that’s very important,” Bedő says. “I know quite a few of them personally. They are not yes-men.” Leading up to the election, Bedő was one of several MPs who agreed to stand aside and make way, under a unity banner, for the election of a Tisza candidate. All over the country, sitting MPs as well as neophyte candidates for open seats made a similar decision, sacrificing their careers, at least in the short term, for a higher good: to deliver a large-enough majority to allow the opposition to undo the worst of Fidesz’s policies.

Despite the fact that Orbán and his cronies had spent more than a decade stacking the deck in their favor, Magyar’s campaign picked up steam, and as thousands of “Tisza Island” volunteers spread his message into Orbán’s strongholds in the rural heartlands and began peeling off more and more supporters of Fidesz, it became clear that the Tisza leader had a real chance at victory.

On the eve of the election, Magyar’s closing rally in Debrecen, the country’s second-largest city, drew a large crowd estimated at roughly 10,000 people; Orbán’s rally, on the grounds of the imposing castle on the Buda side of the Danube River in Budapest, attracted barely 3,000. The next day, April 12, 2026, Hungarian voters went to the polls in record numbers. That night, as the votes were tallied, it became clear not only that Magyar had won but that Tisza had secured an electoral landslide—one that Orbán had no way of chalking up to fraud. Hours after the polls closed, he meekly conceded defeat. There would be no equivalent of January 6, 2021, in Hungary. A month later, there was a peaceful transfer of power, and the new Tisza government rapidly set to work implementing its vision.

For liberal Budapest, which had long been held hostage to the conservative cultural leanings of Fidesz’s rural base, it was as if a great weight had been lifted. The year before, the government had attempted to ban the city’s Gay Pride march, going so far as to deploy drones equipped with facial-recognition software to identify the participants and potentially impose criminal penalties on them. In response, hundreds of thousands of defiant city residents had flooded the grand streets of the city center to take part in the banned demonstration. Now, under a new government, the festivities would take place without incurring the wrath of those in power, marking the start of a much broader liberalization that was taking hold in the city. In late June, huge crowds flooded Budapest’s streets, this time not in defiance but in celebration, not only of LGBTQ pride but of what many saw as a rebirth of their country’s democracy.

Indeed, even as it was still staffing its ministries, Magyar’s government immediately set to work negotiating new anti-corruption measures with the EU, including an agreement to allow Hungary to participate in the European Public Prosecutor’s Office. These steps would enable the €16 billion in frozen EU funds to be quickly sent Hungary’s way. By late June, Magyar’s team had unveiled plans to set up a new agency that would be charged with identifying and recovering public assets that had been stolen by Fidesz elites, as well as five parliamentary committees to investigate Orbán-era corruption. As László Andor, once the European Union’s commissioner for employment, social affairs, and inclusion, told me, there was a growing pressure to prosecute the former prime minister, as well as those members of his inner circle who had amassed their own personal financial empires.

“It’s classic anti-corruption machinery,” says Márta Pardavi, cochair of the Hungarian Helsinki Committee, a human-rights organization, sitting on a red-leather armchair in her second-floor office in the Central European University’s main building. “It’s not inventing the wheel—it’s implementing practices from elsewhere and enforcing existing Hungarian legislation.”

The new government also quickly axed the advertising subsidies to the Fidesz-aligned press and put the state media outlets on notice that the old ways of doing business no longer applied. One after another, the components of the propaganda machine disintegrated, with many outlets folding and others firing their editors and reinventing their newsrooms. The pro-Fidesz oligarchs’ grip on the media was broken—and in a remarkably short period of time. Just one month after the transfer of power, Hungary’s media landscape had been upended, with the restoration of a freedom of the press and a marketplace of ideas that were starting to resemble those of other EU countries once again.

But the reforms went well beyond simply reestablishing a competitive media environment. Faced with a Fidesz-era president who refused to resign and the upper echelons of a court system still dominated by Orbán’s appointees and loyalists, Tisza announced legislation to amend the Constitution and allow the new government to dismiss the president. Party leaders also teased the notion of one day moving to a system of direct election for the ceremonial head of state. And they unveiled a campaign, “Cleansing Fire,” to remove the top echelons of the judiciary and permit lower-level judges to elect the president of the National Office of Judges. This included legislation to enforce age limits on the judiciary as a way to clear out the old guard of pro-Fidesz judges who would otherwise seek to block many of Tisza’s reforms. Additional legislation imposes term limits on members of parliament—including the prime minister—so as to prevent the accumulation of power that Orbán had achieved over 16 years of continuous rule. The party has also floated the idea of a referendum on a broader revision of the constitutional framework to clear out two decades of amendments, regulations, and legislation that Fidesz had pushed through as it remade the country in its own image. (The Tisza press office did not reply to repeated requests for comment from ministers and government advisers.)

Other changes were aimed at keeping the Tisza coalition intact after its initial job of defeating Fidesz at the ballot box had been accomplished. For young people concerned with their education, the government quickly began integrating the country’s universities into the continent-wide Erasmus system, which would allow Hungarian students to study at institutions all over the EU. And for parents, it embraced a center-left proposal to double child-benefit payments.

This was an ambitious agenda, and one that not every analyst was convinced Tisza could fully implement. “You have to convert from a Trabant to a Porsche while it is on the move,” says Endre Sik of the Center for Social Sciences, who’s old enough to remember when Soviet tanks rolled into Budapest in 1956, crushing an earlier moment of potential reform. Magyar was promising a clean break with the past, something that Sik felt was well-nigh impossible. “When you rebuild a house,” he says, “you use the scaffolding of the previous one simply because it was there.” Purge the entire elite, and you’re left with no expertise, no scaffolding; retain too many of the old elite, and you ultimately risk preserving the previous government’s structures of cronyism and corruption. And even if you can thread that particular needle, he adds, you may not completely eliminate the temptation to revive Orbánism.

“To win the election, it took two years of hard work and a few months of campaigning,” Sik tells me. “To change the political environment, it’s relatively simple. But to change the value system of the society, there is no way to do it in months or even in years.”

A similar refrain—that of optimism tempered by skepticism—is frequently heard in Hungary’s political circles these days. There’s a surging desire for reform, but also a nagging sense that things could still head south. For Eszter Kováts, a political scientist at the CEU Democracy Institute and a fellow at the University of Vienna, Tisza faced a damned-if-you-do, damned-if-you-don’t scenario: If the party didn’t quickly take advantage of its parliamentary supermajority, it would risk squandering a unique chance to undo Orbánism. But if it did move full-speed ahead, it would risk trampling important constitutional protections and eviscerating deliberative processes—and might even acquire a taste for a bulldozer approach to politics that could persist beyond the current de-Orbánization moment. “It’s hard to believe, on the basis of what we see, that Tisza will stop—that it will do this only for restoring the rule of law,” Kováts says. “What is the basis for trust? That is why we like liberal democracy: because you can’t trust politicians to be the good kings.”

Whether Magyar succeeds in his efforts to de-Orbánize the Hungarian political system—and whether he can do so without replicating at least some of the authoritarian tendencies of the Fidesz era—will have huge global ramifications. In the United States, the forces opposing Donald Trump responded with glee to Orbán’s downfall. And Democrats hoping to take back Congress in the midterms and use that victory as a springboard for the 2028 presidential election clearly see Magyar’s big-tent, pro-democracy strategy as one to emulate over the coming months. The night of Hungary’s elections, Democratic Senate minority leader Charles Schumer issued a warning, as quoted by Reuters: “Pay attention, Donald Trump. ​Wannabe dictators wear out their welcome.” If the Democrats win Congress this November—as well as the White House two years later—on the back of popular revulsion with Trump’s corruption and autocratic behavior, the party will likely be closely studying the early months of Magyar’s new government.

Dávid Bedő, the former member of the Hungarian parliament, is optimistic. “It looks liberating,” he says. “After the election, the crumbling of the old system began. Who Was Emily Brontë?

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

A new biography tries to reconstruct her everyday life.

At first, I felt resistant to the idea of reading an Emily Brontë biography. After all, Brontë did not make herself the most approachable author—or person, for that matter. If you take Elizabeth Gaskell’s word for it, she was “never happy nor well but on the sweeping moors that gathered round her home—that hater of strangers, doomed to live amongst them.” Nobody knows what endowed her with such fierceness and strangeness or made her do what she did or write what she wrote. As someone who has read nearly everything she wrote that survives, I must confess that I didn’t really want to know. Brontë herself, after all, seems to have wanted her readers to know nothing about her, not even her real name.

In her new biography of the unknowable Emily, This Dark Night, Deborah Lutz doesn’t commit the prime biographical sin of trying to make Brontë too easy to understand or relate to. Instead, she asks more questions than she answers.

For this reason, while some readers may be frustrated by the paucity of new facts or explanations for why Emily was the way she was, for others (including this reader) this vagueness is the very reason for the book’s success. Weighing in at a mere 264 pages (before notes), This Dark Night is not the door-stopper that habitual readers of biography might be used to. The fact of the matter is that Brontë didn’t live very long (she died of tuberculosis at age 30), and very few traces of her short life remain. Much of what we “learn” here is speculation, gleaned from the poetry, prose, and fragments of writing—sometimes literal scraps as well as ﻿minuscule productions that Lutz calls “paper crafts”—that she left behind.

From these pieces, as well as those by her siblings Anne, Charlotte, and the good-for-nothing Branwell, Lutz imagines what Emily’s days would have been like. Born in 1818 to an Irish clergyman father and a mother who came from a wealthy Cornish merchant family, Emily had to grow up fast. While she was still a young child, she lost her mother and two older sisters, and over the next two decades her beloved remaining siblings moved away: Charlotte continued her schooling and became a disgruntled teacher, Branwell tried and failed at a variety of professions, and Anne found some success as a governess.

As a result, Emily became the de facto head of the Brontë household at the Haworth parsonage in Yorkshire. While her father, Patrick, was busy with his spiritual and intellectual pursuits as the parson of Haworth, Emily took on some of the domestic tasks of cooking and cleaning. Even after her siblings returned from their positions elsewhere, Emily remained the main caretaker of the family’s home.

Hedging language (such as would and may) predominates here, as Lutz tells us what life in the parsonage was like and describes Emily’s day-to-day tasks, which included “turning” clothes (reversing and restitching them so the less-worn sides faced outward), peeling potatoes, and feeding the dogs, as well as her role in the famous imaginative worlds of the Brontë siblings.

Lutz also has to rely on this hedging language when exploring Emily’s intellectual formation in these years. She makes the assumption that the Brontës’ mother, Maria, had “bluestocking ways” and likely read Mary Wollstonecraft based on her membership in various lending libraries, including the Penzance Ladies Book Club, which seems a little too easy, but otherwise sticks to what we can reasonably know or assume. She tells us about Emily’s attendance at church; her time spent in Keighley, where she borrowed books; and her famous wanderings on the moors with her siblings and her mastiff mix, Keeper.

To that end, Lutz wisely keeps out of the debate surrounding what Emily may or may not have done to get sent away to school at age 17. Emily and the other Brontës, Lutz reminds us, had a social existence in Haworth and in Yorkshire more generally. They weren’t, as is often depicted, merely hermits living alone on the heath.

The mythology of Emily as a free-range “moor nymph” was partly started by Charlotte in her editor’s preface to the posthumous second edition of Wuthering Heights. In her grief, and perhaps also because of an inability to communicate what Emily was really like, Charlotte depicted her genius sister as a kind of feral savant—a “native and nursling of the moors,” as she put it—who was somehow otherworldly.

Lutz seeks to remind us that this was not the case by returning Emily to the domestic sphere as well as the “wild workshop” of West Yorkshire that she loved so much. Her Emily is not some weird sprite haunting the landscape, but something even stranger: a real woman.

Emily was also a writer. With Charlotte and Anne, she wrote a series of poems that were published under their male pseudonyms: Ellis, Currer, and Acton Bell. Although there had been plans for Emily and Anne to publish their novels first (Wuthering Heights and Agnes Grey, respectively, which were published together in 1847), they did not appear until Charlotte’s hugely successful Jane Eyre came out separately, also in 1847.

Unlike Jane Eyre and Agnes Grey, Wuthering Heights does not tell the story of a poor governess making her way in the world. Instead it draws on the twisted tales of violence and drama that Emily had heard in her Yorkshire youth, as well as the stories of the bog bodies and other mysteries that pervaded the local landscape. There is nothing nice or educative about the novel; all of its characters, from the pain-wracked lovers, Cathy and Heathcliff, to the miserable servants who inhabit the Heights alongside them, are at best tortured, and at worst torturers themselves.

Ever since Wuthering Heights first appeared (also under the nom de plume Ellis Bell), it has brought out strong feelings, both good and bad, in its readers. According to Lutz, critics at the time largely agreed that the novel was “coarse, strange, disagreeable.” But as the years have gone on, many others have been drawn to it precisely for its strangeness.

Feature / Sasha Abramsky

One such reader, who found in Brontë an unsettlingly kindred spirit, was another unrelatable Emily, Emily Dickinson, who called her “gigantic” and wanted one of Brontë’s poems read at her funeral. One can only speculate about what drew one Emily to the other, but I fancy that at least part of it was their shared sense of estrangement. Both insisted on a certain distance between themselves and the world; they asked readers not to get closer to them but to remain at a remove.

This challenge—to be drawn into another’s writing but also to stand at some distance from them—persists today. In a post-theory world still laboring in the shadow of the New Critics, most graduate students in literature are taught that identification is the cardinal sin—that to identify with a character, or to identify the speaker of a poem as the author, is naïve. Yet as a teacher, I’ve noticed my undergraduate students using the word relate as though it were a prerequisite to being drawn toward a text rather than a pitfall to avoid. I think that sidestepping this shift—from seeing identification as an intellectual mistake to embracing it as a feelings-based relation—is at the core of how I approach Brontë’s life. I don’t want to view her simply as a cold and intellectual figure. But I also don’t want to relate to her; to the extent that I do, it shows me monstrous capabilities in myself.

The point is not to bring Emily Brontë down to earth by claiming (as the 2019 show Dickinson did to that later Emily) that she was just like us, all anachronistic feelings and hidden Sapphic longings, but to bring her down to an earth that was uniquely hers. Lutz’s book does just that. Her Emily is a thrillingly earthbound creature, as individual and ultimately as inaccessible as we all are. To think we know her or understand her would be reductive. But to realize that we don’t fully know or understand her is to realize that she is just like us.

After reading This Dark Night, I don’t feel like I really know Emily Brontë any better, but I do think I understand her world a bit better—her terroir, the environment she was steeped in and that infuses her verse and prose. Lutz does not try to explain Emily—rather, she leaves her where she wanted to be, on her own. Lutz doesn’t make her do anything; instead, she wonders often about how Emily might have found her eventual role as the keeper of the Haworth parsonage, but she is careful not to put words in her subject’s mouth.

We don’t learn much that’s new about Emily herself, but we do learn what it would have been like to be in her world. Anyone looking for easy answers to Wuthering Heights, a brutal and confusing book, or its strange author will be disappointed—but those readers for whom the mystery is the point will certainly be pleased.

New Arrivals in the ER? Georgia Gears Up To Solve Rural Maternal Health Crisis

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The independent source for health policy research, polling, and news.

LAVONIA, Ga. — It had been a decade since Kara Holden last had a baby. But as the former obstetric nurse prepared for her fourth child, her head was swimming with contingency plans.

This story also ran on The 19th. It can be republished for free.

The labor and delivery unit where she used to work in this rural northeastern Georgia community is no longer open. So, she asked former co-workers to be on standby around her anticipated due date. She and her midwife mapped out emergency rooms along the more-than-50-mile trek to the hospital in Greenville, South Carolina, where she planned to give birth. And, to prepare for a worst-case scenario, she curated items to keep in the car in case she went into labor en route.

“That’s a horrible place to put a pregnant woman in,” said Holden, who on Oct. 11 made the painful, hourlong trip to Greenville Memorial Hospital to deliver her son Tucker.

Holden lives roughly midway between Athens and Greenville, two of the nearest cities with labor and delivery units. Owing partly to Medicaid cuts from President Donald Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act, St. Mary’s Sacred Heart Hospital in Lavonia stopped providing obstetric services nearly a year ago. Its closure is part of a trend touching rural communities nationwide. From South Dakota to Alabama and from Ohio to California, low Medicaid reimbursement and dwindling birth rates are pushing hospitals to shutter these services. Today, fewer than half of rural hospitals offer labor and delivery services.

In Georgia, only 36% of rural hospitals have a labor and delivery unit, and four have closed since 2020, according to data from the Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform.

To address the challenge the closings pose to patients seeking care, the state — which ranks among the worst in the nation for maternal health outcomes — has turned to federal money from the $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program. This fund was created by congressional Republicans to offset concerns about the outsize fallout Trump’s signature legislation would have in rural communities.

More than $12 million — out of the nearly $219 million approved for Georgia in the program’s first year — will be allocated toward placing obstetric carts in 43 rural hospitals without labor and delivery units. The carts will be outfitted with supplies needed for a birth or obstetric emergency. For example, they’ll have neonatal resuscitation tools, thermal blankets, and essential medications such as oxytocin, which stimulates contractions, and magnesium sulfate, used to treat seizures in pregnant people with high blood pressure.

The delivery carts sound fine, in theory, said Justin Peterson, an OB-GYN who practices in southern Georgia and has seen the pressures rural health institutions face providing maternal care.

Like Holden, Peterson worries that the carts, while helpful, will put supplies in the hands of ER doctors lacking extensive obstetric experience. Instead, he wants the state to fill coverage gaps by investing more to prevent the closure of labor and delivery units in rural areas.

“You’re putting a band-aid on a hemorrhage,” he said.

Brittany Mitchell was about seven months pregnant when Sacred Heart’s labor and delivery unit closed. She was reassigned to an OB-GYN in Athens, even though the nearly 50-mile drive from Lavonia was stressful and inconvenient.

That OB-GYN wanted to see Mitchell weekly after finding high levels of protein in her urine — a warning sign of preeclampsia, which can cause organ damage, seizures, and other complications. To make it to those prenatal appointments, she routinely gave up a day of work as a home health aide, a job she said already didn’t pay much.

But if Mitchell were to have another baby, she said, she’d still prefer to drive to Athens to see an OB-GYN than be treated by an ER doctor. When she had her first child nine years ago, the local OB-GYN had to act quickly when her daughter briefly lost oxygen.

“If they were in that situation,” she wondered of ER doctors, “would they know how to do all that?”

In written responses to KFF Health News, Lauren Williams, director of communications for the Georgia Department of Community Health, said that training ER medical staff is the main goal of the obstetric cart initiative. The rollout will include simulation drills, during which ER staff will practice high-risk procedures.

The Georgia Hospital Association will procure and distribute the carts. When exactly that will happen is unknown, but the grant funding must be spent by Sept. 30, 2027, said Anna Adams, the organization’s chief government relations officer.

The concept of obstetric carts is great, said Justin Law, an emergency doctor with SouthlandMD, a physician-owned and -operated healthcare management and staffing company. Most items planned for the carts are already available in ERs. But having them in one organized spot will be helpful, he said.

Still, while ER doctors are more frequently positioned at the front lines as labor and delivery closures persist, advocates argue that they shouldn’t be used as a stopgap in obstetric care.

In the decade he’s worked across rural Georgia, Law said, he’s never delivered a baby in the ER even though he’s trained in emergency obstetrics and has experience delivering babies as a paramedic. When a mother needs an emergency cesarean section or has another high-risk complication, she would likely be transferred to a hospital with labor and delivery capabilities, Law said.

Holden, the former obstetric nurse, said familiarity with obstetric delivery practices and procedures is essential because some complications can’t wait for a transfer. “You’re not going to save anybody’s life just because you got access to some equipment,” she said.

Adams understands the concerns and acknowledged that the ER is not the ideal location for delivering a baby. “But sometimes things happen that are outside of our control,” she said. “And when it does, we want to make sure those hospitals are prepared.”

Robin Clyatt, maternal health community outreach coordinator for Hope for Georgia Moms, said ER doctors have vast knowledge. But they might see someone with a major maternal health complication, such as a hemorrhage, only a few times a year, and therefore may lack the skills to act immediately, she said.

“You can’t practice scared when it comes to obstetrics,” Clyatt said. “And that’s important, because when you practice scared, you do stupid things.”

The labor and delivery unit at the Coffee Regional Medical Center in Douglas serves six counties in southern Georgia. The number of live births there increased from 551 in 2020 to nearly 1,000 in 2024, which shows the impact when nearby facilities close, said Peterson, who practices at the Coffee Regional Hospital Women’s Center.

While he understands that hospitals must consider their bottom lines, business decisions in recent years have often taken precedence over patient care, he said.

“They just say, ‘Oh, you know, the numbers aren’t there. We’re closing,’” he said, adding that when rural labor and delivery units shutter, the chance they’ll reopen is small.

Mark Ralston, public relations manager for St. Mary’s Health Care System, which owns Sacred Heart, did not respond to multiple requests to further discuss the unit’s closure. But in its announcement, the system said the decision followed “an extensive 18-month discernment process that included intensive efforts to recruit additional physicians, create new partnerships, and pursue incremental funding sources.”

Nationwide, states are attempting to narrow gaps in maternal healthcare using federal funds. South Dakota will establish maternal health hubs to determine where resources are needed and help mothers navigate the health system. Ohio is establishing birth centers in rural communities.

In its proposal for federal rural transformation money, Georgia included incentives such as funding 75 new residency slots in various specialties and creating a grant program that would reimburse physicians up to $250,000 in relocation fees if they chose to practice in rural areas.

State legislators could incentivize doctors to stay in rural areas, offer higher reimbursement rates for hospitals, and provide loan forgiveness for physicians, Peterson said. Communities should also look to incorporate certified midwives and community health workers, Holden and Clyatt said, though state law makes it difficult for both groups to practice. Currently, certified nurse midwives are allowed to practice only in partnership with a physician, which maternal health advocates argue creates a steep financial burden.

Holden had the ability to send her vitals to her doctor and meet with them virtually for prenatal care.

As she adorned bookshelves in baby Tucker’s cozy, sage-green nursery with vintage trinkets and family heirlooms, she said she thought of less-resourced mothers in the area, who may be on Medicaid or not have the luxury to travel an hour for a prenatal appointment.

Holden worries that without prenatal care, conditions like preeclampsia could go undiagnosed more often. “They don’t make it, and their baby doesn’t make it, because they never got that diagnosed and managed,” she said.

People like to remain optimistic that everything will go as planned, but “there are so many things that can go wrong,” she said. “And they can go wrong really quick.”

States Are Forcing Hospitals To Offer More People Financial Aid. In Oregon, It’s Working.

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The independent source for health policy research, polling, and news.

This story also ran on Tradeoffs. It can be republished for free.

Four years ago, Oregon hospitals were under fire from state authorities and patient advocates. Hospitals, critics said, had made it too hard for patients to apply for and receive help paying their bills.

Within months, the state enacted a sweeping law that required hospitals to proactively screen patients and give financial aid to those who qualify for it. Oregon’s new rules, which took effect in July 2024, required hospitals to skip applications for nearly all patients — a rarity among hospitals nationwide.

“They will be told up front, Hey, we got you,” said Democratic Oregon state Sen. Lisa Reynolds, who introduced the legislation. “You are here at a very scary time in your life because of illness or injury, and we got your back.”

Oregon Health & Science University’s hospital, one of the largest in the state, saw 64% of its patients automatically qualify for financial assistance in the early months of the new law, up from 12% previously.

In their first full fiscal year under the law, spending by Oregon hospitals on financial assistance more than doubled, on average, according to federal financial reports from about two-thirds of the state’s hospitals. That’s compared with their last full year before the change.

For patients, the switch meant direct relief from high healthcare costs, advocates said.

“They don’t have to have the late nights being like, how am I going to afford my mortgage? How am I going to afford these medical bills?” said Adam Zarrin, a director of state government affairs for the advocacy group Blood Cancer United.

Hospitals in the state have pushed back on the requirements, saying they need better screening tools, and they face added financial strain from the Trump administration’s domestic policy agenda. Millions of people are projected to lose health insurance in coming years under cuts to Medicaid pushed through by Republicans in Congress.

Oregon’s early success is the first big test of a new approach that more states are turning to, known as presumptive eligibility. Hospitals skip the applications and screen patients automatically to determine if they qualify for aid.

North Carolina this year expanded its screening requirement to include patients with income up to triple the poverty line. California and Delaware are requiring hospitals to automatically grant financial aid by July 2027.

As many as 100 million adults in the U.S. have healthcare debt, according to a KFF Health News analysis. That financial burden can alter people’s lives, forcing them to skip prescriptions and follow-up care to avoid further bills.

Hospitals are a major source of that debt, and they have faced growing scrutiny over their prices and billing practices.

Policymakers have focused specifically on nonprofit hospitals, which account for about half the sector. Nonprofit hospitals typically don’t pay taxes, in exchange for providing benefits to their communities — a vaguely defined standard under federal law. Many hospitals meet this requirement by providing free or discounted care to low-income patients.

That financial assistance, however, can be difficult to get. Applications can be onerous and confusing. Congress and state officials have spent years testing policies to promote the help and make it easier to receive, with mixed results.

Research finds hospital financial aid spending varies, as does the degree to which hospitals benefit by not paying property, sales, or income taxes.

“Hospitals have been on the winning side of this deal for a long time,” said Reynolds, the Oregon lawmaker. “They enjoy millions and millions and millions of dollars in tax breaks, and that money is supposed to be then spent on poor people who can’t afford their care or even their copayments. It has not penciled out.”

Reynolds pressed for Oregon’s screening requirement, she said, to hold hospitals “accountable for what they should have been doing all along.”

Early evidence suggests the law is doing what Reynolds hoped. Oregon hospitals’ spending on free and discounted care increased to 2.7% of all operating expenses, on average, from 1.4%, among the roughly two-thirds of hospitals with available federal data.

That brings hospitals in Oregon closer to the national average for nonprofit hospitals, which from 2021 to 2024 was about 2.4%, according to an analysis by Johns Hopkins University researcher Hossein Zare, who studies the industry.

Among those in Oregon who started to get more help: patients with health insurance.

OHSU’s spending on help for insured patients reached about $39 million in the first year under the law, federal data shows, about five times as much as in previous years.

Lawrence Furnstahl, then OHSU’s chief financial officer, said in a public meeting in January 2025 that, before the law, insured patients often didn’t apply for help and instead paid their share of the bills. The new automatic screening often wiped out or reduced what they owed — including for large deductibles that patients have to meet out-of-pocket before insurance will kick in.

Patients who pay hospital bills often still carry medical debt, surveys show, because many use credit cards or loans from family, friends, or banks to afford their care.

A 2025 survey from the Commonwealth Fund found that 1 in 3 adults with private insurance have medical debt.

“It defies the purpose of health insurance coverage, which is to enable people to get healthcare when they need it and protect them from serious costs,” said Sara Collins, an economist and senior scholar with the Commonwealth Fund.

For hospitals, the new requirements come as healthcare costs are climbing and federal support for the safety net is shrinking. Nationally, an estimated 14 million more people will be uninsured in 2034 as a result of President Donald Trump’s signature tax-and-domestic-spending law and the expiration of enhanced Obamacare subsidies that Republican lawmakers allowed to lapse at the end of last year.

Hospitals supported Oregon’s new presumptive eligibility law, said Sean Kolmer, an executive vice president for the Hospital Association of Oregon. But they’re feeling more financial pressure, with the organization expecting the state’s uninsured rate to climb to 10% or higher in coming years, he said, up from about 3% before Oregon’s screening law passed.

“That’s a very different world,” he said.

OHSU saw costs rise rapidly. “It is a good thing, but it is an expensive thing,” Furnstahl said in a public meeting as he gave an update on the first months under the law.

In the first year under the new law, OHSU spent about $64 million on free and discounted care — about 1% of what it spent on operations that year.

The true cost of more up-front screening is hard to quantify, medical debt experts said. It must also take into account how many people would have never paid their bills anyway and how much hospitals save by not chasing payments.

Lawmakers said hospitals pressed them for relief, and in April, Oregon enacted a new law to ease the state’s screening requirement.

The original law required hospitals to screen everyone on Medicaid or who was uninsured and anyone who owed $500 or more. Lawmakers raised the monetary screening threshold for a single visit to $1,500. The Medicaid or uninsured requirement remains.

Reynolds, who spearheaded the original law, was unsympathetic to hospitals’ cost concerns. But she agreed to the rollback because she wanted hospitals to have some relief as they fine-tuned their screening process. Oregon hospitals reported that their screening data was less accurate for patients with higher incomes, leading some people who didn’t qualify for help to receive financial assistance.

OHSU studied economic data for Oregon’s residents and reported that about 57% likely had incomes that qualified them for its financial assistance, slightly under the 64% of patients who were receiving help.

Hospitals’ warning of increasing financial stress was a motivator for Democratic Rep. Rob Nosse, who chairs the healthcare committee in the Oregon House of Representatives.

“I don’t want any suburban or rural hospitals to close on my watch,” Nosse said.

People who are no longer automatically eligible can still apply for help with bills less than $1,500, he said.

“I’ve got to make it more expensive for someone to get healthcare so that the hospitals can stay in business,” he added. “What a country.”

Brazil’s Decision

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The moral compass and basic humanity of political leaders really does matter.

It has been common in many countries, including Brazil and the United States, for voters to choose a president on the basis of their perceived character. In some elections, it has been the determining factor; if not, it has still been an important one.

But Jair Bolsonaro was elected president of Brazil in 2018 despite having said in public, “I’m in favor of torture—you know that. And the people are in favor of it too.” It wasn’t a slip of the tongue or a hideous joke. In 2016, as a congressman voting to impeach then-President Dilma Rousseff, he dedicated his vote for impeachment to the military officer who was responsible for her torture in prison, under Brazil’s military dictatorship. Bolsonaro has also praised Brazil’s (1964–85) dictatorship itself, saying that its mistake was that it didn’t kill enough people.

Now comes Jair’s son, Flávio Bolsonaro, headed for the second and final round of Brazil’s presidential election on Sunday, October 25. He defended his father’s salute to Dilma’s torturer and has also repeatedly praised the dictatorship.

The relevance of these common traits, as well as moral values—or lack thereof—is not guilt by association. The Bolsonaros are a team: It was Jair’s political capital from his presidency that brought Flávio to where he is today. Flávio has publicly owned this political convergence, branding himself as “Bolsonaro 2.0.”

Flávio is running against incumbent President Lula da Silva. Lula got his political start as a union leader working in a factory and was president of Brazil from 2003 through 2010. Of course, Lula is pro-democracy and would never praise the dictatorship, which he fought against, or political killing. He has different values than the Bolsonaro team.

This difference in values also shows up in major disagreements over policies that shape people’s lives, opportunities, and living standards. Most recently, on October 16, the Brazilian Congress adopted a constitutional amendment that provides for a major improvement in the lives of millions of workers. It reduces the workweek from six days to five, also establishing a 40-hour workweek within 14 months; the reduced workweek comes without any loss of pay.

For months, Flávio and his allies vigorously opposed and blocked this reform, while Lula was fighting for it. This continued right up to the week before the vote; at the last minute, Flávio voted for it as a senator. But according to press reports, it appears that Flávio and his allies would try to roll it back if he wins, so that workers would have to take pay cuts for reduced hours.

Lula’s current presidency has succeeded in reducing poverty by 27 percent and extreme poverty by more than 40 percent; unemployment fell from 9.3 percent to a record low of 5.4 percent; and the real minimum wage increased by 12.7 percent. His first two terms produced even more dramatic gains. The Bookstore Revolution in Taiwan’s Conservative Heartlands

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After Taiwan legalized same-sex marriage, gender-themed bookstores are working to build the social acceptance that the law alone couldn’t deliver.

Tucked inside a labyrinthine wet market in Taichung, a city of 2.8 million in west-central Taiwan, A Room of One’s Own looks less like a landmark bookshop than the home of a reclusive aesthete. At its doorstep, an acrylic sign asks visitors to take off their shoes before entering the narrow, three-story townhouse. Owner Shan-Wen Tsai greets customers from behind a café counter. Behind her hangs a postcard collage with Virginia Woolf beside Frida Kahlo. Before her, a packed bookshelf displays Stefan Bollmann’s Women Who Read Are Dangerous.

“It’s a little cut off from the outside world,” Tsai said of the bookshop’s location. “I kept a bit of distance from the outside—and that was the distance I wanted to maintain at the time.”

Tsai initially avoided outside attention to protect the mission of the independent, gender-themed bookshop. She founded A Room of One’s Own in 2009, naming it after Woolf’s 1929 essay. At the time, LGBTQ groups in Taichung were largely underground and frequently ostracized by the cultural mainstream, she said. Witnessing the expanding queer and feminist scene in Taipei, Tsai, who is bisexual, wanted to usher in gender resources to the more conservative Central Taiwan. She also longed to bring its underground community “above ground.” Most of all, she wanted to do so with books.

“I treated books as a well-intentioned medium for advancing a cause,” she said. “I felt that if I wanted to push a new issue forward, a bookstore would be a pretty good way to go about it.”

Over the last 30 years, gender bookstores specializing in feminist, queer, and trans-inclusive texts have become a cultural fixture across the socially conservative island. In Taipei, Fembooks, founded in 1994 by women’s activists, stands as one of the oldest feminist hubs in the Chinese-speaking world. GinGin, an LGBTQ-themed bookstore that was open from 1999 until 2024, was the first of its kind in the Sinophone world. Beyond the capital, bookstores find themselves serving as gathering spaces, resource centers, and cradles of taboo knowledge, paving the way for a hard-won legislative milestone.

In May 2019, a decade after Tsai had founded A Room, Taiwan appeared in international headlines as the first nation in Asia to legalize same-sex marriage. Queer communities and allies celebrated what appeared to be a triumphant culmination of a two-and-a-half-year legislative struggle. Scholars scrutinized its success and the backlash that followed.

Yet, as gay rights moved towards institutionalization, new cultural flashpoints surfaced in Taiwanese society, much as they have in the United States: Should gender be included in school curricula? Can gay couples adopt? What bathrooms should transgender people use? Across the island, where progressive values regularly confront Confucian norms and conservative Christian movements, NGOs and activists strained to turn legal recognition into social acceptance. But the bookstores offer a different model of everyday engagement—public spaces that treat gender as a subject of civic discussion and education, rather than partisan debate.

For the three gender bookstore owners I spoke with outside Taipei, advancing LGBTQ rights in conservative communities is an exercise in quiet persistence. As each faces local resistance, they decide what it means to stay open among neighbors who oppose their stance. Customers wander in, some linger, and a few carry books and conversations out. The owners, however, struggle to quantify how much of a tangible difference they are making. With no ballots to win and no headlines to earn, many question how to advance a cause with no finish line—and what to offer the people who do walk through the door.

Between 2009 and the mid-2010s, A Room of One’s Own functioned much like its name: a gathering place for the LGBTQ community’s own. Tsai joined a bisexual group called Bi the Way and, through it, fell into a network of the city’s queer circles. After it opened, A Room quickly became their designated home base. “The connections were incredibly tight-knit,” Tsai reminisced, holding a glass mug of black tea in front of a wall of bookshelves organized by themes—gay, trans, society and culture, motherhood.

Many of Tsai’s peers were estranged from their biological families. Thus, the group found a chosen family in each other. “Our closeness surpassed every kind of relationship,” Tsai said. She called this era “utopia-like.”

For a community that largely existed underground in the early aughts, having a physical gathering place at A Room mattered more than Tsai initially expected. She distinctly remembers one longtime customer telling her the store’s existence transformed how she viewed her hometown. “There’s a real feeling that a bookstore can open up another world,” Tsai said. She wanted customers to flip through texts and experience the kind of epiphany she felt after reading Simone de Beauvoir’s The Second Sex. “That process of opening up a new perspective—I genuinely felt it was wondrous.”

For many, A Room also carried immense representational value. One young visitor, Tsai recalled, passed through the market one day and stopped in her tracks after seeing A Room’s rainbow decor. Stunned, she stood at the entrance and wept. “She couldn’t believe that there was a place devoted to these issues. So she got overwhelmed, and it took a long time before she worked up the nerve to walk in.” Tsai learned that the young woman came from a Taichung family that kept her under tight control. She wasn’t able to move out until her early 30s.

That woman’s situation was hardly anomalous. Tsai said she knew many young people who, years into adulthood, still had curfews and weren’t permitted to live on their own. She also knew many who had overcome “enormous” pressure to love and live freely. Working within a society that hewed firmly to traditional family values and gender roles, Tsai focused on what she could control: broadening the public’s acceptance of progressive values, and urging her community to be daring, but safe. The bookstore, she decided in its early years, should be a place that encourages women and LGBTQ people to challenge the status quo and take a leap toward the life they want.

“What our existence offers is this: Someone with few resources can still hold onto the idea that ‘someday, I can rely on myself,’” she said. “‘I don’t have to be bound by what other people think of me. I still have a choice.’”

As Taiwan’s marriage-equality movement crested in the mid-2010s, Tsai noticed the climate shifting. In May 2017, its constitutional court ruled that the civil code, which defines marriage as the union between a woman and a man, violated core constitutional tenets. The high court gave the Legislative Yuan, Taiwan’s supreme law-making body, two years to pass legislation that addresses the ruling. If they failed to act within two years, same-sex couples would automatically be able to marry.

Opposition arrived quickly. A conservative Christian family-values group, Coalition for the Happiness of our Next Generation, pushed for a popular referendum on marriage and gender education, which would go to voters in November 2018. Pro-gay-marriage groups soon began signature collection for their own referendum questions, while overseas donations poured in. For supporters of LGBT rights, Taiwan became a global battleground for marriage equality.

Stigma surrounding gender and sexuality quickly loosened. Tsai noticed that older relatives who once seemed adamant on the subject registered that “the world is changing.” Progress, however, created its own disorientation. Within the queer community, Tsai said, the old certainty of “us against the world” no longer held. She watched her circle disintegrate as peers sought support in other places—chief among them, the Internet. At the same time, she felt society more generally was lost; gender and marital norms had shifted, but no new order had yet emerged. “In the process of searching for new answers, people were very confused,” she remembered.

Tsai began adapting A Room’s mission and audience to fit the times. The LGBTQ question was no longer just a minority group’s personal turmoil. It was a civic issue. “The time has come for me to break through what you might call our own in-group bubble, and start having a dialogue with the general public,” she said. “There are a lot of issues now that I must talk about directly with them.”

Tsai hosted events and workshops meant for a wider demographic. She even entered electoral politics in 2023, running as a third-party candidate for a Taichung seat in the Legislative Yuan. “I treated this election as a good opportunity to reach outward, a strategy for letting more of the public see gender issues,” she said. Gender equity, children’s rights, and education were among her top campaign priorities. She lost to the Nationalist Party candidate.

The limelight on same-sex marriage also invited clearer upsets. An hour north of Taichung by high-speed rail, Mia Chang runs Langhuan, a gender-themed bookstore in Taoyuan. After the 2017 constitutional court ruling, Chang, who identifies as lesbian, dove into public-facing activism and registered Langhuan as an official signature-collection site for pro-LGBTQ petitions ahead of the 2018 referendum. Taiwan required 280,000 signatures (1.5 percent of the electorate) to get a question on the ballot. “We did not hesitate,” Chang said.

The infamous referendum eventually included five questions addressing LGBTQ rights, education, and same-sex marriage, proposed by two rival camps. The conservative coalition put three on the ballot, including one asking whether marriage in the Civil Code should remain a union between a man and a woman and keep same-sex unions out (the Central Election Commission approved the questions, despite the May 2017 ruling). The pro-LGBTQ side put forward two, one of which asked for same-sex marriage to be included in the Civil Code. In the end, 72 percent of voters sided with the conservatives on the marriage question. The legislature opted not to amend the civil code, but instead passed a “special law” in May 2019. It legalized gay marriage but, in Chang’s opinion, fell short of full equality.

For Chang, the post-referendum setback, though demoralizing, was purpose-affirming. Similar to Tsai, Chang saw her home city, Taoyuan, as an LGBTQ resource desert. She wants to run Langhuan until women and LGBTQ groups are “equal to the point where it’s no longer needed.” The results showed that she must carry on. “There’s a lot of work left to do,” she said.

Public-facing political involvement also invited covert backlash against Langhuan. During the referendum period, conservative Christian and family-values advocacy groups had risen in vocal opposition to same-sex marriage legalization. Chang remembered members of the church coming into the store, pretending to browse, and slipping anti-gay-marriage pamphlets between book pages. “I accidentally found them later, while I was reorganizing the books,” she said. “It was actually pretty funny, really quite discreet.”

Less-reserved opposition also surfaced. Across the coast in Hualien, a picturesque, seismically restless seaside city, Peggy Chiang opened Haihao Bookshop (孩好書屋), or Good for Kids, as a birthday present in 2017 to her then-1-year-old son. Chiang, who is bisexual, knew she wanted to set up a dedicated gender section in the store, but never intended it to become a defining theme. That changed right before the referendum period, when an anti-gay-marriage coalition called for a boycott against her business.

Just before its grand opening in 2017, Good for Kids agreed to cohost and sponsor a civic lecture series on gender organized by Hualien’s district office. After the promotional poster went out, a local “family values” organization altered and recirculated it—the new version condemned the event and cast the list of partner venues as businesses to boycott. Good for Kids was one of them. “We thought, ‘OK, we hadn’t originally intended to position ourselves around this issue [of gender] specifically,’” Chiang said. “But it turned out they basically did our marketing for us.”

Overt gender advocacy was especially fraught in Hualien, one of Taiwan’s most socially and politically conservative cities, Chiang said. Since the early 2010s, local politicians and family advocacy groups have held large-scale rallies protesting the “undermining” of traditional family ethics. Chiang remembered a 2013 march opposing an LGBTQ “diverse family formation” draft bill. At the rally, Hualien’s sitting county magistrate, Fu Kun-chi, vowed that if such a bill passed, Hualien’s household registration office would simply refuse to process the paperwork for gay couples.

“That’s just what the social atmosphere in Hualien is like,” Chiang said. “A county magistrate, in a public setting, voluntarily declaring he’d do something illegal.”

If Taoyuan is an LGBTQ resource desert, Hualien is a resource vacuum. Almost all active queer groups were based exclusively at local universities, Chiang said. Among residents, traditional values still reigned. An outspoken LGBTQ advocate, Chiang guides customers she hardly knows to critical resources. She once offered a first-time visitor step-by-step instructions for accessing gender-affirming surgery abroad, in Thailand. She pays close attention to gender issues at the national level—and bears in mind how much Hualien lags behind. Currently, she said, Taiwan is debating topics like transnational marriage and reproductive technology. “That’s the more advanced end of the gender conversation. In Hualien, we’re still stuck at basic gender equality between men and women.”

Still, the very appearance of Good for Kids, which hangs a large rainbow flag above a tatami-style kids’ reading area, repeatedly sparked controversy among local residents. Over the past decade, Chiang has become a veteran at responding to (or ignoring) online vitriol and backlash from relatives and strangers alike. Everyday opposition, though, comes in quieter moments. She recalled an instance when a mother and daughter browsed the store, flipped a sex-education comic book face-down, and left without comment. “There are still a lot of people in Hualien who are afraid of gender or of sex,” she said. “There really is still a very long way to go.”

As a result, Chiang chose not to let gender alone define her bookstore. Today, she curates Good for Kids’ shelves around several overlapping themes that emerged over the past decade: gender, marine conservation, picture books, special education, and civic participation. She hopes the breadth of themes will let customers have accidental encounters with gender. Perhaps, then, certain ideas, questions, or senses of acceptance will take hold. “One step at a time,” she said.

But how do you change minds? Tsai, Chang, and Chiang all responded the way one would expect bookstore owners to: the transformative power of reading. As for those who won’t pick up a book? In-person events that enable civic engagement, earnest interactions, and, crucially, chance encounters.

“Every talk we hold might gather just a little bit of strength,” Chang said. “It builds up slowly.”

All three owners host speaker series, reading groups, courses, and book talks that have grown crucial to their incremental style of activism. Most recently, Tsai led a workshop on The Ethical Slut, based on the book of the same name about open relationships. A few years back, she staged a workshop titled “A Dating Course for Straight Men,” which, to her chagrin, saw zero registrants. “Nobody dared to come. It’s actually hilarious,” she said.

Chang acknowledged that most people who come to Langhuan’s events are LGBTQ community members and allies. But she’s always on the lookout for conversations with people holding opposite beliefs. “What you really hope for, more than anything, is that the issue can spread beyond your camp. That people from outside the bubble actually show up—whatever their motivation, even if they’ve come to stir up trouble.”

One of Chiang’s most vivid memories came after a talk about the history of Taiwan’s gay rights movement. An older woman, a nearby resident who simply wandered in for the event, approached the speaker after the talk, visibly moved. She said she was a devout Christian, but had no idea the gay rights struggle had been so difficult and had lasted for so long. “She told the speaker, ‘You’ve all worked so hard. You really haven’t had it easy.’”

Moments like this make Chang realize what her work is all about. “[Those kinds of attendees] pass a message along to the people around them, to their close friends, their relatives.” Slowly, she hopes, enough voices will permeate the gender desert to create an oasis.

Beside A Room’s signature pink spiral staircase, Tsai explained that doubt and skepticism, more than anything, led to her bookstore’s founding. “I knew from a very young age that things weren’t the way adults told me they were,” Tsai said. “So I’ve always approached what adults told me about gender with a certain sense of doubt.”

The doubts she harbored in her youth led her to search for alternatives to gender and marital norms. “A lot of these gaps, these differences, kept pushing me to go looking for answers,” she said. Eventually, the questions led her to literature. “It was really through books that a different way of seeing opened up for me.” After 17 years, she said she still cannot predict which exact conversation will change someone’s mind or which book will give someone the courage to leave home. But she knows the questions must remain open—that the bookstore will welcome strangers and friends alike, sow the seed of doubt, and let the search take root.

We can do impossible things

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s all too easy to lose sight of ambition when we’re busy playing whack-a-mole with the world’s problems.

I’d wager everyone has a rehearsed response to what’s absolutely one of the world’s worst interview questions: “What’s your biggest weakness?” Whenever I’ve gotten it, I take a slow—even pensive—breath and rattle off my line: “I say no to myself too much.”

I won’t lie. It’s a bit shameless—a definite humblebrag. I’m a systems thinker: When an idea pops up, I immediately spin up a feasibility assessment in my head. I run lists, plot timelines, and tally budget line items. Sometimes a rough Gantt chart starts to take shape. (I’m a gas at parties, as you can surely tell.)

My brain, throughout my 20-plus-year career as a science and tech journalist, has played host to a near ­constant tug-of-war between ambition and reality. Learning to find that balance is a big part of what’s helped me survive in this industry and, ultimately, led me here. This past spring, I had the great privilege of joining the incredible team of journalists behind MIT Technology Review to take the reins of the print magazine.

If you’ve been reading along with us for the last handful of issues, you may have noticed a few changes starting to materialize. We’ve brought back a regular roster of lists and infographics (both things that magazines do particularly well). But the biggest change is only just beginning to take shape, which is why Mat Honan, our editor in chief, asked me to hop in to introduce myself—and the issue you’re holding in your hands right now.

We’re giving ourselves permission to play around and experiment. To be a little … audacious.

Because, practical though I may be, I do still have plenty of ideas. It’s just that I sometimes need a reminder to stop blocking myself and do the thing. That’s the spirit driving this issue in particular.

We wanted to end 2026 with a slate of stories that check in on impossible ideas. Why? Because it’s frankly a lot of fun to dream about living on the moon or uploading our brains. Because losing sight of ambition is all too easy when it feels as if we’re playing whack-a-mole with the world’s problems. But also because the scientists and engineers who absolutely refuse to give up on even the most daring endeavors—like making synthetic blood or decoding animals’ speech—remind us what this whole technology thing is supposed to do to begin with.

It’s supposed to take cutting-edge research and development and apply it to make life better, safer, healthier, even more joyful. And when we eff up and make things worse (see: forever chemicals, traffic deaths, and AI that encourages potentially dangerous user behavior), we’re supposed to dig back in, assess the damage, and find ways to fix what we’ve broken. Even when unraveling the mess might itself seem impossible.

Right before I came to MIT Technology Review, I edited a newsletter about individual actions that can help address the climate crisis, which some would call—and have called—a fool’s errand, given the sheer scale of the problem. They might say it’s as implausible as an AI kill switch, practical nuclear fusion, or a superconductor that works at room temperature. But if I learned nothing else from three years spent finding ways for li’l old me (or li’l old you!) to chip away at one of humanity’s biggest existential threats, it’s that focusing on what we can’t do only makes the darkness darker.

So yeah, this one’s for the folks who have the audacity to try, and who refuse to let anyone—themselves included—tell them no.

Your B.C. election guide to key climate and environment issues

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Shannon Waters has been reporting on B.C. politics for nearly a decade. Since joining The Narwhal in 2024, her work has focused on energy, resource extraction and Indigenous Rights.

With the provincial election just days away, promises from B.C.’s main political parties are piling up. Voters have been offered tax breaks, tax increases, price caps and new health-care resources. But none of the main parties vying for votes has had much to say about the environment or climate change. It took more than two weeks for the BC NDP and BC Greens to release their climate platforms. The BC Conservative Party put out its full platform — with the party’s first explicit mentions of climate change — on Oct. 18, two days after advance voting began and just six days before the final vote.

After nearly a decade in government, the BC NDP isn’t offering much in the way of new climate-focused policies in this election. The party’s climate platform, released on Oct. 10, includes a pledge to cap the amount of power available for AI and data centres, a policy already enacted into law last year. If re-elected, Leader David Eby is promising to hold data centres in B.C. to high standards for water efficiency and require them to report their water use publicly.

The BC Conservative platform mentions climate just once, stating “climate decisions” should be based on three principles: protecting people from extreme weather, reducing emissions in B.C. and beyond and delivering measurable results. “The need to develop our natural resources must always be weighed against the need to conserve our bounty through responsible resource management,” the platform states.

Unsurprisingly, the BC Green campaign platform promises to put climate policy at the centre of the provincial agenda by making climate targets central to “major policies, infrastructure investments and industrial decisions.” In addition to a dedicated climate platform, many of the party’s campaign promises include climate-conscious components. The Greens’ economic platform focuses on ways to build “a prosperous, resilient, low-carbon economy” and the party’s housing platform includes a commitment to reduce emissions related to buildings by focusing on lower-carbon designs and materials, as well as conserving existing housing stock.

With all three party platforms published, The Narwhal dug in to see where the parties stand on key issues — and what they’re promising to get rid of.

Both the NDP and the Conservatives are committing to expanding the liquefied natural gas (LNG) industry in B.C. Producing natural gas usually involves hydraulic fracturing, or fracking, a process that shoots high-pressure water mixed with chemicals underground to fracture the rocks and release the gas. Making LNG requires running massive compressors 24-7 to cool gas into a liquid. It’s an energy-intensive process that — so far in B.C. — is largely powered by burning natural gas.

The Conservatives have promised to make B.C. an “energy superpower” by doubling LNG production by 2032 and tripling it by 2035. When that policy dropped, the NDP was quick to point out the five LNG projects in development are already on track to more than triple the province’s LNG production by 2035.

The Conservative energy platform also pledges to get rid of B.C.’s industrial carbon tax and replace it with new rules that it says would ensure B.C.’s gas exports are competitive with the United States and Qatar.

A BC Conservative government would take a new approach to tallying B.C.’s greenhouse gas emissions, one that runs counter to the 2016 Paris Agreement. The Conservative platform proposes taking credit for “emissions avoided through cleaner B.C. exports,” using the example of exported gas replacing coal.

If his party forms government, Leader Lorne Doerkson has also promised to use deregulation as a tool to spur economic development, partly by bringing back a ministry dedicated to getting rid of rules, processes and laws it deems burdensome or unnecessary. For every new regulation a Conservative government makes, it would axe two existing regulations, according to Doerkson.

The NDP is promising to use some oil and gas royalty revenue — $50 million to start — to promote climate action and create jobs. The “Clean Futures Fund,” as the NDP platform dubs this promise, might sound familiar, possibly because some of the revenue raised by B.C.’s consumer carbon tax was once dedicated to similar spending priorities.

Speaking of oil and gas revenues, Eby refused to confirm whether his government is still committed to its original plan to collect 50 per cent of profits reaped by gas companies in B.C. The new gas royalty system is scheduled to take effect in 2027.

“We’re going to hold on to high royalty rates,” Eby said in response to a question from The Narwhal about whether he remains committed to the 50 per cent target announced by the B.C. government in 2022.“We’re going to demand as much as possible and we’re also going to build the economy.”

The BC Greens’ platform promises a 50-50 profit split under the new gas royalty framework. The Green climate platform notes B.C. is expecting to collect about $1.1 billion in gas royalties in the 2027-28 fiscal year. Bumping the province’s share of that royalty revenue up to 50 per cent would yield over $4 billion, according to the Greens.

The Greens have also pledged to end further fossil fuel development in B.C., banning new fracking operations, LNG facilities or expansions and pipelines, and axing provincial subsidies, favourable tax policies and public financing for fossil fuel production.

Leader Emily Lowan was the only provincial party leader to criticize LNG Canada’s decision to proceed with Phase 2 of its Kitimat LNG facility. The final investment decision to proceed with the expansion — which has been fully permitted since 2018 — was announced on Sept. 28 and celebrated by both Eby and Doerkson.

Neither party leader mentioned the integrity issue with the existing LNG Canada facility’s flare stack which resulted in Kitimat community members being subjected to excessive noise, black smoke, a towering flame — at times reaching the height of New York’s Statue of Liberty or London’s Big Ben — for months.The consortium of companies behind LNG Canada wants the facility’s permit changed to accommodate higher emissions levels to account for its need to keep the flare burning 24-7 until the stack can be redesigned in 2028.

One of Doerkson’s first public statements after becoming interim leader of the BC Conservatives was to declare his desire to repeal B.C.’s Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, or DRIPA.

The pledge, which is a top priority for the Conservatives, appears in the party’s platform under a section titled “protect private property rights” — despite the fact that DRIPA doesn’t deal with private property.

A Conservative government would pass a law requiring information about Aboriginal Title claims and ongoing negotiations to be made public. The proposed law would also publicize information about legal actions and agreements related to First Nations Rights and Title.

The party’s platform also promises to “restore equal access to Crown land and provincial parks,” a pledge that appears to target annual temporary closures of Pipi7íyekw/Joffre Lakes Park that restrict visits to members of Líl̓wat and N’Quatqua First Nations, whose unceded territories encompass the park. These reconnection periods have been taking place since 2023 and are intended to allow the Nations to conduct traditional harvesting, as well as ceremonial and cultural practices.

Under a BC Conservative government, reconciliation with First Nations would focus on “practical cooperation, clear and fair treaty agreements and economic opportunities,” per the platform. It emphasizes opportunities for First Nations to benefit from projects via revenue sharing and ownership opportunities.

The BC NDP platform doesn’t mention DRIPA but Eby confirmed near the beginning of the election campaign that he would stick to his plan to amend the law to block further court challenges.“We do have to respond to that court decision, it’s not up for debate,” he said on Sept. 25. “We’ve been working with First Nations over the summer to see if we can find a path forward that we all agree on.”

The Greens are promising to leave DRIPA as is and to work with First Nations to develop a process aimed at resolving disputes proactively as a way to avoid court cases — something some NDP MLAs have often stated the law was intended to do.The Greens are the only party with a dedicated Indigenous Rights section in their platform.

An NDP government would stick with its commitment to protect 30 per cent of B.C.’s lands by 2030, a promise the party has been working on since 2022.

In the party’s 2026 campaign platform, the 30-by-30 goal is linked to the Northwest Critical Conservation Corridor, a federal initiative announced by Prime Minister Mark Carney in November 2025 and referred to the Major Projects Office as a potential national interest project. According to the NDP, the corridor will “add protections to an area equivalent to 12,000 Stanley Parks, while enabling responsible natural resource development.”

The NDP is also promising to continue funding the Watershed Security Fund at $30 million per year. The fund was created in 2023 with a $100-million commitment from the province making this another stay-the-course pledge from the party.

The BC Conservative platform claims conservation is a conservative value but doesn’t offer many specific conservation-focused commitments. The party pledges to increase enforcement in parks and wilderness areas with more conservation officers in the field, and to establish sustainable fish and wildlife population objectives.The platform promises a science-based approach to wildlife management with input from hunters, anglers and First Nations. It also repeats a 2024 campaign commitment to develop a made-in-B.C. species-at-risk law. The Conservative platform doesn’t mention B.C.’s existing 30-by-30 commitment. In May 2024, former BC Conservative leader John Rustad told The Narwhal he would abandon the goal.

The Greens would make 30-by-30 a minimum goal. The party also promises to boost funding by BC Parks and recreation sites across B.C. to $114 million, a 50 per cent increase.

In its tourism strategy, the Green Party platform promises to put environmental impacts front and centre when considering development opportunities for new and existing tourism destinations.

The platform has a section dedicated to water management and protection, including a commitment to implement a proactive, long-term approach to protecting water security and ensuring industrial users pay a fair share for the water they use.

None of the party platforms mention the future of Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas (IPCAs), which have been a key tool for advancing conservation efforts in B.C. and Canada. In northern B.C., members of the Kaska Nation say the snap election has slowed efforts to finalize protection of Dene Kʼéh Kusān, a proposed Indigenous-protected area that would cover one of the province’s largest intact wilderness areas.

B.C.’s forestry industry has struggled for years with multiple mill closures and hundreds of jobs lost since the 2024 election. At the same time, the province’s forests have been impacted by wildfires and drought.

On the campaign trail, Eby pointed to the support his government secured from Ottawa to offset the impact of U.S. tariffs on softwood lumber and efforts to get more woodfibre to mills. The NDP platform promises to continue to support the forest industry by providing more cash for mills to upgrade their operations, spending $120 million to protect jobs at mills that are at risk of closing and focus on making sure mills get the fibre needed to keep operating.

The party’s promises to protect B.C.’s forests are less robust.

A re-elected NDP government would dedicate $50 million to permanently protect some of B.C.’s “most irreplaceable and rarest” old-growth forests. The party’s platform makes no mention of the 2020 old-growth strategic review, despite a 2024 campaign commitment to fulfill all of its recommendations.

The BC Conservatives are promising to do many of the same things the NDP has done but better, bigger and faster. The party’s forestry platform promises to make more trees available for harvest — but does not address how the party would ensure companies actually harvest the maximum amount allowed, something that has not happened in years due to largely to the dwindling availability of economically viable trees to log. The Conservatives are also promising to focus on streamlining permitting, reduce costs for forestry companies and support value-added manufacturing.

The platform projects Conservative forestry policies would double government revenue from stumpage fees by 2028-29. The 2026 provincial budget did not include a specific revenue estimate for stumpage but estimated total forestry-related revenues will increase 3.3 per cent per year through 2029.

A Conservative government would “encourage Indigenous participation in the forestry industry by investing in skills training” and dedicate a portion of federal funding for targeting the forest industry to First Nations.

The party is also promising to “resolve” the issue of old-growth deferrals by “protecting original old growth” and making the rest eligible for logging. The platform does not define original old growth but does say that B.C.’s “most endangered and rarest ecosystems” would also be protected.

The Green platform proposes shifting B.C. forestry from a fibre-supply model to one that prioritizes ecosystem health, wildfire resilience, cultural values and healthy communities. A Green government would permanently protect old-growth forests and large second-growth forests from logging, fully implement the old growth review’s recommendations and provide conservation financing to help First Nations replace forestry revenues in favour of protecting forests.

The Greens are also promising to ban the use of glyphosate and other chemical herbicides as part of forest management practices — a pledge the NDP and Conservatives both made in 2024.

Beyond boosting natural gas and LNG production, the BC Conservatives are promising to boost B.C.’s energy self-sufficiency, a commitment that would mean building a considerable amount of additional energy generation capacity. The province currently balances its energy needs through a mix of energy imported from other jurisdictions (mostly Alberta and western U.S. states) and locally generated power.The strategy, basically, is to use local sources when they are cheap and convenient and buy imported power — such as solar-generated power from California — when it is plentiful and cheap. To date, the strategy has worked out well, though recent drought conditions have put a squeeze on those margins.

The Conservatives are committed to boosting B.C.’s energy production “from every available source,” according to its platform.

Boosting biomass production is a key part of the Conservatives’ energy pitch. The party is proposing to increase the use of logging and mill waste to make renewable fuels. On renewables, the Conservatives support wind, solar and small-scale hydro power “where economics and reliability make sense,” as well as geothermal projects where a business case can be made — i.e. with little investment from the provincial government.

Notably, the 2026 B.C. Conservative platform doesn’t mention nuclear power, a source the party touted as part of the solution to B.C.’s energy crunch during the 2024 election.Beyond increasing exports, a Conservative government would also consider opening the door to more natural gas power production in B.C. by making them eligible for BC Hydro’s call for energy project proposals.

The Conservatives are also promising to “restore choice in energy for home heating and cooking” — a commitment to attacking restrictions and bans on natural gas use implemented by local governments — and overhaul B.C.’s Energy Step Code, which sets energy efficiency standards for new buildings.

While the NDP government hasn’t exactly shied away from natural gas as a power source, the party’s platform touts efforts to ensure nearly all of B.C.’s electricity is generated from renewable and low-emission sources.

An NDP government would continue funding for heat pump rebates to the tune of $50 million per year and dedicate $16 million to building out the province’s electric vehicle charging network.

The Green platform promises to one-up the NDP on most of its existing energy promises. This includes heat pumps, insulation and energy-efficiency upgrades, restoring BC Hydro’s abandoned net-metering system for residential solar power and allowing plug-in balcony solar in B.C.The Green energy platform also emphasizes community-owned energy projects, an area where B.C. currently lags behind several provinces.

A Green government would ban oil and gas hookups for new homes and residential buildings and set a goal of using electricity to supply at least 35 per cent of B.C.’s total energy needs by 2035. Currently, about 20 per cent of all the power used in B.C. comes from electricity, according to the Canada Energy Regulator, while 34 per cent comes from petroleum products and 31 per cent is natural gas.The final voting day in the B.C. election is Oct. 24.
