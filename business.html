US oil companies tripled profits during Iran war – NYT

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The US’ largest oil companies almost tripled their profits during the US-Israeli war on Iran, the New York Times has reported, at a time when soaring energy costs threaten to wipe out US President Donald Trump’s Republican Party in the upcoming midterm elections.

The American oil giants – including Chevron, Exxon Mobil, and Valero – earned $130 billion in profits between April and September, according to six months worth of financial results covering more than 50 companies, combined by the New York Times. In the same period last year, these companies made less than $47 billion.

This windfall is an almost direct mirror of the extra $147 billion Americans spent on gasoline and diesel since American and Israeli warplanes began striking Iran on February 28.

With the Strait of Hormuz closed to shipping for almost all of the last six months, and Yemen’s Houthis attacking Saudi extraction sites and shipping in the Red Sea, American producers have reaped the benefit of high oil prices, with Chevron and Exxon Mobil recording higher profits than at any point since the Ukraine conflict escalated in 2022.

Exxon’s third-quarter profit more than doubled this year, while Chevron’s almost tripled.

American refiners have taken advantage of the widespread destruction of refining sites in the Gulf region, and the threat of Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refineries, leveraging their safe location inside the US. Valero and Phillips 66 both posted all-time record third-quarter profits this year.

Europe’s oil giants have posted similar results, with Shell, TotalEnergies, and Repsol all more than doubling their earnings last quarter over the same timeframe in 2025, as Europeans grapple with record energy bills. Some European finance ministers have demanded that the EU impose windfall taxes on these companies in response.

At $4.29 per gallon as of Monday, US gasoline prices are now higher than they were at any point during former President Joe Biden’s four years in office, with the exception of the first three months after the escalation of the Ukraine conflict. Trump has described the elevated prices as a “small price to pay for” preventing Iran from obtaining a nuclear weapon, but voters aren’t convinced.

This week, 72% of respondents told a Gallup poll that gasoline prices are “extremely” important in how they plan to vote in next week’s midterm elections. Most polling organizations show the Republican Party on track to lose one or both houses of Congress next week, with the GOP trailing the Democrats by between eight and nine points nationwide.

Trump has tried to alleviate some of this damage by lifting sanctions on Russian diesel, and at least three diesel tankers have left Russian ports en route to the US. Trump has also pressed Ukrainian leader Vladimir Zelensky to cease attacks on Russian refineries, but Zelensky has apparently ignored these requests.

Lavrov at G20 leaves EU diplomats fuming

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov’s presence at a G20 meeting in the US has angered European diplomats, with several reportedly threatening to boycott a group photo. Germany’s top diplomat, Johann Wadephul, objected to being photographed alongside Lavrov but ended up in the same frame as him.

Lavrov arrived in Atlanta, Georgia, on Friday for the two-day gathering, which precedes the G20 leaders’ summit scheduled for mid-December in Florida.

US President Donald Trump has expressed hope that Russian President Vladimir Putin will attend the summit, although Moscow has yet to decide whether to accept the invitation, according to the Kremlin.

Lavrov’s presence at the ministerial meeting has proved particularly contentious for several European delegations, which have opposed Washington’s decision to invite Russian officials.

“There will be no family photo featuring our European partners together with Mr. Lavrov,” Wadephul told reporters, insisting that “we must avoid sending the wrong visual signals.”

According to several media outlets, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio eventually scrapped the traditional group photo after European foreign ministers reportedly refused to stand alongside Lavrov and threatened to walk out.

However, the German minister was unable to avoid appearing in the same frame as his Russian counterpart.

A photograph of the G20’s first plenary session, published Friday on X by DW correspondent Benjamin Alvarez, showed Wadephul casting a sidelong glance at Lavrov.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul looks over to his Russian counterpart, Sergey Lavrov, during the first plenary session of the G20 Foreign Ministers's Meeting in Atlanta. pic.twitter.com/Z01xQmpO7A

Russian Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova seized on the image, suggesting in a Telegram post that Western journalists had disregarded the German diplomat’s objections by capturing the two ministers together.

Zakharova also pointed out that Wadephul himself had requested a last-minute meeting with Lavrov on the sidelines of the 81st UN General Assembly just a month earlier.

The encounter was their first since the escalation of the Ukraine conflict in 2022. Lavrov later described the discussion as disappointing, saying his German counterpart had merely repeated Berlin’s familiar anti-Russian positions.

The dispute in Atlanta follows earlier European objections to Washington’s decision to restore Russian participation in G20 gatherings.

When Russian Finance Minister Anton Siluanov attended G20 talks in late August, becoming the first Russian finance minister to do so since 2022, Trump defended his administration’s approach.

“We like getting along with everybody. One of the reasons I’m so successful, I get along with everybody,” the US president said at the time.

Lavrov last attended a G20 ministerial meeting in September 2025, on the sidelines of the 80th UN General Assembly in New York. Despite Western efforts to isolate Moscow diplomatically, he has maintained an active international schedule, including official visits to countries across Africa and Asia.

The latest gathering also comes ahead of a fourth round of Washington-mediated trilateral negotiations involving Russia, Ukraine, and the US, expected next month.

Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said Wednesday that European representatives would be excluded from the talks despite their efforts to secure a seat at the negotiating table.

DHK-Warntag: Lohn-Preis-Spirale gefährdet heimische Unternehmen

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Die DHK-Generalversammlung in Wien warnte eindringlich vor einer gefährlichen Lohn-Preis-Spirale, die die Wettbewerbsfähigkeit heimischer Unternehmen nachhaltig untergräbt. Der Kernproblem liegt in der Diskrepanz zwischen steigenden Lohnkosten und...

Die diesjährige DHK-Generalversammlung in Wien hat erneut deutlich gemacht, dass die Lage am Arbeitsmarkt das zentrale Sorgenkind der heimischen Wirtschaft ist. Bernhard Pötsch, Präsident der Wirtschaftskammer Österreich, nutzte die Versammlung als Plattform, um vor den weitreichenden Folgen einer möglichen Lohn-Preis-Spirale zu warnen. Seine Aussagen sind nicht isoliert zu betrachten, sondern als Symptom eines strukturellen Wandels, der die Wettbewerbsfähigkeit österreichischer Unternehmen nachhaltig untergräbt. Der Kern des Problems liegt in der Entwicklung, dass die Lohnkosten nun schneller steigen als die Produktivität. Dies stellt insbesondere jene Branchen vor Herausforderungen, die in den letzten Jahren noch als resilient galten. Die Botschaft von Pötsch ist klar: Werden die Löhne stärker angehoben als die Wertschöpfung pro Kopf, so steigen die Einheitlichen Arbeitskosten. Bleibt die Produktivität hinterher, schmelzen die Margen, und die Unternehmen sind gezwungen, entweder Preise zu erhöhen oder die Investitionsfähigkeit zu einschränken.

Diese Warnung fügt sich nahtlos in ein breiteres ökonomisches Bild ein, das durch diverse aktuelle Indikatoren untermauert wird. So berichtete das Wirtschaftsfoerderungsinstitut (Wifo) erst im Oktober, dass die heimische Wirtschaft im dritten Quartal nur leicht um 0,3 Prozent gewachsen sei. Dieses moderate Wachstum ist ein direktes Indiz dafür, dass die hohen Inputkosten – von Energie über Rohstoffe bis hin zu Löhnen – die Expansionsbremse der Unternehmen bereits jetzt spürbar machen. Die Konsequenz ist ein abnehmender Spielraum für Investitionen in Forschung und Entwicklung oder neue Kapazitäten. Ergänzend dazu ergab die Santander Finanzbarometer eine besorgniserregende Entwicklung im Verhalten der privaten Haushalte. Demnach parken aktuell jeder vierte Österreicher mehr als 5.000 Euro Zinslos auf dem Girokonto. Dies ist kein Phänomen der Geldverschwender, sondern ein direktes Symptom für die gesunkene Kaufkraft. Wenn liquide Mittel auf dem Konto verbleiben, weil die Löhne nicht mit der Inflationsrate von 2,8 Prozent Schritt halten oder Ausgaben für Energie und Nahrungsmittel das Budget sprengen, fehlt der Nachfrage der notwendige Impuls, um das Wachstum der Unternehmen zu befeuern. Hier schließt sich der Kreis: Hohe Löhne belasten die Unternehmen, niedrige reale Löhne bremsen den Konsum, und das gesamte Wachstumspotenzial des Landes leidet darunter.

Ein weiteres, sehr konkretes Beispiel für die Belastung durch den Kostenfaktor Lohn ist die jüngste Entscheidung des US-Silizium-Herstellers Infineon. Dass der Konzern von der EU eine Beihilfe in Höhe von 319 Millionen Euro für den Standort Villach erhielt, mag auf den ersten Blick nach einer Unterstützung der Hochtechnologie-Industrie klingen. Genauer betrachtet ist dies jedoch ein Signal dafür, dass selbst globale Player die Standortkosten in Österreich als erheblich empfinden. Die Zusage von Mitteln aus dem Europäischen Fonds für regionale Entwicklung unterstreicht die These, dass die Wettbewerbsfähigkeit am Standort nicht nur von der Qualität der Produkte, sondern auch von der Höhe der Lohnabschlüsse abhängt. Diese Entwicklung reiht sich ein in eine europaweite Tendenz, bei der Staaten gezwungen sind, Unternehmen mit Subventionen zu stützen, um Standorte zu halten, die andernfalls in Regionen mit geringeren Lohnkosten abwandern würden. Für den Standort Österreich bedeutet dies eine permanente Wachsamkeit und die Notwendigkeit, die Balance zwischen fairen Löhnen und Standortattraktivität ständig neu zu kalibrieren.

Die konkreten Zahlen aus der DHK-Generalversammlung zeichnen ein Bild der Branchenunterschiede. Pötsch wies insbesondere auf die Sektoren Bau, Produktion und Dienstleistungen hin, in denen die kollektiven Vereinbarungen bereits jetzt die Kostendeckung der Unternehmen herausfordern. In der Baubranche beispielsweise haben die Tarifrunden zu Lohnsteigerungen geführt, die in einigen Kollektiven die Marke von 3,5 Prozent pro Stunde überschritten haben. Für mittelständische Betriebe, die oft mit schmalen Margen und langen Vorlaufzeiten für Bauprojekte arbeiten, ist dies ein Risikofaktor von hoher Tragweite. Wenn die Lohnkosten pro erbrachter Leistungsstunde steigen, ohne dass gleichzeitig die Output-Pro-Zahl steigt, dann kippt das Kosten-Nutzen-Verhältnis. Ein konkretes Beispiel aus dem Einzelhandelssektor verdeutlicht die Mechanik: Einige Ketten haben bereits angekündigt, die Preise um bis zu zwei Prozent anzupassen, um die durch die Tarifrunden entstehenden Mehrkosten abzufedern. Dies zeigt die direkte Kausalität: Höhere Löhne → höhere Produktionskosten → höhere Endpreise für den Konsumenten. Die lokale Dimension ist besonders deutlich in Regionen wie Kärnten und der Steiermark. Dort dominieren Tourismus und Energieproduktion die Wirtschaftsstruktur. In diesen Saisongeschäften ist ein hohes Maß an Kostendisziplin überlebenswichtig. Sinken die Margen aufgrund höherer Lohnabschlüsse, gefährdet dies nicht nur die Jahresergebnisse, sondern auch die Anzahl der verfügbaren Arbeitsplätze in der Saison, was die lokale Beschäftigungssituation direkt beeinträchtigen kann.

Blickt man in die Zukunft, so steht die kommende Runde der Tarifverhandlungen im Zeichen eines Rückholens von Verlusten. Nach Jahren, in denen die reale Lohnentwicklung hinter der Inflation von 2,8 Prozent zurückblieb, fordern die Gewerkschaften nun Catch-up-Anpassungen. Die Unternehmen andererseits müssen nicht nur die Erwartungen der Mitarbeiter, sondern auch die Sicherung ihrer eigenen Profitabilität im Blick behalten. Die Prognose ist eindeutig: Sollten die Lohnabschlüsse im kommenden Jahr durchschnittlich über vier Prozent liegen, ohne dass entsprechende Produktivitätsgewinne folgen, wäre dies ein fatales Signal für die Wachstumsprognosen des Wifo. Das Risiko einer Lohn-Preis-Spirale würde von einer theoretischen Diskussion zu einer realen Gefahr für die heimische Konjunktur. Bis zum vierten Quartal 2027 wird sich letztlich entscheiden, ob es österreichischen Unternehmen gelingt, ihre Exportmomente trotz der Lohnkostenbelastung zu halten oder ob der Kostendruck zu einer strategischen Neuausrichtung hin zu mehr Automatisierung und möglicherweise auslagernder Produktion führt. Die Balanceakt zwischen sozialer Verträglichkeit und wirtschaftlicher Notwendigkeit wird in den nächsten Monaten und Jahren das zentrale Feld des gesamtwirtschaftlichen Geschehens in Österreich bleiben.

IN BRIEF: How humanitarian mission in Alyoshki went

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

MOSCOW, October 30. /TASS/. Some residents of Alyoshki in the Kherson Region travelled freely towards Russia, while the others travelled to Ukraine, said Russia’s Commissioner for Human Rights Yana Lantratova.

TASS has compiled the key facts about the humanitarian mission.

- Lantratova said that Alyoshki residents thanked all parties for carrying out the humanitarian mission: "It's very important that people waited, they thank both sides."

- According to her, some of the residents travelled "absolutely unhindered" towards Russia, the others to Ukraine: "People gathered, families with children, the elderly, and buses were organized."

- She said that the residents took their pets with them.

- Humanitarian aid was provided to those who were unable to leave the town due to various circumstances.

- Lantratova said that everything was going on normally: "Both offices of the ombudspersons are monitoring the situation."

- She noted the contribution of the First Lady of the United States Melania Trump to the organization of the humanitarian mission: "This is very important to us, and we thank the First Lady for helping people, for saving lives."

Letztere ist ein entscheidender Indikator, der die Differenz zwischen den Einkaufskosten für das Rohöl und dem Verkaufspreis der daraus raffinierten Endprodukte wie Benzin, Diesel oder Kerosin misst. Wenn diese Marge wächst, bedeutet das für einen Konzern wie BP, dass jeder verarbeitete Barrel Öl deutlich profitabler ist als noch vor sechs Monaten. Im dritten Quartal erreichte diese Raffineriemarge in Europa einen Wert von 15,3 US-Dollar pro Barrel – ein Niveau, das seit 2018 nicht mehr verzeichnet wurde.

Für den österreichischen Durchschnittshaushalt, der monatlich etwa 120 Liter Kraftstoff verbraucht, sind dies keine abstrakten Zahlen, sondern eine konkrete Belastung: Die aktuelle Preissteigerung schlägt mit etwa fünf Euro pro Monat direkt auf das Haushaltsbudget durch. In einem Jahr summiert sich dieser Betrag auf 60 Euro allein durch die jüngste Preisanpassung. Trotz dieser massiven Gewinnsteigerung gibt es von BP keinerlei Signale für eine Entlastung der Kunden durch Preissenkungen. Im Gegenteil: Die Unternehmensführung deutet an, dass das derzeitige Umfeld mit hohen Margen voraussichtlich bis zum Jahresende anhalten wird. Diese Erwartungshaltung ist ein Signal an den Markt, dass die Phase der hohen Spritpreise vorerst der neue Standard bleibt. Die wirtschaftliche Logik dahinter ist klar: Solange die Nachfrage nach raffinierten Produkten das Angebot übersteigt und die Margen stabil bleiben, gibt es für den Konzern wenig Anreiz, die Preise zum Nachteil der eigenen Bilanz zu senken.

Wer trägt die Kosten? Haushalte und kleine Unternehmen

Der Welleneffekt der BP-Gewinnsteigerung beschränkt sich keineswegs auf Privatpersonen, die ihren Pkw für den Arbeitsweg oder private Fahrten nutzen. Vielmehr entfaltet die Preisentwicklung eine Breitenwirkung, die tief in die österreichische Wirtschaftsstruktur eingreift. Besonders kleine und mittlere Unternehmen (KMU) geraten hier in eine schwierige Lage. Ein Transportdienstleister aus Leonding, der beispielsweise eine Flotte von drei Lieferwagen betreibt, steht vor einer signifikanten Mehrbelastung: Basierend auf den aktuellen Verbrauchsmustern summieren sich die zusätzlichen monatlichen Ausgaben auf etwa 180 Euro. Für ein kleines Unternehmen, das ohnehin mit engen Kalkulationsspielräumen operiert, ist dies eine Summe, die nicht einfach geschluckt werden kann.

Diese Mehrkosten werden in der Regel nicht absorbiert, sondern weitergereicht. Das bedeutet, dass Handwerker, Lieferdienste und Logistikunternehmen gezwungen sind, ihre Dienstleistungen oder Warenpreise anzuheben, um die gestiegenen Energiekosten zu kompensieren. Es entsteht ein inflationärer Kreislauf, in dem die Energiepreise die Kosten für Güter des täglichen Bedarfs nach oben treiben. Während BP Rekordgewinne einfährt, kämpfen andere Akteure um ihre Margen. Ein Blick auf den Wettbewerb zeigt ein ähnliches Bild: OMV meldete für den gleichen Zeitraum einen Rekordgewinn von 2,1 Milliarden Euro. Dies unterstreicht, dass es sich hierbei nicht um ein isoliertes Phänomen bei BP handelt, sondern um einen sektoralen Trend, von dem die großen Energiekonzerne überproportional profitieren. Das österreichische Finanzministerium hat sich in dieser Angelegenheit bisher zurückhaltend gezeigt und betont, dass der Markt die Preise bestimmt. Ein Eingriff in die Preisgestaltung, wie etwa eine Deckelung, wird derzeit nicht in Erwägung gezogen, was die Konsumenten und kleinen Betriebe weiter auf sich allein gestellt lässt.

Zeitversetzte Anpassung an den Tankstellen

Die Dynamik an den Zapfsäulen folgt einer zeitversetzten Logik. Preisänderungen am Großhandelsmarkt, auf dem Ölkonzerne wie BP ihre Bestände einkaufen, schlagen sich in der Regel mit einer Verzögerung von sieben bis zehn Tagen im Einzelhandel nieder. Wer heute an einer BP-Station tankt, zahlt also bereits die neuen, erhöhten Preise, die die jüngsten Marktentwicklungen widerspiegeln. Eine kurzfristige Entspannung ist derzeit nicht in Sicht. Die Prognosen für die kommenden sechs Wochen gehen davon aus, dass sich die Preise auf dem neuen, höheren Niveau stabilisieren werden, während der Markt die veränderten Margen verdaut.

Allerdings bleibt der Markt fragil. Sollten sich die geopolitischen Spannungen weiter verschärfen – etwa durch Lieferunterbrechungen oder neue Sanktionen –, könnte dies den Aufwärtsdruck auf die Kosten sofort wieder verstärken. Die nächste offizielle Überprüfung durch den Energy Control Council ist für Mitte November geplant. Erst dann werden die Auswirkungen der BP-Ergebnisse des dritten Quartals in die offiziellen Prognosen und Preismodelle einfließen. Für Haushalte mit engem Budget bedeutet dies: Die finanzielle Belastung wird in den Wochen nicht nachlassen. Viele Konsumenten versuchen bereits, auf solche Preisschwankungen zu reagieren, indem sie ihr Tankverhalten optimieren. Insbesondere in den frühen Morgenstunden sind die Preise an den Tankstellen oft niedriger, bevor die Nachfrage im Laufe des Tages ansteigt und die Betreiber die Preise nach oben korrigieren. Dieses "Timing" ist für viele Autofahrer zu einer notwendigen Strategie geworden, um den Preisanstieg zumindest marginal abzufedern.

Handelsabkommen im Kontext volatiler Energiemärkte

Die aktuelle Situation wirft auch ein Schlaglicht auf die komplexen Zusammenhänge zwischen internationaler Handelspolitik und der realen Kaufkraft der Haushalte. Das CETA-Abkommen zwischen der EU und Kanada feiert sein zehnjähriges Bestehen, und die Bilanz ist durchaus positiv: Österreich verzeichnete einen Anstieg der Importe aus Kanada um 15 Prozent, insbesondere bei Maschinen und landwirtschaftlichen Produkten. Diese Handelsvorteile sollten theoretisch zu günstigeren Warenpreisen führen. Doch der aktuelle Anstieg der Energiepreise konterkariert diese positiven Effekte massiv.

Die Energiepreise – insbesondere für Heizöl und Erdgas – sind eng mit den globalen Öl-Benchmarks verknüpft, die auch die Spritpreise bestimmen. Wenn die Energiekosten steigen, schmilzt der Netto-Vorteil durch niedrigere Zölle dahin. Ein konkretes Beispiel: Eine Familie, die für diesen Winter von einer elektrischen Heizung auf Öl umsteigt, sieht sich mit einer Rechnung konfrontiert, die etwa 20 Prozent höher ausfällt als im Vorjahr. Die Ersparnisse, die durch günstigere importierte Waren aus Kanada erzielt werden, werden durch diese Mehrkosten bei der Energie regelrecht aufgefressen. Dieses Zusammenspiel verdeutlicht, dass globale Rohstoffmärkte für den österreichischen Konsumenten oft eine direktere und spürbarere Rolle spielen als die langfristigen Vorteile von Handelsabkommen. Die Energiepreise wirken wie ein Filter, der die wirtschaftliche Gesamtsituation bestimmt und die Kaufkraft der privaten Haushalte maßgeblich beeinflusst. Während die Politik Handelsabkommen als Erfolg feiert, spüren die Bürger im Alltag die Auswirkungen der volatilen Energiemärkte, die den Nutzen der Handelsliberalisierung oftmals neutralisieren.

Writing in 1994, Dinan argued that “European integration is a good thing,” and the phrases he used – “ever closer union” and “pooling sovereignty,” for example – have since become staples of EU speeches and press releases.

Western Europe is falling apart – we should prepare for what comes next

By Timofey Bordachev, Program Director of the Valdai Club

For Russia, developments in the rest of Europe matter primarily insofar as they affect our national interests. Above all, this concerns security given that, for centuries, Russia has faced its greatest challenges from the West, and there’s little reason to assume that this historical pattern has disappeared.

Western Europe’s leading countries now stand at a crossroads, yet the choice confronting them isn’t simply between competing models of domestic development, it’s between war and peace with their neighbors, foremost among them Russia, whose size and historical role have repeatedly made it the principal obstacle to Western European expansion.

The unrest sweeping several European countries this autumn marks a new phase in the continent’s systemic crisis as it reflects the convergence of two long-term problems that much of Europe’s political establishment has proved incapable of resolving.

The first is the exhaustion of the socioeconomic model that sustained Western European prosperity throughout the Cold War and its aftermath. That model depended on Europe’s privileged position in the global economy and the absence of serious competitors beyond the Western world, but China’s rise, combined with increasingly limited resources, has undermined those advantages.

Closer economic integration with Russia, and access to its enormous natural wealth, might have offered Western Europe a final opportunity to preserve its exceptional position, but instead regional leaders allowed their own arrogance to prevent an accommodation with Moscow because they couldn’t accept that Russia wished to live peacefully with its neighbors, but on terms consistent with its own interests.

The second problem is Western Europe’s longstanding absence of genuine sovereignty.

Integration into the American-led NATO system has deprived even major powers such as Germany, France, and Britain of the freedom to pursue fundamental political change and one of the alliance’s practical functions has been to preserve the existing political order within its member states.

Any serious attempt to transform that order would require a reassessment of relations with Washington and such independence is something these European governments have long been unwilling, or unable, to contemplate.

Together, these structural weaknesses have left Western Europe’s political and economic establishments effectively paralyzed.

For nearly two decades, governments have substituted the appearance of activity for meaningful reform. Successive emergencies, involving migration, the pandemic, hostility towards Russia, and preparations for military confrontation, have allowed leaders to avoid addressing deeper problem and this helps explain the temptation to interpret today’s unrest as another exercise in political manipulation. Emmanuel Macron, for example, has repeatedly demonstrated an ability to survive the consequences of disastrous decisions through tactical maneuvering.

Yet it would be a mistake to dismiss the protests as artificial or orchestrated.

In France, the initial demonstrations were driven by schoolchildren and university students demanding more teachers and improvements to an education system suffering from declining standards, but these grievances are hardly unique to France and they reflect broader weaknesses in modern education, including funding arrangements that encourage institutions to compete for pupils rather than concentrate on teaching.

By early October, the French protests had expanded as other groups affected by Macron’s liberal economic policies joined the demonstrations. The authorities blamed the political left, while unrest escalated into rioting and looting.

Similar discontent emerged in Spain and Portugal, where soaring housing costs became the immediate trigger. Both countries have spent years occupying subordinate positions within the European Union, with little influence over its economic priorities, even as their former politicians find comfortable positions in Brussels.

Western Europe has consequently entered a period in which conventional political competition is increasingly accompanied by mass protest and the participation of young people is particularly significant.

In France, the EU’s most important military power and its only nuclear-armed member, it was students who provided the initial momentum. While much of the adult electorate has descended into political apathy under Macron, younger generations retain aspirations for a better future and their grievances are genuine. So, too, is the wider crisis that has brought people onto the streets.

There are therefore grounds to believe that public dissatisfaction may eventually bring new political forces to power, which from Russia’s perspective raises an obvious possibility in that perhaps Western Europe’s next generation of leaders will prove more reasonable on questions of security and relations with Moscow.

Such hopes, however, may be premature because they reflect less our actual experience of European politics than our desire to imagine a Western Europe capable of recovering its political judgement.

Indeed, the objective nature of the present crisis makes its possible consequences especially troubling and European history offers little reassurance that profound domestic upheaval produces peaceful relations with neighbouring countries. On the contrary, revolutionary transformations have repeatedly generated external aggression, and the French Revolution provides one example.

The upheavals of the late 18th century were followed by wars against neighboring states and, eventually, Napoleon’s attempt to dominate Europe.

The extraordinary energy released by revolution demanded an outlet, while new rulers also needed resources and opportunities for enrichment beyond their borders.

As Henry Kissinger observed in his early historical work, the internal foundations of Napoleon’s expansionism helped prevent a lasting accommodation with Russia after the disastrous campaign of 1812.

Germany offers an even more destructive precedent, where the political upheavals of the 20th century ultimately brought to power a regime whose ambitions extended far beyond Germany’s borders. The conquest of neighboring countries was followed by an attack on the Soviet Union and the most devastating war in human history.

Again, internal political transformation was accompanied by an effort to direct national energies towards external expansion and the consequences of that war fundamentally altered Europe’s position as many of its leading states lost much of their effective sovereignty and became dependent on the United States for their security.

Paradoxically, that dependence may now offer some protection against the most dangerous consequences of Europe’s internal crisis because governments that lack genuine strategic independence may also lack the freedom to embark upon adventures of their own choosing.

Nevertheless, Russia can’t base its security on the assumption that European political upheaval will produce more sensible leaders even if the temptation to hope for a peaceful transformation of Europe is understandable. Hope isn’t a strategy, and history provides ample grounds for caution.

Russia must therefore prepare not for the miracle of a Western Europe reconciled to peaceful coexistence, but for the possibility that its internal crisis will once again find expression in confrontation abroad.

Whatever political forces eventually emerge from the present turmoil, preparing for a military confrontation with our European neighbors must remain one of Russia’s central strategic priorities.

The parliamentary elections in Israel were a major test of public confidence in Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

Israelis went to the polls in the aftermath of the events of October 7, 2023, the wars in Gaza and Lebanon, the confrontation with Iran, and increasingly strained relations with several international partners. For now, one thing is evident: Neither side has secured a clear parliamentary majority.

The turnout reflects a profound political mobilization within Israeli society. Over the past several years, decisions made by the leadership have become increasingly intertwined with questions of personal security, economic well-being, and the future of individual families. As a result, even citizens who previously remained relatively disengaged from politics have become more actively involved.

The events of October 7, 2023 were the decisive catalyst for this transformation. The failure of the national security system severely undermined confidence in the political leadership, and the subsequent wars failed to produce lasting national unity around the government. Instead, they intensified questions of political accountability, the human and economic costs of military decisions, and the absence of a convincing strategy for ending the conflicts. Even supporters of a hardline security approach have grown disillusioned with its effectiveness.

Socioeconomic pressures have also played an important role. Rising military expenditure, the high cost of living, housing difficulties, and prolonged reserve military service have shown the people the price of continuing instability. Particularly contentious has been the exemption of sections of the ultra-Orthodox community from military service, while other segments of society bear the burden.

Israel’s deteriorating international standing has further compounded these challenges. Disagreements with traditional European partners over the war in Gaza, developments in the West Bank, and the policies pursued by far-right members of the government are leaving the country with less and less room for diplomatic maneuvers. Some voters increasingly blame the government for growing diplomatic isolation and economic risks.

The high turnout was not just against Netanyahu. Right-wing voters were also highly mobilized, as was the Arab electorate, whose parties achieved a notable result. Nevertheless, the elections suggest that the existing political situation is no longer widely seen as sustainable.

In Israeli politics, electoral success does not necessarily translate into political power. In the 120-seat Knesset, a governing coalition requires the support of at least 61 members, a threshold that neither of the two main political camps is so far projected to reach. The decisive struggle will now move to negotiations between parties.

After the official results are released, President Isaac Herzog will consult parliamentary factions and entrust the task of forming a government to the candidate considered most capable of securing a majority. Netanyahu’s camp will have to seek additional support beyond its traditional allies, while the opposition faces the difficult task of reconciling the positions of centrists, right-wing opponents of Netanyahu, and more liberal parties.

Arab parties, which are projected to hold 19 seats, could become particularly important. Eisenkot and some of his potential coalition partners, however, have ruled out including them in the government. External parliamentary support or abstention during a confidence vote could theoretically alter the balance, but this would require politically sensitive compromises from the centrist camp.

Netanyahu’s extensive experience in coalition-building gives him considerable opportunities to negotiate with smaller parties and persuade individual political actors to abandon competing alliances. Even if he fails to assemble a majority, he will remain in office as caretaker prime minister until a new government is formed. His political position has undoubtedly weakened, but this does not yet amount to his removal from power.

Netanyahu entered these elections with a loyal electoral base intact, but with substantially weakened support beyond the right-wing camp. National security has always been his central political argument. Yet the consequences of October 7 and the prolonged military campaigns have seriously damaged his reputation, raising questions about the effectiveness of his leadership.

With this in mind, reports of preparations for a new military operation against Iran shortly before the elections deserve particular attention. Axios reported on discussions between Netanyahu and US President Donald Trump, the Financial Times described preparations for a large-scale strike in early October, and CNN reported on Israeli efforts to secure American support. According to these accounts, the Trump administration prefers to avoid another major escalation ahead of the US elections scheduled for November 3. Netanyahu denied reports that Trump prevented a planned Israeli attack, and some of the reported details have also been disputed by Israeli media.

A swift and successful military operation against Iran could have unified a substantial part of society around Likud, restored Netanyahu’s image as a decisive defender of national security, and pushed criticism of his domestic policies into the background. While this does not prove that a strike was being planned for electoral purposes, the potential political benefits are obvious.

On the other hand, a short war against Iran would have been an extremely risky gamble. Iranian retaliation, the involvement of Tehran’s regional allies, additional pressure on Israel’s missile defense systems, and potential disruption of global energy markets could have produced another crisis of public confidence. The combination of American constraints and unpredictable military consequences appears to have prevented this from happening before the election.

Ultimately, the election took place without a major military development capable of fundamentally shifting votes. Should Netanyahu remain in power, the most likely outcome would be the continuation of his hardline approach towards Iran and aligned armed groups. Continued dependence on far-right coalition partners could push this policy in an even more confrontational direction. Even so, Israel would still have to take Washington’s position into account, given the importance of American support for major military operations.

Gadi Eisenkot, who served as chief of the General Staff of the Israel Defense Forces from 2015 to 2019, has emerged as Netanyahu’s principal political alternative. His main advantage lies in offering a change of leadership without abandoning the centrality of national security. For voters dissatisfied with the incumbent prime minister but unwilling to embrace a fundamental ideological shift, this is an appealing compromise. Eisenkot presents himself as a centrist, institutionally minded political figure with extensive experience in military leadership.

Particular emphasis in his political program is placed on soldiers, reservists, and their families. His party has proposed expanding housing, tax, and education benefits, increasing social assistance, and raising the remuneration of conscripts by 15%, with a 50% increase for combat soldiers in their third year of service. These proposals have the potential to attract military families that have long demanded more equitable social protection. It would, however, be an exaggeration to suggest that Israel’s entire security establishment supports a single candidate.

Another important part of Eisenkot’s electoral base consists of citizens disillusioned with the governing coalition’s style of leadership, its disputes with state institutions, and its dependence on far-right parties. Eisenkot promises to restore confidence in government, broaden the principle of compulsory military or national service, and reduce divisions within society. His political appeal is centered on effective governance rather than ideological transformation.

On the Palestinian question, however, fundamental changes are unlikely. Eisenkot opposes the establishment of a fully independent Palestinian state under present conditions and insists on maintaining Israeli control over essential security matters. Although some Jewish parties support the two-state solution, the core of a new coalition would most likely consist of nationally oriented parties. Nevertheless, Eisenkot’s criticism of illegal settlement outposts and settler violence suggests that a change in political methods is possible.

There is much more room for change in foreign policy. A departure from a government of far-right politicians whose rhetoric has complicated Israel’s relations with European partners and Arab states could, in itself, help reduce diplomatic tensions. A more pragmatic cabinet would have the opportunity to restore working relationships, ease Israel’s international isolation, and pursue a gradual normalization of relations across the region.

However, a more restrained diplomatic approach should not be mistaken for the abandonment of military deterrence. Eisenkot regards Iran’s nuclear program, its missile capabilities, and its network of regional allies as serious threats to national security. Under his leadership, Israel would likely maintain a firm policy of containment while seeking closer coordination with Washington and a more balanced combination of military and diplomatic instruments. This approach could reduce certain risks, but it would not eliminate the underlying confrontation.

The main dilemma emerging from the election is that many Israelis who voted against Netanyahu expect greater stability and a reduction in regional tensions, yet the country’s security environment and the positions of opposition leaders themselves impose significant constraints on any rapid political compromise. Eisenkot could change the style of governance and improve the atmosphere of Israel’s relations with the outside world, but he cannot guarantee peace. Netanyahu, by contrast, would be more likely to continue along the current path.

Until the official results are published and coalition negotiations concluded, it is impossible to predict with confidence who will become the next prime minister. The elections have nevertheless exposed profound public fatigue with a prolonged period of crisis and the absence of a clear political mandate for overcoming it.

The ultimate outcome will determine far more than the composition of the next government. It may also shape the future trajectory of the entire Middle East, influencing whether the region moves towards a period of cautious stabilization or enters another phase of escalating confrontation.

From Pushkin’s ancestor to Mandela’s envoys: The untold story of Russia-Africa ties

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In 1944, an unusual pavilion dedicated to the Soviet Union opened in Johannesburg, South Africa. Visitors could discover more about life in the USSR, view exhibitions, relax in a tea garden, and even stop by a vodka bar. The pavilion was very popular and attracted a total of about 250,000 visitors. This little-known episode is just one example of the rich and varied history of Russia-Africa relations. From Russian officers at the court of the Ethiopian emperor to African students who studied in Russia under the patronage of Nicholas II, the New Moscow Cossack settlement on the Red Sea coast, and the son of a Russian officer who became one of Ethiopia’s first pilots, we explore some of the highlights of Russia-Africa relations below.

Perhaps the most famous African figure in the history of the Russian Empire is Abram Gannibal, the great-grandfather of the great Russian poet Alexander Pushkin. In the early 18th century, the African boy found himself at the court of Peter the Great, who became his godfather and later sent him to study in France. Gannibal received military training, became an engineer, and eventually rose to the rank of general. Historians still debate the exact location of his birth, with modern-day Cameroon and Eritrea among the possible locations.

However, Gannibal was far from the only African at the Russian court. Africans appeared in Russia as early as the 17th century, and later, a specialized court position was instituted: ‘Arap (Moor) of the Imperial Court’. Some of them feature in paintings depicting the life of Russian monarchs.

For a long time, Russians had a limited understanding of Africa, largely shaped by books and traveler accounts. Russian pilgrims traveled to Egypt, merchants traded with North African ports, and scholars gradually discovered Africa’s geography and history. However, by the late 19th century, interest in Africa began to take on a more practical dimension.

One of the most unusual expressions of this interest was Nikolay Ashinov’s expedition. Ashinov called himself the ataman (leader) of the free Cossacks. In 1889, he landed with a group of like-minded individuals on the shores of what is now Djibouti, proclaiming the creation of a Russian settlement called ‘New Moscow’. They raised the Russian flag over it, but it soon turned out that the territory fell under French colonial interests. French military forces commanded the settlers to abandon the fort; when the latter refused, the French shelled the area and detained the expedition members. The Russian government, which did not support Ashinov’s initiative, distanced itself from the endeavor, and that marked the end of one of the most peculiar attempts at establishing a Russian presence in Africa.

Russia’s relationship with Ethiopia fared much better. Ethiopia was one of the few African nations that managed to retain independence during the era of European colonial expansion. Russia and Ethiopia shared the Christian faith, and Ethiopian Emperor Menelik II viewed Russia as a potential partner that did not seek to encroach on his territories. One of the first Russians to establish personal contact with him was military officer Viktor Mashkov. In 1889, he traveled to Ethiopia (formally as a correspondent for the newspaper Novoye Vremya) and managed to secure an audience with the emperor. Upon returning to St. Petersburg, Mashkov brought back a personal letter from Menelik II to Alexander III, along with gifts, including ceremonial weapons. He later returned to Ethiopia with a reply from the Russian monarch. These visits laid the groundwork for the establishment of official diplomatic relations between the two countries in 1898.

Cooperation swiftly extended beyond diplomacy. Following the Ethiopian army’s victory over the Italians at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, a medical mission from the Russian Red Cross Society arrived in Ethiopia. Menelik II was highly impressed by Russian doctors. According to the reports of Russian diplomat Pyotr Vlasov, during a visit to a hospital in 1898, the emperor didn’t just inspect the wards; he donned a hospital gown, attended a surgical operation, questioned patients, and examined medical instruments. The partnership in the field of healthcare became one of the most enduring traditions in Russia-Ethiopia relations.

The story of the first Ethiopian students in Russia is less well known. In 1897, Menelik II reached out to Nicholas II, requesting that several young Ethiopians be accepted to study in Russia. The Russian emperor agreed, and five young men, aged between 17 and 20, made their way to Russia. They were supposed to study medicine and various trades and then return home. However, the Russian education system wasn’t adapted to the needs of African students. For example, two of the young men were expected to enter a vocational school that typically admitted boys aged 12 to 15. The new students were significantly older and didn’t speak Russian, necessitating special language classes and high-level discussions about funding. In April 1898, Finance Minister Sergey Witte appealed to Nicholas II to allocate 250 rubles annually for the education of the students and an additional 1,200 rubles for their instructor. Sixty years before the establishment of the Peoples’ Friendship University of Russia, the Russian emperor personally supported this small group of African students.

Equally intriguing is the story of the Babichev family. In the late 19th century, a Russian officer named Ivan Babichev traveled to Ethiopia and decided to stay there permanently. He joined Menelik II’s service, married an Ethiopian woman, and eventually rose to the rank of fitawrari (commander). His son, Mishka Babichev, became even more famous. When soon-to-be emperor Haile Selassie began building Ethiopia’s aviation program in the late 1920s, young Babichev became one of its pioneers. He received flight training in France and became one of Ethiopia’s first military pilots. He later played a significant role in establishing the country’s civil aviation. After the Second World War, Mishka Babichev was appointed as Ethiopia’s diplomatic representative to the Soviet Union.

In the same period, the attention of Russian society also turned to South Africa. The Anglo-Boer War broke out in 1899. In the course of this war, two small republics – Transvaal and the Orange Free State – fought against the British Empire. The Russian public strongly sympathized with the Boers (Afrikaners, descendants of Dutch settlers); newspapers extensively covered the situation on the front lines, charity drives were organized, and some Russians volunteered to fight in South Africa. Approximately 200 individuals from Russia fought alongside the Boers. Among them was Evgeny Maximov, who led a contingent of foreign volunteers and was promoted to the rank of general by the Boers, as well as Aleksey Guchkov, who would later become chairman of the State Duma and Minister of War in the Provisional Government.

The most enduring impact of the Anglo-Boer War on Russian culture is reflected in the song ‘Transvaal, Transvaal, my homeland, you’re all on fire!’ which became popular in Russia at the turn of the century. The song, based on Galina Galina’s poem, quickly gained popularity throughout Russia. It was later often heard in movies.

Decades later, the relationship between Russia and South Africa took an unexpected turn. During WWII, the Soviet Union and the Union of South Africa became allies. In South African cities, various organizations emerged to collect aid for the USSR, and the Society of Friends of the Soviet Union held large-scale events to support the efforts.

In July 1944, the University of the Witwatersrand hosted a two-day congress promoting African-Soviet friendship that brought together about 600 delegates. In November 1944, a Soviet pavilion opened in Johannesburg, featuring exhibition halls, a tea garden, and even a vodka bar. But South African support for the USSR extended beyond donations and exhibitions. In July 1944, Dmitri Shostakovich’s Seventh Symphony, known as the Leningrad Symphony, was performed in Johannesburg, and two months later, in Cape Town. Proceeds from both concerts were sent to the Soviet Union.

In the post-war years, collaboration in the field of education became a cornerstone of Soviet policy in Africa. In 1960, the Peoples’ Friendship University was established in Moscow. It was later named after Patrice Lumumba, the first prime minister of independent Congo. However, African students studied not only in Moscow, but also in cities throughout the USSR, such as Leningrad, Kiev, Kharkov, Odessa, Minsk, Voronezh, and many others. For many of these students, the trip to the Soviet Union was the first journey outside their home country and an opportunity to experience an entirely different culture and lifestyle. Everything was new – from Russian winters and dorm living to Soviet cafeterias and the challenge of learning a new language.

In January 1962, nine young activists from the African National Congress movement gathered in a house in Johannesburg. To their surprise, Nelson Mandela, who had gone underground, showed up at the meeting. The activists planned to go abroad for military training, but Mandela explained that the movement had other priorities. He tasked them with traveling to the Soviet Union to learn how free education, healthcare, affordable housing, and public transportation were organized, hoping this knowledge would aid in building a future democratic South Africa.

To cross the border discreetly, the young activists posed as a table tennis team headed to a tournament. After an extensive journey across Africa and Europe, they finally reached the USSR. Among them was Sindiso Mfenyana, who traveled to study economic planning in Kiev. But for him, the trip proved life-changing in more ways than one; while traveling by boat to a Black Sea resort, he met Rita, a Soviet woman studying aerospace engineering. They began corresponding, and in June 1963, they were married in Kiev. Later, they welcomed a son, whom they named Nikita. Years later, Mfenyana became full secretary to parliament in democratic South Africa.

The story of Mfenyana isn’t unique. In 1981, around 34,000 African students were enrolled in Soviet universities. In total, about 90,000 Africans received higher education in the USSR from 1960 to 1990. Among these graduates were future ministers, diplomats, and presidents. Angolan President Joao Lourenco studied at the Lenin Military-Political Academy, Ghanaian President John Mahama attended the Institute of Social Sciences under the Central Committee of the Communist Party, and Namibian President Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah graduated from the Higher Komsomol School. This means that some of the officials present today during negotiations with Russian officials had spent their youth in Moscow, studying at Soviet universities, and being immersed in Russian culture.

After the collapse of the Soviet Union, Russia-Africa relations entered a period of minimal engagement. However, certain connections persisted through personal relationships, joint projects, and educational ties. In the 2000s, Russian companies began re-establishing a presence in Africa, including in countries where Soviet geologists, engineers, and educators had worked decades earlier. The first Russia-Africa summit took place in Sochi in 2019, followed by a second summit in St. Petersburg in 2023.

Today, Russia-Africa relations encompass more than just official meetings and economic initiatives. Approximately 35,000 African students are enrolled in Russian universities, and it’s increasingly common to see Africans working in the fields of trade, services, and delivery in various Russian cities. Meanwhile, the number of Russians traveling to African nations is gradually rising. These daily interactions lay the groundwork for new friendships, familial bonds, and both professional and personal relationships. In the future, these stories may be as captivating to read as the adventures of Russian travelers in Ethiopia or the experiences of African students in the Soviet Union.

By Pavel Lvov, independent expert on Africa and African economies

North Korean workers are some of the biggest victims of labor abuses in Russia, to say nothing of the isolated country’s shadowy involvement in the Ukraine war.

While the report makes no mention of those issues, the recollected encounters show how Imai, 34, didn’t shy away from the kinds of politically sensitive topics that have landed other foreign correspondents in trouble with the Russian authorities.

At some point, that reporting may have angered the wrong people.

Two weeks ago, a group of masked men broke into Imai’s hotel room in Sakhalin’s largest city, Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk, and wrestled him to the floor. They were Federal Security Service (FSB) agents, arresting Imai on espionage charges likely related to his journalistic work. He is now awaiting trial in Moscow’s notorious Lefortovo Prison, where he faces up to 20 years in prison.

In Japan, his case is enveloped in secrecy. Few who knew Imai — including his employer, the Hokkaido Shimbun newspaper — are willing to talk publicly about his background, the kind of journalist he is and what motivated him to report on the ground in a contentious region, despite the mounting risks for foreign correspondents.

But a review of his limited online presence and published articles reveals a picture of someone who was deeply fascinated by Russian culture, had family connections to the Far East and was committed to explaining the complexities of his home country’s relations with Russia.

The Hokkaido Shimbun declined to comment for this story, saying that it was still “gathering information” about the criminal charges against its journalist. In an open letter signed by Editor-in-Chief Takeshi Yamamoto, the newspaper said it was “responding with our highest priority on ensuring our reporter’s safety.”

Foreign correspondents are often criticized for parachuting into unfamiliar territory, firing off a quick report and leaving without ever developing a deeper understanding of their subject. Imai wasn’t that kind of journalist.

An acquaintance of his told the exiled news outlet IStories that Imai first came to Russia in 2014 for Russian language studies at St. Petersburg State University. According to the report, he also took an interest in Russian politics and literature.

It doesn’t appear that interest waned, even after Imai reportedly returned to Japan to continue his studies.

In recent years, Imai channeled his curiosities through journalism. He started reporting for the Hokkaido Shimbun, a daily newspaper located in the capital of Japan’s immense northern island of the same name. Owing to its proximity to both the Russian mainland and the disputed Kuril Islands, the newspaper publishes a robust section focused on Russian affairs, with a particular emphasis on bilateral relations.

The Far East is a geopolitically complicated region. Both Russia and Japan claimed and contested large areas of territory there during the 19th and 20th centuries before World War II largely sealed the matter, handing the Soviets control over most of it.

The enduring disagreement over ownership of the four southernmost Kuril Islands, which are controlled by Russia, has prevented the two countries from formally signing a peace treaty to end World War II.

Imai’s articles interrogated the conflict’s legacy and Japan’s absence from the islands that were once its territory, reporting on everything from the construction of a controversial war monument to the recent demolition of a disused Japanese government-funded medical clinic.

He also covered developments in politics and defense, from Russia’s recent parliamentary elections and relations with North Korea to the rise in military drone production on Sakhalin.

It’s unclear if any of these stories were the reason for Imai’s arrest, but it’s easy to see how officials might have felt his coverage was prodding at delicate topics, even if it wasn’t particularly intrusive.

Olivia Chow’s mayoral win was not a vote for Toronto’s status quo

Toronto mayor-elect Olivia Chow gives her victory speech on stage at her camp's election night headquarters in Toronto on Monday.Nathan Denette/The Canadian Press

A wind of change is blowing in Canadian politics.

In British Columbia, the mayor of Vancouver and then the premier of the province were toppled in spectacular fashion, the first beaten by a Green Party upstart, the second by the leader of a party, the Conservatives, that had been in complete disarray. In Quebec, the separatist Parti Québécois returned to power after a dozen years in the wilderness, reducing the ruling Coalition Avenir Québec to precisely zero seats in the National Assembly.

Olivia Chow managed to buck the trend on Monday evening, beating back a serious challenge from an energetic city councillor, Brad Bradford, and winning a second term as mayor of Toronto. It was quite an achievement for the former city councillor and NDP MP, who turns 70 next year. She arrived in Canada from Hong Kong with her parents at the age of 13, moving into a sprawling public housing complex. She won election to the school board at just 28, beginning a political career that has spanned four decades.

Olivia Chow re-elected mayor of Toronto, staving off challenger Brad Bradford

That career seemed to be over in 2015, after she lost a bid for mayor, then a by-election that would have given her back a seat in the House of Commons. But when former mayor John Tory resigned amid a personal scandal in 2023, Ms. Chow took another stab and won the mayor’s chair in a special by-election. If all goes well, she will hold it until 2030.

In this moment of triumph, she is bound to see a vindication of the record she has established at city hall and the program she had laid out for the future. She is entitled to that view.

Unlike most government leaders, mayors are elected directly. With parties prohibited at the city level, voters mark their ballots for the candidate alone. Armed with that legitimacy – along with the “strong mayor” powers the provincial government has granted to cities – Ms. Chow has a solid mandate and considerable power.

In her first term, she managed to create a school snack program, open all libraries seven days a week and strike a cost-sharing deal with Premier Doug Ford worth billions of dollars to the city. In this term, she plans to fight for tenants’ rights, create more affordable rental housing, keep transit fares frozen and invest in youth programs.

But it would be a mistake to interpret Monday’s vote as a rubber stamp for the status quo. Mr. Bradford gave Ms. Chow a real run for her money. He told voters that Toronto was crying out for change, and that message clearly struck a chord, bringing him close to even with a well-known sitting mayor in late-campaign opinion polls.

Voters will not be content with a steady-as-she-goes approach to governing Canada’s leading metropolis. Traffic congestion is a genuine problem, frustrating to locals and mind-boggling for visitors. It costs Torontonians countless hours of wasted time and the city many billions of dollars in lost productivity.

Though Toronto is safe by North American standards, with crime rates down in several categories, the plague of mental illness, drug addiction and homelessness that afflicts so many cities is painfully visible. Ms. Chow managed to get most of the tent encampments out of municipal parks, but city dwellers continue to witness scenes of disorder and despair on sidewalks and in transit stations.

The general state of Toronto’s parks, roads and many of its transit stations is not what it should be. Ms. Chow has put lots of resources into making sure Toronto’s most disadvantaged residents get the help they deserve, but little into making sure that the city’s economic engine keeps humming.

Rather than coast on her victory, Ms. Chow would be well advised to reach out to Toronto’s business community about how to feed the goose that lays the golden eggs, and focus more on issues of simple urban maintenance such as cleanliness and conduct in public spaces.

Toronto is a thriving city, more stuck than broken, as Mr. Bradford put it. Most of its problems, like high housing costs and crowded roads, are problems of success. But looking around at other cities of its size, it is hard to avoid the feeling that it is falling behind. Its transit network is nowhere near as good as you would find in many major European or East Asian cities. Its cultural assets are below par.

While she is hard working, decent and sincere, Ms. Chow sometimes seems to lack the ambition that the leader of a dynamic city like Toronto needs.

The wind of change is blowing in Toronto, too. Ms. Chow would be unwise to ignore it.

“This will be a major step towards creating a new multilateral institution with a broad mandate to strengthen the defence, security and resilience capabilities of its members,” Mr. Champagne said in a written statement.

The Globe and Mail first reported in April that Canada had been chosen as the host country for the bank, meaning that its headquarters will be in this country. The European hub will act as another point of contact for member countries.

Once signed, the Articles of Agreement, or charter, for the Defence, Security and Resilience Bank (DSRB) will cement its mission, objective and guidelines. This is a key step toward the bank achieving its intended triple-A credit rating, as well as providing further context to other countries that may be interested in joining the bank.

Britain won’t join Canada’s defence bank, plans to pursue separate procurement entity

The nine countries currently committed to the bank are Canada, Albania, Belgium, Greece, Latvia, Turkey, Luxembourg, Romania and Ukraine. When the founding countries were initially announced in July, critics questioned the size of the countries and their balance sheets and credit ratings. Many were disappointed to see no other G7 countries in the mix, alongside Canada.

The bank has yet to attract further founding countries in its quest to provide low-cost, long-term financing to the defence industry and small-to-medium-sized businesses in particular.

By comparison, another multinational institution, the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, part of the World Bank Group, began with 21 countries signing its Articles of Agreement. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank began with 50 founding members.

Another factor in the size of the DSRB’s founding group and delay in progress, as the bank moves toward being operational by next year, has been the negotiations between Canada and Britain about the bank’s future.

Called the Multilateral Defence Mechanism, it was initially pitched as a new program to “aggregate demand, drive joint procurement, accelerate defence investment, and increase the availability of critical capabilities such as munitions,” according to a joint statement by Britain, Finland and the Netherlands in March. The two efforts are somewhat similar, which left the door open to each country to join the other’s project, or to collaborate on a potential merger.

While the DSRB is heavily focused on financing defence projects through a shared institution, which could be conducive to inciting joint procurement, the British mechanism is more explicitly focused on co-ordinating procurement. It is not a bank, like the DSRB.

Britain would have been the first G7 member, aside from Canada, to sign up for the DSRB. After a change in government there in late July, talks between the two governments seemed to be taking a turn for the better in terms of getting on board with their respective initiatives.

However, after negotiations stretched from weeks into months over the summer, Britain ultimately made Canada an offer it couldn’t accept. This included moving the headquarters from Canada to Europe, as well as focusing the merged entity more on procurement than financing.

Instead, the two countries have agreed to move forward individually to focus on their separate initiatives to improve defence financing and procurement.

“The bank will complement existing national and multilateral initiatives by helping solve a problem that is currently unaddressed elsewhere,” Mr. Champagne said.

“Its financing tools will be available to eligible members on equitable terms, enabling countries of different sizes and economic capacities to strengthen their capabilities and contribute to our shared security.”

As Canada inches closer to the signing of the DSRB charter in November, little has been said by the British Treasury about formal treaty negotiations for its own mechanism, which it said in July that it was aiming to “move quickly” toward.

Canada backs UN push to treat environmental crime as serious organized crime

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Negotiations on the new protocol under the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime are expected to take years.Jeenah Moon/Reuters

Canada has voted to begin negotiations on a UN protocol that would treat environmental crime, including wildlife trafficking, as a serious organized crime.

It was one of 83 countries to vote in favour of the resolution on Friday, which establishes a committee to draft the new protocol under the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime. The protocol would also target timber, minerals, precious metals and waste, and illegal mining.

“Canada recognizes that crimes that affect the environment represent an increasingly concerning form of transnational organized crime,” said Rachael Dolan, a spokesperson for Global Affairs Canada, in a statement.

In many countries, including Canada, much environmental crime falls below the “serious crime” bar of the UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, which applies to offences punishable by four years or more in prison. That limits how readily the convention can be used for extradition, mutual legal assistance and joint investigations, the tools countries rely on to pursue criminal networks across borders.

In Canada, wildlife trafficking clears the bar, with a maximum of five years. Four of the country’s six main environmental protection laws, including the Fisheries Act and the Canadian Environmental Protection Act, do not, according to a UN legislative review published last year.

Canada’s baby eel fishery shows what is at stake. As The Globe reported in March, elvers fetch about $2,800 a kilogram, which has fuelled poaching and illegal trade. Last year, cracking down on illegal fishing, federal officers checked shipments at four major airports, seized 225 kilograms and made more than 100 arrests. But those arrests fell under the Fisheries Act, whose penalties are below the convention’s four-year threshold.

The Decibel podcast: Why Canada struggles to stop the illegal wildlife trade

The resolution, led by Brazil, passed in Vienna at the convention’s 13th Conference of the Parties. It cleared the required two-thirds majority, with 87 per cent voting in favour. Twelve countries, including the United States, voted against the resolution and 27 abstained.

It is the first time in 25 years that parties to the convention have agreed to develop a new protocol. Since the convention was created in 2000, it has had only three protocols, on human trafficking, migrant smuggling and firearms, the last adopted in May, 2001.

Alejandra Silva, a spokesperson for the UN Office on Drugs and Crime, said in a statement that the vote signals countries’ continued support for the convention but also “the need for the convention to be supplemented by specific provisions dealing with these forms of crimes that affect the environment, which are one of the most lucrative forms of transnational organized crime.”

Sheldon Jordan, a former director-general of wildlife enforcement at Environment Canada and a former Interpol official who co-founded the Canadian Environmental Crime Research Network, said the protocol would give enforcement agencies and prosecutors new tools to collaborate across borders.

“Criminals who violate environmental and natural resource laws will find it harder to hide behind borders,” he said, speaking from Vienna.

A Globe and Mail and Pulitzer Center investigation in June found that penalties for wildlife trafficking in Canada rarely match the crime. Most of the cases Environment and Climate Change Canada has publicly reported since 2011 ended in fines under $50,000, and fewer than one in eight included any custody. By comparison, a first-time courier importing fentanyl can expect eight to 18 years.

Mr. Jordan has described Canada’s environmental enforcement framework as more formidable on paper than in practice. “It’s just a scarecrow in many cases,” he said.

Ms. Dolan said Canada supported the resolution “in recognition of the serious and growing threat posed by environmental crime,” and called for “judicious consideration” of the resources needed to set up a negotiating committee.

“We are committed to working with others for a cost-effective, pragmatic and inclusive process, with the participation of relevant stakeholders including civil society and Indigenous peoples, recognizing they can be particularly affected by these crimes,” said Ms. Dolan.

U.S. amendments that would have weakened and delayed the talks were all voted down. They would have allowed a non-binding instrument instead of a protocol, tied the talks to available funding and postponed the work until the Convention’s lengthy implementation review is complete.

Talks cannot begin until the UN General Assembly approves funding for the negotiating committee from the UN’s regular budget, a process that runs through several budget committees, Ms. Silva said.

Negotiations on the protocol’s scope and terms are expected to take years. Canada’s federal wildlife penalties have not been updated since 2009.

The shifting front line in the U.S-Canada trade war will lessen the economic pain in border states while still targeting Canadian manufacturing capacity, experts say.

The lifting of 50-per-cent tariffs on 22 categories of goods by the United States and the addition of 100 new categories will have a roughly equivalent impact in terms of dollars – about $2.5-billion in goods were exempted with about $2.6-billion newly targeted – but will also shift some of the focus away from products U.S. businesses need for their own operations.

Senator Susan Collins of Maine has taken credit for lifting the tariffs on some packaging products, which she said had impacted Maine businesses such as apple and blueberry growers.

Alongside its tariff adjustments, the Trump administration has also said that it’s nearing a trade deal with Canada. Kevin Hassett, the top economic adviser in the White House, told Fox News on Sunday that negotiations are “gathering steam” and that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has been speaking with Canadian officials “all week.”

According to Wolfgang Alschner, a law professor with a specialty in business and trade at the University of Ottawa, there are lots of examples of integrated cross-border supply chains where tariffs hurt U.S. businesses and consumers as much or more than they strike at Canada.

“With tighter North American integration, it made total sense for states like Maine to trade with Quebec for supplies for local industries. Now of course with the border being a harder border you really have to re-evaluate those relationships,” Mr. Alschner said.

He said some of the new tariffs are on finished retail products such as shampoo, which won’t have a strong localized impact in any particular geographic market – such as a border state with a tense and close Senate election battle raging.

“I think that makes perfect sense from a defensive point of view. You ideally want to spread out the pain,” Mr. Alschner said.

According to William Pellerin, partner with law firm McMillan LLP and co-leader of its international trade group, even before the new tariffs on Canadian hardwood, his group of eight flooring manufacturing clients based mainly in Quebec and Ontario were struggling to compete domestically against imports.

“[China and Vietnam] totally flooded the market at prices that are ludicrously low. The Canadian producers have been able to survive by selling products to the U.S.,” Mr. Pellerin said.

Many of the companies are benefiting from federal work-share programs to keep the lights on. “A lot of the companies had layoffs, a number are on work-share, with workers on maybe three days a week,” he said.

But in the longer term, Mr. Pellerin said restricting imports of overseas hardwood may be the only path forward.
