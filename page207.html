Why Democratic Leaders Keep Waging War on the Young

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The party’s silver-haired establishment is caught up in an existential culture war.

Because reactionaries are always with us, every age has its unique assortment of moral panics, both serious and benign. As a late Gen Xer, I faced plenty of eager efforts to pathologize my unforgivable youth, including the failed War on Drugs, inquisitions into the scourge of video games and MTV, which were plainly rotting the brains of children, and a crusade to reverse the alleged epidemic of teen pregnancy. And because I grew up Southern Baptist in Alabama, I also contended with menacing subgenres of these panics. Rooted in religious strictures against secularism, they rhymed with what was happening in the culture at large if you were seeing it narrowly though a pinhole. At the time and place I grew up, I witnessed how culture warriors viewed Salman Rushdie’s novel The Satanic Verses (which they’d obviously never read) as a handbook of occult practices, repudiated the Catholic artist Andres Serrano’s Piss Christ as blasphemous, and reprised rumors from the 1960s suggesting that if you played certain rock songs backwards, demons would talk to you—only this time via cassette tape instead of vinyl.

This vivid array of scares and campfire stories had one key through line: an ostensible concern with the horrors that an amoral and predatory culture were wreaking on the youth of America. There was a far-from-complicated animus at the heart of these complaints, particularly when our Boomer elders were wielding them: A generation that had been thrust onto the center stage of mid-century consumer capitalism was now seething with resentment that a successor demographic was storming the leading edge of culture via art, changing social norms, and especially politics, which, as the right-wing Gen Xer Andrew Breitbart famously argued, is always downstream from culture.

It’s hard to avoid viewing the current generational fractures within the Democratic Party through this lens. Where older Democrats differ from much younger ones, it’s generally along the lines of attitudes toward sexuality and gender and trust in institutions and authority figures (especially the police, the military, and religious and business leaders). These positions on culture and character in public life also readily translate into differing views on the role of government in society and the role of the United States in global affairs.

Nowhere are these fractures more apparent than in the growing, well-chronicled divide between the centrist and progressive wings of the party, the latter of which skews younger. So we’re not quibbling about definitions here, I’m going to use the Pew Research Center’s terminology for the two poles of the liberal coalition: The “leftward progressives” are the farthest left (and the youngest), while the members of the “order and opportunity left” are the farthest right.

Leftward progressives support trans rights and think billionaires are bad for society. They care about US involvement in global affairs but believe we mostly tend to make things worse. The members of the order-and-opportunity left are concerned about safety and are “moderate” on issues of sexuality and gender. This is a nice way of saying they’re happy with some immigration restrictions, generally trust law enforcement, think gender is mostly determined by sex assigned at birth, and tend to whine if anyone asks about their pronouns.

Leftward progressives overwhelmingly vote Democratic (97 percent went for Kamala Harris in 2024) but express some dissatisfaction with the party. In a survey published in 2026, Pew found a clear correlation between higher age and lower progressive sentiment within the party, with leftward progressives accounting for 26 percent of Democrats and Democratic leaners under 30 and 15 percent in the 30-to-49 cohort. From there, the drop-off is steep: Leftward progressives make up just 6 percent of the Democratic-aligned 50-and-older group. Not surprisingly, the picture for the order-and-opportunity left is a virtual photographic negative, comprising 15 percent of Democratic and Democratic-leaning voters under 30, 22 percent of those in the 30-to-49 cohort, and 31 percent of those in the 50-and-older group.

These categories are not perfect, but they still give a strong account of the generational disparities in voter attitudes. They are decent proxies for the younger, more progressive left and the older, more centrist liberals. The issues these two groups disagree on constitute the fault line that runs through the Democratic Party right now, presenting real challenges for a party that’s long been assailed as a stodgy gerontocracy—and, tellingly, the ascribed responsibility for this growing schism tends to increase as one moves down the life-cycle chart. According to much of the discourse coming from Democratic officialdom, the problem—as always—is kids these days.

The leftward progressives align closely in spirit and demography with the Democratic Socialists of America. Despite its relatively small national membership (in the low six figures), the DSA is a ready stand-in for the wide-ranging establishment lamentations about leftist overreach. The DSA’s status as the de facto brand for a political youth movement is not incidental to the critique; it’s a load-bearing wall.

And the DSA has gotten younger over the past decade. In 2013, the average age of a DSA member was 67. After Donald Trump was elected in 2016, it was 33—a trend that, like the overall growth of the organization, largely stems from the insurgent presidential candidacies of the outlying progressive octogenarian Bernie Sanders. Thirty-three isn’t that young, but given the median age of a member of Congress (57.5 in the House and 64.7 in the Senate as of January 2025), DSA members are practically tweens.

The fact that younger members of the DSA—the people most responsible for having increased the national membership so quickly, from 5,000 just before Trump’s first election to more than 120,000 today—are playing a more prominent role, as well as leadership fights within the organization, have led to some generational fractures even in the DSA itself, particularly over the issue of Israel’s genocide in Gaza. Maurice Isserman, who had been a member of the DSA for over 40 years, wrote in The Nation in 2023 about quitting the organization, claiming that it had become too harsh in its broadsides against Israel and wasn’t criticizing Hamas harshly enough. At its core, this is a rehash of the old antisemitism argument: that a particular concern for Palestinian lives is effectively a pointed attack on Jewish ones. Isserman cited “the singularly important role of Palestine in DSA’s rather short list of international concerns, completely overshadowing the attention paid, for example, to Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, or to the rights of other oppressed peoples, like the Kurdish Muslims, or China’s Uyghur Muslims.”

This has become a common refrain among older Democrats: that the situation in Gaza is of interest to young people only because it allows them to criticize Israel and, by extension, Jews. (This argument relies on a disingenuous and dangerous conflation of Israeli nationalism and Judaism that’s been weaponized by the right, but that is a sermon for another time.) It’s telling that Isserman elides the very obvious reason why younger progressives might be more vocal about the Gaza genocide: The United States has provided more economic and military aid to Israel than any other country since the founding of the Israeli state—an outlay of at least $21.7 billion since October 7 alone. We (and here “we” includes DSA members, who pay taxes just like the rest of the Democratic coalition) are not providing billions in aid to Russia, China, or the persecutors of Kurdish Muslims in Syria or Iran.

This posture, in other words, isn’t a consequence of undue hostility toward Israel so much as it’s a reflection of moral injury: the feeling that something must be done, but that structural forces block effective moral action. Older generations remember a very different set of attitudes toward Israel, and many assume that the relationship is or should remain the same as it has always been. Younger generations are more likely to view the US–Israeli relationship through the lens of Netanyahu’s regime and its attendant barbarisms, which they have more direct evidence of thanks to social media and a 24/7 news cycle. Of course they feel more strongly about it than conflicts in which the United States is either ostensibly on the right side (as in Russia’s war with Ukraine) or has far less involvement.

But that doesn’t stop high-profile centrists from insisting that anyone below a certain age who is critical of Israel’s actions in Gaza is antisemitic, especially if they happen to be a brown person. Case in point: The Atlantic’s Jonathan Chait, who wrote a column about Michigan Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed’s relationship with Hasan Piker, whom Chait deems antisemitic for noting that it’s dangerous to conflate Israel and Judaism—a point echoed by many other Jewish people in the political center.

This brings us to another divide between the older generation and the younger: technology. If you don’t know who Hasan Piker is, he’s a political streamer on social media whose views are progressive. He is not the left’s Joe Rogan, because Rogan has said many more offensive things and no one is telling Republicans or California Governor Gavin Newsom to disassociate themselves from him. Nor is Piker formally connected to the Democratic Party apparatus. But he is popular with the youth. He has 3.1 million followers on Twitch and 1.9 million on YouTube. This level of influence is largely why centrist Democrats have determined that he is not just a problem but a “crisis,” in Chait’s alarmist formulation.

Determined to outdo Chait, the Democratic strategist James Carville has threatened to leave the Democratic Party entirely if Piker (who, again, is not a candidate, an operative, a consultant, or an officer of the DNC) becomes more of a “force.” “I am not going to be in the same party with Hasan Piker,” Carville proclaimed during one performance in his endless regress of kids-these-days cable hits.

Such tantrum-throwing may play well on television, but it makes for terrible political judgment. The ’90s-bred cult of studious difference-trimming and capitulation to the right that Carville helped pioneer continues to reinforce the Democrats’ image as a party terminally dedicated to preserving the status quo. In one recent, all-too-typical case, Representative Hakeem Jeffries, who will likely be the next House speaker if the Democrats do well in the midterms, faced a massive backlash after he met with Jared Kushner to discuss ways the Democrats could work with the Trump administration. Jeffries is on the younger end of the establishment (he’s 56) but is politically synched with his mentor Nancy Pelosi and Senate minority leader Chuck Schumer.

Our seniority-heavy caste of democratic leaders have been slower to acknowledge that we are in extraordinary times. In 2019, I wrote about Pelosi’s reluctance to impeach Trump (she said he was self-impeaching) and her simultaneous bigfooting of the newly elected leftist congresswomen who made up “the Squad.” Pelosi came around on impeaching Trump, but she went on to block Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez from the senior position on the House Oversight Committee in favor of Gerry Connolly, a 74-year-old who died of cancer shortly after his appointment. Pelosi’s generation of leaders is quick to protect the management cohort of the party—which is now a full-on gerontocracy—from any encroachment by younger elected officials, even at the expense of the party’s future.

And they are incapable of acting as an opposition party; they don’t have the stomach for it. Their political instincts were formed during an era when elections were not determined, as they are now, almost entirely by negative partisanship. During the ’90s heyday of most of the figures who now make up the party’s leadership, engaging in civility theater was a sign of political maturity. Now voters view it mostly as a sign of weakness.

The former New York City comptroller and current Democratic nominee for Congress Brad Lander, a DSA-aligned progressive who jointly campaigned with Zohran Mamdani for mayor, has described the divide among Democrats as “folders” versus “fighters.” This line breaks down generationally, too. In this political environment, writing “very strong letters,” taking closed-door meetings with Trumpists, adopting Republican talking points about gender expression in the guise of “just asking questions,” and dismissing the concerns of women and people of color as “identity politics” looks like folding. Pushing for big systemic reforms that will trigger formidable political challenges (universal healthcare, tax reform that puts more of the burden on the rich and less on the poor, ambitious housing goals) looks like fighting. Standing up for trans people, criticizing the United States’ role in providing weapons to Israel, and biting back at GOP insults looks like fighting.

Younger Democrats are simply better equipped to act like an opposition party—in part because they don’t have the experience of being in power, either as a party or as an age cohort. They are less willing to wait patiently until the next election—or the next one or the next one—for Democrats to gain new seats or get around to exercising whatever power they already have.

I’m 49 years old and taught at New York University for six years and the School of Visual Arts for six years before that. I’ve worked on Democratic campaigns of all stripes, some with DSA candidates (including one with a younger Zohran Mamdani) and some with older, more moderate candidates in red states. I have been around many young Democrats; I’ve taught them, hired them, and also witnessed directly the pushback they get from Boomers and Gen Xers who resent their efforts to exert real political power instead of just sitting on the sidelines until they’re old and rich enough to own a house and have kids.

Yet the relevant divides remain as stubbornly cultural as they are material. It’s generally the case that older generations oppose student protests on the apparent assumption that students should earn their right to dissent, as if the First Amendment is something you graduate into.

When I defend younger activists and candidates, I always prepare for a barrage of facts about the alleged erratic levels of political engagement among this cohort that are technically true but often willfully misinterpreted. The first of these cherished data points is the one showing that younger people don’t vote as reliably as older people. But the people who cite this statistic assume it’s because the youths are too self-involved, or lazy, or possibly hungover from last night’s party. They almost never acknowledge that young people’s voter participation is heavily dictated by systems that fundamentally work against them.

Voting is a habit, and it’s instilled over many years as people get used to the cadence of elections—particularly in traditionally low-turnout contests such as primaries and downballot races. In addition, campaigns can’t afford to target everyone, so they often seek to identify likely supporters among those who have consistently voted over a period of years, which obviously leaves out younger people. Plus, the political participation of college students is actively suppressed by the right, which assumes that they’re more liberal than the general population. And it’s astonishingly easy to impose voter-suppression measures at universities and colleges where students are living away from home.

As a simple matter of self-preservation, elected officials (many of whom are much, much older if we’re talking about Congress) are less responsive to the needs of people who don’t have children or own homes. Younger voters are much less likely to fall within this charmed circle of the electorate than their grandparents—in part because of the political and economic windfalls that the older generation received.

Despite all of this, young people have been going to the polls in greater numbers over the past decade. As of this writing, the 35-year-old mayor of New York City is the seventh-most-popular Democrat in the country, and AOC is the fourth. Both are dues-paying members of the DSA.

If Democrats are in crisis, it’s not the fault of the young. They should not be held collectively responsible for a badly worded comment from a popular influencer who is not running for office. By contrast, Boomer Democrats have done nothing to hold their own cohort responsible for its ties to an ex-president and an ex–treasury secretary who palled around with Jeffrey Epstein. (James Carville is fine with Bill Clinton remaining a power broker in the Democratic Party, but a streamer he’d never heard of before last week is a bridge too far.) At 18, young people are deemed old enough by the US government to die for their country, sign up for hundreds of thousands of dollars in student debt, and be forced to bear children if they become unexpectedly pregnant. Many of them have to pay the same effective tax rates as older Americans who are far richer. They deserve political participation—and yet are continuously vouchsafed less than their fair share of it.

Leftward progressivism, as Pew calls it, is not just a youth movement (plenty of us on the left are old!). But it is a youth movement culturally, even as it’s built on the work of earlier generations. Because of that, older elements in the Democratic coalition find it threatening; it marshals a political movement around changing social norms, concerns motivated by a sense of moral injury during a formative period of life, differing expectations about what government can and should do, and a justifiably righteous conviction that it’s better to fight and lose on hard issues than not to fight at all. Which makes it very much like virtually every youth movement that came before it.

The idea that progressive young people are sabotaging the party from within with pronouns, keffiyehs, and wild, irrational ideas about what the government should be able to do for people that governments in other developed countries routinely manage to do is just another moral panic. I expect that the younger members of the party who emerge as leaders will prove their elders wrong. You can indeed see this process unfolding right now in Mamdanistan, where we have less violent crime, faster pothole repairs, a handful of city-owned grocery stores slated to open, and no open revolts.

Elizabeth Spiers is a digital media strategist and writer living in Brooklyn. She is the former editor in chief of The New York Observer.

A few months into President Donald Trump’s second term, political appointees at the Department of Homeland Security made an unusual request of its agency focused on cybersecurity: Secretly acquire a voting machine and hack it.

The appointees wanted the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency to procure the machine, examine it and report back the findings so they could be given to the White House, according to two former administration officials with knowledge of the request.

The proposition was shocking to CISA’s career staffers, according to one of the former officials. CISA typically worked to thwart hackers. Moreover, the agency had been directed not to alert the voting machine company if it uncovered flaws, contrary to its standard practices. The request made the CISA employees fear they were being asked to take part in political interference.

“My thought was they could keep it in their back pocket to invalidate any [election] results they don’t like,” one of the former officials said. “This goes against everything CISA represents.”

The CISA employees repeatedly told the political appointees that their request was impossible, in part because the agents capable of pulling off such an operation had just been laid off by Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency initiative. The appointees eventually stopped pressing CISA.

Though not acted on, the request shows how the Trump administration has tried to use the agency for its own ends while draining it of resources for one of its critical missions: protecting the nation’s voting systems.

In Trump’s second term, his administration has laid off or pushed out more than 1,000 CISA employees, shrinking the agency by about one-third. The president’s 2027 budget proposal would reduce funding for the agency by another $700 million, which could necessitate 900 additional layoffs.

In response, Democratic lawmakers have called for an investigation into CISA’s ability to do its job and demanded answers as to why federal funds are not being used to support election security as directed by Congress.

Since Trump signed the CISA Act in 2018, the agency has worked with state authorities and voting machine vendors to help protect election infrastructure from cyberattacks. During Trump’s second term, though, CISA has abandoned much of this work. Several state election authorities said the agency provided them no assistance — and no explanation for its absence — during the most critical planning stages for the upcoming midterms.

“There is a difference between deprioritizing and being nonexistent,” said Ryan Macias, an election technology and cybersecurity expert who has worked as a consultant for CISA. In 2025 and through mid-September 2026 — when the bulk of preparations for the election typically would have been completed — “they were nonexistent. Literally nonexistent.”

While Trump has repeatedly warned that America’s elections are vulnerable to hacking, in some cases repeating disproven claims, experts said his administration is undermining the federal government’s role in helping secure them.

Though CISA re-emerged in September with a last-minute security plan for the election, some state officials told ProPublica they fear that what is left of the agency has been compromised by a conspiracy-obsessed presidential administration. In recent months, in reviving debunked theories about the 2020 election, the Trump administration has declassified troves of records, including sensitive material that state officials provided to CISA. Some state authorities said they no longer trusted the administration enough to share information with CISA, a key to the agency’s success in past elections.

“They’ve violated the entire premise of that information-sharing relationship,” said one former high-ranking federal cybersecurity official who now advises state governments on national security. “There’s no fucking way they’re ever going to give any information to CISA. And it’s unclear if they ever will in the future.”

A spokesperson for the White House declined an interview request but defended Trump’s handling of election security in an emailed statement. “President Trump has done more than any other President to ensure we have free and fair elections,” said Lauren Bis.

Bis referred specific questions about CISA’s handling of elections to DHS. DHS and CISA did not respond to interview requests or to a detailed list of questions.

“There’s no fucking way [state governments are] ever going to give any information to CISA. And it’s unclear if they ever will in the future.”

Current and former CISA employees and state election officials describe an agency that has become significantly less equipped to help states fight legitimate cyber threats. State leaders say they have used their own resources to shore up gaps left by CISA, such as paying for sensor technology that can detect unusual activity in election networks. But some services, like intel on foreign actors, can’t be replicated. Some people interviewed said they were worried that Trump and his allies continue to ratchet up rhetoric about voting security, particularly about threats to voting machines. Officials in one large voting jurisdiction said they have met privately with machine vendors to come up with a contingency plan if the federal government seizes their machines or declares them unusable.

“I don’t pay attention to what the president says. I pay attention to what his administration does,” Arizona Secretary of State Adrian Fontes said. “And what they’ve done is basically cut us off and left us on our own.”

Defending elections from hackers wasn’t always part of DHS’s remit.

Throughout the mid-1990s and 2000s, a series of cyberattacks rocked the federal government, demonstrating the emerging threat. In 2007, hackers broke into the Pentagon and stole terabytes of sensitive data. The digital heist, by an unknown foreign adversary, was so damaging that one intelligence leader later described it as an “espionage Pearl Harbor.”

Under George W. Bush, DHS underwent a reorganization to more effectively combat terrorism and respond to disasters like Hurricane Katrina. The department dedicated resources under an office called the National Protection and Programs Directorate to help fortify advanced cyber defense systems and important infrastructure. The office’s work ranged from helping secure events like the Super Bowl to protecting chemical plants to issuing cybersecurity warnings to the private sector and the public. In 2018, Trump signed a bill making the office a stand-alone agency, allowing for more federal funding and transforming it into, essentially, the ultimate tech support desk for anything DHS designated “critical infrastructure.” The bill renamed the agency CISA.

Though Trump helped create CISA, he didn’t much appreciate the work the agency did under its old name to expose the extent of Russia’s meddling in the 2016 presidential election. Russian agents had unleashed a massive disinformation campaign, with a Kremlin-backed troll farm creating thousands of social media accounts posing as Americans and promoting Trump, according to bipartisan reports from the U.S. Senate Select Committee on Intelligence released before the 2020 election. A dozen Russian military agents were indicted for what the Justice Department described as “a sustained effort to hack into the computer networks of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee, the Democratic National Committee, and the presidential campaign of Hillary Clinton.”

Trump publicly downplayed this interference, but DHS leadership saw the Russia attack as an escalation. In January 2017, before Trump took office, DHS upgraded America’s elections to the status of critical infrastructure. This placed the polls and all voting technology used in federal elections under the watch of what would soon become CISA.

To carry out this function, CISA needed help. But states and voting machine vendors were wary about sharing data on weaknesses in voting systems, fearing it could be exploited if certain details were to become public. State officials were also reluctant to cede any role in election preparation to the federal government.

Gradually, though, CISA and state election officials negotiated a fragile trust. At its center was a commitment: The sensitive information states and vendors shared with CISA would be kept confidential.

“It took years and years of building trust, of talking to election officials, of hiring people with elections experience who could be validators for the work that CISA wanted to do, before they finally built strong working relationships,” said Derek Tisler, who specializes in election security as counsel for the nonprofit Brennan Center for Justice, a nonpartisan institute based out of New York University School of Law.

Under Director Chris Krebs, CISA hosted penetration testing in which its agents would try to hack into a state’s system to locate weaknesses that could then be patched, along with tabletop exercises simulating a series of potential catastrophes around elections. CISA also offered regular secret-level briefings to secretaries of state to share federal intelligence. And CISA paid for sensor technology that looked for potential malicious online activity on election networks, notifying officials about emerging threats.

“The staffing was there, the expertise was there, the visibility was there, the funding was there,” Macias said.

The consortium shared data as run of the mill as spambots as well as more serious threats. If Virginia caught unusual activity in its system, it could let other states know to be vigilant.

While CISA had prepared for attacks from the Chinese and Russian governments, or lone wolf hackers, few there had expected the threat to elections would come from inside the White House. But when Trump refused to concede to Joe Biden in 2020, claiming the vote had been rigged, CISA became a key component of the guardrails that ultimately led to the election results being certified.

The agency, in a statement co-signed by secretaries of state and voting machine vendors, vouched for the election’s integrity, calling it “the most secure in American history.” When Krebs publicly rebutted claims amplified by the White House as “unsubstantiated” or “technically incoherent,” Trump fired him over Twitter.

As Congress prepared to vote on certifying the election results, it was members of CISA, along with the FBI, who convinced then-Attorney General Bill Barr that the conspiracy theories embraced by the president could be explained by human error, not systemic fraud or hacking.

When Trump returned to the White House in January 2025, his administration immediately started to dismantle CISA. The administrative notices were sent out just a couple of weeks after the inauguration and included the team in charge of dispelling disinformation.

For the employees under the DOGE microscope, work became impossible, said one former senior CISA employee. “Those groups couldn’t even order a pizza.”

DOGE laid off people critical to the agency’s election work, including those who’d worked closely with states. The Trump administration cut funding for the technology that monitored web and election servers, blinding the federal government to breaches it had detected.

When it came time for states to prepare for the 2026 midterms, CISA had disappeared.

On the evening of July 16, in the Black Hills of South Dakota, dozens of election officials and their staffs gathered for a reception near Mount Rushmore. It was the third night of an annual conference for secretaries of state, drawing participants from red and blue states. They’d spent the week discussing state voter roll maintenance and voter ID — issues that at times made for disagreements.

At about 8 p.m., a wave of discomfort swept through the crowd, visible on attendees’ faces as they glanced frequently at their phones, according to people at the event. Any minute now, Trump was going to address the nation on election security. Some at the reception worried he would declare a national emergency, as his allies had urged him to do months earlier, and use it as a pretext to direct the government to seize voting machines.

Then reports on the speech began rolling in, and the mood shifted. The claims Trump was making were unsubstantiated and outlandish. Many had been examined ad nauseam, including by those at the reception, and roundly debunked. The collective anxiety that was palpable moments earlier melted away, as many saw the speech as another attempt to revive disproved theories unlikely to be taken seriously by most voters.

“Everyone just sort of guffawed and put their phones away and went to the bar,” recalled Quinn Raymond, co-founder of VoteShield, a nonprofit that works with states to help monitor voter registration data, who was at the event.

Still, if the speech made little impact, records published by the Trump administration to support the president’s claims damaged the already eroding relationship between CISA and state election officials. The material included raw information states had shared with the agency that was supposed to remain private, such as a log of communications from Election Day 2020 detailing activity being monitored in several states.

“It betrayed the trust of all partners who were willing to share information at the time,” said Geoff Hale, who worked on CISA’s elections team from the agency’s inception until last year. Now he specializes in election security at the Center for Democracy & Technology, a nonprofit that advocates for digital and civil rights.

Bis, the White House spokesperson, denied that the Trump administration broke any agreements in releasing the information. “The release of such information is exactly the sort of transparency news organizations should applaud,” she said. Bis also defended Trump’s speech, saying it had been fact-checked “by the agencies whose evidence was released and did not mischaracterize any information.”

Hale and others said the speech was one of several instances demonstrating the Trump administration’s reckless attitude toward sensitive data collected by CISA. Earlier this year, Politico reported that acting CISA Director Madhu Gottumukkala had uploaded records to ChatGPT, even though DHS had barred its employees from using the unsecure program. A CISA spokesperson told Politico that Gottumukkala had been permitted to use the technology for a “short-term and limited” basis.

Last May, an independent journalist named Brian Krebs exposed that CISA published private data, including passwords, cloud keys and other information, on its public GitHub page, in a folder labeled “Private-CISA.” A CISA spokesperson told the journalist there was no indication that sensitive data was compromised.

Some state election authorities were also alarmed to see the lengths to which the Trump administration went to legitimize the president’s fixation on the 2020 race.

In fall 2025, Missouri election officials produced a memo stating Andrew McCoy Warner, a Department of Justice official and prominent 2020 election conspiracy theorist, had called several clerks asking for access to voting machines. Tulsi Gabbard, then the director of national intelligence, had also led an operation in Puerto Rico to seize election machines. When the private cybersecurity contractor hired to examine the machines, Mojave Research Inc., failed to find evidence supporting claims about 2020, the federal government became “dissastified” with its services and stopped renewing its contract, its CEO later told reporters.

A spokesperson for the Office of the Director of National Intelligence told reporters in August that Mojave’s work ceased because it had completed its contract. Testifying before Congress in March, Gabbard said the seizing of voting machines came at the request of an assistant U.S. attorney in Puerto Rico.

“What happened in the second Trump administration is from almost day one — I mean, literally the first two months — they took all that was built up by Trump 1 and just decimated it,” said David Becker, founder of the nonpartisan Center for Election Innovation and Research, a nonprofit that works with state election authorities. “Election officials lost trust in the federal government.”

It didn’t help that, in his speech, the president mocked CISA’s defense of the 2020 election, calling the statement about it being the most secure in history a “very pat line” concocted as part of a deep-state cover-up. One former career CISA employee described being crestfallen at the Trump administration’s erosion of all they’d accomplished.

Defending elections from hackers wasn’t always part of DHS’s remit.

Throughout the mid-1990s and 2000s, a series of cyberattacks rocked the federal government, demonstrating the emerging threat. In 2007, hackers broke into the Pentagon and stole terabytes of sensitive data. The digital heist, by an unknown foreign adversary, was so damaging that one intelligence leader later described it as an “espionage Pearl Harbor.”

Under George W. Bush, DHS underwent a reorganization to more effectively combat terrorism and respond to disasters like Hurricane Katrina. The department dedicated resources under an office called the National Protection and Programs Directorate to help fortify advanced cyber defense systems and important infrastructure. The office’s work ranged from helping secure events like the Super Bowl to protecting chemical plants to issuing cybersecurity warnings to the private sector and the public. In 2018, Trump signed a bill making the office a stand-alone agency, allowing for more federal funding and transforming it into, essentially, the ultimate tech support desk for anything DHS designated “critical infrastructure.” The bill renamed the agency CISA.

Though Trump helped create CISA, he didn’t much appreciate the work the agency did under its old name to expose the extent of Russia’s meddling in the 2016 presidential election. Russian agents had unleashed a massive disinformation campaign, with a Kremlin-backed troll farm creating thousands of social media accounts posing as Americans and promoting Trump, according to bipartisan reports from the U.S. Senate Select Committee on Intelligence released before the 2020 election. A dozen Russian military agents were indicted for what the Justice Department described as “a sustained effort to hack into the computer networks of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee, the Democratic National Committee, and the presidential campaign of Hillary Clinton.”

Trump publicly downplayed this interference, but DHS leadership saw the Russia attack as an escalation. In January 2017, before Trump took office, DHS upgraded America’s elections to the status of critical infrastructure. This placed the polls and all voting technology used in federal elections under the watch of what would soon become CISA.

To carry out this function, CISA needed help. But states and voting machine vendors were wary about sharing data on weaknesses in voting systems, fearing it could be exploited if certain details were to become public. State officials were also reluctant to cede any role in election preparation to the federal government.

Gradually, though, CISA and state election officials negotiated a fragile trust. At its center was a commitment: The sensitive information states and vendors shared with CISA would be kept confidential.

“It took years and years of building trust, of talking to election officials, of hiring people with elections experience who could be validators for the work that CISA wanted to do, before they finally built strong working relationships,” said Derek Tisler, who specializes in election security as counsel for the nonprofit Brennan Center for Justice, a nonpartisan institute based out of New York University School of Law.

Under Director Chris Krebs, CISA hosted penetration testing in which its agents would try to hack into a state’s system to locate weaknesses that could then be patched, along with tabletop exercises simulating a series of potential catastrophes around elections. CISA also offered regular secret-level briefings to secretaries of state to share federal intelligence. And CISA paid for sensor technology that looked for potential malicious online activity on election networks, notifying officials about emerging threats.

“The staffing was there, the expertise was there, the visibility was there, the funding was there,” Macias said.

The consortium shared data as run of the mill as spambots as well as more serious threats. If Virginia caught unusual activity in its system, it could let other states know to be vigilant.

While CISA had prepared for attacks from the Chinese and Russian governments, or lone wolf hackers, few there had expected the threat to elections would come from inside the White House. But when Trump refused to concede to Joe Biden in 2020, claiming the vote had been rigged, CISA became a key component of the guardrails that ultimately led to the election results being certified.

The agency, in a statement co-signed by secretaries of state and voting machine vendors, vouched for the election’s integrity, calling it “the most secure in American history.” When Krebs publicly rebutted claims amplified by the White House as “unsubstantiated” or “technically incoherent,” Trump fired him over Twitter.

As Congress prepared to vote on certifying the election results, it was members of CISA, along with the FBI, who convinced then-Attorney General Bill Barr that the conspiracy theories embraced by the president could be explained by human error, not systemic fraud or hacking.

For the employees under the DOGE microscope, work became impossible, said one former senior CISA employee. “Those groups couldn’t even order a pizza.”

DOGE laid off people critical to the agency’s election work, including those who’d worked closely with states. The Trump administration cut funding for the technology that monitored web and election servers, blinding the federal government to breaches it had detected.

Le Premier ministre français, Sébastien Lecornu, tient lundi une réunion de crise du gouvernement pour évaluer les violences et les pillages qui ont secoué le pays ce week-end, constituant certaines des pires scènes de troubles publics de l'histoire française moderne.

Le ministre de l'Intérieur, Laurent Nuñez, a tenté de démissionner, a annoncé lundi matin l'Élysée (siège du président français Emmanuel Macron). Mais ce dernier lui a demandé d'examiner les mesures à prendre pour améliorer la sécurité dans les rues de France et de traduire en justice les auteurs des violences du week-end.

Samedi, plus d'un demi-million de manifestants, majoritairement pacifiques, ont protesté contre la hausse du coût de la vie et la dégradation de l'accès aux services publics de qualité, notamment à un enseignement public de qualité. Cependant, les manifestations ont dégénéré en affrontements après l'apparition de groupes d'activistes et de vandales masqués et vêtus de noir.

On estime à 1 500 le nombre de commerces pillés et détruits. Les forces de l'ordre ont fait usage de gaz lacrymogène et de canons à eau contre la foule. À plusieurs endroits, elles ont été vues frappant des personnes à coups de matraque. Le ministère de l'Intérieur a indiqué qu'environ 1 000 policiers avaient été blessés samedi, dont sept grièvement.

Le ministre de l'Intérieur Nuñez a été accusé par ses adversaires politiques, certains syndicats et sur les réseaux sociaux d'être mal préparé et insuffisamment encadré par la police en prévision des nombreuses manifestations prévues samedi.

La présidente de la Chambre des députés française, Yaël Braun-Pivet, a déclaré vouloir ouvrir une enquête afin de déterminer précisément ce qui s'est passé.

« Les choses sont sombres dans la Ville Lumière » – le surnom de Paris – était un graffiti que j’ai lu, peint en rouge, sur un mur jouxtant une rangée de magasins dévastés dans la capitale française, dimanche soir.

Comment les vandales et les émeutiers seront-ils désormais tenus responsables de leurs actes ?

Comment mieux protéger les villes françaises, surtout depuis l'annonce de nouvelles manifestations de masse dans quelques semaines ?

Espinacas: hierro y mucho más + 7 recetas rápidas

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Seguro que te suena la imagen de Popeye comiendo un puñado de espinacas para volverse fuerte como un roble. Aunque aquello fuera un dibujo animado, la realidad es que este vegetal es un auténtico superalimento cargado de nutrientes y muy ligero, lo que lo convierte en el aliado perfecto para cualquier tipo de dieta, ya sea porque buscas cuidar la línea o porque quieres darle un empujón a tu salud sin gastar mucho dinero.

A pesar de su fama, hay mucha gente que pasa de las espinacas porque las ve aburridas o no saben cómo sacarle partido en la cocina. Sin embargo, el llamado oro verde es mucho más que un simple acompañamiento; es una fuente increíble de vitaminas y minerales que, si sabemos aprovechar, pueden marcar la diferencia en nuestro bienestar diario y en nuestro rendimiento físico y mental.

Tanto si las prefieres comer crudas en una ensalada como si te gustan cocinadas, las espinacas ofrecen una cantidad ingente de ventajas. Por un lado, su combinación de hierro y vitamina C es clave para combatir la fatiga y reducir las probabilidades de sufrir anemia. Además, gracias a que son ricas en fibra, nos ayudan a que el sistema digestivo funcione como un reloj, evitando esos molestos problemas intestinales.

No nos podemos olvidar de la vitamina A, que es fundamental para lucir una piel radiante y un cabello sano. Pero hay más: contienen nitratos que favorecen la producción de óxido nítrico, el cual actúa como un vasodilatador que protege el corazón. Asimismo, el aporte de calcio, magnesio y vitamina K es esencial para que nuestros huesos se mantengan fuertes y se reparen correctamente.

Aquí es donde entra la parte técnica. Aunque es cierto que las espinacas tienen hierro (aproximadamente 2,7 mg por cada 100 g), no todo es tan sencillo. Este vegetal contiene oxalatos, unos compuestos que interfieren en la absorción de ciertos minerales. Esto significa que el hierro de las espinacas es de tipo no hemo, el cual el cuerpo absorbe con más dificultad que el de la carne.

Ojo con esto: si acompañas un filete de ternera con una ensalada de espinacas crudas, los oxalatos podrían bloquear la absorción del hierro tanto de la verdura como de la carne. Por ello, los nutricionistas recomiendan moderar el consumo en crudo si tienes anemia. Un truco muy útil es desechar el agua de cocción, ya que gran parte de los oxalatos se quedan ahí, haciendo que el alimento sea más seguro y aprovechable.

No todo el mundo puede comer espinacas a manos llenas. B.C. election casts uncertainty over future of Indigenous protected area

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In Kaska territory, summer turns toward fall earlier than in much of British Columbia. Pressed up against the Yukon border, where the Alaska Highway cuts across the landscape, it’s a place where leaves start changing colour in August, and by September, snow already dusts the mountaintops. Seasonal change has always held meaning for Kaska, and this year autumn was supposed to mark something new: the final few steps in their effort to protect a massive swath of their territory, called Dene Kʼéh Kusān.

But when BC NDP Leader David Eby called a snap election in late September, Kaska Nation leaders say it stalled that process. Now, they’re worried the election could derail, or at least slow, the entire multi-year planning process for the roughly three-million-hectare protected area.

“We’re disappointed an election call has once again interrupted our discussions with the government as we had hoped to achieve a protection designation within the last political mandate,” Liard First Nation Chief Harlan Schilling said in an emailed statement.

Liard First Nation is one of four nations that make up the broader Kaska Nation, whose traditional territory stretches across northern British Columbia and southern Yukon. Two decades ago, the nation created the non-profit Dena Kayeh Institute to protect its language, culture and lands. In June 2019, the institute announced plans to create a massive Indigenous Protected and Conserved Area to protect one of the province’s largest intact wilderness areas. It is called Dene Kʼéh Kusān, which, translated from the Kaska language, means “always be there.”

“Dene K’éh Kusān is a vision that includes all British Columbians. It will protect some of Canada’s most important wildlife habitat and create world-class opportunities for hunters, anglers, naturalists and backcountry recreationalists,” Schilling said.

In June 2024, the British Columbia government signed an agreement with the Kaska nations to undertake land-use planning in its territories. In the coming years, the ministry explained, the government, the nation and other stakeholders would sit down for a comprehensive land-use planning process that would formalize provincial protections for Dene Kʼéh Kusān. That process stalled a few months later when the province went to the polls in October 2024, but picked up again after the election. On July 2, 2026, the province issued a press release announcing public consultations on the new protected area.

“This work shows what’s possible when we all come together to protect the places that matter most,” Tamara Davidson, then-minister of environment and parks, said at the time.

That process was supposed to conclude this fall, with Schilling expecting to finalize protections for the area before the end of the year. Now, the election has stalled the process. He’s worried about the lasting impact the delay could have, especially since a temporary ban on mineral staking inside the area is set to expire in January.“The current pause on mineral staking in Dene K’éh Kusān is set to expire just three months after the election,” he said.

Since the election was called, polling shows the BC Conservatives with a narrow but consistent lead over the BC NDP. Projections from 338Canada point to a likely change in government, with the Conservatives currently expected to win the most seats.

Tim Burkhart, director of landscape protection at the conservation group Yellowstone to Yukon, said in an emailed statement the next government will have “a generational opportunity” to protect the unique wilderness area, but added time is running out, given the scheduled expiry of the mineral tenure pause. “The window to act is closing,” he said.

“The years of planning, broad stakeholder endorsement and public engagement are done, and there is consensus to secure this wild legacy forever,” Burkhart, whose group supports the Kaska project, said.

“We want to ensure all parties know protecting this unique wilderness is important for British Columbians and Canadians,” Schilling said.

No major party directly addresses Dene Kʼéh Kusān in its platform, but the BC NDP has reaffirmed a commitment to meet the pledge to protect 30 per cent of B.C. lands and waters by 2030, known as 30-by-30, and “work with First Nations on this goal.” As of December 2025, 15.9 per cent of the province was protected areas, according to the federal government. Since Dene Kʼéh Kusān covers around four per cent of the province, it would go a long way toward closing the gap.

The BC Conservatives have made no specific promises or platform announcements on conservation in this election. They have promised to repeal the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, which the previous government cited as part of the reason it entered land use planning with Kaska. And in May 2024, former BC Conservative leader John Rustad described the 30-by-30 conservation goal as “nonsense” and pledged to scrap it if he formed government.

The Narwhal reached out to the BC Conservative and BC NDP candidates running in the riding of Bulkley-Stikine, where most of Dene Kʼéh Kusān would sit, but neither responded. The BC Green Party is not running a candidate in the riding.

For now, Schilling and others working on protecting the region will have to wait until the votes are counted on Oct. 24 to figure out the path forward.“We’re hopeful the next government will see the importance of this initiative and bring it to completion,” Schilling said.

Pluralistic: The technopolitics of ad-tech (19 Oct 2026)

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

"Technopolitics" are the politics embedded in technology. The Ur-text of technopolitics is "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" by the rock critic turned scholar Langdon Winner:

https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~beki/cs4001/Winner.pdf

With "Do Artifacts," Winner demands that we move beyond the idea of technologies having "intended consequences" and "unintended consequences" and towards a political economy of tech. In service to this, he divides the politics of artifacts into three categories:

I. Artifacts that have politics embedded in them by their creators, like Robert Moses's infamous bridges over the Long Island parkways. According to Moses biographer Robert Caro, Moses deliberately specified that these bridges must be too low for a city bus to pass under, in a bid to block Black people from traveling from New York City to the beaches he'd developed on Long Island.

Today those bridges are more than a century old, and they are still too low for city buses to pass under them, and this continues to mean that people who don't own private vehicles find it more difficult to get to the beach, and as it's still the case that race is a predictor of low incomes, those are still racist bridges.

II. Artifacts whose politics are accidental but unavoidable. Winner cites a kind of tomato harvester that vastly improved the efficiency of tomato pickers. These harvesters became very popular, which allowed tomato farmers to lay off large numbers of agricultural workers, and also allowed them to price the mechanically harvested tomatoes so cheaply that they crowded out other kinds of tomatoes.

Because these machines created a large pool of unemployed workers, they shifted power from labor to capital, and because these picking machines were too rough for all but the hardest varietals of tomato, they changed the flavor profile of tomatoes in America for the worse, towards a bland, tough tomato.

These picking machines were not designed to make workers poorer, nor were they designed to make tomatoes taste worse, but because of their technical characteristics, this was the unavoidable outcome, the inextricable politics of these machines.

III. Artifacts whose politics are intrinsic to their design. Here, Winner contrasts solar panels with nuclear reactors. Solar panels may require highly centralized industrial systems to produce, but once they are in the field, solar panels are compatible with looser, less coordinated political arrangements. A world of small anarchist communes is fully compatible with solar energy.

Contrast this with nuclear reactors: the prudent ongoing operation of a reactor requires a highly centralized state that is capable of fielding reliable, trustworthy independent inspectors. This state must also be capable of exercising the coercive power to shut down badly maintained or incompetently operated reactors.

The politics of nukes are the politics of a centralized, powerful state; the politics of solar are less determined. Solar is compatible with a wide variety of political systems, including (but not limited to) weak, decentralized states.

(For a further exploration of "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" I highly recommend Ginger Coons' reading guide:)

https://www.not-so-obvious.net/reading-do-artifacts-have-politics/

I really like Winner's framework for exploring technopolitics, but I have my own gloss on it. Winner's framework doesn't explicitly contemplate the idea of technological countermeasures. For example, what if someone were to design a city bus that was low enough to fit under Robert Moses's bridges? At that point, they'd cease to be "racist bridges," and merely become "bridges built by a racist":

https://locusmag.com/feature/commentary-cory-doctorow-technopolitics/

Which brings me to the technopolitics of ad-tech. Lots of people think that advertising is "the original sin of the internet," setting in motion the surveillance dystopia that is closing in on us from all sides today:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/17/mercenary-spyware/#discover-tech

I think this is wrong. I think that finding ways to communicate ideas and pitches to the general public is fine. Without some channel for reaching people with ideas and propositions they may not be familiar with, it would be infinitely harder to reach people for political causes. It would also be harder for new books, movies and music to find new audiences. It would be much harder to get people out to your independent craft fair, your zine show, or your No Kings march.

For me, the internet's original sin isn't advertising, it's surveillance. Contrary to what you have been told – and pax Winner's framework – the internet is fully compatible with privacy:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/10/31/losing-the-crypto-wars/#surveillance-monopolism

It wasn't the internet's technopolitics that made it a surveillance nightmare. It was just plain politics. Specifically, our politicians decided to stop enacting and enforcing privacy laws. The last consumer privacy law we got in America was a bill that made it illegal for video store clerks to disclose your VHS rentals:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_Privacy_Protection_Act

(As I type these words, the Supreme Court is poised to eliminate even that dried-up old crumb of privacy protection:)

https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2026/10/supreme-court-privacy-rights-case-bork-kavanaugh.html

The widespread phenomenon of ad-tech companies spying on us is the result of the decision to allow them to do so. That decision has had innumerable negative downstream effects, such as skyrocketing "surveillance prices":

https://pluralistic.net/2025/12/11/nothing-personal/#instacartography

https://pluralistic.net/2024/12/18/loose-flapping-ends/#luigi-has-a-point

It's created vast surveillance databases that are critical to Trump's racist pogroms, providing dossiers and precise location data on the people ICE kidnaps to Salvadoran slave-labor camps:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/03/10/ice-tech/#foreseeable-outcomes

Surveillance also promotes oligarchy. Without surveillance, oligarchs must give up more of their fortunes to keep the public happy, because the alternative is spending even more money on guards to keep the public from building guillotines on their lawns. Surveillance lets oligarchs make targeted strikes on restive radicals, which is much cheaper than broad-spectrum policies that make us all better off and neutralize the case for guillotines:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/06/26/autostabilizer/#slicey-bois

These are the technopolitics of surveillance, not advertising. There is nothing about advertising that demands surveillance. For centuries, advertising was not meaningfully linked to surveillance. The decision to fuse surveillance with advertising was just that – a decision, not an inevitability.

Any time we forget this, we let spying tech bosses off the hook. After all, if advertising demands surveillance, then tech bosses are just the avatars of the great forces of history, prisoners of the imperative to spy in order to advertise. By remembering that surveillance advertising is a choice, we put the blame squarely where it belongs: on the guillotineable oligarchs who made that choice, and amassed spectacular fortunes thereby.

Surveillance is political, but surveillance advertising has its own distinct technopolitics, and to understand those technopolitics, we must contrast them with the technopolitics of contextual advertising. Up until a decade or two ago, contextual advertising was simply called "advertising." Contextual advertising is the practice of running refrigerator ads next to articles on cooking and kitchen remodeling; ads for roadside restaurants on highway billboards; ads for Disney vacations in parenting magazines.

Your intuition probably insists that context advertising is less effective than surveillance advertising. After all, surveillance advertising allows a running shoe company to target users who are training for a marathon no matter where they are and no matter what they're doing. Surely this translates into increased "conversions" (clicks and/or purchases, depending on who you're talking to)?

It does, but not by much. Advertisers get about 5% more value from surveillance ads than they do from context ads:

https://weis2019.econinfosec.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2019/05/WEIS_2019_paper_38.pdf

Partly, that's because surveillance ad companies grossly overrate the efficacy of surveillance advertising:

https://peoples-things.ghost.io/youre-doing-it-wrong-notes-on-criticism-and-technology-hype/

But surveillance advertising's anemic returns to advertisers are down to another aspect of the system: its tendency towards monopolization:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Google_LLC_(2023)

Surveillance is a capital-intensive activity: spying on billions of people as they move through the digital and physical world is incredibly expensive. That is a good part of the reason that online advertising has collapsed into the Meta/Google duopoly, who control about 80% of display advertising.

The harms of surveillance monopolies have a severely distorting effect on policy debates. For example, it's led some anti-monopolists to call for government-issued surveillance identifiers that follow us from birth to death in order to facilitate commercial spying to foster more competition in surveillance:

https://www.thesling.org/what-to-do-about-googles-dominance-of-online-advertising/

Here's the thing about monopolists: they cheat. Google and Meta have an illegal, collusive arrangement to rig the advertising market, overcharging advertisers and underpaying publishers:

The ad-tech giants have taken in hundreds of billions that they simply can't account for. It's disappeared down a fraud-hole:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/05/25/lying-spies/#spying-liars

All of this has driven the majority of web users to install a privacy-blocker (like EFF's Privacybadger.org), meaning that surveillance ads are disproportionately likely to not even show up in a reader's browser:

https://doc.searls.com/2023/11/11/how-is-the-worlds-biggest-boycott-doing/

So while it's possible that, in a state of nature, a surveillance ad would outperform a context ad, delivering more sales for advertisers and more revenue for publishers, the monopolistic tendency of ad-tech means that publishers earn less from surveillance ads (because more users block them, and because monopolistic ad-tech platforms extract higher commissions), and advertisers see fewer sales from them (because more users block them, and because monopolistic ad-tech platforms extract higher prices).

Context ads, meanwhile, are always going to give power to publishers, because publishers will always understand the materials they publish better than any ad-tech platform could. Commissioning, editing and publishing an article, podcast or video gives a publisher unique and inalienable insight into that material.

Surveillance data about audiences is a kind of ambient vapor floating around websites and places and payments that has to be condensed through a heroic and expensive effort that gives power to whichever company can muster the capital and infrastructure to do so. Meanwhile, information about the content of publishers' offerings arrives pre-refined into ingots that are already in publishers' own possession.

It's possible to run "programmatic ads" without surveillance. Rather than building an ad-tech stack through which publishers transmit sensitive information about their audiences ("Who will pay me to advertise to this 18-34 year old man-child from the outer boroughs who owns an Xbox and has recently researched gonorrhea symptoms?"), we can build systems through which publishers transmit information about their own materials ("Who will advertise to a user with a New York IP address who is reading an article comparing running shoe brands?").

Not only is this possible, but it actually generates more revenues for publishers than the surveillance-based ad-tech stack:

https://techcrunch.com/2020/07/24/data-from-dutch-public-broadcaster-shows-the-value-of-ditching-creepy-ads/

On top of that, publishers who don't gather personal information about users also don't need to show their users those awful pop-up ads asking their permission to gather their data. Everyone hates those notices, and they are yet another negative aspect of surveillance advertising that disappears with the switch to context ads.

An ad-tech stack for context ads is far less prone to monopolization. Such a stack can be "federated," with multiple "demand-side platforms" (the part of the system that advertisers use to place bids for the right to show their ads) all communicating with the same "supply-side platform" (the part of the system publishers use to transmit opportunities to place ads).

This is also in the technopolitics of context ads: they do not tend toward the "efficiencies of scale" that lead to abusive monopolistic middlemen who steal from publishers and advertisers and sell out the public to fascists who want to arrest them and deport them to Salvadoran slave-labor camps.

When the ad-tech sector is composed of smaller companies that are more competitive with one another, it is much harder for any of them to collude to rig the market. That fierce competition also means it's harder to create a cozy conspiracy to lobby together to block privacy laws, and it leaves the companies with lower margins and less retained earnings to spend on those lobbying efforts.

Surveillance ads also promote all kinds of parasitic businesses. The fact that a publisher can serve ads related to users rather than the subject of their media means that sensationalistic and deceptive websites can sell ads that run against content that's totally unrelated to advertisers' products and services. And because it's cheaper to produce lies and garbage, these publishers can undercut the ad prices demanded by high quality outlets and let advertisers reach the same users. This is the whole reason that "chumboxes" exist:

Just as nuclear power favors a centralized state, surveillance advertising:

favors low-quality media over high-quality production;

provides authoritarian states with an off-the-books, warrantless source of surveillance data.

And just as solar allows for decentralized, anti-authoritarian societies, context advertising:

tends towards pluralism and diversity of voices;

favors high-quality media by reserving the lion's share of the rewards to the producers;

does not rely on a weak privacy environment;

does not militate for "democratizing spying" by issuing every person with a permanent advertising identifier.

There's nothing (necessarily) wrong with building systems to tell people about things you want them to know. There's nothing (necessarily) wrong with paying for media with ads. It's possible for both of these things to go awry, but they are not in and of themselves bad. They have contingent technopolitics.

But surveillance ads have completely unavoidable, absolutely ghastly technopolitics. Surveillance ads are bad for publishers, for advertisers, and for democracy. We should ban surveillance ads:

https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2023/05/save-news-we-must-ban-surveillance-advertising

Recetas Reconfortantes y Saludables para Combatir el Frío

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Cuando el termómetro baja y los días se vuelven más cortos, es totalmente normal que nuestro cuerpo nos pida a gritos comidas calientes y energéticas. En lugar de caer en platos excesivamente pesados, la clave está en apostar por recetas que nos arropen y nos den bienestar, utilizando productos de temporada que aporten los nutrientes necesarios para afrontar la estación invernal con fuerza.

Cocinar en casa durante el invierno no tiene por qué ser un engorro ni implicar platos complicadísimos. Podemos disfrutar de sabores intensos y tradicionales mediante preparaciones sencillas, ya sea recurriendo a la freidora de aire para agilizar procesos o organizando un menú semanal con tápers para llevar al trabajo, asegurando así que comamos sano incluso fuera de casa.

Los platos de cuchara son, sin duda, los reyes indiscutibles de esta época. Una opción magnífica es la sopa de pollo con jengibre y limón, que gracias al toque cítrico y el calor del jengibre resulta ideal para revitalizar el organismo. Si buscamos algo más tradicional, la sopa de ajo es un básico español que combina pan, pimentón y huevos escalfados, siendo una herramienta fantástica para activar el sistema inmunológico.

Para quienes prefieren texturas diferentes, la sopa de sémola de trigo o cous-cous ofrece una suavidad increíble, mientras que una sopa de fideos con garbanzos y champiñones se convierte en una comida completa y nutritiva. Asimismo, la clásica sopa de ave con arroz y zanahoria sigue siendo un valor seguro para quienes buscan una receta que reconforte el alma y sea fácil de digerir.

Las legumbres son nuestras mejores aliadas para mantenernos saciados sin necesidad de recurrir siempre a los embutidos más grasos. Un ejemplo perfecto son las alubias blancas con espinacas, un plato único rico en fibra y vitaminas que se puede cocinar a fuego lento y «asustando» las legumbres para que queden perfectas. Por otro lado, el potaje de garbanzos con espinacas es una alternativa equilibrada que aporta proteínas vegetales esenciales.

Si tenemos ganas de algo más contundente y con sello regional, el cocido madrileño es la opción más exhaustiva, ya que combina carnes, verduras y garbanzos servidos en varias etapas. De igual forma, la fabada asturiana, con sus fabes y embutidos, es un plato que abraza y es ideal para compartir en familia. Para los amantes de la carne, la caldereta de cordero cocinada a fuego lento con vino tinto ofrece ese sabor rústico de montaña que tanto nos gusta.

No todo tiene que ser guiso; la pasta también puede ser una opción sana si elegimos ingredientes adecuados. Los spaghettis integrales con boloñesa de lentejas son una alternativa brillante para obtener energía física y mental mediante proteínas vegetales. Para quienes buscan algo más gourmet, el spaghetti XL con gambas y salsa de ajo confitado aporta una calidez irresistible y un sabor sofisticado.

Si preferimos algo más ligero para la cena, las lasañas de setas del bosque capturan toda la esencia del otoño y el invierno. Por último, las cremas y purés son la solución más económica y sana para aprovechar las verduras de la nevera. Una crema de calabacín con un toque de queso light es una opción cremosa y nutritiva que se prepara rápidamente y ayuda a entrar en calor antes de dormir.

Para elevar el nivel de cualquier receta, el uso de un buen caldo de base es fundamental. Ya sea un caldo casero hecho con puerro y cebolla o una opción de calidad ya preparada, esto añade una profundidad de sabor y una cantidad de nutrientes que marcan la diferencia. Un buen caldo no solo hace que la cocina sea más rápida, sino que transforma una sopa sencilla en un manjar lleno de matices y calidez.

Tener a mano recetas que mezclen la tradición de los potajes españoles con la ligereza de las cremas vegetales y las pastas integrales nos permite disfrutar del invierno sin descuidar la salud. La combinación de ingredientes frescos de temporada y técnicas de cocción lenta garantiza que cada bocado sea una caricia al paladar que nos proteja del frío exterior.

What does Bill C-39 mean for First Nations? Senators question Carney government

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Carl Meyer has been reporting on environmental and climate policy for a decade and is a member of the Parliamentary press gallery in Ottawa.

When Senator Michèle Audette questioned the Canadian government over its latest plan to fast-track industrial projects, she started by speaking in Innu-Aimun.

For the senator, an Indigenous women’s rights advocate who was a commissioner in the National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls, speaking the language was a way to centre her Innu community’s past, as Canada tries to shape its future.

“It was to remind, in that important space, that there was a moment when the government had tried to erase us through legislation and policies,” Sen. Audette, who comes from the First Nation of Innu Takuaikan Uashat Mak Mani-Utenam in eastern Quebec, told The Narwhal in an interview.

The people she was reminding were a trio of federal ministers who had appeared at a special Senate hearing on Oct. 6 to answer questions about Prime Minister Mark Carney’s Bill C-39, the Building Canada Strong Act.

The 243-page omnibus bill is the latest step in the government’s controversial reshaping of how major projects are scrutinized and approved in Canada. Major projects are things like mines, ports or pipelines, and can affect everything from watersheds to wildlife and can lead to changes in greenhouse gas emissions and public health.

Among other changes, the bill would centralize many Indigenous consultations under a new hub within the Impact Assessment Agency of Canada, limit environmental assessments of all projects subject to federal reviews to a maximum of one year, and consolidate all required permits into a single decision issued by a single minister after consulting with colleagues.

It would also let projects inside designated zones, deemed “regions of national interest,” bypass normal approval processes, allowing some to start before the assessment is complete and empowering the government to change or remove conditions that have already been imposed on a project.

Carney’s government has said the bill is a response to complaints from workers, investors, communities and Indigenous groups that approval processes are too slow. It said new legislation is needed to get federal authorities to work together more effectively, so big projects are built faster.

“This in no way reduces our responsibility to assess projects against environmental standards or to fulfill our obligations to Indigenous Peoples,” Privy Council president Dominic LeBlanc, who is responsible for the government’s major projects and free trade push, told the senators.

But a wide swath of Indigenous leadership across the country has united in opposition to what they perceive as the bill’s infringement on the constitutional rights that guarantee an ability to influence decisions that affect their territories.

Many have also recoiled at the majority government’s hurry to transform its bill into law before the end of the year. Special committees in the House of Commons and Senate have been set up to examine the bill simultaneously, instead of the standard procedure where committees at each level take up a bill in succession.

“Prime Minister Carney is weaponizing the parliamentary process and his majority to force the bill through Parliament [and] override the inherent, Treaty and constitutionally protected rights of First Nations,” a joint statement from the Chiefs of Ontario, the British Columbia Assembly of First Nations, the Assembly of First Nations Quebec-Labrador and the Nishnawbe Aski Nation of northern Ontario said on Oct. 8.

“This extraordinary maneuver has one purpose: to push the most consequential economic legislation in a generation through Parliament before First Nations, or anyone else, can meaningfully scrutinize it. It is fundamentally antidemocratic.”

The Carney government has given itself broad discretion to determine what exactly is in the “national interest.”

Last year’s Building Canada Act, which first established this special designation for projects, laid out different elements the government “may consider” when determining “national interest” status.

These are: Canada’s autonomy, resilience and security; the economic or other benefits to the country, the likelihood of the project’s success, how it advances Indigenous interests and how it contributes to clean growth or tackling climate change. But the government also allowed itself to consider “any factor” it deemed relevant, and these elements are not required to all be met as a prerequisite to projects being given the “national interest” designation.

In fact, the first project the Carney government listed was the West Coast Oil pipeline, now named Pacific Link, which the Canadian Climate Institute expects will usher in 20 million tonnes per year of new greenhouse gas emissions — not exactly the type of project that tackles climate change. Meanwhile, the Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis has said the economics of the pipeline are also debatable.

The new bill extends the same set of broad parameters and discretion to determining “regions of national interest.”

As she questioned the federal ministers who had come to answer questions on Bill C-39, Sen. Audette asked if their examination of these regions would include “not only the Indigenous Peoples who are directly affected by the projects but also those upstream and downstream who will feel the environmental, social and economic impacts.”

She told The Narwhal she is concerned the bill may frame Indigenous consultations over these zones as primarily being related to development. Some First Nations’ traditional territories cover vast areas and may overlap with the zones, for instance, even if their actual reserves may not.

“They’ll say, ‘Oh, there’s no First Nation, there’s no reserve on that road,’ but go back before it became Canada, we were there,” she said. “We are still there. We never surrendered.”

The government has said “national interest” regions could be anything from an industrial area to one earmarked for “energy production” or a telecommunications network. The label could even be applied without a specific project in mind, but for “future development needs.”

It said it would first carry out a regional assessment to consider cumulative impacts of development in the area, which would include Indigenous consultations, before designating a zone.

“We don’t want to be a third party or a stakeholder,” Sen. Audette said. “Even if you don’t see a reserve, we are involved, and we want to make sure that what you do, we are at the same level, on the same page.”

In his response to the senator on Oct. 6, Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon, who is also the bill’s sponsor, said First Nations would be involved “at the very beginning of the process, from the conception stage onwards.” He also said he and LeBlanc have already “experienced a lot of very positive feedback from rights holders” about the government’s new approval process for projects.

Senator Suze Youance, a civil engineer, told the federal ministers she felt Bill C-39’s shortening of approval timelines was “causing a lot of concern among environmental groups and for certain communities.”

She asked whether the government had considered changing its proposed process “in order to find an acceptable middle ground between economic haste and environmental prudence.”

LeBlanc responded, “The way we reconcile those priorities is reflected in our commitment to act rigorously and in the fact that environmental standards have not changed, nor have our responsibilities toward Indigenous Rights holders.”

Senator Rosa Galvez, an expert on pollution and health who introduced legislation to align the financial sector with climate objectives, said that while Canada has been slow at building some projects, the reason “was not always because of environmental concerns or Indigenous Peoples’ rights. It was also due to a lack of investment.”

She said, during some previous nation-building moments in Canada’s history, reforms that were meant to simplify the regulatory process actually “brought new forms of fragmentation.”

“How will scientific independence be maintained when a specialized regulator assumes greater responsibility for environmental reviews?” she asked.

LeBlanc said the responsibility around oversight evaluation was not being diminished and that the regulators that are responsible will still work with other federal, provincial and independent agencies, and include public consultations.

“The public participation process under any of these agencies doing these reviews has not been changed,” LeBlanc said. “Public participation remains.”

Sudan: Klimawandel und Drohnenangriffe verschärfen humanitäre Krise

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Während die Kämpfe im Sudan immer mehr Menschen zur Flucht zwingen, warnen Hilfsorganisationen vor einem drohenden Zusammenbruch des Gesundheitssystems. Der UN-Sicherheitsrat hat das Waffenembargo für Darfur verlängert.

Im Sudan haben sich die Kämpfe zwischen der sudanesischen Armee (SAF) und der paramilitärischen Miliz Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in Nord-Kordofan und Teilen des Bundesstaates Blauer Nil verschärft. Tausende Menschen mussten fliehen, zu einer Zeit, in der die Bauern ihre Felder bestellen.

Seit Ausbruch des Krieges im April 2023 kämpfen die Armee und die RSF mit ihren jeweils verbündeten Milizen um die Macht im Land. Doch keine der beiden Konfliktparteien konnte bislang die Kontrolle über den gesamten Sudan erlangen.

Die reguläre sudanesische Armee kontrolliert den Norden und das Zentrum des Landes einschließlich der Hauptstadt Khartum. Die RSF sind vor allem in der westlichen Region Darfur und Teilen des Südens präsent.

Der Krieg hat im Sudan eine der schwersten humanitären Krisen weltweit ausgelöst. Darauf weisen die Vereinten Nationen und internationale Hilfsorganisationen hin. Schätzungen zufolge wurden zwischen 40.000 und 250.000 Menschen getötet. Wie viele Menschen tatsächlich ums Leben gekommen sind, lässt sich angesichts der anhaltenden Kämpfe kaum feststellen. Mehr als 12,4 Millionen Menschen wurden aus ihren Heimatorten vertrieben. Sie suchen innerhalb des Sudans oder in den Nachbarländern Schutz.

In dieser Woche warnte die Hilfsorganisation Aktion gegen den Hunger erneut vor der dramatischen Versorgungslage.

"24,6 Millionen Menschen sind von schwerer akuter Ernährungsunsicherheit betroffen", sagte Jan Sebastian Friedrich-Rust, Geschäftsführer der deutschen Sektion der Hilfsorganisation, der DW.

Nach Angaben von Friedrich-Rust leiden 2026 schätzungsweise 825.000 Kinder unter fünf Jahren an schwerer akuter Mangelernährung. Insgesamt seien vermutlich 4,2 Millionen Kinder sowie schwangere und stillende Frauen akut mangelernährt.

Auch das internationale Frühwarnsystem für Ernährungskrisen FEWS NET (Famine Early Warning Systems Network) warnte im Oktober vor einer weiteren Verschärfung der Lage. Das Netzwerk beobachtet die Ernährungssicherheit und bewertet sie anhand der international anerkannten IPC-Skala. Demnach droht in mehreren Gebieten, darunter auch in Lagern für Binnenvertriebene in den Großregionen Darfur und Kordofan, die höchste Stufe akuter Ernährungsunsicherheit. Die sogenannte IPC-Stufe 5 steht für eine katastrophale Ernährungslage.

"Die Lage im Sudan verschlechtert sich weiter, und der Bedarf an humanitärer Hilfe wächst, während gleichzeitig immer weniger Mittel zur Verfügung stehen", sagte Fabrizia Falcione, Sudan-Vertreterin des Bevölkerungsfonds der Vereinten Nationen (UNFPA). Die UN-Organisation setzt sich unter anderem für sexuelle und reproduktive Gesundheit ein.

"Ich habe vor Kurzem das Vertriebenenlager Tawila besucht und finde noch immer kaum Worte für das, was ich dort gesehen habe", sagte Falcione der DW. "Stellen Sie sich vor, mehr Menschen als in Frankfurt am Main werden innerhalb von weniger als drei Jahren gezwungen, ihre Häuser zu verlassen und an einem einzigen Ort Zuflucht zu suchen."

Mehr als 700.000 Menschen seien dort auf ein einziges Krankenhaus angewiesen, in dem Kaiserschnitte durchgeführt werden können, erklärte Falcione.

Obwohl Hilfsorganisationen immer wieder vor der sich verschärfenden Notlage warnen, fehlt es weiterhin an Geld. Der humanitäre Hilfsplan für 2026 sieht 2,9 Milliarden US-Dollar (2,59 Milliarden Euro) vor. Bislang haben internationale Geber jedoch nur 1,2 Milliarden Dollar (1,07 Milliarden Euro) bereitgestellt. Damit ist der Hilfsplan erst zu 41 Prozent finanziert.

Neben dem Krieg machen auch extreme Wetterbedingungen den Menschen im Sudan zu schaffen. Das UN-Nothilfebüro OCHA warnte kürzlich, dass große Hitze und schwere Überschwemmungen die ohnehin dramatische humanitäre Lage weiter verschärfen.

"In diesem Jahr hat eine massive Hitzewelle in einigen Regionen zu Stromausfällen von bis zu zwölf Stunden am Tag geführt", sagte die Sudan-Expertin Hager Ali vom German Institute for Global and Area Studies (GIGA) der DW. "Flussbetten und ausgetrocknete Felder sind kahl. Hinzu kommen die Schäden durch die Kämpfe, die Taktik der verbrannten Erde der RSF und die Umweltzerstörung."

Während etwa die Hälfte des Landes unter Dürre leidet, haben heftige Regenfälle und örtliche Überschwemmungen die Lage in anderen Regionen weiter verschlimmert. "Als wäre das nicht schon genug, gibt es auch einen massiven Mangel an Düngemitteln und Diesel", sagte Ali der DW.

Der Sudan ist auf die Einfuhr von Düngemitteln und Diesel aus der Golfregion angewiesen. Die Lieferungen kommen auf dem Seeweg durch die Straße von Hormus und die Meerenge Bab al-Mandab. Doch durch den Krieg mit dem Iran sind diese wichtigen Handelsrouten zunehmend beeinträchtigt. Der schränkt den Schiffsverkehr in der Straße von Hormus massiv ein. Zugleich haben die mit Teheran verbündeten Huthi-Rebellen im Jemen Schiffe in der Meerenge Bab al-Mandab bedroht und den Schiffsverkehr dort zeitweise gestört.

"Die Versorgung mit Düngemitteln ist 2026 von 450.000 Tonnen auf nur noch etwa 50.000 Tonnen eingebrochen", sagte Ali. Auch Diesel sei knapp und erheblich teurer geworden. Das verschärfe die Krise gleich in zweifacher Hinsicht.

"Landwirtschaftliche Maschinen brauchen Diesel, und der Transport von Hilfsgütern und landwirtschaftlichen Erzeugnissen innerhalb des Landes wird deutlich teurer", erklärte Ali.

Wie stark die Preise inzwischen gestiegen sind, zeigt auch eine Warnung des Internationalen Forschungsinstituts für Ernährungspolitik (IFPRI) vom Oktober. Demnach verteuerten sich Grundnahrungsmittel und Treibstoff im Sudan im September 2026 erheblich.

Beobachter weisen seit Langem darauf hin, dass der Krieg im Sudan kaum enden wird, solange ausländische Staaten die Konfliktparteien weiter mit Waffen versorgen. Die sudanesische Armee wird offiziell von Ägypten, der Türkei, Russland und dem Iran unterstützt. Die Vereinigten Arabischen Emirate gelten als mutmaßlich wichtigster Unterstützer der RSF. Abu Dhabi bestreitet die Vorwürfe.

Dennoch hat der UN-Sicherheitsrat im Oktober lediglich das bestehende Waffenembargo für die Darfur-Region bis zum 11. Dezember verlängert. Forderungen, das Embargo auf den gesamten Sudan auszuweiten, wurden fallengelassen.

"Eine Ausweitung des UN-Embargos auf den gesamten Sudan hätte insbesondere auch Drohnen eingeschlossen", sagte Hager Ali. Sie verwies darauf, dass der Sudan zu den Ländern gehört, in denen Drohnen besonders intensiv im Krieg eingesetzt werden. Im Jahr 2026 seien Drohnenangriffe die häufigste Ursache für zivile Opfer im Land.

Ali erklärte im Gespräch mit der DW außerdem, dass sich der ständige Vertreter des Sudans bei den Vereinten Nationen, Al-Harith Idriss al-Harith Mohamed, gegen eine Verlängerung des Embargos ausgesprochen habe. Er befürchte, dass dadurch vor allem die sudanesische Armee auf dem Schlachtfeld benachteiligt würde.

"Ein Waffenembargo würde potenzielle Unterstützer davon abhalten, mit den sudanesischen Streitkräften zusammenzuarbeiten. Die Rapid Support Forces hingegen beschaffen sich ihre Waffen nicht unbedingt über offizielle Wege", sagte Ali.

"Die RSF sind auf den Schmuggelhandel mit anderen Milizen, privaten Militärdienstleistern und Händlern in der Region angewiesen", erklärte sie. Weil die Miliz ihre Waffen und andere Ausrüstung aus zahlreichen unterschiedlichen Quellen beziehe, sei es schwierig, ihre Versorgung mit einem einzigen Embargo gezielt zu unterbinden.
