The digital publishing industry has relied on Google for a very long time. Many outlets spent time and money optimizing their webpages to show up at the top of the search engine, with the goal of getting readers to click on their articles or discover their websites for the first time. For years, the relationship between Google and publishers was largely reciprocal; online publishers filled out Google’s search index with content, while Google supplied publications with exposure and traffic.

But that relationship has changed. The launch of Google’s AI search features — coupled with changes to the way it ranks search results — has made it harder for publishers to survive on traffic from Google alone. In 2024, Google rolled out AI Overviews, a feature that slots an AI-generated search result summarizing pages from across the web at the very top of Google Search, with links to sources its AI system determines to be the most useful placed below the summary. Instead of vying for clicks on the first page of Search, publishers became beholden to an AI system that regurgitates results, with no guarantee that users will actually click on the source of that information.

Though Google’s AI Overviews got off to a shaky start, the feature immediately raised concerns among publishers, with the News/Media Alliance saying it will be “catastrophic” to web traffic. A 2024 investigation from the Press Gazette found that Google’s AI Overviews appeared for almost a quarter of news-related searches in May 2024. By August, the outlet found that AI Overviews had begun impacting the visibility of publishers by replacing media sites that previously held the top spots on Google’s search engine for certain queries.

A future where Google could eventually stop sending traffic to outside sites on the web has only gotten closer as the company builds out its vision for an AI-powered search. In May 2025, it launched AI Mode, a feature that replaces Google’s standard search engine with a chatbot-like interface that provides detailed summaries from results on the web.

The concerns raised by the News/Media Alliance were real: Traffic to online publishers tanked following the launch of Google’s AI search features. A June 2025 report from The Wall Street Journal revealed how traffic to Business Insider, The Washington Post, HuffPost, and the WSJ itself has declined since 2022. Data from analytics company Similarweb suggested that search traffic to The New York Times sank from 44 percent to 35.5 percent in three years, the WSJ reported.

A report from Digiday pointed to similar dips at Digital Content Next (DCN), a trade association that represents outlets and media companies like Bloomberg, The Associated Press, The New York Times, Condé Nast, and Vox Media. Between May and June 2025, DCN says 19 of its members experienced a traffic loss on Google, ranging from 1 to 25 percent, Digiday reported. DMG Media, which owns Daily Mail and Metro, told the UK’s Competition and Markets Authority that click-through rates dropped by up to 89 percent between June and July 2025, citing impacts from AI Overviews. On top of all of this, Pew Research found that people are “less likely” to click on links to other websites when they see an AI Overview.

These numbers starkly contrast with Google’s claims that its AI search features are helping the web. Last year, Google Search head Liz Reid said click volume from the search engine remained “relatively stable,” adding that AI search is sending more clicks to different types of websites — especially those “with forums, videos, podcasts, and posts” from other users. Nick Fox, Google’s senior vice president of knowledge and information, told the BBC, “From our point of view, the web is thriving.”

In addition to scrutiny from regulators, publishers from around the globe have filed lawsuits against Google, claiming the company’s AI search features have negatively impacted traffic. Many lawsuits haven’t been successful. US District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed an antitrust case from Chegg and Penske Media, which owns The Rolling Stone, Billboard, The Hollywood Reporter, and The Verge. The same judge also rejected a proposed class action lawsuit surrounding AI Overviews filed by Arkansas newspapers.

Online publishers have had to adjust their strategies in the wake of falling search traffic, with many leaning on social media, newsletters, and subscriptions to promote and make money from their work. Others are using a toggle that allows them to opt out of appearing in AI Mode and AI Overviews without affecting their visibility in search — an option Google broadly rolled out following an order from the UK. But this still doesn’t fix sinking traffic.

Pushback from online publishers appears to have resulted in some changes to Google’s AI search features. The company has begun to make sources more prominent in AI Mode and AI Overviews, as hovering over a link in either feature will show a pop-up with a list of articles and accompanying images. Another AI search change puts in-line links “higher up in responses more frequently.”

Google will likely continue making changes to how it presents sources in AI search, while coming up with ways to compensate publishers that inform its results. The company says 400 publishers, including The Washington Post, CNN, Axios, and the Financial Times, are now part of its News AI pilot program, which licenses content to develop new AI features. It’s also testing a program that pays publishers for the use of their content in AI Overviews and AI Mode, according to reports from The Information and Digiday. Whether this program is enough to repair Google’s relationship with publishers remains to be seen.

Un buhonero de fama afable se convirtió en el hombre lobo más célebre de España después de confesar crímenes que atribuía a una bruja. Manuel Blanco Romasanta había sido sastre antes de recorrer como vendedor ambulante Galicia, León, Asturias, Cantabria y Portugal.

Por qué las brujas llevan sombreros puntiagudos: el origen de una imagen que nació mucho después de las persecuciones

En las aldeas lo conocían como un hombre educado, familiarizado con caminos difíciles y dispuesto a ayudar a quienes necesitaban desplazarse. Esa confianza acabaría formando parte de una historia de desapariciones, asesinatos y una explicación muy ligada al folclore.

La actividad de guía permitía a Romasanta acompañar a mujeres y niños que buscaban trabajo o pretendían emigrar lejos de sus aldeas. Había nacido el 18 de noviembre de 1809 en Regueiro, en el municipio orensano de Esgos, y había cambiado de oficio después de enviudar joven. Según las acusaciones, aprovechaba los viajes para atacar a algunas de las personas que confiaban en él. La justicia le atribuyó nueve asesinatos, aunque su confesión llegó a 13.

Durante años, algunas familias creyeron que sus parientes continuaban vivos porque recibían noticias que parecían proceder de ellos. Romasanta les mostraba cartas supuestamente escritas por los desaparecidos, aunque las había falsificado él mismo. Los cuerpos no aparecieron, una ausencia que dificultó reconstruir los hechos y que más tarde aprovecharía la defensa. Cuatro de las 13 muertes confesadas tampoco pudieron acreditarse plenamente durante el proceso.

Ante los jueces de Allariz, el acusado ofreció una explicación distinta de las que sugerían aquellos viajes y aquellas cartas. Sostenía que una bruja lo había maldecido y que se transformaba en lobo, especialmente durante las noches de luna llena. La metamorfosis podía prolongarse varios días, incluso una semana. Contó que, en la montaña de Couso, había encontrado dos lobos, sufrido convulsiones y rodado tres veces por el suelo antes de adoptar, según él, la forma animal. Aseguraba que después atacaba por hambre.

La figura del lobishome pertenecía desde mucho antes al imaginario gallego, dentro de una tradición europea que asociaba la transformación en lobo con maldiciones y hechicería. Las leyendas también incorporaron mordeduras, rituales y la influencia de la luna, aunque esta última no aparece de igual manera en todas las versiones.

Durante siglos, las acusaciones de licantropía llegaron a relacionarse con los procesos por brujería. En el caso de Romasanta, la creencia en los hombres lobo adquirió una dimensión judicial cuando el acusado aseguró todos los crímenes que había cometido era porque se escapaban a su control. Su relato también abrió la posibilidad de una explicación psiquiátrica: la licantropía clínica, un trastorno en el que una persona cree ser un lobo o convertirse en él aunque eso nunca llegue a pasar.

La defensa, encabezada por el abogado coruñés Manuel Rúa Figueroa, discutió tanto la suficiencia de las pruebas como la responsabilidad mental del acusado. Argumentó que confesar 13 muertes no demostraba por sí solo su autoría, especialmente cuando faltaban cadáveres y testimonios presenciales. También planteó que una persona convencida de convertirse en animal podía padecer una perturbación que afectara a sus recuerdos y a su imputabilidad. Rúa Figueroa publicó posteriormente la Reseña de la causa, en 1859, donde comentó todo el proceso judicial contra Romasanta.

La hipótesis de que Romasanta padecía una enfermedad mental recibió el apoyo de un supuesto especialista francés conocido como profesor Philips, cuya identidad sigue sin estar confirmada. Philips propuso estudiar a Romasanta como posible caso de enfermedad mental monomaníaca. Los médicos que lo examinaron, sin embargo, rechazaron que hubiera perdido la razón. El propio acusado afirmaba recordar sus actos después de recuperar la forma humana.

El 6 de abril de 1853 fue condenado en Allariz a morir por garrote vil y a pagar 1.000 reales por víctima. El expediente se conserva como Causa 1.778 en el Archivo Histórico de Galicia, aunque la condena nunca llegó a ejecutarse porque Isabel II conmutó la pena capital por cadena perpetua tras las gestiones para estudiar su supuesta licantropía. No consta que aquel examen médico riguroso llegara a realizarse.

Según las fuentes más repetidas, Romasanta murió el 14 de diciembre de 1863 en la prisión de Monte Hacho, en Ceuta, posiblemente por cáncer de estómago. El hombre que decía convertirse en lobo terminó sus días encarcelado por crímenes cometidos contra personas que habían confiado en él.

Pläne der Koalition: Wahlrechtsreform kommt kaum voran

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Neben dem Umbau der Sozialsysteme hat die schwarz-rote Koalition eine Reform angekündigt, bei der es für manche um die politische Existenz geht: Sie möchte das Wahlrecht ändern. Doch es geht nur langsam voran.

Wenn CSU und Linke im Bundestag an einem Strang ziehen, dann muss es sich schon um ein ganz besonderes Thema handeln. Beim Wahlrecht geht es für beide Parteien um die politische Existenz. Beide haben von der alten Regelung profitiert: Die CSU gewann viele Direktmandate und dazu Überhangmandate, weil sie über Erststimmen mehr Sitze erhielt, als ihr nach dem Zweitstimmenergebnis zustanden.

Die Linke kam 2021 mit drei gewonnenen Wahlkreisen in den Bundestag, obwohl ihr Zweitstimmenergebnis unter fünf Prozent lag.

Aber die Ampel-Regierung hat das Wahlrecht so geändert, dass bei der jüngsten Bundestagswahl nicht alle, die einen Wahlkreis gewonnen haben, auch ins Parlament kamen. CSU-Landesgruppenchef Alexander Hoffmann und Linken-Fraktionschef Sören Pellmann sind sich im Widerstand dagegen einig: Das Wahlrecht müsse sicherstellen, dass alle, die Mandate gewinnen, auch im Bundestag vertreten sind. CSU und Linkspartei haben gegen die Ampel-Änderungen vor dem Bundesverfassungsgericht geklagt.

Das hat zwar den Wegfall der Grundmandatsklausel beanstandet: Demnach zieht eine Partei mit weniger als fünf Prozent der Zweitstimmen trotzdem in den Bundestag ein, wenn sie mindestens drei Direktmandate geholt hat. Diese Regelung blieb bei der jüngsten Bundestagswahl bestehen.

Das Bundesverfassungsgericht wird das neue Wahlrecht in Teilen aufheben. mehr

Aber Karlsruhe hat im Kern das neue System bestätigt, bei dem allein die Zweitstimme über die Sitzanzahl entscheidet und ein Direktmandat nicht mehr automatisch den Einzug in den Bundestag garantiert. Die Folge: Nach der Bundestagswahl durften 23 Wahlkreisgewinnerinnen und -gewinner ihr Direktmandat nicht antreten; die meisten von ihnen gehören CDU und CSU an.

Durch den Wegfall von Überhang- und Ausgleichsmandaten wurde das Ziel der Ampel erreicht, die Zahl der Abgeordneten von 736 auf 630 zu senken. An der Verkleinerung wollen Union und SPD festhalten. Aber sie haben im Koalitionsvertrag vereinbart, das bestehende Bundestagswahlrecht so zu ändern, dass jeder Kandidat mit Erststimmenmehrheit in den Bundestag kommt, ohne dass der wieder anwächst.

Das ist kompliziert und bisher haben beide Seiten in der zuständigen Arbeitsgruppe keine Alternative zum von der Ampel eingeführten Wahlrecht gefunden. Nach den Worten von Bundeskanzler und CDU-Chef Friedrich Merz ist die schwarz-rote Koalition mittlerweile ein Jahr im Verzug. Die Zeit dränge, weil Fristen für eine notwendige Neuordnung der Wahlkreisgrenzen eingehalten werden müssten: "Das ist ein aufwändiger Prozess, der braucht Zeit, da müssen die Länder einbezogen werden und deswegen muss klar sein, mit welchem Wahlrecht wir bei der nächsten Bundestagswahl wählen." Weil die Union besonders betroffen ist, wächst der Druck in der Bundestagsfraktion.

"Es liegen genügend Vorschläge auf dem Tisch", sagte die Bundestagspräsidentin der dpa. mehr

Der Koalitionspartner SPD zieht grundsätzlich mit, hat es aber weniger eilig und setzt andere Prioritäten als die Union: Sozialdemokratinnen und -demokraten wollen vor allem dafür sorgen, dass im Bundestag künftig genauso viele Frauen wie Männer sitzen. Das sehen CDU und CSU mehrheitlich kritisch.

Derzeit liegt der Frauenanteil im Bundestag bei rund einem Drittel der Abgeordneten. Der Abgeordnete Johannes Fechner, der für die SPD in der Koalitionskommission sitzt, betont: "Wir sehen Handlungsbedarf darin, den Frauenanteil im Bundestag zu erhöhen. Und wir wollen auch dafür sorgen, dass jeder Wahlkreis mit einem Abgeordneten vertreten ist im Deutschen Bundestag."

Im neuen Bundestag werden weniger Frauen sitzen. Das liegt vor allem an den Wahlergebnissen von Union und AfD. mehr

Es geht etwas voran, sagt Fechner. Aber Ergebnisse sind weiter nicht in Sicht. CSU-Landesgruppenchef Hoffmann verweist auf den Zeitplan: "Bisher ist es nicht zur Umsetzung gekommen, weil der Koalitionspartner da bisher nicht mitgezogen hat beziehungsweise einen anderen Blick auf die Dinge hatte. Und das ist ein Thema, was jetzt im Herbst eine Rolle spielen muss."

Für Union und SPD ist das Wahlrecht eine Reformbaustelle, bei der es für einige Beteiligte ums Eingemachte geht. Ob die Koalition rechtzeitig liefern kann, ist unklar.

New evidence suggests spinosaurus spent most of its life underwater

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For millions of years, evolution helped animals make the transition from life in the ocean to life on land. But the journey did not always move in one direction. Many species eventually returned to the water, developing new adaptations that allowed them to thrive in aquatic environments.

Dozens of major groups of mammals and reptiles made this remarkable evolutionary reversal. Now, a new study from Yale University is shedding light on when and how these transitions occurred, as well as which ancient animals became fully aquatic and which continued to depend on land.

The findings were published in the journal Current Biology.

"These secondarily aquatic groups adapted in strikingly similar ways to their new aquatic home -- evolving flippers and a suite of other features that made them better swimmers," said lead author Caleb Gordon, who completed his doctorate at Yale's Graduate School of Arts and Sciences (GSAS) earlier this year and is now a postdoctoral researcher at the Florida Museum of Natural History. "As a result, they've become textbook examples of convergent evolution, which can tell us a lot about the processes driving and constraining adaptive change in response to similar environmental cues."

Using Ancient Fossils and AI to Reconstruct Evolution

To investigate these evolutionary changes, the researchers examined hundreds of fossil and modern specimens housed at the Yale Peabody Museum and dozens of other institutions worldwide. They collected extensive new data, including measurements (more than 11,000), photographs, and CT scans.

The team combined traditional paleontological techniques with machine learning models that account for evolutionary relationships. They also incorporated a statistical approach originally used by naval forces during World War II. Together, these methods offered a new way to investigate one of the most remarkable transitions in vertebrate evolution.

"Reconstructing the lives of extinct life forms in a scientifically rigorous way, as opposed to just telling stories, is a precise and delicate undertaking," said Bhart-Anjan Bhullar, senior author of the study. Bhullar is an associate professor of Earth and planetary sciences in Yale's Faculty of Arts and Sciences (FAS) and an associate curator of vertebrate paleontology and vertebrate zoology at the Peabody Museum.

"It requires a careful interweaving of data from modern organisms, of which our understanding is necessarily far deeper, and knowledge of these living organisms' genealogy relative to fossil forms," he said. "Caleb's work accomplishes all of this and more, and on a massive evolutionary scale."

For decades, scientists have disagreed about when particular groups of prehistoric animals became fully adapted to life underwater. The fossil record often provides incomplete or conflicting clues, making it difficult to determine whether an extinct species primarily lived on land, in water, or in both environments.

Some skeletal features may indicate an aquatic lifestyle, while others suggest that the same animal remained capable of moving and feeding on land. These contradictions have led researchers to different conclusions about how ancient species behaved.

"In these cases, paleontologists are often stuck, as different lines of evidence disagree about what the ancient animal was like," Gordon said.

To address this uncertainty, Gordon developed a method using relatively simple but effective machine learning models trained on living animals. By identifying relationships between skeletal proportions and known lifestyles, the models can estimate how extinct species used aquatic environments. They can also predict soft tissue adaptations, including swimming structures that rarely survive in fossils.

Spinosaurus May Have Spent Most of Its Life Underwater

One of the most striking findings concerns Spinosaurus, a giant prehistoric dinosaur whose relationship with water has generated considerable controversy.

Spinosaurus lived approximately 113 million to 94 million years ago in what is now northern Africa. Although scientists generally agree that it was associated with aquatic environments, exactly how it moved, hunted, and spent its time remains a subject of debate.

Some evidence points to an animal that frequently submerged itself and pursued prey underwater, much like seals or penguins. Other findings suggest a lifestyle closer to that of a modern heron, with Spinosaurus walking and searching for food along shorelines and in shallow water.

The Yale team's analysis provides new support for the more aquatic interpretation.

"Our results shed new light on how much time Spinosaurus spent submerged, which could support the underwater hunting view," Gordon said. "We confidently recovered highly aquatic habits for Spinosaurus, indicating that it spent the vast majority of its time submerged in the water."

Although the findings do not settle every question about how Spinosaurus hunted, they strengthen the case that this enormous predator spent much more of its life underwater than on land.

Ancient Marine Reptiles That Never Fully Left Land

The researchers reached a different conclusion about mesosaurs, small marine reptiles that lived between 290 million and 274 million years ago in regions now forming parts of South Africa and eastern South America.

Mesosaurs have long interested paleontologists because they gave birth to live young, a characteristic some researchers have interpreted as evidence of a fully aquatic existence. However, the new analysis suggests that their limbs were not adapted for a life spent entirely in water.

Instead, their skeletal proportions indicate that mesosaurs regularly moved between aquatic and terrestrial environments.

"They spent a lot of time on land, like a modern-day alligator or platypus does," Gordon said. "Mesosaurs didn't completely leave the land behind."

The findings also suggest a broader pattern among the earliest marine reptiles. According to the researchers, all Paleozoic marine reptiles (all marine reptiles around before the dinosaurs appeared) continued to return to land regularly. None appeared to have evolved a completely aquatic lifestyle, with even the most water adapted species remaining amphibious.

The researchers found that forelimb proportions were particularly useful for distinguishing these lifestyles. Using this information, their models could predict with more than 90% accuracy whether an animal possessed soft tissue flippers and whether it had highly or fully aquatic habits.

A World War II Technique Helps Solve an Evolutionary Mystery

An especially unusual part of the research involved adapting a statistical method developed during the 1940s.

"Caleb resurrected a statistical technique from a bygone era," said co-author Jacques Gauthier, a professor of Earth and planetary sciences at Yale and curator-in-charge for vertebrate paleontology at the Peabody Museum. "It was developed during WWII to estimate the probability that an approaching blip on the radar screen was an enemy plane. Caleb cleverly used it to get at whether or not an extinct animal had limbs modified for swimming."

By repurposing this approach, the researchers could assess the likelihood that an extinct species possessed specialized swimming adaptations, even when direct evidence of those features was missing.

The method could eventually help scientists investigate other major changes in evolutionary history. Potential applications include studying when human ancestors developed the ability to walk on two legs and how the dinosaur ancestors of birds evolved the ability to fly.

Because most prehistoric vertebrates are known primarily through their fossilized skeletons, reconstructing their behavior is one of paleontology's greatest challenges. Bones preserve valuable information, but they rarely provide a complete picture of how an animal lived.

Traditionally, scientists have interpreted particular skeletal features to make arguments about the lifestyles of extinct species. Those interpretations can differ significantly, especially when fossils are incomplete or several anatomical characteristics point toward different conclusions.

The new approach offers a more systematic way to evaluate that evidence, using patterns observed in living species to make testable predictions about animals that disappeared millions of years ago.

"Bones are all that is left to us of the vast majority of vertebrate animals that have lived on Earth," Bhullar said. "We want to know so very much about these creatures and the succession of lost worlds they inhabited, but we have so little on which to base our conclusions. From skeletal remains, people have tried to discern life habits, generally making an argument on the basis of one preferred set of features or another. Caleb's method removes the subjectivity from this process and allows the data to speak for themselves."

The study's additional co-authors are Lisa Freisem, a doctoral student at Yale's Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, and Christopher Griffin, an assistant professor of geosciences at Princeton University who previously worked as a postdoctoral fellow at Yale.
