Let’s face it: McDonald’s is everywhere. Whether you’re making a quick Micky D’s run to fuel up for a busy day or your body is craving the McNuggets you fell in love with as a kid, it’s normal to dine now and then at one of the most recognizable fast food chains on the planet.

The good news is that you’ll find some options at McDonald’s that will let you enjoy your favorite fast food restaurant without derailing your nutrition and weight loss progress. We’ve rounded up the 9 healthy McDonald’s food orders from breakfast through dinner so you always know what to reach for.

Yes, you can find healthier options at McDonald’s. It’s possible by choosing lower-calorie items and smaller portions. (1)

Fast food is often high in sodium, calories, and fat. Too many of these things can affect both your mental and physical health. That’s why moderation is key. If you crave those iconic fries or a classic burger, it’s okay to indulge once in a while. Making smart choices and simple swaps at fast food joints like McDonald’s means you can honor your cravings in a way that feels good for your mind and body. (2, 3, 4)

Here are some handy things to remember the next time you order at McDonald’s:

Katherine Basbaum is a registered dietitian who works with MyFitnessPal. We asked her what she looks for when considering the healthiest McDonald’s items.

“The healthiest options, in my view, tend to include the menu items that aren’t loaded with a ton of sauces and cheese as well as those with options for reduced serving sizes (4-piece instead of 10) and a lower calorie count (400ish and below),” Basbaum explains. (20)

Keeping that in mind, some of these picks are the healthiest food at McDonald’s that follow Basbaum’s guidelines. We’ve covered you for breakfast, lunch, and dinner so that you can enjoy McDonald’s anytime!

Snag healthy McDonald’s food for breakfast with a bowl of fruit and maple oatmeal. Oats have tons of health benefits since they’re high in antioxidants, are a source of whole-grain fiber, and can help lower cholesterol.

“When I think of healthy food at McDonald’s, their Fruit & Maple Oatmeal is at the top of my list. The main ingredients are fresh, whole foods that come together to create a warm, filling breakfast under 400 calories. The rolled oats provide fiber and complex carbohydrates, natural sweetness comes from the fresh and dried fruit, and there’s just a touch of light cream and brown sugar for a bit of richness,” Basbaum says.

For these reasons, oatmeal is considered one of the healthiest breakfast items to help you start your day. (5) This oatmeal has 320 calories, 4.5 grams of fat, and 6 grams of protein, making it an all-around healthy choice. (6)

If you like a savory fast-food breakfast, the original Egg McMuffin is one of the top McDonald’s healthier options to consider. It has 310 calories, 13 grams of fat, and 17 grams of protein.

This could be a better option if you’re counting macros, as it has 30 grams of carbs compared to oatmeal, which has 64. (6,7)

Thanks to the egg, cheese, and lean Canadian bacon, it also has a nice protein boost. In fact, at 17 grams of protein for just 310 calories, the Egg McMuffin is one of the highest-protein breakfast options at McDonald’s.(1,7)

McDonald’s chicken nuggets are iconic. If McNuggets are your jam, go for the 4-piece size, which has just 170 calories, 10 grams of fat, and 9 grams of protein, making it one of the lowest-calorie healthier options at McDonald’s on the menu. “Chicken options like the McNuggets,” notes Basbaum, “generally contain much less saturated fat than the options that go heavy on the beef and cheese.” (1,8)

And thanks to the low-calorie count, you can pair your nuggets with a side of small fries and still keep your meal under 500 calories. An order of small fries has 230 calories and 11 grams of fat, not including dipping sauces like ketchup. (8,9)

The Filet-O-Fish is a McDonald’s classic with 380 calories, 19 grams of fat, and 16 grams of protein. (10)

Even though it’s better to eat broiled or seared to save on fat, fish has several health benefits and is usually quite lean. You can ask for it without cheese or sauce to lower the fat content or enjoy it as is. (11,12)

When thinking about the lowest-calorie food at McDonald’s, you might be surprised to discover that the classic burger has just 250 calories. With the included toppings, it has nine grams of fat and 12 grams of protein, which isn’t too bad. It’s widely considered the healthiest burger at McDonald’s (1,13,15)

As with the nuggets, you can pair a burger with small fries and still come in under 500 calories. (9,13)

A side of apple slices is the healthiest food at McDonald’s, so feel free to pair it with anything else on the menu. One serving has just 15 calories, zero fat, and just 4 grams of carbs. When considering sides, it’s a much healthier choice than fries or other sweet treats. (1,9,14)

Indien: Der Mut der Frauen hinter den "Kakerlaken"-Protesten

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Trotz Polizeigewalt, Festnahmen und Online-Hetze gehen Frauen in Indien weiter auf die Straße. Sie lassen sich nicht einschüchtern und weigern sich, in die Opferrolle gedrängt zu werden.

Am 20. Juli machten sich Navkiran Natt und ihr Partner auf den Weg zum Büro des Magistrats in Neu-Delhi, um zu heiraten. Dort angekommen, trafen sie auf eine angespannte Lage: Die Polizei war aufmarschiert, um einen von Jugendlichen angeführten Marsch zum Parlament aufzulösen.

Die Mitarbeiter der Behörde ebenso wie die Trauzeugen des Paares, verspäteten sich, da der Weg durch Barrikaden versperrt war.

Die Protestbewegung, bekannt als "Cockroach Janta Party" (CJP), hatte den Marsch organisiert, um den Rücktritt des damaligen indischen Bildungsministers Dharmendra Pradhan zu fordern.

Die Demonstrierenden stießen auf erbitterten Widerstand der Sicherheitskräfte, die Schlagstöcke, Tränengas und Schrotflinten mit Projektil-Munition einsetzten, was zu Verletzungen und zahlreichen Festnahmen führte.

Knapp drei Monate später, am 10. Oktober, verließ Natt frühmorgens ihr Zuhause und verbrachte den Tag damit, die abgeriegelte indische Hauptstadt zu durchqueren – auf der Suche nach einem Protest, dem sie sich anschließen konnte.

"Der Erfolg dieses Protests bemisst sich nicht daran, wie viele Menschen Jantar Mantar erreichen", sagt sie gegenüber der DW – in Bezug auf den Protestort im Zentrum von Neu-Delhi.

"Der Erfolg liegt vielmehr darin, dass die Menschen trotz all der Barrikaden und der staatlichen Gewalt ihre Häuser verlassen, um sich dem Protest anzuschließen."

Die CJP entstand als satirische Online-Bewegung, nachdem der indische Oberste Richter Surya Kant in einer beiläufigen Bemerkung arbeitslose junge Menschen als "Kakerlaken" bezeichnet hatte. Der Name ist eine ironische Anspielung auf die Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) – die Partei von Premierminister Narendra Modi, die derzeit an der Macht ist.

Nachdem die Proteste der Bewegung Ende Juli zum Rücktritt von Bildungsminister Pradhan führten, fordert sie nun den Rücktritt des umstrittenen indischen Chef-Wahlleiters Gyanesh Kumar. Hintergrund ist die Streichung von mehr als 130 Millionen Namen aus den Entwürfen der Wählerverzeichnisse im ganzen Land.

Da sich die Führung der CJP in richterlich angeordnetem Gewahrsam befindet oder ihr die Inhaftierung droht, ist ein Name zum Synonym für die Proteste geworden: Die studentische Anführerin Neha Bora, die für sich das geschlechtsneutrale Pronomen "they/them" verwendet.

Bora ist vor der Polizei durch die Straßen gerannt und vor den Augen der Sicherheitskräfte auf Barrikaden geklettert, hat aus Polizeifahrzeugen heraus Parolen gerufen und gemeinsam mit CJP-Sprechern und anderen an Pressekonferenzen teilgenommen.

Sie ist Vorsitzende der linken Studierendenorganisation AISA, "All India Students' Association", der Studierendenorganisation der Kommunistischen Partei Indiens (Marxistisch-Leninistisch) – Liberation (CPI-ML).

"In Zeiten, die zutiefst enttäuschend, beängstigend und tyrannisch sind, versuchen die Menschen, irgendwoher Hoffnung zu schöpfen… Wenn diese Leute kämpfen, dann können wir vielleicht auch kämpfen", sagte Bora gegenüber der DW.

"Das ist eine Verantwortung, die man den Menschen gegenüber hat", fügte sie/er hinzu.

Die von der "Kakerlaken"-Gruppe angeführten Demonstrationen finden zudem in einer Zeit statt, in der insbesondere Frauen nach der Gruppenvergewaltigung einer 17-Jährigen Ende September in Neu-Delhi mehr Sicherheit und Selbstbestimmung für Frauen fordern.

Nach Angaben des "National Crime Records Bureau" wurden im Jahr 2024 insgesamt 441.534 Straftaten gegen Frauen registriert – das entspricht durchschnittlich 1.210 Fällen pro Tag. Experten weisen darauf hin, dass diese offiziellen Statistiken nur einen Teil der geschlechtsspezifischen Gewalt im Land abbilden.

Doch obwohl sie sich auf der Straße mit sexualisierten Übergriffen durch die Polizei und im Internet mit Hetzkampagnen konfrontiert sehen, wehren sich die Frauen und genderqueeren Personen, die die Bewegung anführen, dagegen, in die Opferrolle gedrängt zu werden.

"Der Slogan 'Beti Bachao' [Rettet die Tochter] zieht bei dieser Generation einfach nicht mehr. Die Forderung ist ganz klar: Betrachtet uns als politische Wesen, als freie Menschen", erklärte die Aktivistin Kavita Krishnan gegenüber der DW.

"Beti Bachao, Beti Padhao" (Rettet die Tochter, bildet die Tochter aus) ist ein zentrales Regierungsprogramm der BJP, das 2015 ins Leben gerufen wurde.

"Widerstand darf kein isoliertes Ereignis sein – kein einzelner großer Tag, nach dem man einfach zu seinem gewohnten Leben zurückkehrt", sagt Gurmehar Kaur gegenüber der DW.

Die 29-Jährige ist Autorin des Buchs "The Young and the Restless: Youth and Politics in India" über Jugendproteste in Indien.

Kaur erklärte, ihr politisches Engagement verschaffe ihr Zugang zu Ressourcen, mit denen die Bewegung unterstützt werden könne; so entstehe ein Sicherheitsnetz für Demonstrierende – "ungeachtet ihrer politischen Zugehörigkeit".

In einer Zeit, in der die Behörden hart gegen die Protestierenden vorgehen, sieht sie für sich und andere Mitglieder der Opposition eine klare Pflicht: "Präsenz zeigen und den eigenen Körper einsetzen."

Kaur berichtet, dass ihre männlichen Kollegen oft "sehr erfreut sind, wenn ihre Fotos in den Medien auftauchen". Für sie hingegen bedeute Aufmerksamkeit vor allem eines: Mord- und Vergewaltigungsdrohungen, Demütigungen und Misshandlungen.

Navkiran Natt, eine Aktivistin, die vor allem durch die Bauernproteste der Jahre 2020 und 2021 bekannt wurde, bestätigt Kaurs Erfahrungen.

"Wenn eine Frau einen Protest anführt, ist ihre Sexualität das erste Ziel von Angriffen", sagt sie. Auf Bildern in den sozialen Medien sind Demonstrierende zu sehen, deren Kleidung bei Auseinandersetzungen mit der Polizei zerrissen wurde.

Offizielle Stellen in Delhi wiesen die Vorwürfe der Misshandlung zurück und erklärten, die weiblichen Demonstrierenden seien "würdevoll" behandelt worden.

Die Studierendenvertreterin Bora widersprach dieser Darstellung der Polizei. Sie wies darauf hin, dass für die große Zahl an weiblichen Demonstrierenden nur sehr wenige Polizeibeamtinnen zur Verfügung standen.

"Mehrere Demonstrierende kamen, um mich zu schützen und zu verhindern, dass ich festgenommen werde", berichtete sie.

Die Behörden haben ein Strafverfahren gegen eine Journalistin eingeleitet, die einen hochrangigen Polizeibeamten in Delhi der sexuellen Belästigung beschuldigt hatte.

Das Verfahren stützt sich auf ein indisches Gesetz, das eigentlich dazu dienen soll, Straftaten gegen Angehörige der sogenannten "Scheduled Castes" (registrierte Kasten) und "Scheduled Tribes" (registrierte Stämme) zu verhindern. Nach Angaben der Behörden werden die Vorwürfe der Journalistin selbst derzeit untersucht.

Zudem kursieren im Internet zahlreiche KI-generierte, sexualisierte Bilder von protestierenden Frauen, die nachträglich in Fotos von Demonstrationen eingefügt wurden.

Dies hat die Protestierenden nicht davon abgehalten, auf die Straße zu gehen. Die Behörden gingen offenbar davon aus, dass sexuelle Demütigungen die Frauen abschrecken würden, so die Aktivistin Krishnan. Doch "diese Generation betrachtet sexuelle Gewalt nicht als etwas, das mit Scham behaftet ist."

Diesmal jedoch, so Krishnan, gehe es bei der Wut "nicht um ein männlich geprägtes Ehrverständnis." Es gehe um "Würde und Freiheit... Frauen sehen sich in jedem Land mit verschiedensten Problemen konfrontiert – da bilden indische Frauen keine Ausnahme", fügte sie hinzu.

Laut Krishnan sind die von jungen Menschen angeführten Proteste eine Reaktion auf eine breitere globale Welle des "zivilisatorischen und kulturellen Nationalismus". Dieser nutzt Geschlechterfragen und eine Rhetorik des "Schutzes" – etwa von Töchtern, Familien oder Traditionen –, um Unterstützung für eine Politik gegen Minderheiten, Einwanderer und Trans- Personen zu mobilisieren.

"Hören Sie sich die Rhetorik von Donald Trump, Viktor Orbán oder der Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) an – es ist genau dasselbe", sagt sie mit Blick auf die politische Lage in den USA und Europa.

"Wenn man betrachtet, was in Indien geschieht, versteht man vielleicht besser, was im eigenen Land vor sich geht." Diese jungen Menschen", so sagt sie, lassen sich nicht länger täuschen. Sie "kämpfen für eine Sprache der Zukunft, eine Sprache, die Hoffnung birgt."

Verwerten statt Verbrennen – Kopenhagen will Windeln recyceln

Windeln füllen einen von zehn Abfallsäcken. Dänemark will nun gebrauchte Windeln wiederverwerten. Keine leichte Aufgabe.

Was hat Kopenhagen vor? Am Montag hat die Hauptstadt Dänemarks begonnen, separate Abfalleimer rund um Mehrfamilienhäuser und Kitas aufzustellen. Dort können Eltern und Betreuer gebrauchte Windeln gesondert entsorgen. Später sollen diese recycelt werden, statt wie bisher verbrannt. Ab Februar sollen die separaten Abfalleimer auch bei Altersheimen installiert werden. Die Behörden Kopenhagens hoffen rund 90 Prozent der Materialen, aus denen die Windeln bestehen, wiederzuverwerten. In den nächsten zwei Jahren sollen so 5000 Tonnen Abfall eingespart werden.

Wie gross ist das Problem mit dem Abfall? Die Menge: Wegwerfwindeln machen zehn Prozent des Haushaltsabfalls aus. Pro Tag werden in der Schweiz eine Million Windeln weggeworfen. «Wenn man alle diese Windeln aneinanderreihen würde, dann wäre das eine Strecke von Zürich bis nach Mailand», sagt Pascal Lago. Der SRF-Wirtschaftsredaktor hat bereits zum Thema Windeln recherchiert und darüber berichtet. Die Menge an Abfall entstehe auch, weil ein Kleinkind im Schnitt 5000 Windeln verbrauche, bevor es lerne, aufs WC zu gehen.

95 Prozent aller Windeln in der Schweiz sind Wegwerfwindeln. Stoffwindeln machen 3 Prozent aus, und es gibt noch andere Nischen, wie kompostierbare Windeln.

Ein Kind braucht durchschnittlich 5000 Windeln, bis es selber aufs WC kann. Das entspricht einer Tonne Abfall. Pro Kind geben Eltern 3500 Franken für Windeln aus.

Welche Materialien können aus Windeln gewonnen werden? «Eine Windel ist ein Verbund aus Kunststoff und Zellstoff», sagt Anne Zeck. Die Chemikerin am Naturwissenschaftlichen und Medizinischen Institut der Universität Tübingen hat selbst nachgeforscht, wie Windeln recycelt werden könnten. Aus dem Kunststoff könnten letztlich Rohöl und Plastik gewonnen werden, aus dem Zellstoff eine Zuckerlösung oder sogar Biogas.

Wie ist die Recycling-Situation in der Schweiz? Rein technisch betrachtet, sei Recycling hierzulande möglich, sagt SRF-Redaktor Pascal Lago. Bisher gibt es aber keine Recyclinganlage für Windeln. Die Wiederverwertung der Windeln ist mit einem enormen Aufwand verbunden, weil diese aus vielen verschiedenen Stoffen bestehen. Sie zu trennen, zu transportieren, zu lagern und von den Fäkalien zu säubern, braucht sehr viel Energie und Infrastruktur. Laut Pascal Lago lässt sich deshalb auch die Ökobilanz schwer berechnen. It has got the same cheerful cobalt exterior but no furniture, customers, or meatballs inside, only racks and heat. “Last week, Trump eliminated the, what was it? Oh yeah, the emissions level requirements for these power plant. For people like us, that basically screws us. We don’t have those protections anymore.” (The Trump EPA had recently exempted data centers from publicly disclosing their emissions.)

Medeiros and her neighbor Greg Pirio, 75, a government contractor who specializes in foreign aid, are two of a number of neighbors who have thrown themselves into protesting the Vantage plant, attending community meetings and town halls, doing press, creating systems for monitoring noise and air quality, and petitioning their local representatives.

But the center has also taken its toll, they say, on their bodies. Both have gone to doctors and Pirio twice to the ER, with extremely dry eyes that they believe were caused by the plant. (After an hour, my own eyes were throbbing, and I was blinking for tears to relieve the irritation.) For more vulnerable people in the area, the effects have been even worse. According to Medeiros and other neighbors, one family’s four children have all developed respiratory problems as a result. Medeiros’ voice is a rasp, now, as well, which she attributes to the centers.

They aren’t just imagining things. A recent report, “Health-Informed Computing: Estimating and Addressing the Public Health Impact of Data Centers,” modeled emissions from data center electricity and on-site generator usage, projecting health risks and costs particularly in host communities. One of the key culprits is the fine particulate matter these centers create.

But that’s not their only threat. Their constant whining noises should be understood as noise pollution, which the World Health Organization has found to cause high blood pressure, heart disease, and sleep disturbances. (Two of the people I spoke to mentioned sleep disturbances since the centers opened near their homes.) Hearing damage is a possibility if the roar of the center goes above 85 decibels—and the constant sound, all day, near schools has also been connected to lower educational outcomes, according to a forthcoming study from researchers at Brown University. How would the football team I watched, playing at a high school field hemmed in by an Amazon data center complex, be affected?

As Medeiros says, “It’s not an escape to get outside anymore.”

On the upside, Loudoun County has become a symbol for how these centers have given rise to new assemblies of people fighting back. These would-be rebels included two middle-aged men who worked in tech, who were clad in polo shirts, one whom I met overlooking the giant pool at his housing development, and a couple employed in real estate and construction that own two vintage Jaguar cars.

Another of these unlikely mavericks is Ben Keethler. Wearing one of those polo shirts, his hair close-cropped, the 54-year-old father of three co-owns an IT business that often serves federal agencies. The night I met him, after he had returned from ferrying his 12-year-old to golf practice, he was quick to say he was “skeptical of sensationalism,” which is why he casts some allegations of data center health consequences as insufficiently substantiated. Nevertheless, he did add that he was no fan of how closely two data center campuses abut his own housing development in Ashburn, Virginia, looming over the $1.5 and $2 million five-bedroom homes and the association’s man-made pond. “This should not happen,” he says, gesturing at the data center across the water. “What was once a beautiful view of a lake is now a beautiful view of Microsoft.”

It’s “invaded us,” Keethler says, before quickly shifting gears, noting the tax revenue that has been raised and the quality athletic complexes that now exist thanks to Big Data. We get closer to the data center in the moonlight: I can hear it buzzing like hundreds of air conditioners, its klieg lights blaring at 8:30 on a weeknight. Keethler’s vibe shifts. “I’ve told them not to leave the lights on like this!” he says. We walk right by the house in his neighborhood that’s closest to the data center, a mere 140 feet away, behind a wood fence and fir trees.

There are now over at least 3,000 operational facilities like this across the country, that account for 5 percent of the United States electric usage, and by 2030 they could amount to up to 12 percent, the amount of yearly electricity consumed by New York City—times 12.

To me, the growth and ubiquity of the data centers embody the theorist Mark Fisher’s idea of “science fiction capital.” The centers are avatars of tech monopolies, which calculate probabilities and promise surplus value in the future but also threaten humans’ jobs and sense of meaning in the present. According to an essay by Maha Hosain Aziz, a professor of international relations at NYU, an “AI Precariat” is now being created, whose jobs have been made contingent or volatile by the large language models.

Data centers and the AI they enable are sci-fi enough to have turned public opinion decisively against them. For instance, the House just approved a federal bill to keep AI data-center power costs from being put back on citizens’ electricity bills. The rare nearly unanimous congressional vote reflects the immense negative outcry over hyperscaling of physical computing infrastructure, although even this watered-down victory is sure to be eventually voted down by Trump.

The function of these centers is to know everything about us, analyze, and sell our information, but what do we know about them? As an early Internet hub near the nation’s capital, Loudoun County first saw data centers in the late 1990s. But the boom really began when, with the takeoff of cloud computing, county leaders aggressively courted tech companies with economic and regulatory incentives to build data centers in its communities. Now the AI race has only driven up demand for more facilities, and quickly. And their transmission lines, with towers sometimes as tall as 185 feet, can be viewed from their gardens and heard from their porches: While the area is called Data Center Alley, it might as well have been called Data Center Boulevard, given its wild sprawl.

I started to think of their uncanny physical presence as a Toxic Sublime. The Sublime, as it was defined by Edmund Burke, is a larger structure or natural occurrence that produces the “strongest emotion which the mind is capable of feeling,” almost always pain rather than pleasure. I feel ripples of the Toxic Sublime when I look at these dehumanized and overwhelmingly large server farms with their corporate names emblazoned—say, AWS or Equinix—that seem to have no beginning or end, appearing among other overblown elements of the suburbs, like golf courses, high-voltage power lines, and innumerable, evil-looking, perimeter fences. They look as if they could devour us in their Brutalist jaws. Their aesthetic is the literal visualization of how humans have colonized nature.

They are clearly villainous. But to complicate matters and our complicity in these monstrosities of hyperscaling, by some estimates AI only makes up as much as 20 percent of data center energy usage, while 80 percent flows toward other stuff that isn’t AI, that many of more of us rely on, like cloud storage and Web hosting.

Thus, for Vicky Hu, longtime resident of Ashburn and one of the accidental activists of Loudoun County, the enemy isn’t AI at all but rather the companies who want to build on her property. Her self-created dream house in Ashburn, Virginia, has 200 orchids. She also has a large outdoor garden, including an elaborate set of vegetable patches with statues of birds in it. All of it is now threatened, though, by a 185-foot-tall data center transmission line.

She takes me on a walk around her property, where the planned pole and attendant line that would serve a nearby data center would be built. Hu and her family have tied pink bands to the trees that would have to be cut down to make room for the power line, as well as yellow caution tape to show where it would stand, 122 feet from her home: The yard now looks like a set from Law & Order: SVU.

Hu is a real estate broker and a contractor, who runs her own construction company. “When Dominion told me about the zoning and construction, I told them to zip it because they’re thinking I don’t know anything,” says Hu. “I’ve been [working in construction] for 27 years.”

Hu and others discuss which local leaders have rallied to the cause and which haven’t. Again, the breakdown doesn’t always adhere to political party here either. Juli Briskman, a member of Loudoun County’s Board of Supervisors, is planning to challenge fellow Democrat Phyllis Randall for board chair, on the grounds that Randall has been “pro–data center” and insufficiently critical. Briskman has pushed hard for more restrictions to data center growth.

AI experts like Edward Ongweso Jr., cohost of the podcast This Machine Kills, see the anti–data center cross-partisan coalitions as more than just people joining together in NIMBY-ified hatred. They are also angry at AI itself as well as how they feel like they’ve been kept in the dark about the tech firms’ machinations: “how these companies plan the centers, what their local governments have offered the data centers, what the negotiations were like.” In an age of multiple varieties of insecurity and instability, we can feel as if we are losing the narratives of our lives, especially as Big Tech and its oligarchs have all the power and agency. No one else seems to have a say in determining how these machines will change the future.

To take back their towns and suburbs, activists want to see more pauses on data center creation as well as better environmental and noise protections for people in places like Loudoun County, where the buildings drone on.

For Medeiros, being so close to the Vantage 2 center has become so intolerable that she and her partner are now planning to move to Ecuador. But if they want to sell the house first, tough luck. Jessica and her neighbors worry that their property value has fallen. The reason for the devaluation is clear: the ominous center and its endless, inhuman hum.

Massive study of 460,000 people challenges ultra-processed food cancer fears

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A massive study involving more than 460,000 Americans is challenging assumptions about ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer. Researchers found that eating more ultra-processed food (UPF) was not associated with a higher overall incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the findings revealed an important distinction: Some types of ultra-processed foods were linked to lower cancer rates, while others were associated with higher rates.

The research, led by Erikka Loftfield of the National Cancer Institute and published October 15 in the open-access journal PLOS Medicine, suggests that the health effects of ultra-processed foods may depend heavily on what those foods actually contain.

Ultra-processed foods account for more than half of the calories consumed by American adults. These products have attracted increasing scrutiny because of their potential connections to chronic diseases, including colorectal cancer (CRC). Yet the category includes a remarkably diverse assortment of foods, making it difficult to judge their health effects as a single group.

For example, some ultra-processed whole grain and dairy products have been associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer, while processed meats have been linked to a higher risk. This raises an important question about whether the level of processing alone is a useful indicator of a food's potential health effects.

A Massive Study Tracks Cancer Risk for Nearly 19 Years

To investigate these differences, researchers examined health and dietary records from 461,682 Americans who were between 50 and 71 years old when they joined the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study in 1995 and 1996.

At the beginning of the study, participants completed questionnaires describing how frequently they consumed different foods. Researchers used these responses to estimate each person's intake of ultra-processed foods.

Participants were then tracked through 2018, with a median follow-up period of 18.8 years. This allowed the team to examine how dietary patterns were associated with the development of colorectal cancer over nearly two decades.

The results showed no overall association between total ultra-processed food consumption and colorectal cancer incidence. In other words, participants who consumed more ultra-processed foods did not, as a group, experience higher rates of colorectal cancer.

However, a closer examination of individual food categories revealed a much more complicated picture.

Some Ultra-Processed Foods Linked to Lower Cancer Risk

The researchers found that higher consumption of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium from ultra-processed food sources was associated with a lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

Meat from ultra-processed sources showed the opposite pattern, with greater consumption associated with higher cancer incidence.

The team also examined what might happen when ultra-processed foods were replaced with less processed alternatives. Their analysis found that substituting minimally processed foods for 10% of ultra-processed food intake by weight was associated with a 2% lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

These findings suggest that the nutritional characteristics of individual foods may be more informative than simply classifying everything as ultra-processed. Foods placed in the same broad category can have very different associations with cancer risk.

Why the Findings Come With Important Limitations

Despite the study's large size and lengthy follow-up period, the researchers caution that its conclusions may not apply equally to everyone.

Most participants were older, white Americans, meaning the findings may not accurately reflect the experiences of younger adults or more racially and ethnically diverse populations.

Another important limitation involves how dietary information was collected. Participants reported their eating habits only once, at the beginning of the study. Researchers therefore could not account for changes in individual diets over the following decades.

The availability and variety of ultra-processed foods also increased during that time. As a result, the initial dietary assessments may have underestimated participants' actual consumption during the study period.

Because the research was observational, it also cannot establish whether particular foods directly increase or decrease cancer risk.

Rather than treating all ultra-processed foods as equally harmful, the findings support evaluating foods according to their nutritional content and specific health associations.

The results also align with existing dietary recommendations for colorectal cancer prevention, which encourage eating whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium-rich foods while limiting processed meat.

"In this large population of older US adults, we did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence; however, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention such that higher whole grain and dairy but lower meat intakes were associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence. "

Ultimately, the study highlights why the label "ultra-processed" may not tell the whole story. When it comes to colorectal cancer, the kinds of foods people eat appear to matter more than their processing category alone.

In 1871, this nightmarish ‘Creeping Baby Doll’ was all the rage

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 21, 2026 1:13 PM EDT

Already looking for children’s toys for the holidays? Are you also a big Halloween fan? Well, do we have a  gift for you! Allow us to introduce the present no child could ever love (unless maybe they were possessed by a demon or a big fan of The Nightmare Before Christmas): The Creeping Baby Doll!

This nightmarish 1871 creation is everything you don’t want in a children’s toy. It’s noisy. It moves—seemingly propelled by painted plaster baby arms and legs. And it’s way creepy.

The mechanical baby doll has two legs, two arms, and a head all made of painted plaster (which is undoubtedly a bit chipped after more than 150 years). These plaster bits are really the only thing that actually look somewhat like a baby. The rest of the doll’s body is made up of brass clockwork. Some sort of dress or outfit would’ve likely covered its brass body back in the day.

Both the arms and legs are attached to gears that make the plaster limbs move so it looks like the baby doll is crawling (hence, the name—though we would argue there’s perhaps a reason they went with “creeping” instead of “crawling”). The entirety of the toy moves using two toothed wheels hidden under the baby’s body. A flat piece of wood covers the baby’s back to hide the mechanical instruments that make up the baby’s insides.

New York City-based inventor George Pemberton Clarke developed the horrific toy as an improvement to his colleague’s own creeping baby doll. That’s right, there were two! His associate, inventor Robert J. Clay, had filed the patent for his own creeping baby doll on March 14, 1871. Clarke later filed his patent on August 29th of that same year. They really thought this toy would be a hit apparently.

To be fair, there’s a long history of inventors trying to mimic human movement in machines. As far back as ancient Greece, there are stories of human mechanical models dumbfounding awestruck audiences. In the ninth century, Persian alchemist Jabir ibn Hayyan created moving statues. In the 10th century, Chinese engineer Yan Shi (nicknamed the Artificer) created life-size, human-like, moving figurines to the delight of King Mu of Zhou.

Mechanical automata reached their heyday during the Enlightenment and into the Victorian age. Automated sailing ships decorated tables, mechanical birds sang from gilded boxes, and silver swans floated down peaceful streams.

But even when you take into account the long history of automata, it is hard to look at Clarke’s Creeping Baby Doll and not think it’s a horror movie prop. In fact, it kind of was.

According to a Boing Boing, the antique toy actually inspired a very obscure 1972 horror film called Miriam: The Wind-Up Killer Doll. The film follows a group of friends who find a vintage crawling baby doll at a garage sale. But, in true horror movie fashion, an evil force possesses the baby doll, and the demonic doll starts killing off the friends one by one. (We didn’t say it was an original scary movie.)

That said, maybe we don’t actually recommend getting your favorite child Clarke’s Creeping Baby Doll. That decision may come back to haunt you.

"I am giving you money!" Nvidia's CEO wants every country to build its own AI

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is crisscrossing Europe with a multibillion-dollar checkbook and a grand ambition: Make his company the indispensable backbone of the global AI economy.

Why it matters: As fears of an AI bubble rattle Wall Street, Huang is working to spread the boom beyond the U.S. and China — courting governments, bankrolling startups and cultivating massive new markets for Nvidia's chips.

The big picture: The godfather of AI is trying to single-handedly build a new market for Nvidia chips, betting that governments and institutions around the world will want their own, highly customized data centers running open models.

  • If it works, it could shore up demand for Nvidia chips just as another big group of customers — neoclouds — appear financially shaky.

Driving the news: Part showman, part salesman, Huang has spent the past week preaching the gospel of AI across Europe ahead of a keynote Wednesday at Nvidia's flagship conference in Berlin.

  • In France, Huang announced investments in four startups and joked, whisky glass in hand, that he's "the biggest VC in Europe." President Emmanuel Macron welcomed him to Paris, while France's economy minister compared the AI revolution to the Gold Rush.
  • In Britain, Huang channeled Oprah Winfrey while announcing stakes in five startups. "I am giving you money! And I am giving you money! And I am giving you money!" he shouted from the stage of a converted London church.
  • In Sweden, Huang unveiled a supercomputer backed by the country's industrial elite, likening Nvidia's technology to a "time machine" capable of compressing years of scientific research into days.
  • In Germany, Huang challenged Chancellor Friedrich Merz to loosen Europe's regulatory instincts. "There's nothing to regulate if the industry doesn't thrive," he warned Monday. "Give Germany a chance. Give this industry a chance."

Zoom in: At the heart of Huang's global strategy is "sovereign AI" — the idea that every nation should build and control its own AI infrastructure, data and models, rather than depend on U.S. or Chinese tech giants.

  • In addition to roughly $10 billion in investments in Europe, Nvidia is helping build national AI ecosystems across Saudi Arabia, South Korea, India, Malaysia and Africa.
  • For Huang, the prize is a vast new class of customers, including entire countries that see AI infrastructure as an economic and national security imperative.

Between the lines: Huang's global offensive comes at a precarious moment for the AI industry, with Wall Street increasingly questioning whether the technology's enormous promise can justify its astronomical costs.

  • Shares and bonds of Nvidia-backed CoreWeave and other "neocloud" providers have plunged, fueling fears that the companies borrowing billions to buy Nvidia's chips may struggle to pay their debts.
  • Investors are also scrutinizing a circular flow of money in which Nvidia helps finance the very companies buying its products, raising questions about how much demand is self-sustaining.

What to watch: Huang has emerged as the AI industry's chief cheerleader during periods of market turmoil, countering predictions of a bubble with assurances that demand is still accelerating.

  • His Berlin keynote offered another chance to calm investors by arguing that the next wave of AI spending will come from governments treating the technology as a national necessity, not a speculative bet.
  • Nvidia announced a number of initiatives oriented around the "AI factories" theme at the Berlin event, including a design for modular computing pods that can be quickly deployeds, and a "full-stack" option to offer "sovereign" AI solutions by combining the company's chips and a new family of open-weight models.

The bottom line: Countries racing to secure their independence in the AI age may find themselves more reliant than ever on one American company.
