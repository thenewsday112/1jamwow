Reason No. 1 to watch “Wicker” is the natural intrigue of seeing Alexander Skarsgård molded out of twigs: face, arms, pecs and abs — and yes, there’s also a shot of that.

Reason No. 2 is that the married filmmaking team of Alex Huston Fischer and Eleanor Wilson have made a mischievous, crowd-pleasing folktale with several sweet beats of romance. Their wicker man isn’t an object of punishment, like the one that made Nicolas Cage flail around screaming about bees. His creator, an unnamed basket-weaver (Peter Dinklage), braided him as a mate for a lonely virgin, a fisherwoman (Olivia Colman) who stomps around her rustic village smelling like sushi and tossing her head back in an Eliza Doolittle guffaw.

In public, the fisherwoman acts tough. But alone in her candlelit cottage, she’s visibly weary of her joyless routine. The liveliest noise in her spinster hovel is the sound she makes using the chamber pot. Once married, however, she wakes up the whole town screaming with delight; her wicker spouse is the handy type who breaks the bed at night and fixes it in the morning. His competence is a major turn-on.

Fischer and Wilson’s bawdy fable is predominately about how this flammable hunk makes the rest of the villagers burn up with jealousy. He’s not like the other men — he’s better. The time period is vaguely medieval and the accents British, save for Skarsgård, who speaks in a flat, neutral exhale, the sound of breeze riffling through straw. Meanwhile, Dinklage’s pizzicato mutterings always sound like he’s about to burst into song.

Based on “The Wicker Husband,” a 10-page short story by Ursula Wills-Jones, “Wicker” is pitching itself as kooky smut, which it is, for about 15 worthwhile minutes. Romantasy is a hot-selling genre. Forget Fabio: People are reading bodice-rippers about sexy dinosaurs, gargoyles, aliens, orcs and even trees. (Those curious to explore beyond mammalian biology should check out Chuck Tingle, the author of “I’m Gay for My Living Billionaire Jet Plane.”)

That audience will want more scenes than they get of Skarsgård swooping Colman to bed as the sound design adds an array of naughty creaks. “Wicker’s” louder point is societal. It’s “Frankenstein” if the rabble wanted to be (or be with) the monster — and a parable with plenty to say about today.

Shareholders of both companies would have to approve the deal.

“I think self-driving tech is the most important thing for Tesla to solve in its current form,” Brauer said. “If it’s tied to SpaceX ... they can share expenses and they become one big company.”

The World Is Set to Blow Past 1.5 Degrees Celsius of Warming and the Consequences Could Be Devastating

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Under the most optimistic scenario, global warming would rise 1.7 to 1.8 degrees above pre-industrial levels, despite renewables expanding considerably over the last decade.

This article originally appeared on Inside Climate News, a nonprofit, non-partisan news organization that covers climate, energy and the environment. Sign up for their newsletter here.

A new report from the United Nations Environment Programme suggests that limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels is no longer a realistic outcome, even under the most ambitious emissions-reduction scenarios.

The report’s lowest-warming scenario, which would require steep cuts in greenhouse gas emissions, would only limit peak global warming to 1.7 to 1.8 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels.

The 2015 Paris Agreement, adopted by almost every country in the world, set a goal of keeping global warming below 2 degrees Celsius (3.6 degrees Fahrenheit) while pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5 degrees. The United States has withdrawn from the agreement twice under President Donald Trump, most recently last year.

The report’s projections do not mean the world should abandon the 1.5 degree target, said Inger Andersen, UNEP’s executive director, at a press briefing.

“Every additional fraction of a degree matters, and every additional year of warming above 1.5 will increase climate impacts such as deadly floods, heatwaves, wildfires that have plagued nations this year,” Andersen said.

Robert Kopp, a climate scientist and professor at Rutgers University in New Jersey, said the report’s findings were not surprising. Even in 2015, he said, the 1.5 degree target was at the edge of what was considered feasible.

Reaching the report’s lowest warming scenario would require emissions to fall by 18 percent by 2030, 45 percent by 2035 and 69 percent by 2040, relative to 2025 levels. It would also require removing substantial amounts of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, an effort that would likely involve not only reforestation, but also the rapid development of carbon-capture technologies that remain in the early stages of development.

Meanwhile, climate impacts are mounting, putting pressure on efforts to adapt to global warming. Western Europe saw thousands of heat-related deaths last spring and summer, a period which encompassed four of the region’s 10 most severe heat waves since 1950.

Still, the report highlights the expansion of renewable energy as an area of progress. Despite what Jan Minx, head of the Evidence for Climate Solutions working group at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, described as “bleak” predictions for global warming, the growth of renewable energy capacity, particularly wind and solar, has outpaced expectations. Minx co-authored a chapter in the report.

Global renewable electricity capacity expanded three times faster than was anticipated in 2015, according to the report, while solar photovoltaic capacity grew seven times faster.

Setu Pelz, a research scholar at the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis who co-authored a chapter in the report, said countries must sustain that momentum to triple renewable capacity by 2030, a priority in keeping with Paris Agreement targets.

Global emissions in 2025 were comparable to those a year prior—a “welcome divergence from a growth trend of past decades,” Anne Olhoff, the director of the UNEP Copenhagen Climate Centre and the report’s lead author, said at a press briefing.

But the expansion of clean energy has yet to drive down fossil fuel emissions. According to the report, emissions from the combustion of oil, gas and coal continue to rise.

“We provide some evidence that can inform the discussions about 2030, 2035 and 2040 targets in these sectors, so that we can see the same level of ambition in the fossil fuel transition as we have seen in the real renewable capacity scale-up,” said Pelz.

Released annually, UNEP’s emissions gap report is part of a growing body of research warning that current climate policies fall short of what is needed to meet international temperature targets. Under existing policies, global warming could reach 2.8 degrees by 2100—far above the Paris Agreement’s 2-degree threshold.

When asked what a world that is more than 2 degrees Celsius warmer than pre-industrial levels might look like, Andersen referenced recent extreme weather events.“Think of the heat wave … in much of Europe last year, think of the wildfires that we are seeing in Scandinavia as an example, think of droughts and harvest failures,” she said.

“Think of that happening much more frequently.”

The Fight for Educational Equality Is Also a Fight for a Just Supreme Court

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The court has severely weakened our ability to make an education system that fulfills the promise of Brown.

Last month, a piece by New York Times journalist Nikole Hannah-Jones sparked conversations across America about race, education, and parenting. In the piece, Hannah-Jones described in painstaking—and often painful— detail the consequences of the deliberate decision she and her husband made to send their daughter to a mostly Black, lower-income, underperforming New York City public school rather than a school with better educational prospects and, perhaps, fewer Black students. Hannah-Jones’s story is personal, but it also represents one that Black parents in the United States have faced for generations: How do we make the best choices for our children in an education system that has never kept its promise to them?

For Black parents, the question of where to send our children to school has never been simply a matter of parental preference. It has been shaped by government decisions regarding where we are allowed to live, how resources are allocated, how school districts are drawn, and ultimately what the Supreme Court of the United States considers to be a fair and just education for our kids.

While much of the current debate has boiled down to scrutiny over which choices are right for Black parents, it is imperative that we ask the deeper question running through Hannah-Jones’s piece: Why has any Black parent been forced to make such consequential trade-offs in the first place?

Long before the Legal Defense Fund, the organization I lead, won the Brown v. Board of Education case in 1954, ending state-sponsored racial segregation in public schools, Black Americans understood education as essential to freedom. Immediately after Emancipation, Black communities got to work building schools, training teachers, and raising money to invest in educational pursuits because they knew this would help smooth their rocky path to full citizenship.

In doing so, Black communities were helping to establish the very infrastructure of universal public education. Across the South, according to the National Park Service, newly enfranchised Black voters and elected officials fought diligently “to create the frameworks for public education in the 1860s and 1870s.” In 1868, Black state legislators in South Carolina helped pass laws that created one of the first statewide systems of public schools in the nation.

Brown was a constitutional milestone in the ongoing struggle to ensure that Black children could receive a quality education in the same public school system their ancestors and communities worked to build. Yet, over 70 years later, Black families must still navigate the education system of a country that fails to make it real, too often forcing them into choosing between their child’s psychological safety and the quality of their educational opportunities.

That’s in part because, in the decades after Brown, the Supreme Court has repeatedly weakened the structures that might have lessened the need for Black families to consider these trade-offs. As a result, our public schools continue to struggle with hyper-segregation.

Take 1973’s San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez. In that case, the court upheld a Texas school funding system that overly relied on local property taxes, despite the fact that such a system produced significant differences in resources available to different districts due to state-sponsored racial residential segregation and its residual effects. This ruling, in effect, failed all students seeking government protections from an unequally funded school system, and was reinforced by the fact that education is not a fundamental right under the United States Constitution.

Nearly 30 years later, in Alexander v. Sandoval, the court held that individuals and other impacted parties could not use civil rights statutes, like Title VI, to challenge policies and practices, including those in the education system, that disproportionately harm certain children on account of race.

And in 2006’s notorious Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1, the Supreme Court cannibalized its own legacy by striking down valiant efforts by Seattle school districts to maintain racially diverse schools through parent-led initiatives.

Taken together, these decisions cut off avenues to enact true change and fulfill Brown’s promise, instead placing the burden on families to navigate a defective system. That is the true tension at the heart of the school-choice debate that Hannah-Jones’s confessional conjures. And that is why LDF is fighting to advance policies that aim to expand educational opportunities across the country, including in New York, where we represent a multiracial coalition of students seeking an equal chance to compete for admission to New York City’s specialized high schools.

The broader debate over the future of the Supreme Court cannot be separated from the debate over the future of public education. The court, ironically, once described public education as “the very foundation of good citizenship.” But it has issued critical decisions that undermine that project and ignore that high-quality, equally accessible public education is essential for our multiracial, multiethnic democracy to thrive.

The lesson from Hannah-Jones’s experiences is not that individual parents need to make “better” decisions; it is that our institutions need to give every family a meaningful choice. At its core, Brown promised America’s children equal educational opportunity and equal citizenship. Our branches of government, including the judiciary, must ensure that our society is legally and lawfully equipped to fulfill that promise.

For decades, the Supreme Court has been unwilling to do so, at the expense of tens of millions of children subjected to a woefully substandard education in taxpayer-funded schools. A court that is unwilling to fully uphold the breadth of its canonical decision on equality cannot be entrusted to define the boundaries of our Constitution and the rules of our democracy without appropriate checks on its authority. That’s why the fight for educational equality also requires a fight for a just Supreme Court. Otherwise, most Black families will continue to have school choice in name only, the scourge of school segregation will persist, and America’s foundation of good citizenship will remain unlaid.

Rising healthcare costs aren’t a problem just for those who buy their own health insurance. Increases hit employers and their workers this year, too, and more are expected for 2027 plans — adding to growing concerns about affordability heading into the midterm elections.

This story also ran on U.S. News & World Report. It can be republished for free.

Polls show that healthcare costs — and affordability, in general — are among top concerns cited by likely voters. Much attention has been focused on mounting costs and declining enrollment in Affordable Care Act plans, the insurance used by roughly 19 million people who buy their own. But work-based plans are adding financial pressure for the more than 150 million Americans covered through their jobs.

One of the biggest changes this year, for instance, came in the amount workers pay toward their own coverage, rising an average of 9% to $7,471 annually for a family, according to a broad annual survey released Oct. 22 by KFF, a health information nonprofit that includes KFF Health News. That’s up from the 7.6% jump seen from 2024 to 2025.

Their employers, too, faced additional costs, with the average family coverage premium rising 5% to hit $28,303, according to the survey.

In response to that increase — which comes on the heels of a 6% increase the previous year — 44% of large employers, with at least 200 employees, said they upped what workers had to pay toward coverage.

“When we start to see higher use or higher prices, there are only a limited number of things employers can do” to try to slow premium increases, said Matthew Rae, associate director of the Program on the Health Care Marketplace at KFF.

Employers reported taking other actions in the past year to hold down their costs, such as increasing deductibles (the amounts people pay toward their care before insurance pitches in), changing insurers, adding preapproval for some services, or offering workers a narrower choice of healthcare providers, the KFF report said.

Workers’ earnings rose an average of 3.7% this year, the survey found, while overall inflation was 2.7%.

People who work for small companies, with fewer than 200 employees, paid more for healthcare than their counterparts at larger companies.

For example, health plans at large companies had a $1,772 average annual deductible for an individual employee, while small workplaces averaged $2,624.

Overall, though, the share workers pay toward the total premium across all companies ticked up only slightly from the previous year, hitting 27% of the cost for family coverage and 18% for individual coverage, with employers picking up the remainder.

Other groups have recently posted projections of what employers might face next year.

Consulting firm AON, for example, projected employers could pay 9.5% more next year, the fourth consecutive year of big increases, while a similar company, Marsh, estimated 8.2%, the highest jump since 2003. Costs are going up for many reasons, from people utilizing more services to skyrocketing prices of prescription drugs and rapidly rising hospital prices, the reports say.

Also helping drive up costs is artificial intelligence, say other reports, including one from the Business Group on Health, a membership organization of self-insured employers. The report said hospital systems and other medical providers use the technology to maximize insurer and patient billing.

The business group predicted a median premium increase of 9.2% next year, although employers might reduce that by making benefit changes.

Until recently, employers absorbed the lion’s share of cost increases, “but that’s starting to change a bit,” said Jim Winkler, chief strategy officer for the group. “When open enrollment starts, most people will see their out-of-paycheck costs and probably their out-of-pocket costs going up.”

Looking ahead, the group suggests employers take further action to forestall increases in 2028, Winkler said. One idea is to offer lower copayments for some health providers based on their prices or quality, similar to “tiered networks,” a strategy employers and insurers have tried, with only 4% of companies sticking with it, according to the KFF survey. “What’s old is new again,” Winkler noted, saying it might work better now because it’s easier than ever for apps to show consumers the providers with the lowest copayments, which would be set by an employer or insurer.

Overlooked in much of the discussion on rising health costs, said Elizabeth Mitchell, president and CEO of the large employer organization Purchaser Business Group on Health, is the role played by the healthcare industry — from hospitals to drugmakers to insurers.

She asked: Why are the customers — the employers, in this case — being asked what they are doing about it? “The industry is doing absolutely nothing to control costs, as evidenced by the relentless growth in costs,” she said.

Businesses are using new pricing information made available under federal rules to contract directly in some cases with hospitals and other providers and to invest in primary care.

Mitchell and others say Congress should also get involved. Health policy researchers at Georgetown University, for example, suggest capping annual deductibles, setting hospital price caps, and extending lower drug prices negotiated by Medicare to the private sector.

Not all companies saw an increase this year.

Take KFF survey respondent Phifer Inc., a company based in Tuscaloosa, Alabama, that makes insect screens and precision woven products, such as outdoor furniture fabric. Its health costs remained flat this year, likely because it has put a strong focus in recent years on providing zero-cost primary care to its workers through on-site clinics and direct contracts with doctors’ offices, said Russell DuBose, the company’s vice president of human resources.

Only 7% of companies in the KFF survey reported having an on-site clinic, although 24% said they had one on-site or nearby.

Workers get in quickly to see a primary care doctor, DuBose said, and that can help with both prevention of disease and catching problems early. The company also directly contracts with other types of specialists.

“We steer plan members to the best providers that we can find,” DuBose said. “When we do that, we’re paying a more reasonable price.”

The company is self-insured, like many employers. That means it pays all the bills itself and buys what’s called stop-loss insurance to help with very high bills. That coverage has also jumped in price, DuBose said.

He often gets calls from other employers asking about his company’s strategy — and how to control costs. Now, far more so than in recent years, he said, employers are nervous, worried about costs continuing to rise and what that would mean.

“The worry is ‘Could the rising cost of healthcare be the next job killer?’” he said.

Achy from sitting at a desk all day? Try doing this

Back pain, eye strain, sluggishness, shoulder aches, restlessness.

If you sit in front of a computer for long periods of time, whether it's for work or leisure, you've probably experienced one of these symptoms.

But there are ways to relieve some of this discomfort — and even combat some of the health risks that may come with prolonged sitting. That includes walking breaks, ergonomic solutions and quick, simple stretches.

Here's a roundup of tips that have been previously featured on Life Kit.

We know that a sedentary lifestyle isn't great for human health. Studies have shown it can lead to fatigue, weight gain and back and joint pain — and it's been linked to chronic diseases like obesity, heart disease and cancer. Yet adults in the United States spend an average of 9.5 hours sitting per day, involved in activities tied to computers, laptops and phones.

But there may be a way to mitigate its harms. In 2023, researchers at Columbia University Medical Center set out to find the least amount of movement you need to counteract the dangers of prolonged sitting. And they found that walking for five minutes every 30 minutes was the most effective strategy.

In 2023, our colleagues at NPR's Body Electric tested out this five-minute walking exercise on our NPR listeners. Over 20,000 participants joined the challenge — and reported higher productivity and lower fatigue.

Want to give this walking challenge a try? Get instructions from Body Electric — and use this worksheet to keep track of your walking breaks.

Anyone who works at a desk knows how easy it can be to slip into a slouch. If we're not careful, that mid-back rounding can creep into all of our sitting time and create pain by putting stress on the middle section of our spine.

This movement above seeks to relieve that stress and encourage flexibility by mimicking the natural movements of the spine — side bending, bending backward and rotating, says Vinh Pham, a physical therapist. The idea is to get the body comfortable with these motions so that when you encounter them in real life, they're not painful.

To do this modified movement, which Pham calls the 3D T-spine rotation, stand up and drop your right foot behind the left, about a foot apart. Starting with the right arm straight up and the left arm behind you, as if you're shaking the hand of the person behind you, simultaneously drop your arms — still in a straight position — and twist toward your right foot. Move them back to their original position and hold for two seconds. Repeat the movement ten times before switching legs and doing the same exercise on the other side.

When you stare at a screen for long periods of time, your eyes have to work hard. They must first turn inward to look at a close subject, then adjust the focus for short vision and hold that position.

Digital eye strain, as eye doctors call it, can cause eye fatigue, headaches or blurry vision. Try the exercise below to relieve that strain.

Fidgeting is a sign you need to break out of a stagnant posture, says Katy Bowman, a biomechanist and author of Rethink Your Position and Movement Matters. Even a position that feels comfortable at first can begin causing back pain, headaches or stiffness if you hold it long enough.

So listen to your body and mix things up. Alternate between sitting, standing, reclining and moving. That might mean opting to stand in the back of a conference room during a big staff meeting, taking a call while going on a walk or reading a report with your feet propped up in the break room.

The goal is to put yourself in configurations that contort your body in different ways. They should be "adaptive and dynamic" and easy to "change in and out of," says Leada Malek, a physical therapist and author of the book Science of Stretch.

Don't worry too much about what is "good" and "bad" posture. Both Malek and Bowman agree on a common phrase among physical therapists: "Your best posture is your next posture."

Your monitor screen should be at eye level so you're not craning your head down to see. And, your keyboard and mouse should be low enough so you're not shrugging your shoulders and lifting up your arms like a praying mantis to type and click.

Meg Honan, a professional ergonomist, says this common "head down and arms up" position sets you up to feel "achy, sore and tired." To combat this uncomfortable and oftentimes painful position, try repositioning your tech. Bring your laptop or desktop monitor screen up to eye level with a stack of books or some other platform.

Place your keyboard and mouse at a lower height where you don't have to shrug your shoulders to use them. If your keyboard is attached to your laptop, consider connecting an external keyboard and mouse so you can have the computer screen at your eye level and lower your arms. External mouses don't just help lower our arms, but they also prevent the constant strain of using a trackpad.

The Golden Rules of Airplane Etiquette—Because We Have Simply Seen Enough

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It is my firm belief that everyone—no matter their age, gender, race, or Delta SkyMiles Medallion Status—needs to learn proper airplane etiquette, and the earlier the better. As a freelance travel writer, I get on lots of flights to and from different parts of the world, and I’ve seen the same silly mistakes, rude behavior, and even downright harmful stunts enacted by all types of travelers. But by that same token, I’ve also witnessed acts of generosity and helpfulness that all people universally appreciate.

So what, exactly, are the rules at 35,000 feet? To draft such a constitution, I tapped three of Condé Nast Traveler’s Top Travel Specialists who are experts in air travel, in addition to my own first-hand experience: Sophie Caulcutt, co-founder of The Voyager Club; Vikram Seshadri, co-founder of In Residence; and Paul Tumpowsky, chief revenue officer of Fora. And to get the real insider take on how we should behave in the air, I spoke with two working flight attendants: Rich Henderson, a flight attendant for a US-based airline and co-host of the podcast Two Guys on A Plane; and a US-based flight attendant and content creator (@lifeinlayovers on TikTok) named Liam.

In general, we agreed that there’s no environment where etiquette (or the lack thereof) matters more than when you’re sitting shoulder-to-shoulder with dozens of other seatbelt-strapped passengers inside a metal tube hurling through the skies. And while commercial air travel may not always be kind to us, we can choose to be part of the solution. As Tumpowsky of Fora pleads,: “Perhaps we can look inside ourselves to make the overall traveling experience better for us and our fellow travelers.”

In the spirit of doing unto others as we would have them do unto us, below are the golden rules of airplane etiquette. Keep these principles and good habits in mind, and you’ll find that, even when everyone else around you is behaving badly, you’ll feel all the better for being a kind and considerate traveler. A true professional, some may even go so far to say.

Among travelers, especially those seated in economy, no one is dying to spend more time on the plane than they have to. So instead of crowding the gate—a rookie move that will earn you the unattractive nickname of “gate lice”—be patient and wait to board last. Your assigned seat will still be there at the end of boarding. (After all, a queen is never late. Everyone else is simply early.)

Jostling to get on board early to secure overhead bin space? The crew will almost always find a spot, even if it’s not directly above your seat; or they’ll check your bag into the hold at no extra cost. To mitigate the inconvenience, pack light and put together your “seat bag” so you have all your essentials (more on these points below).

Are you flying in business or first class? Fabulous. “Linger in the lounge, enjoy the restaurant, and board last,” says Seshadri of In Residence. “Your overhead bin space is dedicated and waiting for you.”

For parents traveling with kids, you might actually want to be among the last passengers to board. “Why spend an extra 30 or 40 minutes sitting on a plane with young children if you don’t have to?” Tumpowsky says. “But I completely understand why new parents, in particular, may want to board early to make sure they have enough storage within reach for everything they’ll need during a long flight.”

Make your personal item count: Before getting on the plane, prepare a “seat bag” that contains all your in-flight must-haves and keep it close to you in your seat. For me, that’s usually my fanny pack with my passport, boarding pass, phone, AirPods, eye mask, lip balm, hand cream, phone charger, and battery pack. Maybe my Kindle or a copy of The New Yorker; a toothbrush and toothpaste for overnight flights. This will eliminate or reduce the need to rummage through the bag under the seat in front of you, or step out into the aisle to reach for your luggage in the overhead bins.

Turns out, doing this is also helpful for the people around you, according to Henderson of Two Guys on a Plane: “Boarding is basically a giant traffic jam, and one person standing in the aisle reorganizing their entire suitcase can hold up a hundred people behind them. Have what you need for the flight ready before you board, find your seat, put your bag away, and step out of the aisle as quickly as possible.”

First off, acknowledge the flight attendants when you board with eye contact, a smile, a quick hello. These are the people here to make sure you stay safe on the plane and everything goes smoothly, first and foremost. Be a good human and treat them with the dignity they deserve, and you’ll get the same treatment in kind. At the end of the flight, do the same thing and thank the crew—you all made it!

Next, carry-on bags. People tend to overpack heavy carry-on bags because most US airlines do not weigh them, explains Liam of @lifeinlayovers, so boarding becomes even more hectic and slow when you can’t lift your own stuff into the overhead bins. Try to pack only what you can literally carry. Consider too your own physical abilities, height, and strength, and pack accordingly. And if you see someone struggling with putting away their carry-ons and you can help, do so. It helps everyone out, and makes the boarding process go more smoothly. The flight attendants will thank you. Speaking of: Note that many airlines discourage flight attendants from helping with your overhead luggage because of the risk of injury, says Henderson, so don’t expect that every FA can or will lend you a hand.

While we’re on the topic of overhead bins, Henderson says, “Please don’t put your small purse or jacket in the overhead bin when people are still looking for space for their actual suitcases.” Save putting them away in the overhead space until boarding is mostly complete; there will always be space on the plane for your stuff, Liam says.

As for deboarding the plane, a few more best practices: Do not get into the aisle while the plane is still taxiing to the gate. Once the plane comes to a complete stop, that’s when deboarding can begin. Wait for the rows in front of you to get up and head to the exit, rather than scrambling to snatch your items from the overhead bins and just crowding the aisle. As space opens up in the aisle, that’s when you stand up, get your bags, and exit.

It’s possible someone behind you will react exasperated, believing you should have gotten your stuff earlier—when there was no room to do so. If that happens, give them a smile (or a sly wink, if you’re feeling cheeky), and retrieve your luggage calmly, knowing that you’re in the right.

When you’re transiting and have limited time to get from Flight A to Flight B, kindly inform a flight attendant prior to landing. “I believe the appropriate protocol is to speak with a flight attendant rather than attempt to negotiate your way to the front of the aircraft,” Tumpowsky says. “The crew can assess just how tight your connection really is, determine whether special consideration is warranted, and, if appropriate, make an announcement asking other passengers to remain seated. That request carries considerably more authority coming from the flight crew than from a passenger who has decided that their connection is more important than everyone else’s.”

Caulcutt of The Voyager Club agrees, saying that the crew may even consider it essential to move your seat closer to the exit—or at least closer to the aisle—if your connection is extremely tight or if they anticipate a delayed arrival. When approaching the crew, Caulcutt says, “I often find that a trip to the galley, during boarding or shortly after, to politely explain the situation one-on-one works best.”

My seat, my choice! When we select our seats on a plane, especially when we pay to get assigned places or use status benefits to do so, we are well within our rights to keep it. Able-bodied spouses, families, and friends can reunite after the flight. You can politely and firmly refuse to swap. (Exercise caution if using the correct but risky phrase: “Your lack of planning is not my problem.”)

“But just apply common sense,” says Caulcutt. “You don't want to separate an elderly couple,” who may physically need each other for assistance, or “a mother from a weeping child.” Because did you really want to sit next to that wailing five year old anyway? Swap with the parent who can calm them down.

Some of us love making friends wherever we go, but when you’re going to be stuck on a plane with hundreds of other people in a steel projectile hurtling through the air, the rules of social engagement get a touch more delicate. So what’s acceptable, and where do you draw the line?

“A brief conversation and a little cordiality are perfectly appropriate,” says Tumpowsky. It’s not a bad idea, though by no means required, to establish some rapport with those around you—but don’t force anything. “If your seatmate engages and the feeling is mutual, by all means, enjoy the conversation,” Tumpowsky continues. “But no one should feel obligated to continue a conversation longer than they're comfortable with. A polite exchange of pleasantries is not a binding commitment to discuss your respective life stories until the landing gear is employed.”

If you’re traveling with a friend or friends and you’re all prone to yapping, keep a few things in mind: “Keep the tone of the conversation low,” says Caulcutt. “People in planes tend to talk louder than they realize because of engine noise. Try to keep your voice at a level that your seatmate can hear comfortably but the rows around you can’t easily follow.” Try to keep topics light and disagreements to a minimum, she adds. If you get into a fight, you won’t have much room to put some distance between you on a plane.

And on night or red-eye flights, the lights in the cabin are usually dimmed and people are trying to sleep, Caulcutt says, so keep conversations to a whisper whenever possible. Otherwise, save them for later on the ground.

Be patient, and take deep breaths. “No one hates it more than the parents,” says Caulcutt. Period!

OK, well, it really is for our own safety in the air. As Henderson says, “I promise we flight attendants are not doing the safety demonstration for our own entertainment!” Do as advised and check where your nearest exits are; and see where the closest bathroom is, while you’re at it. Brush up on the brace position appropriate for the cabin you’re in. Enjoy the safety video, if there is one (the Philippine Airlines “Safetynovela,” depicted as a soap opera, is my current favorite). If the FAs are doing a live demo, give them your attention and a smile.

This is all the more important when you’re seated in an exit row. When the FAs check in with you about the responsibilities you have in the case of an emergency, answer truthfully regarding whether or not you're physically, mentally, or even spiritually able to carry out what needs to be done. This is the cost of the exit-row legroom. (Also, the exit-row legroom comes with the price of not being able to stow luggage in front of you, as there’s no seat to keep them under, so bear that in mind when picking your place on the plane.)

The fasten-your-seatbelt sign is not a suggestion. “Turbulence can happen without warning, even when the sky looks perfectly clear,” Henderson says. Liam explains that the flight deck turns on the sign when there's a forecast of bad weather up ahead, either from radar or aircraft ahead of the plane you’re on. So keep your belt fastened whenever you’re seated, Seshadri says, “even if loosely! You’ll never regret it.”

All our experts and FAs agree that reclining your seat is fair game. That’s what it’s there for. As Tumpowsky says, “Whether it’s the overhead light, window shade, or reclining, I generally believe you have the right to use the product as it was designed.” And everyone is also on the same page when it comes to meal time: Don’t do it while the person behind you is eating. It’s just good manners.

“A little courtesy goes a long way,” Seshadri says. “Recline slowly, be mindful if the person behind you is using it—like working on a laptop—and put your seat upright when the crew asks, especially for takeoff and landing.” On a plane, as in life: Be considerate of others.

This is another point on which all our experts were in agreement. The flight attendant call button is absolutely there to be used, especially if you need assistance, have a medical concern, or there’s something that requires a crew member’s attention—like if your phone fell into the cracks of the seat. (A very real concern! If the machinery ends up cracking your phone and the battery leaks, it could be a fire hazard.)

“But if the FAs are visibly in the middle of beverage service and you just want another soda, it’s usually easier to ask us when we reach your row,” Henderson says. And don’t use the button to summon them every five minutes for things that can wait. There are sometimes hundreds of other passengers and only a few of them. Liam says, “Staffing has decreased and cabins have expanded. Please be patient with us, especially when we’re busy with service or there’s turbulence.”

Again, among our experts, this is a universally acknowledged truth: “The middle seat passenger deserves both middle armrests,” Henderson says. After all, the window passenger gets the window, and the aisle passenger gets a little extra breathing room. “The poor person in the middle deserves something!” he says. (Liam agrees: “As someone who often gets the middle seat when flying standby on benefits, please, for the love of all things good, give me both middle armrests.”)

“No bare feet on display ever!” Caulcutt says. And rightly so. You can remove your shoes, especially on long-haul flights; our feet do swell in flight. Keep your socks on (try compression socks); swiftly and discreetly swap into a fresh pair if you’ve lost the ability to smell yourself. Do not ever prop your feet up on the armrests, seatbacks, or tray tables. (Quick trick: Use your backpack or duffel bag, under the seat in front of you, as a foot rest.)

Henderson’s advice: “Never walk into an airplane bathroom barefoot or even just in socks. That mysterious liquid on the floor is probably not water.” And Seshadri’s: “Please never clip your toenails or file them on a flight!”

For better or worse, Wi-Fi connectivity is getting better on planes, meaning that what often used to be a relatively quiet airborne sanctuary is now another space in which the cacophony of modern life can invade. So, again, don’t be a jerk about it.

Our experts agree that headphones and earbuds are a must, whether you’re listening to music, watching a movie on your own device, or scrolling through Instagram Reels. Do not take your high-level meeting from 35,000 feet in the air. “Voice and video calls are usually prohibited by airline policy or regulations,” Henderson says. “An airplane is a shared space, not your personal living room or office.”

Expect these principles to be sticking points in the coming years. “High-bandwidth internet—and potentially the ability to make calls more easily in the air—is going to create the next battleground in flight etiquette,” Tumpowsky says. “Just because technology allows you to do something doesn’t necessarily mean 200 other passengers want you to do it.”

Alas, from personal experience, it has to be said! And think about your surroundings when you’re watching a movie and there’s a graphic scene in the story, whether sexual or otherwise. Consider speeding through if there are, say, children around. That said, it’s really up to you—especially if the movie or show in question is offered through the in-flight entertainment system. I was once on a flight watching an episode of And Just Like That… and they got to the infamous Snapple scene in Carrie’s apartment and, well, I didn’t skip it since I was pretty much alone in the business class cabin.

As long as you’re not doing a full-on downward dog in the aisle, Caulcutt says, stretching in the aisle is fine. Keep it quick and respectful. Do some standing spine twists, lymphatic taps, and gentle walking, then return to your seat. Don’t do it during meal service or when the FAs are working. If an FA tells you to return to your seat, don’t make a fuss and sit back down.

“We encourage passengers to get up and move around when it’s safe, especially on long flights, but the aisle is a workspace for flight attendants and an important pathway for everyone on board, especially in the event of an emergency where we must move quickly,” Henderson says. So don’t block the aisle, congregate around the bathrooms, or interfere with the crew doing their jobs. “A little stretching is one thing, but turning the plane into a yoga studio is another,” he says.

There’s nothing wrong with politely waking someone if you need to get out of your row to reach the toilet. Just be considerate (there it is again, basic kindness). Liam says, “I’d hate to be woken up after I’ve just gotten comfortable; for me, that’s having an eye mask and headphones on. Watch out for those signs. But if you have to go, you have to go.” Wake someone up the way you’d want to be woken: Respectfully.

The flip side is that if someone in your row gets up to use the bathroom and disturbs you, it might be a good opportunity for you to go as well. “Think of it as airplane efficiency,” Tumpowsky says. “You may save everyone another round of climbing over one another later in the flight.”

Whether they physically need to or not, many people allow their intrusive thoughts to win and grab the top of the seat in front of them to pull themselves up. This is awful and rude. If you really need support, Tumpowsky says, “the proper etiquette is to use your own row of seats for support whenever possible. Pulling on the seat in front of you can jolt the person sitting there—and if they’re sleeping, you’ve just unnecessarily woken them up.”

I was gonna say, “leave the bathroom the way you leave yours,” but the mileage may vary on that one. Keep the place tidy, wipe anything you made wet as a courtesy to the person who comes in after you, and dispose of any waste properly, as the laws of common decency intend. “Just because you don’t want to push down the flap to discard your trash doesn’t mean you can just start a pile of it on the ground or counter,” Liam says. “And for the love of aviation, wear shoes in there!” Henderson adds.

Maybe there was once a time, in a faraway land, when dressing nicely for a flight might have improved your chances of an upgrade. No longer. “Today, upgrades are much more likely to be determined by status, algorithms, bidding, or other systems, so I don’t think your wardrobe is moving you up the list,” Tumpowsky says. “Dress comfortably. Being comfortable will have a much bigger impact on your flight experience than looking like you belong in first class.”

You can do so comfortably! May I interest you in a cashmere jogger set or a wrinkle-free traveling suit? Figure out a travel uniform that works for you—something comfortable that also makes you feel put-together. It’s a proven fact (by me, anecdotally) that looking good helps you feel good, like you can handle any travel hiccup like flight delays or lost baggage with dignity and grace. But hey if it’s not your literal style, no one says you have to.

“Always be polite to the plane crew,” Caulcutt says. Flight attendants aren’t responsible for the weather, delays, baggage fees, or most airline policies, so don’t take your frustrations out on the people who are there to keep you safe and comfortable to the best of their abilities.

Patience and kindness can go a long way, Henderson says, and Seshadri reminds everyone to extend that respect to the airport staff and your fellow passengers. Moving with respect and courtesy, he says, “will earn you not just a better flight but a good dose of karma and happiness too.”

Yes, cabins are getting stuffed with more seats, and those seats are getting smaller, while crew staffing keeps getting cut. And yet, even in the face of all this, on top of everything going on in our world, we get to travel. So it’s more important than ever to cultivate a sense of solidarity as travelers. As Liam puts it, “We’re truly all in this metal tube together.”

‘Gratifying and humbling’: First Nation leads ghost net cleanup from Interior B.C. rivers

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This article has been co-published with IndigiNews, and was produced as part of our 2026 Indigenous journalism fellowship.

After starting the day with a prayer and a safety demonstration, a group of volunteers gears up in water-resistant clothing and lifejackets, ready to hit the water.

The crew is preparing to scour a stretch of the Fraser and Thompson rivers, removing any fishing gear or waste left behind. Fishing nets left underneath the water are commonly known as ghost nets, which trap and kill fish.

As the saying goes on a volunteer call out poster, “ghost nets create ghost fish.”

TLKemchEEN (Lytton First Nation) has led the river clean-up program in the area for the past 20 years, all in an effort to preserve salmon populations and aquatic life following harvest season.

Over two days in September, a group of 40 volunteers spent a total of 20 combined hours on the water.

From Lillooet to Lytton during the first day, and from Lytton to Yale during the second day, volunteers passed through white-water rapids and rugged terrain such as the famously turbulent passage of Hell’s Gate.

When the raft leader yells “hold on,” that’s exactly what participants do, reaching down for a rope attached to the raft, laughing and hollering, ripping through the Fraser Canyon rapids.

The cold river water splashes everyone — waking up the crew as countless bald and golden eagles watch from above.

“I didn’t realize how impactful everything is along the shores. I guess I didn’t realize that some leave their garbage and nets out there,” volunteer Rebecca Riley, who is from the Stʼatʼimc Nation, said.

“Once I saw that with my own eyes, I wanted to jump in and be a part of it.”

It’s a sentiment shared by many volunteers on the boat, who eagerly approach the shoreline when a ghost net comes into sight.

Whether the net will take one person to remove, or up to five to pull out of the water, is unknown until people get their hands wet.

During the non-rapid portion of the cleanup, countless stories were told, Indigenous people exchanged words in their languages to compare similarities and differences and folks even had hot chocolate along the shore, courtesy of Kumsheen Rafting, which was contracted to support the program this year.

During the first year of the program, 126 ghost nets were collected.

“This year we picked up six nets and some garbage, some years prior we’ve picked up zero. We have to remind our fishermen not to be lazy and take their gear home,” Kevin Duncan told the Narwhal.

Duncan is an elected councillor for TLKemchEEN in B.C.’s Southern Interior, part of the Nlaka’pamux Nation Tribal Council. He has been involved with the program since its inception. He attended both cleanup days this year with his son Aaron.

For TLKemchEEN, stewarding their waters as they always have is crucial — especially as salmon numbers decline. Life along the Fraser and Thompson rivers is deeply connected to salmon. And TLKemchEEn is home to where the two rivers meet, in the country’s largest salmon-bearing watershed.

“For future generations, sometime down the road, I’m afraid all they’re going to be looking at is pictures of salmon in a book,” said Duncan.

The nets recovered by the program are given to those in need, including single mothers, low-income families and Elders from the nation.

“As a Nlaka’pamux or Stʼatʼimc member … that fishery is a communal right, it’s not an individual right. It’s looked after by your nation,” Duncan said.

“Most times, you just get to see portions of the river from the highway, but once you’re down on the water, floating for eight hours from Lillooet to Lytton, it gives you a better perspective on where people fish.”

Removing fishing gear from the cold and fast-moving river can be dangerous, which is why Duncan starts each day of the program with a yémit, or prayer in Nlaka’pamxcín.

“Salmon are the lifeblood of our people, and when these fish pass through coastal communities, they pray for them in the lower Fraser. We’re doing the same thing as they pass through our territories in the mid Fraser. … These fish hear that prayer.”

Volunteer Riley is Lillooet Tribal Council’s fisheries manager. She has participated in the river cleanup program three times, beginning in 2018 when she saw an advertisement in the newspaper.

Although she’s been on the Fraser and Thompson rivers many times throughout her fisheries career, she said that the ghost net removal program was “eye opening,” regarding the number of fishers out on the water.

She said that Lillooet Tribal Council is happy to help as the neighbouring nation to Nlaka’pamux territory, given the large area to cover and the shared value of protecting salmon.

“When we’re having the long days and we’re getting splashed and it’s cold, if we save a salmon or two that are still alive and let them free, or we clear out a net so we know that other animals can’t go in, it’s very gratifying and humbling,” she said.

“At the end of the day, when you’re riding the bus home and you’re cold and hungry, you know in the back of your mind it was so worth it.”

While the focus of the program is cleaning up the river, volunteers take the opportunity to support other initiatives while on the water, such as mapping traditional fishing sites to prove occupancy.

According to Conservation Biology, “Proof of occupancy is the foundational legal and moral requirement needed to establish Aboriginal Title, regain territorial sovereignty and protect ancestral lands from unauthorized exploitation.”

Lillooet Tribal Council’s extreme heat resiliency planner, Lyza Adolph, did a lot of heavy lifting over the two days removing ghost nets, but also had a mission of her own.

A member of the Stʼatʼimc Nation, this was her first time participating in the ghost net removal program.

“It was very cold, but with the group that we had, it was very enjoyable and uplifting,” she said.

With a focus on planning for extreme heat in her position at the tribal council, Adolph monitored fishing sites during the program to see if there were any shade trees nearby, scoping out areas to potentially plant some.

“The program tied in with my traditional shade tree planting. I know more about the upper river, but from Lillooet to Lytton and beyond, I didn’t know too much about it. It was definitely nice to see the various fish camps and see if there were shade trees,” she told The Narwhal.

Shade trees are beneficial for both humans and aquatic life.

As spawning salmon break down, their nutrients feed the trees. In turn, trees offer shade to cool the water, which is critical for survival on a warming planet, and tree roots and branches give salmonids space to hide from predators while also preventing erosion.

In the Fraser Canyon, which includes the communities of Yale, Lytton and Lillooet, fishing sites can be hard to access. Adolph’s shade tree monitoring data, collected by boat, will be used in future tree planting projects.

“I would definitely do it again,” Adolph said. “Although it was very cold, it was very refreshing and rewarding by the end of the day.”

How to Stop Trump’s Coup

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

South Korea’s lessons for the US midterms.

How do you stop a would-be dictator? The first requirement is knowing that it’s possible. In 2024, the people of South Korea took to the streets and stopped a would-be coup in its tracks. Their example offers lessons for how Americans can counter Donald Trump and his allies if they disrupt or defy the results of November’s midterms.

After escalating conflicts with South Korea’s legislature and bribery accusations against his wife, President Yoon Suk Yeol abruptly declared martial law on December 3, 2024, at 10:23 pm. Like Trump, Yoon had already appointed loyalists to key military posts, and his state prosecutor used libel laws to attack political opponents and journalists. Now Yoon banned all political activity, protests, and strikes, and claimed the right to detain anyone he chose. He ordered the military to enter the National Assembly by force to remove the members, “including the use of firearms if necessary.”

But 16,000 protesters rushed to the building to protect the legislature. As Korea’s opposition leader and others headed there, they livestreamed calls for more people to join them. Citizens went on to nonviolently block armored vehicles. When military helicopters landed inside the assembly grounds, staffers constructed barricades from furniture and used fire extinguishers to slow soldiers breaking through the windows.

By 1 am, enough parliamentarians had reached the chamber to form a quorum, and they unanimously exercised their constitutional right to revoke Yoon’s martial law. For the next 10 days, citizens surrounded the parliament despite freezing temperatures—chanting, singing K-pop songs, waving light sticks, and calling for Yoon’s impeachment. The protests grew to over a million people by December 14, when the assembly impeached him. Yoon’s party lost the presidential election the following summer, and he is now serving a life sentence for insurrection.

Here are five key lessons for the US from South Korea.

1. Elections matter all the more under a would-be dictator.

Because South Korea’s opposition party had won major gains in the April 2024 election, it was already blocking some of Yoon’s initiatives before he declared martial law. Party leaders had defied previous attempts to silence them, and their leadership on the night of the attempted coup was critical. Because overwhelming vote margins are our best guarantee of securing our elections, we can’t let fear of Trump’s interference deter us from doing all we can to get people to vote.

2. Act quickly to confront attempted seizures of power.

As soon as Yoon declared martial law, activists and opposition party leaders called on people to protest. Had they waited, martial law would have become the status quo, making protest much harder.

For the US midterms, most points of contestation will be local: Citizens and local governments need to protect polling places and vote-counting centers, including having police protect them from disruption. Groups like the League of United Latin American Citizens are suing in advance to block ICE from polling places, state attorneys general and secretaries of state preparing emergency legal briefs, and the Brennan Center has published checklists refuting potential administration arguments. But massive, immediate nonviolent protest will also be essential.

3. Encourage security forces to stay loyal to the Constitution.

South Koreans who confronted the martial law forces said many soldiers showed open or subtle resistance to the coup. For example, soldiers didn’t actively repel the crowds in front of the parliament or remove the aides who were resisting inside. A major and a sergeant who were ordered to issue ammunition refused, saying that replenishing it would take too long. Officers commanding two military buses ordered their troops to stay aboard rather than fully deploy. A sergeant refused flight approval for a special forces transport helicopter. When one unit was ordered to seize computer servers to prevent a parliamentary vote, they delayed by stopping to eat ramen noodles at a nearby convenience store.

Ordinary citizens also urged soldiers to refuse unlawful orders. Former special forces members invoked their shared backgrounds with the soldiers tasked with putting down the protests. Protesters asked soldiers to remember the 1980 Gwangju massacre and not support a dictatorship.

In our own context, it mattered enormously that six US senators and House members with military and national security backgrounds created last November’s video on the duty to refuse illegal orders. We need to keep highlighting this duty to service members. We hope we’ll never reach a moment like South Korea’s, but if we do, we shouldn’t assume that the entire US military will do Trump’s bidding.

4. Prevent the coup’s initiators from consolidating power.

The day after the attempted coup in South Korea, civil society organizations held press conferences demanding the president’s arrest. Daily protests outside the National Assembly culminated in the demonstration by over a million people on December 14, pressuring legislators to impeach Yoon. Protests then shifted to the presidential residence and the Constitutional Court, demanding Yoon’s arrest. Labor unions demonstrated at the offices of legislators who refused to support impeachment and created a website to swamp those legislators’ phones with text messages. A widely circulated PowerPoint debunked Yoon’s arguments and offered counter-slogans.

The lesson for the United States is that power gets consolidated in stages, and every stage that’s blocked makes the next stage easier to prevent. Legal challenges are essential, but so are the actions of ordinary citizens. For example, Indivisible and No Kings have been conducting Hands Off the Vote trainings and recruiting poll monitors, and the long-standing 866-OUR-VOTE national hotline is expanding its presence. Veteran nonviolent organizers the Freedom Trainers have mapped out how people can respond to multiple scenarios, from preelection attempts to suppress the vote to attempts to block vote certification.

5. Create festive, diverse forms of resistance.

As South Korea’s demonstrations kept growing, K-pop light sticks replaced the traditional candles as the protest symbol. The sticks gave the rallies the feel of a concert, as protesters turned K-pop hits into chants. Musicians and artists showed up, adding to the festive atmosphere. Supporters prepaid for hundreds of cups of coffee, snacks, and meals at nearby cafés and food trucks. This atmosphere was crucial in drawing younger Koreans, the core K-pop fan base.

America’s major rallies, like No Kings, already combine festivity with defiance, a combination key to helping people keep going. Let’s embody that in our final election push!

Have pro-democracy organizers across the US engaged enough people to prevent Trump from overturning election results he doesn’t like? We won’t know until Election Day and the days immediately after. Meanwhile, South Korea’s example can inspire and instruct us as Americans defend our own beleaguered democracy.

The Fall of “Scientific American”

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When the beloved science publication got too political, its corporate owners stepped in. Now its future is uncertain.

In March of 2026, in a nondescript, 46th-floor office overlooking lower Manhattan, about two dozen members of Scientific American’s editorial staff had gathered in person and online to talk openly about their approach to political coverage.

David Ewalt, the editor in chief, wanted them all to understand something: The magazine was not “kowtowing” to the Trump administration. In fact, according to a recording of the meeting obtained by The Nation, he asked staff present to “raise your hand if you’ve ever heard me say not to cover a political story.”

For well over a year, rumors had been circulating among the staff about top-level editors’ rejecting pitches they saw as too political, controversial articles triggering angry phone calls from Scientific American’s corporate owner, and even, on at least one occasion, ownership forcing editors to blunt language critical of the Trump administration.

Then there was what happened to the opinion department. In the first months of the new administration, it had been publishing about 15 pieces a month, many of which criticized Trump’s attacks on scientific research. But after the department published a particularly provocative column in May 2025—“Science Tells Us the U.S. Is Heading toward a Dictatorship”—management, in what one staffer would later call an “unprecedented” move, had told the department to “pause” publishing new articles until the new editor in chief started.

The opinion department’s hiatus had worried some people more than others—there were legitimate journalistic reasons to want to emphasize breaking news, even if not everyone agreed with them. But by March 2026, nine months into Ewalt’s tenure, the opinion section seemed to be gone for good—and other departments were feeling political pressure, too. That, along with a round of layoffs, a restructuring of the newsroom, and veiled threats about the future of the magazine, had left the staff frustrated and looking for answers. Now that the new editor in chief was finally coming to the full editorial staff “to listen as well as to speak,” as one staffer put it, they began asking questions.

What about that rumor, someone asked him: Had editors really been told to not put the word “Trump” in headlines?

That was true, Ewalt said. Last year, a Breitbart article had criticized Springer Nature, Scientific American’s German owner, for attacking those “skeptical of the coronavirus vaccine” and spreading “gender insanity.” “No more taxpayer money for woke publishers!” declared Donald Trump Jr., retweeting the article. In June 2025, Axios reported that all federal subscriptions to Springer Nature publications had been canceled. Although Trump officials later walked back the bulk of the cuts, they had shocked the scientific community: Without access to Springer Nature’s thousands of journals, including the prestigious Nature publications, researchers at federal agencies—from the NIH to NASA—would struggle to stay abreast of scientific developments. The company itself, meanwhile, risked losing as much as $25 million in federal contracts.

So if Ewalt had asked people to keep Trump out of the headlines last summer, it was because corporate higher-ups were visiting the site all the time. “And if they saw the word ‘Trump,’” he said, according to the recording, “they were wetting their fucking lederhosen.” People had no idea how “lily-livered and weak-kneed and cowardly” Springer Nature was, he said. “They are so fucking afraid of the Trump administration.”

The opinion editors had long since been reassigned to other roles, but now, almost a year later, Ewalt was telling his staff that even news stories sometimes had too much of a slant to them. “I am not ever telling anybody here ‘Don’t go after the Trump administration,’” he told them. “I’m saying I don’t want to hear your opinion.” He just didn’t think it was their “strongest weapon.” If everyone stuck to objective, hard-hitting reporting, Ewalt believed that they would be able to stay out of trouble with Springer Nature.

Mentioning Trump in headlines was now allowed. “But let’s be realistic about the world we live in,” he told his staff. Last August, Ewalt warned, Springer Nature could have easily decided that the magazine’s “$4 million in losses” wasn’t worth risking millions more that the company earned from federally funded subscriptions to its scientific journals. He had been “trying to keep Springer Nature from freaking out and shutting us down,” he said.

As the meeting came to an end, Ewalt seemed optimistic. “This is useful,” he said. “We’ll do this more.”

But staffers still felt uncertain. Could people really trust their editor in chief to stand by their reporting, like he promised? Or would he retract articles if readers sent too many angry emails, or if Springer Nature’s billionaire CEO called? Those concerns, one staffer said, weren’t “quelled in that meeting at all.” Rather, it had transformed accumulated gossip and implication into something far more concrete—and “set everybody’s teeth on edge,” said another staffer, who, like a number of sources for this story, chose to speak anonymously because they feared retaliation from current or prospective employers. “He was trying to be honest with us,” the staffer said. But, also, it “alarmed the shit out of everybody.”

And on June 23—three days before the National Labor Relations Board was scheduled to count the unionization votes—Springer Nature announced that it was selling the magazine to a little-known company called LabX.

In recent years, however, Springer Nature’s hands-off reputation has begun to falter. Interviews conducted this summer and fall with a over a dozen current and former Scientific American employees depict a magazine that, especially over the past two years, has struggled to protect its editorial independence in the face of sometimes overwhelming pressure from its corporate ownership—a picture corroborated by audio recordings and documents obtained by The Nation.

Back in 2017, Springer Nature had made headlines when it admitted that it had censored hundreds of academic articles in mainland China at the behest of the Chinese government. But for most staffers, the first sign of a more hands-on approach came in 2021, when an opinion article about the medical situation in Gaza was pulled from Scientific American’s site after right-wing backlash.

At the time, the article’s authors received no explanation. But current and former Scientific American staffers familiar with the situation told The Nation that the reason was simple: Stefan von Holtzbrinck himself—known for his enthusiasm for Israel—had called then–editor in chief Laura Helmuth and ordered that it be taken down.

It wouldn’t be the last conflict between Helmuth and Springer Nature. Helmuth, who joined the magazine in 2020, had pushed it to cover more politics, arguing that science cannot be disentangled from its social context. Scientific American had issued presidential endorsements for the first time under her leadership. In 2024, after Helmuth called Trump supporters “bigots” and “fascists” on social media on election night, the magazine again became the target of right-wing backlash. This time, facing pressure from Springer Nature, she resigned.

The magazine had been struggling financially for years, but the pressure to be profitable only increased as Springer Nature repeatedly tried and failed to go public; in 2024, it finally succeeded. After Helmuth’s departure, “it felt like a pendulum swing back to ‘let’s stick to the science,’” said Kelso Harper, who was then the magazine’s senior multimedia editor. “Let’s not get involved in too much messy political stuff.”

In the first months of Trump’s second administration, though, messy politics was almost unavoidable. Trump brought disaster after disaster for science writ large—thousands of grants suspended or canceled, tens of thousands of workers fired or pressured to resign, billions of dollars of funding slashed or frozen. Scientific American’s opinion department critiqued Republican attacks on everything from trans health care and measles vaccines to fluoride and the National Weather Service.

Then, in April 2025, as the administration’s war on DEI in science continued to accelerate, psychiatrist and anthropologist Eric Reinhart posted a letter to X that the Trump-appointed US attorney for Washington, DC, Ed Martin, had originally sent to the Illinois-based medical journal CHEST. The letter, dated April 14, asked: Was CHEST protecting the public from misinformation? Was it disclosing when viewpoints were influenced by funders? Did it publish articles from competing viewpoints? “It has been brought to my attention that more and more journals like CHEST Journal are conceding that they are partisans in various scientific debates,” the letter read. “The public has certain expectations, and you have certain responsibilities.”

CHEST is not well-known outside its field, but the letter made national headlines as an example of the administration’s unprecedented efforts to undermine what the right called “woke” science. Reinhart submitted an op-ed to Scientific American senior editor Dan Vergano arguing that the letter represented “an authoritarian threat to science.” As the story moved toward publication, news broke of similar letters to other journals, including the prestigious New England Journal of Medicine and JAMA. Reinhart’s op-ed quickly progressed through an initial edit, a “top” edit, and a copy edit; according to e-mails and documents obtained by The Nation, a legal reviewer made only one, minor change.

But when the draft returned from another round of edits, this time from acting editor in chief Jeanna Bryner, the word “fascist” had disappeared, along with a reference to the genocide in Gaza. A paragraph in which Reinhart argued that fascists benefit from “self-interest, automatic obedience, and ethical cowardice” had been cut entirely. In a comment in the document, he protested: “Shying away from publicness and directness on these issues will only make them worse.”

The draft next went to Springer Nature’s US head of legal for review. He left a comment declaring, with no explanation, that the cuts were “necessary.” “Got it,” Bryner responded.

A tranche of recently released documents show that Springer Nature had good reason to fear retaliation: On April 14, 2025, Ed Martin had sent Scientific American a threatening letter nearly identical to that received by CHEST. He sent similar letters to Nature Medicine—also owned by Springer Nature—along with at least 10 other publications. (Martin is currently facing disciplinary proceedings from the DC bar for sending a different threatening letter to Georgetown University.)

At the time, Scientific American president Kim Lau responded to Martin privately, asserting that its content was “fully protected by the First Amendment.” Behind the scenes, however, Springer Nature leadership was insisting on substantial changes to Reinhart’s op-ed, against Lau’s objections.

Those objections were why they were “ultimately allowed to print it” at all, Lau told Jeanna Bryner and the two opinion editors in a May 2025 meeting, according to audio obtained by The Nation. The only reason things didn’t “blow up in a bigger way,” she said, was that Reinhart eventually agreed to the changes. But when the piece was eventually published, on May 1, it received little attention—probably, she said in the meeting, because they had “watered it down so much.”

And when Dan Vergano published his column “Science Tells Us the U.S. Is Heading toward a Dictatorship” two weeks later, it was the final straw. Shortly after, Lau called Bryner, Vergano, and the chief opinion editor, Megha Satyanarayana, into the May 2025 meeting.

“There’s no way to sugarcoat this,” Lau told them. Springer Nature felt that Scientific American’s criticism of Trump was threatening their broader business—selling subscriptions to their scientific journals. The federal government was a major client.

“I have reiterated repeatedly that we have a history of editorial independence, and that these things are crossing that line,” said Lau, referring to Springer Nature’s repeated editorial interference. “It’s very clear that they understand that. But given where our business is, and the threats to the larger business, they don’t care.”

The department’s work, she said, was being placed on “pause” until the new editor in chief, David Ewalt, arrived.

Lau was explicit about the pressure they were all under. “We’re upsetting the company on a daily basis,” she said. Germany was six hours ahead of New York, and she and Bryner would wake up to early-morning calls from the German board. “If I thought me quitting would help the situation, I would have done it,” Lau said. “They are terrified about the things that are happening in the US.”

Although Lau gave the editors reason to hope that Ewalt might manage to resurrect the opinion department—“I think he sees value in it,” she said—he would turn out to be on the same page as the Springer Nature executives in at least one regard: It was time to reduce opinion output significantly. During his interviews, Ewalt had pitched them his new vision of the magazine: less editorializing, more facts.

In May 2025, though, the opinion editors—like the rest of the magazine—still didn’t know much about their new boss. Lau assured them they could all talk things over with Ewalt and see how he wanted to approach the issue. “We just need to be clear that what we’re fighting for is worth it. And I’ll be honest with you,” she said, “I don’t think this is the thing I want to die on the hill for.”

“We are in a fight right now for the future of SciAm in general,” she said. “There is nothing that’s a given in our future. We are losing a lot of money, and we do not necessarily have a clear path to closing that gap.”

Over the course of the next year and a half, at least 53 of the approximately 84 people employed at the magazine would either be laid off, resign, or retire.

Ewalt took over the magazine on June 2 with a mandate to increase traffic and subscriptions. But there were other changes, too. Helmuth’s departure had triggered a new attention to employees’ political expression, and Ewalt’s arrival reinstated objectivity as the journalistic North Star. Things that had produced no objections in the past became contentious: People were discouraged from following only left-leaning social-media accounts, for example, to avoid the appearance of bias. Although he initially promised that his restructuring efforts would not include layoffs, in October 2025 10 employees lost their jobs, replaced by a slate of hires to new positions, including three breaking news reporters. In December, Kim Lau threatened further changes if revenue didn’t start picking up—more layoffs, workers guessed, or maybe a sale.

Though October’s layoffs galvanized the then-incipient union campaign, staffers had additional concerns. Vergano, who had been reassigned to report on Washington, remembers hoping that the union could serve as a counterweight to the “clear, corrosive corporate interference and influence” he’d witnessed at the magazine. That emphasis on editorial independence, said WGAE organizer Andy Greenberg, was one of the things that set the campaign apart from others he’d worked on. “We have to look out for us,” one staffer realized, “because Springer Nature is not.”

But it was becoming difficult to enjoy the moment. In the weeks after the vote, rumors began to circulate that Springer Nature executives were “basically measuring everybody’s desk to sell it,” Vergano said. “It was not subtle.” The bad news hit on June 23: sale to LabX, salary cuts, mass layoffs.

“The amount of panic, the amount of tears, the amount of ‘I don’t know the next time that I will see you, goodbye forever maybe’ conversations that were happening in the newsroom—I mean, traumatic puts it lightly,” said books and culture editor Brianne Kane. Even those who had received job offers from the new owners were told to clear their desks and that their laptops would be shut off by midnight.

The next week, a few dozen workers, freelancers, and allies gathered at the 1 New York Plaza office. Despite the previous week’s events, the mood on the picket line was jaunty. A small but energetic brass band accompanied chants of “New York is a union town,” and the signage was heavy on puns (“I Do Not Cosine These Layoffs”).

Behind the scenes, Springer Nature was still doing its best to nullify the election, including by trying to withdraw the petition for an election by pointing out that it had laid off everyone at the magazine. “This was a case where a corporate parent restructured and sold its subsidiary in a transparent attempt to defeat an election,” the WGAE wrote in a legal filing. “The Board should not reward such conduct.” When the NLRB counted the votes, the tally was 31 to 1 to unionize.

In the immediate aftermath of the sale, staffers were left scrambling to learn more about Scientific American’s new owner. Previously, LabX’s best-known publication had been Discover, a onetime dentist’s office stalwart that was hollowed out by private equity buyers in the aughts; according to multiple staffers, the ownership transition seemed rushed, chaotic, and disorganized. When one worker reached out to a former employee of one of LabX’s other publications, the stories they heard—including ones related to editorial independence—were part of why they turned down their job offer, they said. (LabX did not respond to multiple requests for comment.)

The NLRB case with Springer Nature remains open, but the union also filed for a new election. LabX hasn’t hired any outside union-busters, but it has held three meetings asking for more time to prove that a union isn’t needed. “It’s comical,” said one staffer, given that the company’s first action as the new owner was to lay off “many very talented, very successful staff members.” The bargaining unit is much smaller this time around—from 35 to less than 20, with a few positions still in dispute—but workers remain as committed to organizing as ever. The result, counted this week, was 12 to one.

When Dan Vergano reached out to the Society of Professional Journalists’s ethics committee for advice in May 2025—shortly after the department’s work was put on pause—he was told to start by working through official channels: Going public could come at a high personal cost. Besides, as a journalist, “you don’t want to be part of the story,” Vergano said. “It’s literally stomach-churning.”

He declined to speak to particular incidents, citing legal concerns. Still, he said, when a company is willing to interfere in critical journalism, how could reporters not end up pulling their punches? “We are trying to deliver you the raw stuff of democracy,” he said. “If a company allows their own greedy interests to shape that, then that’s a problem for democracy.”

It was clear that the “pall” Vergano described extended beyond the opinion section. “When you hear about people’s pieces getting cut to pieces, when you hear about pitches getting turned down,” said another editorial staffer, “these things ripple out to other departments.” That March meeting with Ewalt was the first time the newsroom had discussed editorial interference so openly, but by that point the problem was far from new: It was often unclear whether decisions were motivated by corporate interference or editorial judgment; stories of infamous incidents circulated for years.

“Reporters like to think that there should be a 60-foot brick wall between the business side of a news outlet and the editorial side,” Dan Axelrod, the chair of the Society for Professional Journalists’s ethics committee, told The Nation. “The reality is there isn’t, and there never has been.”

For most staffers, it’s still unclear exactly what motivated the sale—the money Scientific American was already losing, or the far vaster sums that Trump had threatened to take away, or both. Journalists at Nature News, also under the Springer Nature umbrella, were enduring “the same scrutiny” of their reporting on the Trump administration; Lau had told the opinion editors as much last year. But, Lau said at the time, Nature was “not a brand that’s losing money.” Scientific American, meanwhile, was a public-facing magazine owned by a company with little interest or expertise in public-facing journalism.

“The decision to divest reflected a strategic focus on our area of greatest expertise, academic research and scientific publishing,” Springer Nature’s North American president said in a statement to The Nation. David Ewalt had put it more clearly, perhaps, during the March meeting: “We are journalists working for a weird multinational corporation,” he told his staff. “We are the appendix of this organization. They don’t need us. They don’t even, frankly, want us anymore.” (Both Ewalt and Lau declined to comment on this story, and both have left Scientific American.)

Though it’s not ideal for democracy or free speech, Axelrod said, the journalism code of conduct does not bar a publication’s owners from steering it away from public service in search of profit. But “it’s sad and surprising to think that they would potentially do that on the fly, in a way that breaks readers’ trust.”

It was tragic, he added, “that the journalists who are supposed to be reporting on undue influence by the government on the creation and dissemination of scientific findings appear to be subject to that same undue influence.”

For one staffer, who started working in journalism after the recession, the field had been in bad shape for as long as they could remember—but Scientific American, they said, was always held up as “one of the icons, if not the icon, of meaningful, impressive journalism about science.” It was their dream job. On its own, they said, Scientific American wasn’t going to solve all the challenges at the intersection of science and society—attacks on public health, a lack of climate action, widespread disinformation. Still, they worried about “losing the spirit of it,” and that it would become just another website publishing less-meaningful “cool stories about science.”

In a letter to readers in Scientific American’s September issue, the new editor in chief—Jeanna Bryner, promoted once again—announced that the magazine was moving out of its Battery Park office. “I can’t promise that the next chapter will be free of mistakes or tumult,” Bryner wrote. But the change in ownership was “an opportunity for SciAm to be even better”—and, she added, “have some fun.” (Bryner did not respond to multiple requests for comment.)

At least one freelancer has already written publicly about their negative experience with the new Scientific American, and two more editorial staffers were laid off at the end of September, including Megha Satyanarayana, Vergano’s former colleague in the opinion department—who declined to comment for this piece—and a data reporter. Vergano remains worried that LabX seems more interested in “gee-whiz stuff” than critical, substantive reporting. From what he’s seen of their publications, he said, “they produce things that are schlockfests. And we were doing good work at Scientific American.”

In the May 2025 meeting that shut down the opinion department, Vergano and Satyanarayana had grappled with the unintended consequences of that work, work that leadership had once championed. “We thought that by being more provocative and by becoming more of a voice ourselves that we would hopefully make a difference,” Satyanarayana said to Lau. The experiment, she said, was “costing us more than I ever thought it would.”

Satyanarayana was talking about their columns, but she could as easily have been referring to a broader project: Scientific American’s efforts, dating back to Laura Helmuth’s tenure, to advocate for science at a time when science was under attack more than ever before.

“This is something that many outlets are struggling with right now,” Lau said. “That doesn’t excuse it, or make it better. Probably it makes it worse.”

Could Mississippi Really Send a Democrat to the Senate?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Think about the races that are likely to determine control of the Senate in this year’s midterm elections. The chances are pretty high that the Mississippi Senate contest doesn’t feature on your list. After all, the last time the state sent a Democrat to the Senate was in 1982. But a growing number of observers on the ground in that deepest of Deep South states believe there’s more than a fighting chance that Democratic nominee Scott Colom, the district attorney for Mississippi’s 16th Circuit, could unseat sitting GOP Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith.

Hyde-Smith, whose lead in recent polls ranges from 3 to 6 percent, has what critics might call “baggage.” In November 2018, she raised a storm of controversy by stating that if she were invited to a public hanging, she would want a front-row seat. In a state with an ugly history of racist mob killings, this was widely taken as a shout-out to vigilante lynching. More recently, Hyde-Smith has hailed both the Supreme Court’s evisceration of the Voting Rights Act and the stampede toward mid-decade redistricting in the South, aimed largely at erasing Black-majority congressional districts. She has also been a particularly vociferous Trump supporter. She failed to stand up to Trump on tariffs, which have hit farmers in rural states such as Mississippi particularly hard, and she uttered not a peep of protest against last year’s “one big, beautiful bill,” even though the bill’s vast cuts to Medicaid and SNAP hit a poor state like Mississippi, which gets far more back in dollars from federal programs than it sends to DC in taxes, particularly hard. More than 100,000 of the state’s residents will lose Medicaid in the coming years because of the bill that Hyde-Smith voted for; tens of thousands of Mississippians will also lose their SNAP benefits, and hundreds of local grocery stores, reliant on income from customers using SNAP, will be put at risk of going under.

In fact, the only thing on which Hyde-Smith has distanced herself (albeit ever-so-mildly) from Trump is his recent decision to flood the beef market with imports of cheap beef—a move that, as a cattle farmer herself, Hyde-Smith knows is likely to hurt the American cattle industry.

Historically, Mississippi, whose population is roughly 37 percent African American, has been one of the most racially divided states in the country. For a hundred years after the Civil War, it was the epicenter of the Jim Crow South, and the Mississippi Democratic Party was a redoubt of segregationist white politics. In the civil rights era, as the national Democratic Party embraced the Voting Rights Act and other reforms that, cumulatively, dismantled the Jim Crow infrastructure, the parties flipped: Increasingly, whites in Mississippi aligned with a GOP that was veering ever more rightward, while the African American electorate moved into the Democratic Party’s camp. The divisions have remained so rigid in recent decades that, even as the African American electorate expanded, the white majority’s overwhelming adherence to the GOP ensured continued Republican dominance in statewide elections.

But the electoral math has been slowly changing in recent years. Since 2018, the size of GOP victories in statewide races has frequently shrunken to a few tens of thousands of votes. In 2023, incumbent Republican Governor Tate Reeves won reelection by only 27,000 votes—the state’s closest gubernatorial race since 1999. “We’ve been chipping away over the last more than half-decade,” Charles Taylor, executive director of the Mississippi NAACP, told The Nation. “Mississippians are fed up. That’s going to translate at the ballot box. There’s a lot of voter education going on. Voters in Mississippi and across the country are seeing the midterm elections as an existential crisis.”

In an election year in which many GOP voters are increasingly uncomfortable with the country’s direction under Trump, and with African American voters hyper-motivated to vote in the wake of the recent assaults on voting rights from the Supreme Court and a string of Southern state legislatures, Democrats have an opportunity to over-perform in the November Senate election. “We have a fighting chance here,” says Taylor. “And the country will be surprised by what happens.”

In a year with high African American voter participation—Democratic turnout in the 2026 primary was up 90 percent compared with the previous electoral cycle—only 20 percent of white voters would need to vote for Colom for the Democrat to flip the Senate seat. Given the conservative bent of white Mississippians, it’s still a long shot, but certainly not an impossibility. “There are a lot of social justice and civil rights [themes] bubbling up in Mississippi and throughout the South, and one of those has to do with what the US Supreme Court did,” Mississippi-based civil rights attorney Carroll Rhodes explained. “There’s a lot of hostility and anger and angst—and motivation within the African American community to vote and not to lose their right to vote. There’s probably going to be a bigger turnout than usual.”

“The demographics have been trending in this direction,” Derrick Johnson, president and CEO of the NAACP, told The Nation. “The current economic climate and restlessness with policies that have not worked, all of those things—people see it is possible to win a statewide race.” For Johnson, the confluence of national and state issues represents a potential tipping point for Mississippi. “Any candidate that speaks up to an administration in this moment who are taking resources out of Mississippians’ pockets will do well. The quality of life for Mississippians is not improving. We don’t have a single Fortune 500 company headquartered in this state. People are looking for a change.”

Colom, an African American attorney whose mother was a judge and father was a Republican who worked for Ronald Reagan, believes he can tap into that desire for a new politics. “We’re a state that can’t afford the corruption we’re seeing in DC right now,” he told The Nation. “Our farmers are already starting to feel the tariffs; fertilizer prices are spiking because of the war in Iran; and now the president is importing cheap beef. To me, what’s happened is a betrayal. They told the farmers, ‘We’re taking your votes for granted.’ It’s so stark, and the betrayal is so personal.”

Colom believes that in the lead-up to the election he can erase Hyde-Smith’s small but persistent lead in the polls. “A lot of people are going to be surprised,” he said. “But I’m not going to be surprised, and a lot of people in Mississippi won’t be surprised. You defeat the corruption by showing up and using your power in a moment when they’re trying to take it away.”
