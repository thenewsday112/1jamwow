The Political Upstarts That May Decide Israel’s Election

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Less than a week before an election that many Israelis consider the most significant in the country’s history, the two leading candidates are deadlocked. On one side is Benjamin Netanyahu, battle-hardened, beset by corruption charges, and increasingly desperate. (Early this week, his campaign polled voters on whether they would be more likely to turn out if this were his last run for office.) On the other side is Gadi Eisenkot, a centrist former military chief who is temperate and broadly liked but lacks the political instincts to become a clear favorite.

On October 18th, Donald Trump tried to tip the balance. Breaking a long tradition in which American Presidents at least pretend not to intervene in foreign elections, he praised Netanyahu on Truth Social as a “Great Warrior Prime Minister.” He also suggested that he would abandon the war against Iran if Netanyahu lost: “If Israel has the wrong Leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi, America will have to do this work ourselves, and I am not inclined to do so.”

Trump remains hugely popular in Israel, and in a normal news cycle his endorsement would have given Netanyahu an electoral boost. Instead, it was cancelled out by one of the Prime Minister’s most vocal supporters. Yinon Magal, a right-wing television host, aired a segment in which he called Eisenkot a “monkey.” Many Israelis viewed this as a racist slur against Eisenkot, whose parents emigrated from Morocco. To make matters worse, Magal also suggested that Eisenkot was the puppet of Jews of European descent: “All the Ashkenazi élitists who are managing him stand behind him, telling him what to say.”

Yet, even with all the media frenzy, the polls have hardly budged. For weeks, Eisenkot’s party, Yashar (meaning “straight,” or “just”), has generally maintained a small lead over Netanyahu’s Likud, while both candidates remain far from securing a majority in the parliament. After Israeli elections, the candidate with the most support from legislators in the Knesset (typically the head of the largest party) has forty-two days to assemble a majority coalition. If that candidate fails, his rival gets a chance. In practice, this gives smaller parties disproportionate power in determining who becomes the Prime Minister.

The upcoming election may well be decided by two small parties that didn’t even exist a few months ago. They are strikingly different, but both are headed by hawkish politicians, and both seem designed to appeal to right-wing voters who hold Netanyahu’s government responsible for the attacks of October 7, 2023, but don’t feel prepared to join the opposition. Polls show that both parties are currently hovering around the threshold necessary to enter the parliament.

The first one, Amcha Yisrael (People of Israel), was formed in August by Ofer Winter, a former brigadier general in his mid-fifties. Winter, a stern nationalist who famously encouraged the “voluntary” migration of Palestinians from Gaza, represents a new generation of military leaders who grew up on Jewish settlements and belong to the Religious Zionist movement. For months, he seemed to have electoral momentum—but his numbers dipped recently, as he declared an unequivocal allegiance to Netanyahu. “Winter is an enigma,” Amnon Abramovich, a veteran commentator on Israel’s mainstream Channel 12, told me. “He entered politics because of October 7th, with a slate of candidates who were not in charge on October 7th, but he pledged to go only with the Prime Minister of October 7th.” Abramovich said that Winter’s supporters wish he hadn’t tied his political fate to Netanyahu: “If he were smart—and he is not smart—he would have vowed that he would go according to principles and not according to people.”

Winter’s support comes overwhelmingly from voters who previously cast ballots for Likud or one of its coalition parties, Abramovich said. After the catastrophe that befell Israel on the coalition’s watch, many of those voters would likely have stayed home this time if not for a candidate like Winter—which led many observers to suspect that Netanyahu had encouraged him to run in the first place. But as polls began to suggest that Winter’s party may not gain enough votes to enter the parliament—that he might be “wasting” right-wing ballots—Netanyahu embarked on a campaign to force him from the race. It recently emerged that Netanyahu had met with a rabbi close to Winter, hoping that he could persuade Winter to drop out. “Netanyahu is right to be fearful,” Abramovich said. “He sees a scenario in which Winter brings in two to three per cent and still fails to pass.”

The second party, announced just last month, is an alliance between a center-right faction called the New Economic Party, which is focussed on pocketbook issues, and another one, the Reservists, which is intent on making the military draft mandatory for everyone—including ultra-Orthodox men, who are currently exempt. The alliance’s leader, Yoaz Hendel, is more sophisticated than Winter, and more telegenic: in campaign appearances, he presents himself as part tech executive, part combat soldier. He served as a senior aide to Netanyahu and as a communications minister under both Netanyahu and Naftali Bennett. Since leaving Netanyahu’s office, he has become a prominent critic.

“We’re the protest vote,” Hendel told me by phone recently. He said that his target voters were Israelis under the age of forty: reserve-duty soldiers with young families who are buckling under the high cost of living and disillusioned with the government’s promises of security. “We want to convince everyone in the State of Israel not to vote for Netanyahu,” he said. “I think he is responsible for October 7th.” Still, Hendel was quick to add that he had no qualms about praising Netanyahu where he felt it was due, as in the case of the Israeli operation that blew up Hezbollah militants’ pagers in Lebanon and Syria two years ago.

The Reservists Party has attracted roughly equal support from the pro-Netanyahu and the anti-Netanyahu blocs. Abramovich calls it a “splits party”—“one leg here and one there.” The ruling coalition and its opposition have thus largely spared Hendel the barbed political attacks that they direct at their enemies.

But it is unclear how Hendel would align himself after the election. He does not endorse the opposition motto “Just not Bibi.” He told me, “I am concerned with how to establish a Zionist state. If [Netanyahu] insists on staying with ultra-Orthodox parties, I will not join him. By the same token, if Eisenkot joins Arab parties that do not denounce Hamas, then I am not there, either.” He is also unwilling to contribute to a minority government—an increasingly likely scenario, in which Eisenkot fails to secure a majority but still manages to form a coalition with the support of Arab parties. As Hendel sees it, “You put together a government like that and you’re out in about two weeks, while giving Netanyahu seventy seats in the next election.”

If either party succeeds in securing enough votes to enter the parliament, it will be tougher for Eisenkot to cobble together a majority. If both fail, Netanyahu may be unable to continue as Prime Minister. Given that polls in Israel can be off by as much as four per cent these days, there is no way of predicting the outcome.

Yet the new parties’ emergence, so close to election day, indicates a real reckoning. “They are both a response to the frustration of many voters with the polarized bloc voting that is common among the parties of yesterday,” Yagil Levy, a professor at Israel’s Open University, told me. They also underscore the outsized importance of military service in a country that has been at war for three years.

Yohanan Plesner, the president of the Israel Democracy Institute, a nonpartisan research center in Jerusalem, told me that Israelis remain so entrenched in “identity politics and tribalism that new political frameworks are needed in order to create any kind of movement.” Still, he is skeptical of Hendel’s attempt to provide a platform for disaffected voters of all kinds. “There are a ton of reservists, and only a fraction of them will vote for him,” he said. What his party seems to represent is a kind of “safe haven for right-wingers who are uncomfortable with the government’s polarizing approach, its surrender to ultra-Orthodox demands, and an over-all sense of incompetence.” Plesner noted that this leaves some crucial concerns unaddressed: “There’s no substantive debate about Israel’s security approach, no real maturity to have a genuine discussion about the Palestinians or the war.”

A poll released earlier this month shows few points of commonality on pressing political issues. There is overwhelming support among Jewish Israelis for establishing a state commission to investigate the events of October 7th, and for imposing the draft on the ultra-Orthodox. (The exemption has rallied Israelis of all political persuasions around the idea of “sharing the burden.”) There is far less pronounced agreement on restarting peace talks with the Palestinians; three-quarters of those surveyed rejected the idea. On all the rest, the country is split—often bitterly so. It seems astounding, in light of what has happened in Israel over the past three years, that what may end up tipping the election is a pair of niche parties that no one in the country had heard of until this summer. The two versions of Israel that could result are so divergent as to seem impossible to reconcile. ♦

The Republicans’ Remarkably Bad Midterm Campaigns

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The midterm campaigns have been going badly enough for the Republicans that they have inspired in White House principals some rare candor. “You don’t turn the Titanic around overnight,” Vice-President J. D. Vance said on the trail earlier this year, in Toledo. He was speaking about the economy, but the same might be said now about the second term of Donald Trump, or, perhaps, about Vance’s own political prospects; on Tuesday, the President said that it was “very early” to endorse him as his preferred successor. Later that day, Trump was in North Carolina, a state that he won in the 2024 Presidential election, but where his handpicked Senate candidate, Michael Whatley, is trailing by so much that the G.O.P. has essentially stopped putting money into the race. On Wednesday afternoon, at a rally in Greenville, Trump said of Whatley’s Democratic opponent, Roy Cooper, “He’s going to become your senator. And it’s crazy.”

It is at least a little crazy how sharply politics have turned on the Republicans. Last week, prediction markets favored Democrats to win Senate races not just in North Carolina but in Ohio, which Trump won in 2024 by more than ten points, and in Alaska and Texas, each of which he won by thirteen points. Democrats are still underdogs in Kansas, which their party last represented in the Senate in 1939, and in Iowa, but they are drawing close. By Wednesday, when a headline on Axios read “Senate Republicans Fear Mississippi Is in Play,” things had started to feel a little surreal. The Republican predicament was summed up by an apparent A.I. prompt, mistakenly published on the website of Eric Pratt, a nominee for Congress in Minnesota: “The President is not popular, so all messaging should be focused on local politics and needs.”

On this point, most Republican candidates have seemed on message. A Times analysis found that more than ninety-nine per cent of G.O.P. ad spending is now going to spots that do not mention Trump. But even that strategy is backfiring. The campaign of Vivek Ramaswamy, who is running for governor in Vance’s home state of Ohio, paid for an ad in which the estranged mother of his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton, a doctor and the former director of the state health department, denounced her. The ad neglected to note that Acton had been removed from her mother’s care when she was twelve, after her stepfather was indicted on a charge of raping her. Several prominent Ohio Republicans, including Senator Jon Husted, who is up for reëlection, disavowed the ad; Vance dismissed their objections as “whining,” while Trump said that the ad had hit “pay dirt.”

A similar fissure has appeared in some Senate debates. In Minnesota, the G.O.P. nominee, Michele Tafoya, answered the opening question by referring to “a number of areas where I’m going to disagree with the President.” (She also called the killings in her state of Renee Good and Alex Pretti, by federal law-enforcement agents, “devastating.”) In Michigan, the Republican candidate, Mike Rogers, said, a bit testily, that the President’s high-profile tariffs on goods from Canada “were not well thought through.” In Maine, the longtime Republican incumbent, Susan Collins, bragged that she had voted against Trump more often than she’d voted against Joe Biden. Even the Alaska senator Dan Sullivan, who has been a reliable party-line vote for Trump, said that sometimes he will “forcefully oppose” things “in private.”

One policy choice that Republicans have started to oppose in public is, in retrospect, the pivotal decision of the midterms, and likely of Trump’s second term: the war in Iran. Trump’s approval ratings have never been especially high this term, but they began to drop below forty per cent around late March, a month after the war started, and have steadily declined since then. The elections analyst Nate Silver noted last week that Trump’s ratings were nearly as bad as George W. Bush’s were in his second term. The war has, as predicted, driven up both energy prices and inflation—a situation that has made it much harder for Republican candidates to convincingly claim that their businessman President is doing anything about affordability. It has also forced those same candidates to try to defend a war that few believe in. (The Atlantic reported that even Donald Trump, Jr., has taken issue with his father over it—in private.) During an Iowa Senate debate, the moderators displayed images of two soldiers, native Iowans, killed in action in the Middle East.

In Iowa and in other Midwestern states, the price of diesel, which has risen more sharply than other fuel prices, has become a potent political issue. In response, Trump waived U.S. policy and announced a deal in October with President Vladimir Putin to purchase hundreds of thousands of tons of Russian diesel. A week later, Trump suspended a sanctions package on Russia—named for Lindsey Graham, the late Republican senator from South Carolina, who made the war in Ukraine his signature fight—which the Senate had passed and the President himself signed in September. Meanwhile, Elon Musk, who has spent more than four hundred million dollars on Republican campaigns this election cycle, slowed Ukraine’s access to his Starshield satellite network, leaving that country more vulnerable to Russian attacks. For some Republicans, this was too much. On Wednesday, Ashley Hinson, a politically savvy congresswoman who is the Senate nominee in Iowa, said before a joint rally with the President that she would tell Trump to his face that the deal with Putin was “a bad idea.”

It is also a betrayal. On Wednesday night, Trump campaigned in South Carolina with Darline Graham, who was appointed, at Trump’s behest, to finish her brother’s Senate term, and who championed the Russian-sanctions bill. She has often said on the campaign trail, “I don’t trust Putin, and I don’t trust Russia.” But she is in a surprisingly competitive race in a deep-red state, and she needs Trump’s help to win there, even if it is largely his fault that she might lose. At the rally, she watched as the President casually told a story of calling Putin and asking for a favor, as if to make a show of how responsive the Russian leader is to him. War is horrific, but clarifying. It has exposed Trump, to at least a segment of the public that has long stood with him, as someone who stands not with them but with the oligarchs. And it has shown politicians like Graham and Hinson exactly the terms of the deal they have made. ♦

Restaurant Review: Good Time Country Buffet

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The country buffet is a very specific genre of restaurant, with origins in the cafeterias and meat-and-threes of the South. It is defined by its all-you-can-eat business model and by its steam-table offerings, which are likely to include fried chicken and sides such as collards, candied yams, and potatoes with gravy. A country buffet is often found in a strip mall, is generally decorated for utility more than for charm, and almost always has a carpeted dining room. This last feature exists for the same reason that the country buffet’s fixed price generally includes a carve-out for senior discounts: its most devoted customers are old, and the carpet’s friction helps keep unsteady feet anchored to the ground. David Yun, Yong Min Kim, and Steve Choi, the trio of restaurateurs behind the Manhattan restaurants Kisa and C as in Charlie, hail from the suburbs of Atlanta, Georgia. Many of the buffets in the area were run by Korean immigrants, and the three grew up eating and working in them. Good Time Country Buffet, which they opened in September in the East Village, is a monument to nostalgia: in its every obsessive detail it’s a hoary old Georgia country buffet that’s been around for decades, even though it’s barely been there a month.

The restaurateurs’ obsessiveness seems to be a habit. Their most recent establishment, Kisa, an homage to the quick-service canteens that serve South Korea’s taxi-drivers, is a feat of uncannily precise world-building that extends even beyond the restaurant’s walls: on the block outside, you can spot stickers written in Korean adhered to lampposts and scaffold walls, advertising fake taxi companies with Kisa’s address. For Good Time, they’ve upped the level of commitment even more, going so far as to fly the former owner of a Georgia buffet up to New York to vet the details. The result, in both its grace notes and its gestalt, freakishly summons the placeless, timeless, sunless anti-aesthetics of a turn-of-the-millennium strip-mall buffet. I mean this as a compliment, and I know the proprietors will take it as one: this restaurant is so, so spectacularly ugly, in the most honest and love-filled and organic-feeling way. It’s a monochromatic symphony of browns and tans: the laminate tabletops, the melamine plateware, the wood panelling, the rosettes that dot hideous moss-green carpeting. Even the climbing-vine wallpaper is yellowed, as if gradually discolored by the cigarettes of a thousand ancient Sundays.

Entering the slim space, you’ll pass a community bulletin board covered in flyers, most of which are for fake homespun businesses fronted by Good Time’s real-life friends and colleagues: piano lessons from their general manager, graphic design from their architect. A host will direct you into a tidy dining room lined with booths and tables. You sit. You confirm with a server your intent to eat. Then you head to the buffet, which occupies a fluorescent-lit area at the back of the space, decorated with faux greenery and a bricolage of framed pictures and cheesy inspirational art. As at any decent country buffet, dinner is unlimited. (It runs a truly humane $26.99, with discounts for seniors and kids, of course.) The centerpiece and signature dish is fried chicken, which is unreservedly and objectively excellent, with a thin, crackly batter and beautifully seasoned meat. There are plenty of other options beneath the sneeze guards as well, most of more unremarkable quality: ovoid meatballs of Salisbury steak in a salty gravy, Buffalo wings in a tangy-hot sauce, and all the usual sides and trimmings of a Southern-style feast. (Standouts included the candied yams, sunny in both color and flavor, and soupy braised collards studded with little pink buttons of ham.) The buffet’s cold table is largely given over to a standard salad bar—you can’t really mess up lovely hunks of iceberg lettuce in a serving bin, or a little bucket of cherry tomatoes, though neither can you give them much of a glow-up. There’s tuna salad, lightly dressed canned corn, and a mountain of egg salad, briskly mustardy and surprisingly nice.

I’m a buffet lover through and through; there’s something about the combination of unfettered abundance, pick-and-mixery, and culinary economies of scale that really tickles something primal for me. New York, in recent decades, has had its share of all-you-can-eat joints, but they’ve mostly been of the table-service variety. The city’s unlimited-buffet scene—lunchtime spreads at Indian restaurants, a handful of sprawling Chinese banquets—was never terribly robust, and it became even less so during the years of the pandemic and its attendant hygiene concerns. I know I’m not the only person who despaired that I might never again do the awkward “Do I reuse my plate or get a new one . . . ?” shuffle without crossing state lines into more buffet-friendly territory. (N.B.: Always get a new plate.)

Much of the thrill of Good Time is just the familiar pleasures of the format. The restaurant offers more or less the same items every day, though there are plans to add a daily rotating menu—fried fish, sloppy joes—as is customary at buffets down South. The laminated table cards, too, hint at a more robust future: on one side is a drink menu (sweet tea, naturally, comes in pebbled-plastic tumblers), on the other a list of ice-cream sundaes, half obscured by a piece of tape that reads “coming soon.” For now, meals end where they began, at the buffet: skip the banana pudding, which is overly gluey and tastes mostly of cornstarch, and go instead for a scoop of gooey chocolate brownie or a tart dollop of fruit cobbler.

In the restaurant’s first few weeks, a line for tables stretched down the block, but waits are more manageable now. Sundays still tend to draw a bigger crowd. In this heathen city, it’s unlikely that customers are spilling in after church, as many do at more southerly establishments, but there’s something about Good Time that tugs on that Sunday-lunch cord, nevertheless. One evening, I dropped in for dinner with a friend whom the restaurant might as well have been created for, personally, in some lab; she’s of the same micro-generation as the owners, she also grew up near Atlanta, and she also adores a buffet on a deep emotional level. She spent our meal in a state of wonderment over Good Time’s carefully conjured atmosphere. “This has got to be an actual 95.5 The Beat playlist,” she said, referring to an early-two-thousands hip-hop radio station in Atlanta, as “Yeah!,” by Usher, cross-faded on the sound system into Nelly’s “Dilemma.”

A simulacrum this faithful cannot be easily placed along the spectrum of “good” and “bad” by which we tend to assess restaurants. It might even break beyond the boundaries of “restaurant” and start creeping—campily, postmodernishly—toward art. Take the meat loaf, for example, which was very bad: crumbly briquettes of beef with a desultory ketchup glaze. But, as part of a meticulous reconstruction of a type of eatery known for mediocre food, does its badness actually count as a mark of success? Is the flat and burnt-edged corn bread a culinary error, or a flawless re-creation? I kept thinking about “Piccadilly Community Centre,” a 2011 piece by the artist Christoph Büchel, which involved entirely retrofitting the London location of Hauser & Wirth into a working community space, complete with linoleum-tiled floors, baking lessons, and a thrift store. Its eleven-week run so thoroughly fooled so many visitors, including many neighbors who delightedly availed themselves of its services, that—despite the project’s arguable artistic success—it left behind concerns that people had been used as unwitting props in someone else’s joke.

What saves Good Time from this sort of sour misunderstanding is that it isn’t really trying to “say” anything, except that the owners of the restaurant really love buffets. Büchel was a tourist in the world he built; Yun, Kim, and Choi are bona-fide inhabitants of theirs. Any unknowing diners who step in with zero context might wonder how, exactly, such a dated room has managed to survive for so long in a prime East Village location, but they won’t leave feeling hoodwinked. They’ll pay a decent price for decent food, smiling service, a fun soundtrack. How could they feel anything but happy? On one of my visits, I observed a pair of older ladies—the precise clientele for whom the dining room’s industrial carpeting had been laid—at a nearby table. It had dawned on them that they were in a restaurant that was referencing other restaurants—that they were, in effect, dining inside quotation marks. “Oh, but what’s the difference!” one of them exclaimed, reaching for another golden chicken thigh. We were all birds pecking at Zeuxis’ deceptively lifelike painted grapes, though in this case we were eating mashed potatoes, and the mashed potatoes were fluffy and gravy-lashed and real. ♦

Souvankham Thammavongsa on the Gears That Turn Underneath a Story

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

This week’s story, “Wall Cano,” opens: “Mom left. Dad didn’t tell us anything.” The narrator of the story is a ten-year-old girl who has been living with her parents and her younger brother in a small basement apartment until one morning, before school, her mother’s not there. Why did you want to start the story in such a direct way?

I was thinking of writing a poem—just a list of my father’s smiles. But then I began to build in things around the list of smiles I had. A window. Cardboard. I really played around with the arrangement of the story. I felt like I could begin the story anywhere, put it out of order, and no matter how I arranged the details the story remained the same. Mom left. So I began with that.

The narrator’s father is a stylish man. He’s short, she says, but he has suits made up by a tailor that fit him perfectly. It’s clear that her mother is attractive, too. Other women are always trying to get the father’s attention and other men the mother’s. Does the narrator feel as though it was her job to keep them together?

I think she just thinks of it as normal. This keeping them together. It isn’t any different to her than being told to tie her shoe or eat breakfast.

At one point, the narrator’s father tells her that he never got to go to school. In Laos, where he grew up, “the war came, and that was it.” The narrator is left to figure things out by herself, and she often gets it wrong. The concept of homework, for example, is something she can’t understand. If school is the equivalent of work, then surely time at home is for relaxing after work. How hard is it for her to puzzle her way through a life her parents never experienced?

When I write, I know that the room reads me—or the voice on the page reads me—as someone who will tell a sad story. It’s a real surprise for people to discover that the way they have read my sentences in their head is not the same as experiencing my sentences live—in my own voice. I think of my stories as being built around humor. Maybe not laugh-out-loud humor, though that’s there, but other forms of humor like wit, deadpan, understatement, exaggeration, recognition, irony. We know what homework is, but the way the story asks us to suspend our understanding of homework for a bit to live in hers is funny. Doing it this way doesn’t diminish or judge or humiliate her lived experience. I love who I come from and where I come from. When I tell a story, I make sure a reader does that, too.

She does know that she’s supposed to make something for a science project, but she’s forgotten about it in the weeks after her mother left. Her father helps her make something, pulling down some of the cardboard that they use on the windows in place of curtains. What he makes—a model of a house on stilts, like one he’d lived in in Laos—is beautiful, but when she gets to school, she realizes that it’s completely wrong. Does she feel worse for herself or for her father?

I think she thinks everyone else is wrong. It’s a lot like that song Dolly Parton wrote, “Coat of Many Colors”: everyone else at school might think it’s a coat made of rags, but to her it is still a coat and it was made by her mother with what they had around the house. To build a house and make it stand is a science project, too. It might not win anything, but you don’t have to win anything to know that what you made deserves to live in this world.

The project that wins—and that gives the story its title—is a model of a volcano, a word she hears as “wall cano.” It’s ugly, like “a pile of shit,” she thinks, but impressive when smoke comes out of a hole. As in other of your stories, such as “How to Pronounce Knife,” the title story of your 2020 collection, English is the language your characters must use to navigate the world outside the home, but any mistakes, like pronouncing “knife” with an audible “k,” convey a kind of glorious defiance, too. Would the narrator ever think of the “wall cano” as a volcano?

I write about language. I ask us to pretend the English language right in front of us is not there. This is very easy to understand when we read a work and are told it is a translation. We are made to understand our distance from the English language. I write that distance into the English language in front of us with the English language. We all know we don’t pronounce the “k” in “knife.” It’s something we have learned—to see a letter there and agree that even though it is right there in front of us we will not give it a sound. And yet, in the telling, we are made to wonder, When did we agree to this and why did we agree to this? We understand why someone new to the language would question this. Instead of focussing on the “mistake” someone new to the language makes, we don’t see it as a mistake at all, but see that it makes absolute sense. I do a similar thing here, but with a different word. This time, though, I never give the correct word. We remain isolated with the word “wall cano,” and remain in her perspective and point of view and understanding of the language. We live and move through the story the way her father knows the English language. He says, “I just poo-poo paa-paa my way around until something makes sense to them. That’s how English works.” We do what he says of the English language—we, the readers, are given the responsibility of carrying the meaning and understanding of these words.

At the end of the story, the time frame shifts, and the narrator is now a thirty-seven-year-old woman, visiting a friend and watching as she checks her son’s homework. Why did you want to explicitly switch into this adult perspective?

My stories come across as really simple and tiny. Breathe and the story is over already. Blink and it’s done. There are all these gears turning underneath the story that make it tick. I can’t draw attention to all that in the telling, because it would betray the telling of it. I could have certainly done this a different way. I could have had the child go over to another child’s house and discover that their parents helped with their homework, too. Take a reader into a scene where she discovers she doesn’t have what other people have. I could have had this moment come out in a discussion between her and her brother. But I don’t think she could really know how significant the story she just told is. It also feels too easy and cheap and sentimental to keep her that age and have her realize hers is a different experience. I needed a lot of life to happen for her. And there’s a lot of life between that science project and her friend’s house. And I need that life to happen in order to give significant weight to the story that has just been told. The refusal, even as an adult, to say the word “volcano.” That restraint, that absence there in the last scene, is so important to the story.

Do you think of this as a story about education? What’s more valuable to your narrator? What she learns from watching her father, her mother, and her brother, or what she learns in school?

I think of this as a story about love. People leave or they change, and no one is coming to make things better. You will have to learn to love yourself even when there is no one there to tell you that you’re worth loving. In a way, that is what an education is. You learn how to teach people how to love something until they can do that on their own without you. ♦

Maria Bamford Asks for the Check

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On a recent Wednesday morning, in a small black-box theatre in East Los Angeles, I watched the comedian Maria Bamford try out new material. There was no alcohol, but there were free doughnuts. Women in athleisure sipped lattes. Someone brought a baby. The show started promptly, at 9 A.M.

Bamford, among the most daring and charismatic performers of her generation, is always testing the boundaries of what counts as standup comedy. Is it still standup if you’re telling jokes to a group of high schoolers in the aisles of an independent bookstore? Or to friends sitting on a park bench? Bamford does both of these things in her 2017 Netflix special, “Old Baby.” In “The Special Special Special,” she performs for the most intimate audience imaginable: her parents. Bamford’s repertoire includes painfully specific impersonations of her mother and father, and it is uncomfortably thrilling to watch them watch her imitate them, in between jokes about depression and suicide.

Mental illness—and the stigma that surrounds it—is a recurring theme in Bamford’s work. Growing up in Duluth, Minnesota, Bamford struggled with bulimia and intrusive thoughts, though it wasn’t until she was in her thirties that she received the clarifying diagnosis of obsessive-compulsive disorder. (One of her jokes turns on the absurdity of talking about physical illnesses the way people often talk about mental-health problems: “I was dating this chick, and three months in, she tells me that she wears glasses. I was, like, listen, I’m not into all that Western medicine shit. If you want to see, then work at it. Figure out how not to be so myopic. You know?”) When Bamford turned forty, she had a hypomanic episode and was diagnosed as having bipolar II; for the next year, she ended up in and out of psychiatric hospitals.

These struggles are recounted in “Paralyzed by Hope: The Maria Bamford Story,” a new documentary directed by Judd Apatow and Neil Berkeley. The filmmakers interview not only Bamford but also her family and admirers in the industry. Stephen Colbert and Patton Oswalt tell the directors that Bamford is their favorite comedian. Ted Sarandos, a co-C.E.O. of Netflix, calls “Lady Dynamite,” a fictional TV show loosely based on Bamford’s life, his favorite Netflix original series.

In the documentary Bamford is living with her husband, the artist Scott Marvel Cassidy, in their home, in the L.A. suburb of Altadena. But not long after filming ended, around the time of the 2025 wildfires, the couple divorced. Earlier this month, I visited Bamford at her new place, a one-bedroom apartment in Highland Park. Bamford has been touring non-stop this fall; when we met, she was taking care of some laundry between trips to Brooklyn and British Columbia. Over the sounds of her dryer, we spoke about participating in documentaries, how much she made from her Netflix special, and how standup can function as a form of therapy. Our conversation has been condensed and edited for clarity.

You started filming the documentary four years ago. How did Judd Apatow first reach out to you with the idea?

He texted the night before, saying, “Hey, can I stop by and start filming?” I was, like, “Sure,” but I was texting my manager, “Hey, Judd Apatow wants me to shoot something. Can you talk to him about money?” My manager—the darling, the sweet, the extremely proficient and professional Bruce Smith—said, “But . . . it’s Judd.” So then I was, like, “O.K., I think I’m on my own here.” I texted Judd back. I think I asked for eight hundred dollars.

He brings you five hundred dollars in cash in the first scene of the film. And then he gives you ten thousand dollars at the very end, right?

Part of it was, they were going to shoot me performing one on one for a fan at a coffee shop, so I wanted to pay the fan. Then, I also wanted to pay the coffee-shop barista who was working that day—a hundred bucks or something. And then, I wanted to get paid.

It’s not really about the money. I guess I need it, but it’s also that I think of it as a job, and I don’t want to be a victim of my job, where it’s, like,  “Oh, you’re so lucky. Now you have to do this thing that is exhausting.” I was embarrassed to ask, but Judd brought the cash like he said he would, and I got to talk to him a little bit about his relationship with money.

Everyone has different, strong feelings about money. I’ve been in Debtors Anonymous for years, and that’s fascinating, because you’ll meet people who are millionaires, yet they’re filled with this feeling of lack, or panic. Like, “I can’t make my mortgage in the Pacific Palisades, but I can’t possibly take my kids out of this extremely expensive private school.” I think they’ve done research on this: if you’re living below the poverty line, you are often much more generous dollar per dollar than someone who has billions.

I feel I could be more generous. I have a bit of an obsession with giving away eleven per cent of my income, and I can never figure out how to calculate that. This year I was, like, “It should be from the gross. It should be from before taxes.” Now I’m, like, “But I think that’s too much!” Part of this is O.C.D.

I wonder if that self-doubt is also part of the reason people don’t give any of their money away. It’s almost easier to shut out the question of giving entirely than to make the difficult moral decision of, Exactly how much can I afford to give?

The hilarious part is, twenty years ago, I gave what was, for me, a giant chunk of money—I think it was fifty thousand dollars—to a charity I believe in. I got a call saying, “Would you like to come in and have lunch with us?” And I’m, like, “No! No, I don’t want to do that.” That’s my own fear of intimacy, of getting involved in a community. I never want to be a part of anything, because once you become a part of something, you realize how imperfect it is. Anyway, I’m not sure if I’m answering anything properly.

This is great. So, after you texted Judd asking about payment for the documentary, he was gracious and said, “O.K., I’ll bring it”?

Well, first he said, “I can’t pay you, because if it’s a documentary, I’m not supposed to pay you.” And then I looked it up and saw that it’s actually being addressed by the Screen Actors Guild, because there are so many more documentaries about entertainers. At least, that’s what I remember reading. I am not a fact checker.

Also I thought, This isn’t going to be a documentary. Every time I’ve been in a documentary related to show business, there’s story creation. Like, “The Comedians of Comedy” was one of the best things I’ve been a part of. But they edited it to look like I was hanging out with all the guys. That was not true. I didn’t know those guys that well, and was completely freaked out by human interaction. I was too shy. But they tried to make me look cool. Like, “Oh, Bamford’s hanging out,” and it’s, like, “Well, she’s by herself by the pool reading twelve-step literature.”

I remember the first time we shot something [for “Paralyzed by Hope”], my ex-husband Scott had a painting show downtown. The director, Neil Berkeley—who I adore, super nice—had us “do” the entrance to the art gallery several times.

So that’s the work of it. And I know it’s not hard work. I’ve cleaned houses. I’ve waited tables, poorly. That’s hard work.

When the directors started shooting, were there any conversations about how much creative control you would or wouldn’t have?

When did you see the film for the first time?

The première, at Sundance. I think they sent me a cut, but I didn’t watch it. I’ve already had the chance to tell my story a billion times, so it’s, like, if you wanna take a crack at it, all right.

Were there parts of this documentary that felt like “The Comedians of Comedy” in the sense that you were, like, “That’s an imposed story line”? I don’t mean in a bad way, but which parts, if any, felt more like reality TV to you?

I don’t know. What I kept pitching to them was, “This should be all about financial literacy and finding out what everybody’s making.” What Judd is making, what the cameraman is making, what the restaurant we’re shooting at illegally and not asking permission from is not making. . . . I find that super fascinating.

My parents passed away. They both died earlier than they had thought and left me with a pretty hefty sum. I could retire if I moved to Duluth, maybe. I could live pretty O.K., and definitely not tour as much as I tour now. But do I want to do that? No. I wanna be part of the capitalism, man. I wanna earn or burn. What’s that about? What is this false engine that’s also making a lot of creativity happen? I find that interesting. I don’t have any answers on it at all.

But [the directors] were not interested in doing that. They’re, like, Mm-hmm, no, not that. So, I think it just depends on what you’re interested in. I think Judd is interested in watching people do standup. A lot of the documentary is me doing comedy stuff, which is great.

I just shot something else, with a company called Blonde Medicine, and it’s all about open-book accounting. So I tell everyone what I’m being paid. I was paid three hundred and fifty thousand dollars for a Netflix special. It’s the most I’ve ever been paid for a Netflix special, and I was, like, “Oh, hey, how much is the opener being paid?” And they said, “Five hundred bucks.” And I’m, like, “Oh, well, O.K., I think I’m going to hand her five grand.” [Netflix did not respond to a request for comment.]

You’re making a standup special about open-book accounting?

It’s sort of an obsession, because it isn’t spoken about. At all.

One of the things that struck me in the documentary was related to money, and what people think should and shouldn’t be done with it. You do these one-on-one shows where you recruit people from the internet and pay them an hourly fee to watch you rehearse new material. There’s a sequence in the film where a bunch of other professional comedians express their shock at the fact that you’re paying your fans, rather than the other way around. I was curious what you made of their surprise.

I assume people who are huge stars—Conan O’Brien, Judd Apatow—have staff or writers who will go through the jokes with them or who are working on things with them, which is exactly what I’m doing, but it’s just sort of one-offs instead.

You must know from talking to people in your industry: Do people have editors? Do people have coaches?

I think everybody has something different. I know that the co-writer for Jim Gaffigan is his wife, so they write his material together. My friend Jackie Kashian was hired to write jokes for standup, for a comedian who didn’t have the time. I’m very self-protective, so it’s ideal for me to do it on my own. There is the risk, of course, that people won’t laugh. But even if they complain or say, “Oh, that sucked,” I can go [mock-pensively], “Yeah, man. Life is disappointing on the regular.”

You’ve said that the most important thing for you isn’t if someone else finds the joke funny, but if you do.

What are you looking for in the reactions of strangers during these one-on-one rehearsals? Or is the goal to hire an accountability partner?

It’s only accountability. The main thing is that I’m getting out the words in the way that I’m planning to say them, and rehearsing. I don’t have the ambition to rehearse stuff on my own, so I have a babysitter watch me go through the jokes.

What’s wonderful about it is people are not that impressed by fame. People are doing their laundry, they’re picking up their kids, they’re driving. They’re barely listening to me.

The risk that someone doesn’t laugh doesn’t feel devastating, because they’re just one person.

It’s also really meaningful to me, because I get to talk to people who I would never get to talk to. I don’t go out after shows, because I do talk about mental health and I get overwhelmed, if somebody after is, like, “Hey, I need to tell you, my dad just died of suicide, we decided to come to the show.” I just don’t want to give anybody that blank face of “Thanks for coming to the show!” I want to be there for somebody.

You’ve talked extensively about your struggles with obsessive-compulsive disorder. And in the documentary, and in your memoir, you describe never having heard the term O.C.D. until you Googled “unwanted thoughts” when you were thirty-five?

That’s so shocking—no one before then had been, like, “That might be O.C.D.”?

No. I went to a therapist in Glendale who was just a regs therapist, and I tried to tell her about my fears, my intrusive thoughts, and she was, like, “I’m a mandated reporter.”

Oh, that was the therapist who called 911 on you. [The therapist was not familiar with O.C.D. and mistook Bamford’s obsessive fear about committing an act of violence as a threat.]

Yeah. So then I didn’t go for a couple more years. I got a book called “The Imp of the Mind.” Then I looked online, and saw the International OCD Foundation, which was great for me because I’d felt so alone as a kid. It was, like, I have this weird thing that I can’t tell anybody about. And then, once I told a professional O.C.D. person, they were, like [mimes looking up from a notepad], “Uh-huh. [In a bored voice.] So you wanna rape and kill people?” [Yawns dramatically.]

The therapist assigned you this cognitive-behavioral-therapy technique called “flooding.” The practice involves writing down your intrusive thoughts and fears, and even recording yourself talking about them, and playing that recording back to yourself. The idea is that by exposing yourself to these anxieties, you begin to rob them of their power. I wondered, while hearing you describe this: Isn’t that also what standup is?

It totally is. The thing becomes kind of boring and has lost all meaning to it. It’s totally therapeutic.

Saying the shameful thing out loud is a form of exposure therapy for you.

I heard a joke that really made me feel better, where the comedian said, “You know, sometimes I look at my dog’s butt and it’s kinda sexy.” [Laughs.] And I was, like, “Oh, that’s such a creepy thought, and thank you for sharing.” But I can only say those jokes in front of people who are fans or into mental health. I will tank everywhere else. I will bomb right in my neighborhood; I just did at a karaoke night. There are plenty of people who don’t want to hear the kind of material that I’m doing, or think it’s creepy, or weird, or gross, or boring, or tedious, or humorless.

You have permission in standup comedy to talk about things that you might not be able to talk about in other settings.

There are comics out there who are currently touring who have been accused of sexual assault, and nothing is really being done, especially by the comedians themselves. Like, they don’t say, “Hey, I actually went through a program. Here’s what I’m doing as a living amends by giving a million dollars to each of these people whose life I affected.” There’s none of that. I feel sad about the comedy community, because these guys are still working clubs, and no one’s protected who’s working there, at least as far as I can tell.

I think comedy is where you could at least talk about it. It’s where you could say, “Hey, there’s obviously a huge problem with people being isolated with these sexual compulsions.” These comedians who are so good at expressing specific experiences, isn’t there a way they could bring that experience to the world? Like, it’s the most taboo topic ever, and no one wants to hear about it.  Let’s not isolate these people. Let’s find out what’s going on in their minds.

I’ve been an addict—I was bulimic—I know the secretive behaviors. I’ve eaten all the food in somebody else’s refrigerator. I understand doing shameful things, and having those things revealed is a bummer, but it could also be a gift.

You share personal experiences onstage that are painful or embarrassing, but I’m assuming that at the time those things are happening to you, you’re not, like, “This could be great material.” Is the ability to turn something into a joke in a set a sign that you’ve processed the experience?

I would hope so. Some things I’m still not sure about even as I record them. I’m, like, should I? But I’ve read “The Artist’s Way.” You have no control over what blossoms.

I haven’t read it. I have it in my house, though.

Of course you do! Everybody has it. It was life-changing because it was, like, Oh, I could say, “I’m a comedian.” It helped me define that for myself. But some of it is very white privilege-y, very much, like [in a fancy woman’s voice], “Oh, my gosh, I just, I thought of blueberries, and then there was a bowl of blueberries.” It’s like, O.K., are you a fucking rich white lady living in the Hollywood Hills? The odds are pretty good there’s going to be a bowl of blueberries near you.

You recently got divorced. In an interview you did at the start of this year, you said you didn’t know how to talk or joke about your divorce yet.

A few weeks ago, I went to your show, where you brought up your divorce. I was wondering how it felt to start talking about it.

It feels O.K. now. We got very clear in our divorce decree that I will not talk about our divorce onstage beyond the facts of it. I have this verbal platform, and he’s a painter. He doesn’t really have a bunch of people he can spout off to. That felt fair. Also, there are ways to talk about it that don’t involve my ex, but that are about the universal.

The things that were untenable with us were that our views of the world were very different. The joke is, I grew up in Minnesota, so I see everyone as a new friend. Like, “Oh, noooo, we gotta let these two cars into our lane because they might be families of guinea pigs trying to go to a museum!” And he’s from Philadelphia, super tough, had a rough childhood, so for him, everything is incoming. Everything is death asking him to play chess.

He’s not wrong, and I’m not wrong. But it’s just a measure of whether you can live with each other. That kind of came to a head when the fires happened. We had a joyful divorce, and we have a good relationship sharing the doggies.

The documentary is a time capsule, in a way, because it shows the two of you together in your old house in Altadena, before the fires of 2025. What’s it been like to move here and live alone?

It’s been lovely. I have a friend who lives on the other side of this complex, and she’s awesome. All my neighbors are super friendly. Almost everybody’s Spanish-speaking, so I can practice my Spanish a little bit.

We owned a very beautiful house. It’s still there, in Altadena. But it was too isolated. I don’t like suburbs, it turns out. I don’t like that much house upkeep. I loved having people over. I love parties. But in this neighborhood all I have to do is rent a party place. There’s this co-working place that used to be a church, and that’s where I did a singles event the other week.

One hundred and fifty people showed up. A third were men, which was really impressive.

Two of the guys were shaking when they came. It was really adorable. I was proud of everybody who came, and I threw a chunk of change at it, so I think the next time I would want to see if it could support itself and have it truly be a community potluck.

Yes. Two couples left together. Nobody asked me out, which is hilarious. I wasn’t sure what to do about that. My sister was, like, “Maybe it seemed like you were too intimidating.” And I’m, like, “That seems like a punishing point of view. I’m just wandering around, tremoring, just like anyone.”

I’ve gone on a lot of internet dates, which are wonderful, too, but also can be very hopeless. I had a date with one guy, perfectly lovely. It was da, da, da, da, boop, da, da, da, boop, boop, boop, beep. Then he said, “So, I saw your comedy. You’re crazy!” It reminded me of somebody introducing me in the nineties: “This lady, she’s schizophrenic!” No, I’m a middle-of-the-road comedian who does voices. That’s all that’s happening here.

You’ve said that you’ve always been performing for your parents on some level. Your father, who appears in parts of the documentary, passed away in 2023, a few years after your mom. I was curious how their deaths have changed your relationship to your imagined audience.

My dad would watch me do whatever I wanted. I would practice doing speeches for him when I was a kid.

I was just listening to NPR, where Terry Gross was interviewing Yiyun Li, the writer whose two sons died of suicide. She was saying there’s a difference between sadness and depression. I think I feel that about my parents. I’ll never stop feeling sad that they’re not here. I shouldn’t compare myself to the lady losing her two sons. But it really spoke to me about how you never get over grief, but can still enjoy life.

In some ways, I know my parents didn’t always like my material, so they weren’t perfect at being the best audience or the most supportive. My mom had actually told me that when your parents die, there is some kind of freedom. When she was alive, my mom was always, like [imitates Marilyn Bamford]: “Honey, put on some lipstick. What’s going on with your hair? Are you not getting it done?” I don’t have to think about that anymore. That my mom might be disappointed in me in that way, or that my dad thinks I’m lazy.

I need to ask you about the vibrator bit with your dad. Right after your mom died, your dad tried to give your mom’s vibrators away to you and your sister, as though he was passing down a sacred family heirloom.

Do you feel like he knew how funny he was being when he handed his daughters a shoebox and said, “Your mom would’ve wanted you to have these”?

Oh, no. I think my dad, especially during that moment, was very sincere. I did kind of want to take them, but then my sister put them in the garbage. I was, like, “Well, that’s going to be fun for someone.” I still feel guilty for using that as a joke.

Your dad happily talks about it in the documentary, though. The filmmakers interview him, and he reiterates, “I really thought it was something that I could share with the two of you.”

[In the voice of her father, Joel Bamford.] “These things are expensive!”

My sister seemed slightly grossed out, but I thought it was very thoughtful. ♦

Such scenarios may become more common as militaries continue to deploy a once-theoretical technology — AI-assisted or autonomous weapons, capable of making both targeting and launch decisions on their own — on real battlefields around the globe. In this case, the government and private sector are pointing the finger at each other. Some US Central Command personnel, according to Pentagon officials who spoke to Bloomberg, expected Maven to flag stale information or inconsistencies in the underlying intelligence assembled for potential targets. This debate underscores the fact that AI and autonomous weapons are raising a new host of unresolved issues within the law, even as they’re actively assuming positions on the battlefield. To unpack some of these issues, I spoke with international law expert Allen Weiner, a senior lecturer at Stanford Law School, where he teaches courses on the law of armed conflict. We discussed the difficulties in attributing criminal responsibility, achieving justice for those harmed by lethal autonomous weapons, and much more. How do autonomous weapons challenge traditional understandings of responsibility when it comes to the law of armed conflict?

For most of the 20 years I’ve been teaching at Stanford, there’s been a real fascination and fixation on the problem of autonomous weapons. But the truth is that up until pretty recently, nobody really had any [of them]. It was more of an idea than a problem. It’s only in recent years that we have begun to see the deployment of tools that seem to have the capability [to make decisions themselves], so this does raise the question about compliance with illegal orders and where in the chain responsibility lies.

There are different theories about this. Some people say that if we’re engaged in an illegal attack, that has to be because of the programming or the code, so the manufacturers are somehow responsible for committing war crimes.

Other people would say no, that’s not the case, especially if the military has said the tool is not allowed to make very substantial autonomous judgments, or can’t say no. Then it’s basically the commander, at the point they decide to deploy this weapon, who might be responsible for the illegal acts, even without knowing in advance what they’re going to be.

So to finish where I began, this does present a big challenge to our traditional understanding of responsibility for targeting decisions and of what it would mean if there were an obligation [for autonomous weapons] to say no.

The real issue is not the obligation to say no, but this: If you train a tool and it wages war in a manner that’s unlawful, where does responsibility for that lie?

If a commander gives a clearly illegal order, such as, “blow up this school,” it seems clear that responsibility falls on the commander. I can imagine two different scenarios. One is that you say, “We’re going to carry out an attack against our enemy, and we want to hit them really, really hard.” Your tool has been trained to say, “We’re not allowed to hit civilian objects,” so it might say, “I’m not going to hit a school.” But if the military has said, “Don’t refuse any targets,” then it might override the way it’s been trained.

But if we were doing something like a war crimes prosecution, where we’re trying to hold an individual criminally responsible, that would make quite a difference. We would have to deal with all of these difficult questions about responsibility and mental state that you and I are struggling with right now.

So, in most cases, the state would have to take responsibility regardless.

Right, and there are lots of [International Court of Justice] cases involving different aspects of the use of force. The principle that you can bring state-to-state claims for violation of these rules is quite well established.

I think that question is about when the connection of these private companies to the war effort, in a way we’ve never really seen before, meets the standards under international law.

A civilian loses their immunity when they directly participate in hostilities, or if they assume what is known as a “continuous combatant function.” Are there arguments to be made that some of these private companies are participating in hostilities by virtue of supplying the tools the military is using to wage war? What’s really fueling the recent generosity boom

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Generosity is in the air, or so it would seem. The Trump administration recently mailed a round of $500 checks to almost a million users of Healthcare.gov. The AI rivals OpenAI and Anthropic have promised to share their (eventual) wealth with the public beyond investors, and Anthropic this week got a bit more specific, proposing a Public Benefit Fund pegged at 5% of the company’s overall value. And never to be outdone, Elon Musk promptly pledged to give “every kid in America” a share in SpaceX. 

What explains the abrupt rise of these largesse Olympics among some of our most powerful institutions? Presumably there’s actual generosity involved. But let’s face it: Each of these schemes doubles as reputational marketing.

Naturally, the details vary. The government checks are the most straightforward. They are accompanied by a letter ostensibly from President Trump, attacking Democrats and promising to lower healthcare costs. If that’s not a blatant enough pitch to vote Republican in the midterm election, the White House has also promised a $5,000 “Trump dividend” cash payment to every adult U.S. citizen—but only if the GOP keeps control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate. So far this blunt generosity-as-persuasion approach doesn’t seem wildly effective; voters appear highly skeptical that the Trump dividend will materialize under any circumstances.

For the artificial intelligence giants, the scenario is more nuanced. For some time now, the enormous promises attached to the technology have been accompanied by warnings of enormous risks. These range from taking our jobs to energy hog data centers to the more sci-fi-ish possibilities of wiping out humanity. The leaders of some of these companies haven’t exactly been shy about darker potentials—to the point that some have accused them of practicing a kind of doomsday marketing.  

Finding some way to share a portion of whatever wealth AI creates might take the edge off at least some of the doomer worries. It also gives the companies a happier talking point that spotlights their potential impact on life, and the stock market in particular. Anthropic now says it will commit 5% of its shares to the creation of the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, a limited liability corporation. Anthropic is reportedly eyeing an IPO, and was valued recently at nearly $1 trillion, so this new entity would not be trivial. The company doesn’t yet have concrete plans for the fund, but has floated labor market impacts as a potential focus.  

This approach has some precedents. Salesforce, Google, and others have set aside corporate equity for social purposes, and Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan have pledged most of their Meta shares toward a philanthropic LLC. But at this stage, at least, Anthropic’s version strikes some as more performative than transparent or generous. “Trying,” as one critic put it, “to curry public favor.”

Of course they are trying to curry public favor! That’s the point: Just as political actors depend on public sentiment leaning their way, so do high-profile businesses whose mission entails a plan to reshape the society. Which brings us to Musk, who has become both a political figure as well as an aspiring society-shaping tech entrepreneur.

Musk’s announcement was in part responding directly to Anthropic, criticizing its plan for funneling equity through a vehicle it ultimately controls, and arguing for giving shares directly to the public. Granting one share apiece to “every kid” in America would involve about 73 million people under 18, adding up to about $11 billion in stock. (Musk is worth upwards of $900 billion, depending on market conditions.) He would apparently use the government’s new so-called “Trump accounts” to manage the giveaway. Gwynne Shotwell, president of SpaceX, and her husband earlier promised a share apiece to 2 million American children in the program, and Michael and Susan Dell have pledged $6.25 billion from their own foundation to help seed the program.

Many details of Musk’s gift aren’t clear—but the message is hard to miss. His generosity is personal, record-setting, unambiguous, and, not incidentally, smarter than Anthropic’s (in Musk’s view). Musk has long been image conscious, and has more reason than ever to try to burnish his public persona. Aside from being the world’s richest man, he’s attracted increasing attention to his politics and geopolitical influence, from spending millions in campaign contributions on midterm races to accusations that his SpaceX throttled Ukraine’s access to the vital Starshield service associated with the company’s Starlink satellite network. 

It makes sense that a nonelected individual with global influence would want to project benevolence from time to time. And there’s something almost flex-like in handing out shares to every child in the country; even Musk’s philanthropy is a little intimidating.  

To be clear, generosity is a positive force, and there’s no call to be cynical. A gift does not become economically meaningless because it generates good publicity, including warm fuzzies toward the giver. But then again, the image payoff that comes with the announcement of generosity is immediate. What ultimately matters is when—or even whether—the actual generosity manifests.

When a teen posts a selfie, how much filter is 'too much' filter?

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How much filter is too much filter for kids and teens posting selfies on social media?

That is the question state attorneys general across the country are grappling with following a landmark settlement with Meta this summer. As part of the agreement, the social media giant agreed to block some digital beauty filters for minors — specifically those mimicking extreme transformations like cosmetic procedures or heavy makeup.

Now, the burden falls on state officials to tell Meta where to draw the line. But defining where harmless fun ends and harmful manipulation begins is far from straightforward.

For teenagers navigating the digital landscape, the pressure of online perfection is a daily reality. Much of that pressure has been driven by online beauty trends like "Instagram face." Coined in 2019 by The New Yorker writer Jia Tolentino, the phrase refers to a look characterized by a small button nose, high cheekbones and pouty lips. While traditionally only achievable in real life through plastic surgery or extreme makeup, it can be applied instantly online using beauty filters.

Mia Lerner, a 17-year-old high school senior in California and a youth body image activist, knows the toll these digital tools can take. She told NPR experimenting with beauty filters contributed to her eating disorder and left her struggling with her self-image.

"I've even tried to fix my nose with AI," Lerner said. "My face looks a lot better, I think, in [the] photo that I manipulated with these beauty filters. But I'm still living with this nose that I see in the mirror."

Lerner welcomes the aspect of the Meta settlement that aims to restrict beauty filters for minors. "It's a very important step in the right direction," Lerner said.

However, the agreement reached in August between Meta and 47 U.S. states — plus Washington D.C. and three territories — does not institute a blanket ban on all digital effects.

Filters that transform users into playful characters, like elves or puppies, will remain available for young people. But so will filters offering subtle beautification effects — such as smoothing skin tone or adding a slight eye glow.

It is this category of relatively minor editing that particularly concerns Lerner.

"When I see something that has been so obviously AI-generated, it's very easy for me to logically say, 'Well, that's obviously been manipulated so I can't compare myself to that,'" Lerner said. "But when I see these videos of people who are just slightly edited, it's a lot easier for me to go down that comparison path."

Meta has previously implemented measures to manage beauty filters, which are increasingly powered by artificial intelligence.

The tech giant has already banned filters that explicitly promote cosmetic surgery, such as those overlaying pre-surgery marker lines onto skin. It has also shut down its platform for third-party creators of these tools, introduced mandatory labels for AI-generated content, and deployed automated AI detection systems. Despite these systems, harmful aesthetic filters and features that keep children trapped in an endless cycle of scrolling, clicking, and staying online persistently slipped past these guardrails, as a federal judge wrote in a summary judgement denying Meta's defense this summer.

Meta is not the only tech company grappling with teen access to beauty filters. TikTok also restricts users under the age of 18 from using them. Snapchat does not restrict teen access to filters. Neither company responded to NPR's request for comment. YouTube told NPR it does not have any filters. But there's nothing stopping users from posting images or videos made with third party filters to the platform — or onto TikTok and Instagram, for that matter.

Meanwhile, state attorneys general are reaching the end of a two-month window they set themselves to provide Meta with guidance in the form of a letter on what constitutes a bannable filter — and what does not.

They face a moving target, as digital beauty standards evolve rapidly.

"If the states want to define what is a beauty filter, they should define what is beauty," said Florencia Solari, a creative technologist who has spent years developing AI and augmented reality experiences for global brands.

Solari noted that trend cycles move quickly. "Instagram face" is already falling out of favor. "People are starting to move away from that because it's starting to look a bit clownish," Solari said. "Now it's going towards extreme thinness. So that's another problem."

As a result, the tools that are used to achieve the latest look are therefore also changing. So what might be branded by lawmakers as an "extreme" beauty filter in 2026 will likely be different in 2027.

While beauty standards are changing quickly, so is technology.

Instead of sitting in boardrooms debating pixel thresholds for cheekbones, Kelly Frasier, the co-author of a recent study highlighting the negative impacts of social media on body issues in adolescents, said legislators should consider the fact that teens will soon be able to use AI to create entire fake universes around themselves, not just change the way they look. So they should focus on broader AI regulation for minors.

"I think that legislators are asking the wrong question if they're asking where to draw the line for beauty filters," said Frasier. "And to be honest, filters will probably become completely obsolete within the next few years."

On top of the technological and cultural challenges, legal experts warn that individual states may struggle to reach a consensus on what does and does not constitute what the settlement agreement describes as a "cosmetic procedure filter."

"What we don't really have a sense of is what happens if the states disagree with each other?" said David Greene, senior counsel at the Electronic Frontier Foundation. "What if one state finds, you know, eyebrow lifting to be completely acceptable and another one thinks it's awful?"

NPR reached out to the attorneys general offices in all four plaintiff states that led the trial — California, Colorado, Kentucky, and New Jersey — for an update on the guidelines. But officials either declined to comment or did not respond.

Beyond the U.S., some international regulators are hoping to avoid discussions around beauty filter definitions entirely.

In Europe, the proposed KIDS Act — designed to regulate youth social media usage — takes a sweeping approach.

In that plan, "Beauty filters – not extreme beauty filters; beauty filters full stop – are forbidden for minors," said European Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier. Regnier said EU legislators are closely watching the implementation of the U.S. settlement. "We want child-friendly services for our children. A beauty filter has nothing to do with a child-friendly service," Regnier said.

Meta says that it plans to enforce the ban on "cosmetic procedure filters" by early next year.

For youth activists like Lerner, the hope is that tech companies and regulators will include teens in these discussions. "So often it's just the government or these companies in these conversations," Lerner said. "The company should be asking us for our actual perspectives, like 'Are people actually using these extreme AI filters or is that not going to make a difference?' 'Do we need to target something else?'"

Meta said the company has partnered with teens over the years, but did not respond to NPR's question asking if it is doing so currently.
