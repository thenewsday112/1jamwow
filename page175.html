40 years later, Chernobyl's nuclear fallout is not what scientists expected

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Four decades after the Chernobyl (known today as Chornobyl) nuclear disaster, microscopic radioactive particles remain scattered throughout the surrounding soil. The explosions that devastated the reactor released enormous quantities of debris, leaving behind contamination that continues to pose serious health risks. Even after 40 years, particles measuring just 8 to 50 micrometers remain highly radioactive, and entering affected areas still requires protective clothing.

Scientists have now taken a closer look at these tiny remnants and uncovered a surprising finding: some have preserved much of their original internal structure despite decades of exposure to the environment.

"There are three classes of these particles," explains Tobias Weissenborn, a physicist and doctoral candidate at Leibniz University Hannover. "First, there are particles that are chemically and physically still very similar to the nuclear fuel uranium dioxide. Then, there are particles that are partially or fully encased in, or completely fused with, their zirconium layer."

The second category originated during the extreme temperatures reached inside the reactor. As the nuclear fuel melted, it combined with zirconium, a highly resistant metal used in the fuel's protective outer layer.

"The third type of particle was created when the graphite-moderator caught fire," Weissenborn adds.

Under normal operating conditions, the graphite moderator slows fast neutrons, helping sustain the nuclear chain reaction. During the disaster, however, the graphite ignited and continued burning for ten days.

"In the process, the fuel oxidized into various uranium oxides" - such as U3O8.

Unlike more stable materials, this uranium oxide is mechanically fragile and can readily break apart into microscopic fragments. Wind can then carry these radioactive particles through the environment, where inhaling them presents serious health hazards.

Scientists Examine Chornobyl's Radioactive Dust in Unprecedented Detail

Despite decades of research, scientists still do not fully understand why radioactive particles from Chornobyl break down at different speeds. As these materials weather, they can produce several forms of uranium oxide, making their long term behavior difficult to predict.

Investigating these changes requires exceptionally precise equipment, including synchrotron X-ray diffraction, a technique that reveals the arrangement of atoms inside crystalline materials.

Dr. Christoph Hennig, a crystallographer at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf (HZDR), has now completed the first successful structural analysis of radioactive fragments of this kind at the Rossendorf Beamline in Grenoble.

Working with Weissenborn, Hennig analyzed six particles recovered from the Chornobyl region. By identifying the different crystalline phases within them, the researchers gained information that could help determine how quickly radioactive substances escape into the surrounding environment.

"The colleagues from Hanover and I used various methods to isolate the particles from Ukrainian soil samples and attach them to tungsten electrodes," Weissenborn explains.

Because the samples were highly radioactive, they had to be enclosed within several layers of containment before being transported to Grenoble. There, Hennig's research team used X-ray diffraction to examine their internal structures by measuring how X-rays scattered as they passed through the material.

"First, we had to figure out how to examine the particle structure," Christoph Hennig explains. "Ultimately, we fully rotated the particles in an X-ray beam focused down to 100 micrometers - about the thickness of a human hair. We measured each particle from 2,000 different angles to seamlessly capture all the reflections."

The measurements allowed the scientists to distinguish different uranium oxide phases within the radioactive fragments, revealing details that had previously been inaccessible.

A Surprising Discovery About 40-Year-Old Nuclear Fallout

The results revealed something unexpected. Despite spending four decades in the environment, the nuclear fuel inside the six examined particles had retained much of its original crystal structure.

This suggests that at least some of the radioactive debris produced by the Chornobyl disaster is more chemically stable than researchers previously believed.

That stability could have important environmental implications. Rather than rapidly releasing radioactive substances, the particles appear capable of retaining fission products within their structures. If this behavior occurs more widely, it could help limit the movement of radioactive contaminants into nearby soil and water.

However, the researchers emphasize that their findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Chornobyl's radioactive contamination is becoming harmless.

"However, every single particle has a different structure," warns Weissenborn, "and our experiment only studied six such particles from two different locations."

The relatively small sample means the findings cannot yet establish how radioactive particles behave throughout the entire contaminated region. To understand their stability more comprehensively, scientists would need to investigate many additional particles collected from a much broader range of locations.

Why Chornobyl's Radioactive Legacy Remains Unpredictable

Even with substantially more data, predicting the long term dangers of Chornobyl's radioactive debris would remain challenging. Individual particles can differ in composition, structure, and resistance to environmental weathering, potentially affecting when they release radioactive materials.

"And even if we obtained some averages at some point, we still wouldn't be able to make universal statements about health risks in the region," Weissenborn cautions. "Because even if the particles decay in a largely uniform patten, there will always be outliers, more persistent particles, that will release radionuclides at a later point in time."

These persistent particles could continue releasing radioactive substances long after other remnants have broken down, complicating efforts to assess future contamination and health risks.

For that reason, the new findings do not suggest that restrictions on the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone can be removed anytime soon.

Research into the disaster's enduring consequences is continuing. Weissenborn and Hennig are already conducting additional experiments focused on highly radioactive transuranic phases found in the remaining debris. Their work could provide a clearer picture of how Chornobyl's nuclear materials change over time and how long their environmental effects may persist.

Nearly 3,000 exploding stars challenge our understanding of the Universe

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The largest collection of exploding white dwarf stars ever compiled is providing fresh evidence that dark energy, the mysterious force responsible for the Universe’s accelerating expansion, may change over time. The findings challenge a central assumption of modern cosmology and could help scientists better understand how the Universe evolves.

An international research team led by scientists at the University of Queensland’s School of Mathematics and Physics brought together observations of 2,884 Type Ia supernovae. These powerful stellar explosions serve as important tools for measuring distances across the Universe, allowing astronomers to trace its expansion history.

PhD candidate Ryan Camilleri said the project establishes a new international standard for supernova cosmology. By bringing decades of measurements into a unified dataset, researchers have created their most detailed picture yet of how cosmic expansion has changed over time.

“We’ve rebuilt 3 decades of astronomical observations into a single, consistent framework,” Mr. Camilleri said.

“We combined our data with other cosmic measurements including relic light from the Big Bang and maps of how galaxies are distributed through space.

“Instead of confirming the standard model of cosmology which assumes dark energy is fixed and unchanging, we have more evidence that dark energy may change over time.”

The new collection brings together historical supernova measurements and findings from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) published in 2024.

To make observations collected over different periods directly comparable, the scientists revisited older data using updated analytical methods. Advances in understanding supernova behavior allowed the team to improve the consistency and accuracy of measurements gathered over decades.

“Over the years we’ve learned a lot more about how supernovae behave so we’ve been able to go back and apply that improved understanding to older data,” Mr. Camilleri said.

The researchers also had to account for differences among the telescopes used to collect the observations. Other factors, including cosmic dust and the masses of galaxies, can influence the light astronomers receive from distant supernovae and therefore affect distance measurements.

“Extensive work has been done to link observations from different telescopes with different capacities and take into account issues such as cosmic dust and galaxy mass which can affect the light coming from a supernova.

“We also incorporated more subtle effects such as gravitational lensing, which is the bending and magnification of light around large objects as it travels from a supernova to Earth.”

Astrophysicist Professor Tamara Davis said the expanded dataset represents an important advance in efforts to understand the nature of dark energy.

Under the standard model of cosmology, dark energy is treated as a constant property of the Universe. However, recent observations have raised questions about whether that assumption fully explains how cosmic expansion has evolved.

“Our supernova data from DES in 2024 first showed hints that dark energy may be time varying, and this new compilation also sees a deviation from the standard model although in a slightly different direction,” Professor Davis said.

The findings are especially interesting because a separate astronomical project has also produced evidence suggesting that dark energy might not remain constant.

“Similarly results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) found hints of variations in dark energy in its surveys of relic sound waves from the early Universe.

“So, 2 completely independent measurements have found hints of time variation in dark energy, challenging the standard model that dark energy doesn't change.

Although the different measurements do not point to exactly the same departure from the standard model, they provide additional reasons to investigate whether dark energy changes as the Universe ages.

Understanding dark energy could have implications extending far beyond the expansion of the Universe. Researchers believe that learning more about its behavior might also provide insights into one of the greatest unresolved problems in theoretical physics: reconciling gravity with quantum physics.

“All of this research may also hold the clue to explain how gravity and quantum physics fit together.

“We know these 2 theories are each immensely successful in their own realms, so if we can figure out how to put them together that would be a huge step in theoretical physics.”

The researchers plan to expand the dataset as additional observations become available. One important source will be the Dark Energy Bedrock All-Sky Supernova program (DEBASS), which is identifying hundreds of supernovae at distances closer to Earth than those surveyed by DES.

Incorporating these nearby explosions will provide further measurements that scientists can use to investigate the Universe’s expansion and test whether dark energy truly changes over time.

The cosmology findings are available on arXiv here, while the accompanying measurements of host galaxy masses can be found on arXiv here.

The new results build on decades of work by hundreds of astronomers and researchers worldwide. Contributing projects include the Dark Energy Survey, Foundation Supernova Survey, PanStarrs I, Sloan Digital Sky Survey, Supernova Legacy Survey, Carnegie Supernova Project, and the Center for Astrophysics supernova survey.

Cambridge scientists create a $10 chip that could put a medical lab on your wrist

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A new optical sensor costing just $10 could bring laboratory-level precision to wearable health monitors, potentially allowing people to track blood glucose without needles. The technology could also transform food testing, industrial quality control, and environmental monitoring.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge and GlitterinTech (a start-up founded by the same research group) have developed a new type of optical spectrometer that combines exceptional accuracy with a remarkably small size and low cost. Measuring just centimeters across, the device could be incorporated into portable electronics and wearable health technology. Its potential applications range from monitoring important health markers in real time to identifying materials and detecting changes in chemical composition.

Optical spectrometers work by analyzing light to reveal information about the substances it interacts with. They are essential tools in medicine, manufacturing, chemical testing, and environmental research. However, making these instruments smaller has traditionally meant compromising their performance. Compact spectrometers often have limited measurement ranges, reduced resolution, or lower accuracy, making them better suited to identifying substances than performing precise measurements.

The researchers believe their new design could overcome these limitations. Known as a convolutional spectrometer, it uses a mathematical principle called the convolution theorem to analyze light in a fundamentally different way. According to the team, this approach delivers performance that surpasses existing types of spectrometers in several key areas, including designs based on light dispersion, Fourier transforms, and computational reconstruction.

A New Way to Analyze Light With Extraordinary Precision

Traditional spectrometers generally separate incoming light into its component wavelengths or use complex calculations to reconstruct its spectrum. The new device takes another approach by performing a mathematical operation called convolution directly on the incoming light.

To accomplish this, the researchers arranged optical components with repeating spectral responses in a sequence. These components can include unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometers or micro-ring resonators. By adjusting them in proportion to one another, the system can shift its response to different wavelengths in a controlled, linear fashion. Fast Fourier transforms are then used to accurately recover the light spectrum.

"The key insight was to go back to the mathematics and ask whether there was a fundamentally cleaner way to retrieve spectra," said Dr. Chunhui Yao, a lead author of the study. "By using the convolution theorem directly in the optical domain, we avoid many of the limitations that have held miniaturized spectrometers back. This gives us high precision, strong noise tolerance, and very low computational overhead, all in a compact and low-cost system."

The researchers built the spectrometer using a silicon nitride photonic platform and integrated it with onboard electronics. It operates across a broad range of near-infrared wavelengths (1200-1700 nm) and can collect and process measurements in less than a second.

Another important advantage is its ability to expand its measurement capabilities without major hardware changes. Because the system uses repeating optical patterns, its spectral bandwidth can theoretically be extended almost without limit. Adding more optical components in sequence can also increase its resolution exponentially.

A $10 Sensor That Could Transform Food Testing and Healthcare

To evaluate the device outside purely theoretical measurements, the researchers tested it on a variety of materials and substances. The results demonstrated its potential for applications that normally depend on larger, more expensive laboratory instruments.

When used to identify plastics, pharmaceuticals, coffee, flour, and tea, the spectrometer achieved a 100% classification success rate in the reported tests. It also measured concentrations in water-based and organic solutions with accuracies of approximately 0.01%, exceeding the performance of commercial benchtop spectrometers in those experiments.

The healthcare demonstrations were particularly promising. Under conditions designed to reflect real physiological environments, the device accurately measured several human biomarkers, including skin moisture, blood alcohol, blood lactate, and blood glucose.

The researchers also demonstrated noninvasive glucose tracking over an extended period in one participant. Although further testing would be needed before the technology could become a practical medical product, the finding suggests a possible route toward monitoring blood sugar without repeatedly piercing the skin.

"These biomedical demonstrations are particularly exciting," said Prof. Qixiang Cheng, who led the project. "What makes this work stand out is not just performance in the lab, but technical readiness. Dr. Chunhui Yao's contribution was crucial in translating a mathematical concept into a fully packaged, robust system that operates reliably across temperature extremes and real-world conditions. That combination is what opens the door to practical deployment."

The sensor also demonstrated impressive resistance to temperature changes. It continued operating reliably between -20°C and 80°C, an unusually wide temperature range for a miniature spectrometer. This durability could be especially valuable for wearable devices, factory equipment, and sensors operating outdoors.

Why a Simpler Design Could Be a Major Breakthrough

The new spectrometer's advantages extend beyond its accuracy, size, and price. Its internal design also reduces the amount of computing power needed to analyze incoming light.

Many existing miniature spectrometers depend on complicated calibration procedures and demanding algorithms to reconstruct spectral information. These methods can become unreliable when measurements contain noise, and they often require substantial processing resources.

The Cambridge team's convolution-based approach avoids much of this complexity. Because the measurements follow a linear mathematical relationship, the system can recover spectra rapidly and consistently while using relatively little computational power.

"This is a beautiful example of how elegant engineering can unlock real impact," said Prof. Richard Penty, who contributed to the photonic system design and integration. "The architecture is simple, scalable, and manufacturable, yet it delivers precision that rivals or exceeds much larger instruments. Dr. Chunhui Yao played a central role in bringing together the photonic design, system integration, and experimental validation that made this possible."

Its relatively simple construction could also make the technology easier to manufacture on a large scale, potentially bringing sophisticated optical measurements to devices where conventional spectrometers would be too bulky or expensive.

Bringing Laboratory-Grade Measurements to Everyday Devices

The researchers envision a future in which accurate optical sensing becomes a routine feature of everyday technology rather than something largely confined to specialized laboratories.

In industrial settings, inexpensive spectrometers could be installed throughout manufacturing facilities to continuously check material composition and product quality. Similar sensors could monitor food freshness, identify substances, and analyze environmental conditions on a much larger scale than is currently practical.

Healthcare could see equally significant changes. Compact sensors might eventually enable wearables to measure hydration levels, detect signs of alcohol intoxication, track biomarkers related to physical fitness, or continuously monitor glucose levels for people managing diabetes.

"Our vision is to make spectrometry as ubiquitous as temperature or motion sensing," added Prof. Qixiang Cheng. "This work shows that high-quality spectral information doesn't have to be confined to the laboratory -- it can be embedded directly into the technologies people use every day."

As photonic technology becomes smaller and more advanced, the convolutional spectrometer provides a promising example of how mathematical insights and practical engineering can make sophisticated scientific instruments simpler, cheaper, and more accessible. By combining high precision with a device that costs around $10, the researchers have opened a potential path toward a new generation of smart sensors capable of measuring the hidden chemistry of our bodies and surroundings.

Scientists discover a hidden immune weapon against deadly lung cancer

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a previously overlooked type of immune cell that can detect and destroy small cell lung cancer, the most aggressive form of lung cancer worldwide.

The discovery, reported in the journal Cancer Cell by researchers at WEHI, revealed that patients with greater numbers of these specialized immune cells tended to survive longer. The findings point to a previously unrecognized immune defense that could provide a new way to attack tumors that are notoriously difficult to treat.

The research team also demonstrated that medications already in use could enhance the ability of these immune cells to kill cancer. This approach could eventually improve existing treatments and increase the number of patients who benefit from immunotherapy.

Why Small Cell Lung Cancer Is So Difficult to Treat

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is among the deadliest cancers, with fewer than 7% of patients surviving for five years after diagnosis.

Immunotherapy has dramatically improved treatment for several types of cancer, but its success against SCLC has been much more limited. Only a small proportion of patients experience lasting benefits.

A major challenge is the cancer's ability to escape immune surveillance. SCLC tumors frequently avoid detection by the conventional immune cells that many immunotherapy treatments depend on, allowing the disease to persist despite the body's natural defenses.

The findings published in Cancer Cell suggest there may be another way for the immune system to recognize and attack these tumors. By identifying a previously overlooked immune pathway, researchers have uncovered a potential strategy for overcoming the cancer's ability to remain hidden.

Professor Kate Sutherland, the study's senior author and a laboratory head at WEHI, said the discovery could help address a longstanding challenge in lung cancer research: finding ways to make immunotherapy effective for more patients.

"For decades, we've known that small cell lung cancer is exceptionally good at hiding from the immune system," Prof Sutherland said.

"We've found an immune cell that appears to be able to see through that disguise.

"Strikingly, patients whose tumors contained more of these specialized immune cells often had better treatment outcomes.

"This is the strongest evidence yet that gamma delta T cells are present in small cell lung cancers and appear capable of fighting the disease.

"It suggests these cells may offer an alternative immune attack route for tumors that are able to evade conventional cancer-fighting immune cells, opening up new possibilities for treating a cancer that has seen few advances over the past decades."

Miniature Tumors Reveal a Powerful Immune Response

To investigate whether gamma delta T cells could directly attack lung cancer, the scientists developed miniature tumors in the laboratory using tissue samples donated by patients. The samples were obtained through the Victorian Cancer Biobank in partnership with the Royal Melbourne Hospital.

These miniature tumors, often called "mini tumors," provide one of the most realistic laboratory models of SCLC created so far, allowing researchers to study how cancer behaves and responds to potential treatments.

The team then introduced gamma delta T cells collected from the same patients' blood into the tumor models. The results showed that these immune cells were highly effective at identifying and destroying SCLC cells.

The scientists went a step further by testing whether two medications already approved for other uses could make this immune response even stronger. The drugs were tarlatamab (a cancer treatment approved for SCLC) and zoledronate (approved for osteoporosis and cancer-related bone disease).

Each medication contributed in a different way. Zoledronate increased the number of available gamma delta T cells and improved their ability to identify the lung cancer tumors. Tarlatamab helped bring the immune cells and cancer cells together, effectively directing the immune attack toward the tumors.

When used together, the drugs substantially increased the ability of gamma delta T cells to kill cancer cells in laboratory experiments. The findings suggest that combining existing treatments could provide a new strategy for strengthening the immune system's response to SCLC.

Could Existing Drugs Lead to New Lung Cancer Therapies?

Dr. Jin Ng, the study's first author, said the discovery could also support a treatment approach known as adoptive cell transfer. This involves collecting immune cells, multiplying them outside the body, and then delivering large numbers of those cells back into a patient to help fight cancer.

Because methods for expanding and reinfusing gamma delta T cells have already been developed, the researchers believe their findings could help accelerate the development of new treatments.

"For patients with lower gamma delta T cell levels, therapies such as zoledronate may offer a way to boost their cancer-fighting immune cells and responsiveness to treatments," Dr. Ng said.

"As therapies that expand and reinfuse gamma delta T cells already exist, our findings raise the exciting possibility of rapidly translating this discovery into new treatment strategies.

"This could open an entirely new avenue for personalized treatment approaches in small cell lung cancer and other solid cancers that are often missed by conventional immune cells."

Clues From Major Lung Cancer Immunotherapy Trials

The investigation began with an examination of data from two influential clinical trials, IMpower133 and CASPIAN. Both studies helped establish immunotherapy as a standard treatment option for patients with small cell lung cancer.

Researchers noticed evidence pointing toward an important role for gamma delta T cells and used sophisticated genomic and computational techniques to investigate further.

Analyses conducted by WEHI's genomics and bioinformatics teams revealed that patients with stronger gamma delta T cell signatures were among those who benefited most from immunotherapy.

Together with the laboratory findings, these results strengthen the evidence that gamma delta T cells play an important role in the immune response to SCLC and could become useful targets for future therapies.

The WEHI team is now working with scientists at the Doherty Institute to build on the discovery, with the goal of translating the findings into a new class of immunotherapy drugs for small cell lung cancer.

The research received support from Cancer Council Victoria, the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), The CASS Foundation, and The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer.

Junto al banco estadounidense trabajan las españolas Santander y CaixaBank. En la parte legal figuran los despachos Freshfields y Latham.

Jorge Cosmen, presidente de Alsa y consejero de Mobico, junto a Jesús Olmos, máximo responsable de Asterion, iniciarán ahora el periodo de análisis de Mobico con una due diligence que se prolongará días antes de la presentación formal de la opa través de vehículo Autobuses de Leon (AdL) en el que los Cosmen son mayoritarios. Los inversores necesitan un mínimo de 250 millones de euros para abordar la operación teniendo en cuenta que al menos el 26% en manos de los Cosmen no acudirá a la opa.

El valor de la operación se aproxima a 2.000 millones de euros al sumar la deuda de la compañía, que hasta marzo rozaba los 1.400 millones de euros y es el verdadero talón de Aquiles de Mobico.

El operador británico, que ha acelerado cambios en la gestión desde la llegada de Francisco Iglesias como consejero delegado, tiene en marcha un agresivo plan de recorte de costes con venta de negocios poco rentables y ajustes en las actividades con márgenes de mejora, entre ellas los negocios en Alemania, Marruecos y parte de los contratos de Reino Unido.

Este verano, Mobico llegó a un acuerdo para desprenderse de la división UK Bus, que prestaba servicio a través de la franquicia West Midlands, en una operación que generó ingresos por valor de 24 millones de libras (unos 28 millones de euros).

En lo que va de 2026, Mobico también ha puesto orden en el negocio de transporte de Alemania, franquicias ferroviarias que han ocasionado múltiples pérdidas en los últimos ejercicios. En la Línea Express Rin-Münsterland, Mobico acordó la modificación del contrato con vigencia desde enero de este año, eliminando el riesgo de ingreso. Además, los cambios del contrato bruto cumplirán con las normas vigentes del sector. El plazo se prorrogará dos años hasta 2032.

El otro ajuste en Alemania corresponde a la línea Rhein-Ruhr-Express, donde los contratos que generan pérdidas y abarcan las líneas RE 1, RE 5, RE 6, RE 11 y RE 4 se acortan tres años y finalizan en 2030.

En Marruecos, Alsa no se presentó a la renovación del transporte de Marrakech y tampoco al de Agadir, plazas que el grupo español explotaba pero que no alcanzaban los umbrales de rentabilidad exigidos por la nueva dirección.

Fuentes próximas a Mobico indican que esas decisiones no significan abandonar los mercados donde está presente, sino perseguir la rentabilidad con el objetivo de seguir reduciendo el apalancamiento (2,9 veces).

La compañía se ha presentado a nuevos concursos para concesiones de tren en Alemania, está pendiente del concurso para operar el transporte de Liverpool y estudia pujar otra vez por el de West Mindlands, unas 20 rutas que se adjudicarán en 2027.

España sigue siendo el negocio mas lucrativo de Mobico, a través de Alsa. La compañía es el líder de la larga distancia en las concesiones estatales y figura entre los operadores con más líneas de transporte autonómico, además de otros negocios como la gestión de ambulancias o ferrocarriles turísticos.

A pesar de su liderazgo, el grupo ha renunciado a concursos de volumen como el transporte Murcia. Tampoco presentó la mejor oferta para quedarse con el de Vigo. El Ministerio de Transportes también ha lanzado al mercado rutas emblemáticas, como la conexión entre Madrid y Barcelona, que opera ahora Alsa.

La crisis de la deuda francesa tensa el coste de financiación de bancos y empresas

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Las dudas sobre la salud de Francia se unen a la subida de los tipos de interés y provocan un encarecimiento de la factura, pese a los buenos fundamentales.

El idilio que mantienen las empresas y los bancos europeos con los compradores de su deuda está empezando a salir un poco más caro. El amor está lejos de acabarse, pero la factura ya se ha incrementado por la subida de los tipos de interés y ahora lo está haciendo más todavía por la crisis de los bonos soberanos franceses.

El coste medio de la deuda corporativa en euros con grado de inversión (con un ráting de BBB- hacia arriba) estaba en el 3,4% en junio. Ya va por el 4,2% y no se espera que baje de ahí ni este año ni el que viene, según los datos de la firma de análisis especializada en renta fija CreditSights.

El escenario es peor para las entidades con menos calidad crediticia (high yield, de BB+ hacia abajo). Están en el 6,6% y su horizonte es rozar el 7% en 2027, cuando hace cuatro meses escasos les bastaba con pagar el 5,4% para financiarse en el mercado.

Es el resultado de un precio oficial del dinero más alto tras las subidas del Banco Central Europeo (BCE) y del maremoto que están sembrando las débiles finanzas francesas en la deuda soberana.

Los bonos corporativos están ganando la batalla a los que emiten los gobiernos, pero hay heridas. Una de ellas es la prima de riesgo. La parte del precio de la deuda que no depende de los tipos oficiales, sino de las cualidades y flaquezas de cada entidad se ha encarecido.

Los bancos y empresas más solventes de la eurozona se limitaban a añadir 78 puntos básicos de media al tipo de referencia en el mercado para colocar sus bonos antes del verano y la previsión entonces era que en 2027 ese diferencial rondaría los 100 puntos básicos.

Esa cota se ha alcanzado ya y la expectativa ahora es que tenga que incrementarse hasta los 115 puntos básicos a lo largo del año que viene como media ponderada por probabilidad de los tres escenarios que contempla CreditSights. El recorrido en el high yield va de los 270 puntos básicos que se abonaban en junio a los 329 actuales y a los 363 previstos en 2027.

«Los persistentes problemas fiscales, el incremento de la competencia a la hora de emitir entre entidades y gobiernos y el débil crecimiento empujarán los diferenciales», explica CreditSights.

Los países necesitan cada vez más dinero para financiar sus déficits y las subidas de tipos han incrementado la cuantía, como también lo han hecho los encarecimientos de las primas de riesgo, especialmente la francesa.

Eso los convierte en fuertes rivales, tanto por los volúmenes que van a pedir al mercado como por el creciente atractivo de los retornos de soberanos con buena calificación crediticia.

Y eso que el apetito de los inversores por la deuda corporativa no ha decaído ni un ápice. Las mayores rentabilidades están llenando los libros de órdenes en cada emisión que se produce y la fuerte demanda está consiguiendo un estrechamiento de los precios de salida en las subastas, pero desde una altura superior a la que había hace pocas semanas, señalan fuentes financieras.

No es porque se anticipen problemas. «La deuda corporativa europea ha demostrado resistencia a lo largo de 2026 a pesar de los persistentes golpes macroeconómicos y los crecientes riesgos a medio plazo, impulsada por los sólidos resultados empresariales y una continua demanda por rentabilidades más altas», asegura CreditSights.

Nada de ello impide que los diferenciales se estén encareciendo para enjugar la distancia con los soberanos, «en previsión de más deterioro fiscal y de una mayor debilidad de la demanda», concluye la firma de análisis.

Bat mothers’ hearts beat faster when they hear their pups

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:00 AM EDT

A single bat colony contains hundreds or even thousands of the winged mammals. But with less-than-ideal eyesight and so many neighbors, how do mothers identify their pups amid the extremely crowded conditions? Writing in a new study published today in the journal Frontiers in Ethology, researchers say it often comes down to the subtle, unique vocalizations emitted by their individual offspring. To assess their responses, the team relied on an even more delicate biological signal—the mother bat’s own heartbeat.

The investigation began when biologists at Japan’s Doshisha University noticed a surprising—but consistent—behavior among the Japanese house bats (Alionoctula abramus) in their laboratory colony. Whenever a pup wandered too far from its roosting area, only its mother raced to the rescue.

“We knew that pups emit isolation calls—acoustic distress signals—when separated. But seeing this strict behavioral selectivity made us wonder: what is happening inside the mother when she hears these calls?” recalled study co-author and Doshisha University biologist Kazuki Yoshino-Hashizawa.

To learn more, Yoshino-Hashizawa and his colleagues monitored four mother bats who each recently birthed a litter of three pups. Some of the litters only contained females, so they decided to exclude the young males from the study to avoid sex-based variations. At two days old, the team recorded clips of the female pups’ tiny isolation calls. These function like the cries from a human baby, and each vocalization has its own unique tones.

However, Japanese house bats grow quickly. The new generation started making echolocation calls at about eight days old, and they ceased isolation calls after around 12 days. After roughly one month, they began flying like adults and set off on their own within the colony. The researchers then recorded their adult echolocation calls, as well.

Around 40 days after the pups’ birth, the team isolated each mother and played them the various audio clips while monitoring their heart rates using an ECG machine. As a control, they also played the recording of a random adult male bat. The study’s authors quickly saw that a mother bat’s heart rate sped up when they heard cries of their own pups, but not the pups that belong to other bat moms. Surprisingly, the mother bats’ heart rates boosted the most from recordings of the older, independent pups. Researchers theorize this might relate to the bats’ memory of more recent calls, but that idea requires more study to confirm.

Still, don’t get overly sentimental. The team also recorded strong heart rate reactions when the mothers heard calls from the random adult male. It’s possible the bats interpreted the sounds as a threat, but the team stopped short of categorizing the reactions as good or bad.

“‘[Heart rate measurement] cannot distinguish between positive emotions, such as maternal motivation, and negative ones, such as stress or alarm,” Yoshino-Hashizawa added.

Instead, the study authors suggest heart rates may be a solid starting point when trying to analyze bat behaviors and responses to their everyday environment. At the same time, a mother bat’s heart certainly seems to patter a bit more for her pups.

“[This] suggests that these vocalizations instantly physiologically prime the mother to engage in maternal behavior,” explained Yoshino-Hashizawa.

Portugal’s last circus elephant begins a new life at wildlife sanctuary

Meet Julie, the first elephant at a wildlife sanctuary set up in Alentejo, southern Portugal. According to Pangea, she was Portugal's last elephant and the last wild animal remaining in a Portuguese circus.

Julie has moved here through a voluntary agreement with the Víctor Hugo Cardinali circus. Elephant caretaker Johnny Nascimento says she ia asjusting to the new environment. "Julie was shy, we need to accept it's like anyone that arrives to a new place, who knows nobody and does not know the surroundings. Slowly, around one week later, she started trusting and deciding what she wanted to do outside.

There are a lot trails, even if it was thicker, it was twice than it is now, so she has been cleaning the house and in this case, making it her own house,” he explains. Portugal banned wild animals in circuses in 2018, but the law came into effect in 2024 to allow time to adjust and find solutions.

But the circus had nowhere to send Julie. This is part of the reason behind the sanctuary – to be a new home for elephants in need coming from zoos, circuses and other places from all over Europe. The 1,000-acre property aims to provide them with the closest possible environment to their homeland , but in Europe. Julie arrived in June. “We are really happy. She has been with us just over three months now and her weight, she's gained around 100 kilos in that time, but that's perfectly normal," says Ryan Hockley, Manager of Pangea Sanctuary. "Where she was previously she didn’t had the opportunity really to exercise, she was just in a barn most of the time.

Whereas now she has 24 (hour) access, she can be in the barn, she can be in either habitat, she literally owns everything, she makes her own choices. But she is certainly exercising a lot more than she was. So I would like to think that that 100 kilos are more muscle than anything else.” The Portuguese region of Alentejo was chosen due to its warm weather, able to recreate a similar climate to their native Africa.

The sanctuary spreads across two municipalities, Alandroal and Vila Viçosa. The goal of Pangea is to continue expanding to be able to welcome more animals in the future, with a total capacity to host between 20-30 elephants. Julie will not be alone for long. Another elephant from a circus in Belgium is due to arrive soon.

The plan is for Kariba to arrive in November. “If we were going to pick anywhere in Europe I think Portugal is the ideal choice because it really does, the climate gives me that African feeling. Then when I look at her now, and having spent a bit of time in Tanzania or Kenya in Africa, when I see Julie out here particularly at the end of the day when the sun is getting low and you get those shadows and the colour changes, the light changes, it's like a golden colour, I could just be watching an elephant somewhere in Africa,” says Hockley.

Pangea is a UK Trust which opened the sanctuary through collected funds from several other organisations and donors. The sanctuary will not be open to the public.
