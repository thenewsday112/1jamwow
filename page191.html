Controversial US-backed vaccine trial in African newborns halted

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hepatitis B study in Guinea-Bissau that was to be funded by CDC and that drew global backlash ‘will not move forward’

The US-backed hepatitis B vaccine trial in African newborns that drew global backlash was halted, the lead researcher said on Wednesday.

It was “now clear” that the study “will not move forward in Guinea-Bissau”, Frederik Schaltz-Buchholzer, a Danish researcher, wrote on LinkedIn, adding, “we were not given the necessary governmental approval.”

The minister of health in Guinea-Bissau, Quinhin Nantote, previously denied that the project would move forward. “This is not even remotely true,” Nantote told the Guardian in Portuguese last week.

The study was to be funded with $1.6m from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), along with another $1.6m from private partners like the Bill Ackman-backed Pershing Square Foundation and the Bluebell Foundation.

“The solution is clear here: take the money and vaccinate as many kids as you can,” said Paul Offit, a vaccines expert at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, who sharply criticized the proposed study as unethical.

The government of Guinea-Bissau now has control over the researchers’ vaccines, which they apprehended when they searched the offices of the Bandim Health Project on Monday.

It was vital to support Guinea-Bissau in rolling the immunizations out to all newborns as soon as possible, Offit said. Guinea-Bissau currently plans to offer the universal birth dose in 2028.

The Danish researchers, led by Schaltz-Buchholzer, Christine Stabell Benn and Peter Aaby, hypothesized giving live vaccines, like the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), may have an overall mortality benefit beyond simply preventing illnesses like tuberculosis – but giving another vaccine like hepatitis B alongside it could dampen that effect.

Such a hypothesis was “fanciful” because the hepatitis B vaccine is a single protein among billions encountered by the immune system each day, Offit said.

“There’s no evidence for that, and it doesn’t make biological sense,” he continued. He also added that there was a very clear benefit to giving the vaccine to babies as soon as possible, because of high rates of hepatitis B in the country and the possibility of lifelong illness and death from infection.

The Danish researchers did not respond by press time to questions about the study or to this criticism. The CDC did not respond by press time to questions about whether the funding, which was appropriated through its global health work, would now be used to roll out the vaccine to all newborns in Guinea-Bissau, as recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO).

“WHO recommends that all infants receive a first dose of the hepatitis B vaccine as soon as possible after birth – preferably within 24 hours – followed by at least two additional doses,” said Tarik Jašarević, a WHO spokesperson. “WHO remains committed to working with national authorities, researchers, and partners to ensure that all newborns – in Guinea‑Bissau and worldwide – receive timely, evidence‑based protection against hepatitis B.”

Those who declined participation in the study would not have received the vaccines – making true informed consent impossible, experts say.

“Imagine you’re a mother. You want to do the best for your child,” said Henrik Støvring, a Danish researcher who has co-published research raising questions about previous research from the Bandim project.

“If they decide to participate in the trial, they have a 50% chance of getting the vaccine for their children. But if they stay out, they have a 0% chance of getting the vaccine. So how can you say that’s a non-coercive informed consent?” Støvring asked.

The proposed trial led to distrust among Bissau-Guineans, who wondered if the vaccine – which has a decades-long record of being safe and effective – was being tested on Africans because it was dangerous, Schaltz-Buchholzer said in his post.

It “should be clear” that both birth vaccination schedules – with or without hepatitis B – are not “dangerous to anyone”, he wrote. There is a clear benefit to giving hepatitis B vaccines at birth, and not doing so can lead to lifelong danger, experts say.

Conducting a study like this “makes the case that there’s something that’s worthy of study, for which they have no evidence”, Offit said.

“We have millions and millions, arguably hundreds of millions, of children in this world who have gotten that vaccine as a birth dose or haven’t. If there was any evidence that this was problematic, you would know this by now,” Offit added. “We eliminated hepatitis B in the United States in children virtually within years of starting that program, so the benefits are clear.”

Schaltz-Buchholzer also cast doubt on the effectiveness and safety of the hepatitis B vaccine at birth, noting that he believes the study was canceled because it challenges “prevailing medical assumptions, such as that HBV0 does not have non-specific effects”. The study, in addition to looking at mortality, would have also examined side-effects such as skin conditions and neurodevelopmental conditions. Dozens of studies have shown no link between vaccination and autism.

“All newborns should be given a birth dose of hepatitis B vaccine,” Offit said. “Guinea-Bissau is a country that suffers a high incidence of chronic hepatitis B. That country, more than any other country, arguably, needs to launch this program.”

Namibia' Black Rhino Is One of Africa’s Most Exciting Conservation Stories

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We’d been looking for nearly five hours—scanning the dry grass for upturned pebbles and the horizon for a dark, lumbering shape—when we spotted the rhino bull. Next to me, tracker Mensley Karutjaiva shook his head with a resigned chuckle. We’d been up since daybreak, alongside three other members of the Save the Rhino Trust, looking for critically endangered black rhinos in Damaraland, a land of flat-topped mountains, rocky hills, and stark plains in the Kunene region of northwestern Namibia. While we scrambled about in the heat, the bull had been dozing in the shade of a milk bush. We watched as he stood up, as if posing for us, before lying back down to resume his nap.

I was traveling in a loop around northwest Namibia that took me to three new (or recently renewed) camps. Since photographic safari tourism began here over a century ago, Namibia has largely been known amongst international travelers as a self-drive vacation—a destination with plenty of do-it-yourself road trips through otherworldly desert landscapes. Unknown to many visitors, though, human-wildlife conflict—population activities that negatively affect animals through habitat loss or resource competition— has been an ongoing challenge, and past decades of poaching threw rhinoceros populations into a tailspin. But, in the past few years, Namibia has seen a lot of positive conservation momentum, with these northwest reaches acting as a bellwether. From innovative government measures to an Indigenous-stewarded private reserve, this region is now a story of forward-looking change.

In May 2026, the country’s government launched a new conservation finance deal called Namibia for Life in partnership with the World Wildlife Fund. Worth approximately $63 million, it sets aside funds for Namibia’s 86 community-supported conservancies, which span more than 49 million acres.

Gazing at the black rhino, I felt a thrill as my eyes followed the magnificent swoop of the animal’s two intact horns, the larger of the two over three feet long. In an attempt to deter poachers, wildlife management will often cut down a rhinos’ horns—a humane, if humbling, procedure—so that the sight of a rhino, especially one of this critically endangered sub-species, often entails seeing just nubs.

Wild black rhino populations in Namibia came close to extinction in the mid 90s, but thanks to conservation efforts, have recovered.

Rhino horn can fetch up to $5,400 a pound in the illegal wildlife trade—so just seeing this intact horn was a triumph in itself. Decimated by poaching throughout the 1970s and 80s due to demand in Asia and the Arabian Peninsula, wild black rhino populations had dropped by 96 percent in the mid 90s. They came so close to extinction that only 2,410 were left on the entire planet.

That sighting was a harbinger of better days to come. Thanks to conservation efforts, today some 6,000 black rhinos now roam free, with an estimated half of those living in Namibia. In July 2026, the World Wildlife Fund announced that Namibia had experienced a 53 percent drop in rhino poaching since 2024, reaching its lowest numbers of rhino poaching instances in over a decade.

“Over the past 39 years of visiting Namibia regularly, I’ve been heartened to see a growing understanding of the importance of conservation and community-based tourism–and more recently, how to sustain it,” said travel advisor Nicola Shepherd, who arranged my itinerary. “Namibian organizations such as the nonprofit Integrated Rural Development and Nature Conservation are investing in practical ways to reduce conflict and protect both people and wildlife.”

The founder of the conservation-focused Explorations Company, Shepherd and her team have been arranging customized trips to this country for decades. In the past few years, she’s noted a shift in the advent of luxury accommodations, including Zannier Hotels, Habitas, and others, that have broadened the country's appeal to travelers seeking high-end experiences. It’s all welcome news for the country, which is experiencing an uptick in traveler interest—just two months after my trip, local publications reported that the country’s hotel room occupancy was having one of its strongest years.

More than twice the size of California, Namibia’s expanse includes coastline, semi-arid desert, dry and wet woodlands, and golden savanna. The northwest corner is a rich mix of these terrains–including the otherworldly, white-tipped grasslands I walked through as we returned to Wilderness Desert Rhino Camp after our morning rhino sighting.

“In this area of the Palmwag Concession, the rotation of tourism helps protect rhinos,” Karutjaiva told me. I heard this sentiment echoed wherever I went, although specific numbers are carefully guarded. “It’s a state secret. The only thing I can say is that the population is growing,” he said. The guardedness is understandable, as the threat of poaching is still everywhere. And growth isn’t restricted to the rhino populations.

The lounge area at the intimate Wilderness Desert Rhino Camp, which was formerly a field station for wildlife research.

At Desert Rhino Camp, a full rebuild in 2024 resulted in six stand-alone tented suites, where materials like timber, leather, and stone evoke a modern, desert-chic style. With the Etendeka Mountains rising in the distance, red basalt boulders form the walls of the open-air dining lounge where I enjoyed a delicious slow-cooked lamb potjie stew for dinner.

“We really realized how important tourism was during the pandemic, when most businesses closed and a lot of people lost their jobs,” lodge manager Fanus Katondoka told me that evening, as we sipped on rooibos iced teas. “Now we know it. Operators are building a lot of facilities, lodges, hotels–it's booming.” That growth means more resources for community-supported conservation, so Karutjaiva and his team can continue their work protecting rhinos and other species.

Rhinos have adorned conservation headlines recently, but they’re far from the only draw in northwest Namibia. A new camp from outfitter Natural Selection recently opened about an hour’s flight north, in the desert Kaokoveld region, highlighting another species of desert-adapted wildlife. A few days later, I was headed to see it.

Sitting in the cockpit seat of a tiny four-seater Cessna, I waved goodbye to the rhino trackers and flew over the Namib Desert, one of the oldest arid regions in the world. The landscape flowed in ridges and mountains of faded yellow and whisper pink, where welwitschia–large, low-lying plants that live to be hundreds of years old–sprawled like relics from a Dune film set.

The Namib Desert stretches along the Atlantic coast of Namibia and features some of the highest sand dunes in the world.

It was a fitting introduction to my next stop: the Hoanib Valley, home to a new camp with a mission that’s a bit bigger than rhinoceros. I quickly learned what that something was when guide Howard Groenewaldt picked me up from the airstrip, nosed our vehicle toward camp, and then abruptly cut the engine.

We had stopped at the Hoanib River, a seasonal waterway that runs west from the desert until it reaches the Skeleton Coast on the Atlantic Ocean. Now in the dry season, it turned into a sandy avenue lined with camelthorn and giant ana trees that age more than 100 years old. It was a nature-born runway—and something was stirring around a bend.

A giant, desert-adapted elephant slowly walked toward us. His long tusks were pale white; the bottoms of his feet splayed out wide under his skinny legs, the better to stabilize on sand. There was no sound except the breeze that was stirring the wizened, banana-shaped seed pods in the branches above my head, and the soft fwump of the elephant’s footsteps. I watched, transfixed, as he paused and delicately trailed the tip of his trunk on the ground

The author looks on at a desert-adapted elephant.

This is exactly the kind of encounter the new Natural Selection Hoanib Elephant Camp was built around. Located off the northern bank of the river, its 10 solar-powered suites bring a new level of comfort to the valley. Between game drives, guests here lounge on the veranda overlooking the desert hoping to spy more elephants, or relax in their suites with a massage or nap. Each of the natural-hued suites include a private plunge pool, a deep king-size bed, and a writing desk. Dinner is a multi-course affair in the shared lounge; afterward, a bonfire crackles under the stars. During my stay some visitors curled up on the couches against the evening desert chill, sipping hot drinks spiked with Amarula, a South African liqueur, surrounded by flickering candles.

While the resort is stylish and deeply comfortable, Natural Selection’s approach to community partnership also reflects the shift in this region toward local collaboration. The evening I arrived at camp, I met Petty Daniels, who hails from the nearby Torra Conservancy and brings an acutely local perspective. Years ago, she began working in hospitality as a waitress—today, she’s the manager of both this new camp and its giraffe-focused sister property, the Hoanib Valley Camp. Roughly 97 percent of staff are from surrounding community conservancies, creating a working model of local investment in supporting companies that protect their homeland.

Sundowner drinks are accompanied by a knowledgable guide on a Hoanib Elephant Camp safari.

Within Daniels’ generation, change has been flourishing in the hearts and minds of locals. “Before tourism, people drove wherever they wanted, shooting animals to provide food for their families,” she said. “Not anymore. People are getting into conservation tourism and understanding why they shouldn’t do that.”

Even so, human-wildlife conflict is still a problem. Elephants ramble into gardens and cheetahs kill the occasional goat, but the overall opinion of locals is shifting, especially as the Namibian government signals support for conservation that’s more in step with local interests.

Namibia’s Hoanib Valley was made famous by its desert-adapted elephants.

“Namibia’s communities already help look after lions, elephants, rhinos, water, plants and open land,” Rob Moffett explained a few days later, soon after I arrived at my third and final destination in northwest Namibia. “Instead of saying, ‘Good luck finding money every year,’ the Namibia for Life fund says, ‘Here is a proper money box, a rule book, and a team to help you keep protecting nature.’”

Moffett is the general manager of sales and marketing at Ongava Game Reserve, a private stretch of savanna and mopane woodlands that stretches more than 74,000 acres next to Etosha National Park, one of the country’s biggest visitor attractions. It’s also the site of another positive–and long overdue–story of change.

Wilderness operates single-engine flights from camp to camp that come in handy.

To reach it, I rented a gas-electric hybrid car from ATI Holidays and drove east from the Hoanib Valley. This type of travel is still popular in Namibia, with strong infrastructure supporting visitors. After a detailed briefing with an ATI staff member, I was kitted out with a folder packed with maps, a Namibian cell phone pre-programmed with key contacts, and a first-aid kit. I drove for several hours, listening to music and watching the desert scenery flash by. But driving solo can be tiring, and I was glad for the air travel between previous camps, seamlessly arranged by the Explorations Company. For US travelers unaccustomed to driving on the left side of the road, and often juggling a few shorter vacations, brief single-engine flights from camp to camp are increasingly preferred by high-end resorts opening in the northwest.

The new Horizon by Ongava, where I had arrived, is one of those. Consisting of just three hilltop villas, the property debuted from renovations and a name reset in April 2026, and prioritizes seclusion and comfort. My accommodations unfolded in a graceful series of rooms with couches, a four-poster bed, and a dual-vanity bathroom. Outside, overlooking a plain of the greenest scenery I’d seen so far, a wide deck hugged the hillside, and included two dining spaces, an outdoor shower, and a private plunge pool. My favorite touch was the large bench in the walk-in dressing room, carved in the shape of a serval—a wild cat native to sub-Saharan Africa.

The dining, led by chef Festus Ilyambo, is a feat in its own right. That evening, in the open-air lounge, I asked Ilyambo for the recipes behind his potato pavé—thinly sliced, buttery potatoes infused with organic thyme and fresh cream, then sautéed with garlic—and filet mignon, served with a jus and cognac sauce.

Raised walkways lead to villas at the new Horizon by Ongava.

Alfresco dinners at Horizon by Ongava are a highlight of the day.

The next morning, longtime guide Pascalis Kazimbu led me on a private walking safari in Ongava Game Reserve, pointing out everything from lilac-breasted rollers to white rhino mothers with calves tottering close behind and wildebeest that bleated—“gnuu, gnuu”. Later that afternoon we set out on a game drive, pausing at a watering hole for sundowner drinks as dusk began to settle. I was sipping a local Tafel lager in the safari vehicle when a deep, reverberating roar sounded behind me, vibrating in my bones. Moments later, a male lion strolled out of the thin trees behind me, settling by the pond for a drink.

With game viewing like this, verdant Ongava and its neighboring Etosha National Park are a welcome coda to the scenes of desert and dunes I’d left behind. The circular route of my trip is a popular one: travelers arrive into the capital of Windhoek, travel in a rough circle that begins with awe-inspiring desert and ends near Etosha, and then head back to Windhoek to begin their journey home.

Colostrum vs. Collagen: Which One Should You Take?

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A surprisingly common query I get is colostrum vs. collagen—which is better? Maybe it’s the fact that they both start with the same three letters. Maybe it’s that they’re both alternative health darlings currently enjoying a huge uptick in interest.

Whatever the reason for their connection in people’s minds, I thought it would be a good idea to do a quick write-up on how colostrum and collagen might interact in the body, what the relative benefits are, and whether you can take them both together.

Contrary to what the influencers might tell you, there isn’t a ton of research showing that bovine colostrum has a wide range of benefits for adults. It’s definitely helpful for newborns, since their guts are incredibly permeable, and colostrum is literally the first food they’re supposed to eat. Even cow colostrum has been shown to improve outcomes for newborns and premature infants. No arguments there.

But I’d caution against expecting too much from colostrum supplements. The best case for adding it to your diet as an adult is improving gut health, reducing intestinal permeability, and improving immune function—particularly for athletes.

Colostrum has been heavily researched for its ability to reduce gut permeability in various populations, including athletes, medical patients, and everyday folks.1-4 People who take bovine colostrum supplements often report better gut health and a more intact gut lining. Zonulin, a gut compound that increases permeability, frequently drops with colostrum intake.

Researchers aren’t entirely sure how bovine colostrum benefits the gut. Some people claim it regulates specific growth factors in the gut lining that help repair and support its structure. That’s colostrum’s primary role in infancy: improving gut viability so the immune system can develop, enabling digestion of food instead of relying on the womb for sustenance. Thanks in large part to colostrum benefits, this new member of the human race is able to handle what the world throws at it and develop a healthy immune response and gut lining

The challenge is that the adult gut isn’t as permeable as a newborn’s by design. For instance, the IGF-1 in bovine colostrum isn’t absorbed or utilized by adults.5

Colostrum can also regulate inflammation and help maintain iron balance in athletes, largely due to its lactoferrin content.6 Lactoferrin is an immune protein that improves iron absorption while reducing free iron—an inflammatory agent—circulating in the body. Athletes taking colostrum for instance see higher spikes in inflammation during workouts but lower resting inflammation, showing it promotes “smart” inflammation.

Meanwhile, collagen benefits for gut health are much more direct. Collagen provides the actual building blocks—amino acids like glycine—for repairing and growing the gut lining. Whether it turns out that colostrum does indeed trigger growth factors that help the gut repair itself (by no means confirmed), or if it just lowers inflammation via lactoferrin which allows the repair to proceed, you’ll need extra collagen to make those repairs.

Colostrum assists with gut repair while collagen provides a substrate required for the repair to occur.

And so the two compounds are not competing with each other, but potentially complementary. Both colostrum and collagen improve gut health, enhance skin health and appearance, and boost immune function by different but complementary routes.

Like what you’re reading? Share this post so others can find it.

Yes, you can absolutely take collagen and colostrum together. In fact, I’d argue that if colostrum works, they’re almost made for each other. You don’t need to mix them in the same shake or take them simultaneously; just include both in your routine for maximum benefit.

Adding both colostrum and collagen supplements can create a synergistic effect, particularly for gut and immune health. Collagen serves as the substrate, while colostrum provides potential activation via growth factors.

It depends on your dietary needs. If you only take one, take collagen. Collagen is like the fourth macronutrient, providing glycine—an amino acid we no longer get enough of in modern diets. This makes collagen essential for maintaining skin, bones, blood vessels, and more. It’s also widely beneficial, whether you’re looking for joint health, anti-aging, or gut support.

If you regularly consume foods like bone broth, gelatinous meats, or stews, you may already be getting plenty of collagen. In that case, colostrum supplements could add more value, especially for individuals with immune or gut issues that dietary changes haven’t resolved.

Colostrum, derived from the mature milk of cows after giving birth, is essentially absent from the diet unless you specifically seek it out. It’s best suited for people dealing with gut inflammation or immune dysregulation. Athletes might benefit from colostrum’s ability to regulate iron and inflammation, while others may see improved resistance to respiratory infections.

Adults prone to upper respiratory tract infections.

Individuals with leaky gut or gut inflammation.

If you’re on a budget, you can likely replicate most of the colostrum supplement benefits with a high-quality whey isolate (which contains some lactoferrin, but not as much as colostrum) and a dedicated lactoferrin supplement. My guess is that lactoferrin is doing much of the heavy lifting for colostrum’s benefits.

Both colostrum and collagen offer unique benefits, but collagen stands out as the more universally essential supplement. Its role in collagen production, gut lining repair, and overall health makes it indispensable for most people. Bovine colostrum can complement collagen, especially in specific scenarios, but it isn’t as necessary or widely applicable.

For most, collagen is the clear winner. Still, if you have unique needs or simply want to experiment with bovine colostrum benefits, adding both to your regimen can offer the best of both worlds.

Unfortunately, there is nothing simple about such a scheme.

Pratley’s proposal would mean creating a new benefits system inside our privatised energy industry. If the interminable debates about our current benefits system were not enough to put him off, we can look to the failing social care system to see where it might lead. The agency charged with regulating Colorado’s first-in-the-nation legal recreational marijuana market potentially put consumers at risk of using contaminated products, a new state audit found.

The report pointed to inconsistent oversight by the Marijuana Enforcement Division and loose state rules around testing marijuana products or contaminants. As a result, it found, nearly 75% of marijuana processed for eventual sale from 2023 through 2025 evaded testing for pesticides.

The audit, released Monday, identified a host of issues, including regulators’ failure to meet regular inspection schedules or to conduct sufficient random checks at dispensaries for contaminated products. It also found that delayed agency warnings to the public allowed potentially contaminated products to remain on shelves for months, sometimes years.

The audit’s conclusions echo several findings of an investigation this year by The Denver Gazette and ProPublica into the Marijuana Enforcement Division’s lapses in policing contaminants entering the legal marketplace.

In one case highlighted by the newsrooms, it took 15 months for a manufacturer to discontinue production of a marijuana sleep aid pill despite consumers warning regulators that the product was harming their livers. The pills had herbal extracts that researchers say can cause liver damage.

Auditors said they did not identify evidence of “widespread health and safety issues” but did find that deficient regulations still left consumers vulnerable.

The audit found that unsafe products had remained on the market for as long as three years before the Marijuana Enforcement Division issued a public warning. The auditor’s review of the agency’s health and safety advisories found that, on average, from 2023 through 2025, contaminated products that were the subject of an advisory remained on shelves for more than seven months. That’s the same number of months the news organizations found contaminated products stayed on shelves before warnings were issued.

The audit also found that it took the agency three and a half months, on average, in 2025 to issue an advisory once it launched an investigation into potential contamination. Even after investigators determined an advisory was needed, it still took the agency a month on average to issue one.

“These issues limit MED’s ability to meet its core objective to identify product safety risks and to help ensure that licensed marijuana businesses meet product safety requirements,” the audit stated. “As a result, there is an increased risk that marijuana products sold in Colorado may have contaminants or that the potency listed on the labels is inaccurate.”

We reported that a top Marijuana Enforcement Division official acknowledged in a meeting with industry representatives that the amount of chemically converted hemp being sold as marijuana is greater than the agency has publicly disclosed.

State regulators announced a plan to crack down on companies that illegally sell cheaper and potentially hazardous hemp products as marijuana, citing potential tax avoidance and “serious risks to public safety.”

Colorado regulators discussed a proposal to do away with the current testing system that allows marijuana manufacturers to choose their own samples. Instead, independent labs or contractors would collect the samples.

The Denver Gazette and ProPublica reported that intoxicating hemp has seeped into Colorado’s marijuana market because the state had failed to adopt safety measures. Tests found signs of hemp in vapes sold at marijuana dispensaries.

After hemp companies figured out how to convert CBD into the high-inducing compound THC, Colorado became one of the first states to ban chemically converted hemp products, first with regulatory advisories and then with legislation.

President Donald Trump signed the Farm Bill, which legalizes hemp. Congress’ intent was to help farmers along with people who believe hemp’s high levels of the nonintoxicating compound CBD can help with seizures, pain and sleep.

Colorado became the first state to allow retail sales of marijuana for recreational use.

Colorado allows marijuana businesses to test far less frequently than required by state guidelines if they can show consistent compliance with testing rules. The reduced testing program results “in a significant portion of the marijuana product being sold in Colorado not undergoing testing to ensure it is safe,” the audit stated. Manufacturers also can rely on other testing exemptions, such as for certain marijuana-derived skin and body products, but there is “limited MED oversight” of how manufacturers determine they qualify for an exemption.

Auditors reported that a survey of MED investigators found that more than half said there are or may be widespread marijuana product safety issues in Colorado’s regulated market.

In a statement released Monday night, the Marijuana Enforcement Division emphasized that state auditors found no evidence “to support claims of widespread health and safety issues” with marijuana products for sale in Colorado, but the agency agreed the audit identified areas for improvement. It said that among other things, it would be assessing exemptions to tests currently allowed under state laws and regulations.

The state auditor’s review “allows us to take a critical look at how we can continue to carry out our work, consistent with our commitment to integrity, safety, and continuous operational improvement,” Dominique Mendiola, senior director of the MED, said in a statement.

The auditor’s report highlighted a criticism her agency has repeatedly sidestepped: the absence of independent collection of samples for safety testing. The Denver Gazette and ProPublica found that more than half of states with regulated marijuana markets rely on private labs or an independent third party to collect samples. Colorado marijuana businesses are free to collect their own samples submitted for testing.

The enforcement division’s own investigators told auditors that, as a result, Colorado relies largely on an honor code to ensure the safety of marijuana sold in the state’s dispensaries.

Auditors said that allowing businesses to select their own samples “increases the risk that businesses do not pick samples that are representative of the overall quality of their marijuana product.”

MED regulators at the start of the year said they planned to pursue an overhaul that would have required private lab personnel to collect samples, but they abandoned that effort after manufacturers said it would increase their costs. On Monday, Kyle Lambert, the enforcement division’s deputy senior director, told legislators that the agency opted against an overhaul this year, chiefly because he said it would be a geographic challenge to do away with self-sampling for rural businesses.

“It was decided now wasn’t the right time to propose that as a policy change,” Lambert said, adding that the agency still might revisit the issue.

State Sen. Byron Pelton, a Republican from Sterling, pushed back, saying it was a loophole he thinks the agency should consider closing.

“If I’m a shady character, I know which products I’m going to go sampling and which ones I’m not,” he said. “That’s a pretty gray area to me.”

The audit also questioned the agency’s reliance on businesses voluntarily recalling contaminated marijuana products, saying that “some of these recalls did not succeed at stopping the businesses from selling potentially unsafe product.”

Just last month, The Denver Gazette and ProPublica reported that a Colorado manufacturer voluntarily flagged 49 of its popular marijuana vapes for recall, nearly six weeks after a third-party lab notified the company and regulators that it detected methylene chloride, a chemical the Environmental Protection Agency has sought to ban for most uses because it can cause cancer. The MED issued a health and safety advisory a day after the publications first reported the manufacturer had warned dispensaries to stop selling the vapes, which consumers had been purchasing for over a year.

Expect to see quite a few websites serving error pages today: Nearly 70 percent of websites need to update their core software to fix a pair of vulnerabilities that allow an attacker to seize full control over affected servers. The flaws at issue are present in PHP, the programming language that powers sites running on a large number of web publishing systems, including Drupal, Magento, Joomla and WordPress. Earlier today, PHP released an emergency security update to fix two critical bugs that can allow an attacker to execute code of their choosing on a vulnerable server.

Reuters reports that government websites in Europe and elsewhere went offline today after the PHP vulnerabilities were disclosed. Cloudflare said it has already detected some exploitation of the flaw, and that at 3:02 am (UTC) it implemented web application firewall rules to protect websites from attack traffic seeking to exploit the bugs, which could cause a denial of service condition, remote code execution, and data exposure.

OpenAI, which was involved in the vulnerability research and outreach, said “given the severity of these flaws and PHP’s widespread use, we consider this one of the most impactful security issues in recent memory.”

Censys, a company that tracks the technology used by Internet hosts, said a recent scan identified approximately 2.58 million Internet facing hosts and 54.9 million web properties running PHP.

Security experts at Wiz said every PHP version released in the last decade is affected by at least one of these vulnerabilities [full disclosure: Wiz is an advertiser on this blog].

“These findings rank among the most severe vulnerabilities discovered in PHP, and arguably in software history,” reads a blog post published today by a team of researchers at Wiz, which was one of several organizations credited with discovering the vulnerabilities. “The underlying flaws had been present for decades, but demonstrating their exploitability required an exceptionally complex chain of steps, at the edge of what human researchers could practically achieve. Frontier AI models substantially reduced that barrier, turning a longstanding flaw into an exploit.”

The Wiz researchers say it is likely the PHP vulnerabilities will be mass-exploited to install web-based backdoors or “web shells,” which give attackers a web-based panel that allows them to remotely control infected sites. Wiz said that prior to the PHP bugs being publicly disclosed, the company worked with the PHP team, Calif and OpenAI to measure the size of the problem and coordinate patch verification and disclosure.

The PHP zero-day vulnerabilities are being tracked by their CVE numbers, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, and they affect all web servers running PHP 5.4 or later. The bugs are fixed in PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36, and 8.2.35.

According to Wiz, PHP is so deeply embedded in many web applications that we are likely to see organizations struggling to patch these vulnerabilities for some time to come, comparing this PHP vulnerability pair with the Log4Shell weakness that emerged in 2021 — a widespread, severe-as-it-gets security flaw in Log4j, a popular Java logging framework.

“A vulnerability in core PHP reaches far beyond a single application,” the Wiz researchers wrote. “PHP runs across clouds, hosting providers, content-delivery networks and millions of independently managed servers. Reducing exposure before the technical details became public required coordinated work across that entire ecosystem.”

The SANS Internet Storm Center is keeping an eye on major software platforms that are shipping updates to deal with these PHP flaws. For example, Ubuntu just released updated PHP packages.
