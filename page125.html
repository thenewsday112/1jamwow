En una competición como el Campeonato Mundial de Resistencia (WEC), donde los 'hypercars' deben combinar velocidad, eficiencia y fiabilidad durante horas, cada milímetro de carrocería, cada gramo de material y cada grado de temperatura cuentan. Detrás de los prototipos que luchan por la victoria en pruebas como las 24 Horas de Le Mans o la más reciente de las 6 Horas de Barcelona hay un trabajo de ingeniería que comienza mucho antes de pisar el asfalto y que convierte la competición en un laboratorio tecnológico para la industria del automóvil.

Los coches chinos también conquistan el Salón del Automóvil de París

Un ejemplo es Peugeot Sport, que ha abierto su laboratorio para explicar cómo trabaja para la próxima temporada. Una treintena de ingenieros de Stellantis Motorsport, bajo la dirección de Yann Le Fur, participa en los programas del WEC y de Fórmula E con la mirada puesta en la evolución del Hypercar Peugeot 9X8 prevista para 2027. Su cometido consiste en coordinar disciplinas muy distintas para transformar las exigencias de la competición en un prototipo más competitivo, eficiente y fiable.

El objetivo va más allá de mejorar los resultados deportivos. La resistencia permite someter los componentes a condiciones extremas, estudiar su comportamiento durante periodos prolongados y desarrollar soluciones que, cuando resulta viable, pueden trasladarse a los vehículos de producción en serie. Es una transferencia tecnológica que no significa que un coche de calle vaya a incorporar directamente las piezas de un prototipo de Le Mans, sino que puede beneficiarse de los conocimientos adquiridos en materiales, gestión térmica, eficiencia o integración de sistemas.

El punto de partida es la arquitectura del vehículo. Lionel Berthier, diseñador jefe del Peugeot 9X8, coordina la integración de cientos de componentes en un espacio limitado, en el que cada decisión condiciona a las demás. La carrocería debe generar carga aerodinámica para mejorar el paso por curva sin aumentar excesivamente la resistencia al avance, mientras que los elementos mecánicos necesitan encontrar su sitio sin dificultar la refrigeración o las intervenciones de los técnicos durante una carrera.

En un coche de resistencia, el diseño no puede separarse de la facilidad de mantenimiento. Una solución puede resultar excelente sobre el papel, pero perder su atractivo si obliga a invertir demasiado tiempo en una reparación o complica el acceso a un componente. La ingeniería busca, por tanto, un equilibrio entre prestaciones, fiabilidad y operatividad.

También los materiales esconden una parte importante del trabajo. La fibra de carbono, fundamental para reducir el peso y conseguir una estructura resistente, exige definir cuidadosamente la geometría de cada panel y la disposición de sus capas. La orientación de las fibras, el gramaje y el tipo de tejido permiten adaptar las propiedades de cada pieza a las cargas que deberá soportar.

El reto consiste en aligerar el conjunto sin comprometer su resistencia y teniendo en cuenta que, en una prueba de resistencia, los daños deben poder repararse con rapidez. No se trata solo de fabricar una carrocería ligera, sino de conseguir que mantenga sus propiedades durante un uso intensivo y que responda a las necesidades reales del equipo en el circuito.

Buena parte de las decisiones se toman antes de fabricar una sola pieza. Los ingenieros utilizan modelos tridimensionales y herramientas de diseño asistido por ordenador para comprobar cómo encajan los componentes y anticipar las interferencias que podrían aparecer al integrar las distintas soluciones.

Kevin Fishermann, ingeniero de diseño de chasis, trabaja en este entorno digital para desarrollar elementos que deben ajustarse con precisión a la arquitectura del prototipo. Un conducto destinado a refrigerar los frenos, por ejemplo, no puede diseñarse de forma aislada: debe respetar el espacio disponible alrededor de las ruedas y los discos, soportar las temperaturas de funcionamiento y garantizar que el aire circule de la forma prevista.

La simulación permite detectar incompatibilidades antes de pasar a la fabricación, cuando corregir un error resulta más sencillo y económico. Además, cada modificación debe comunicarse al resto de departamentos, porque un cambio en la carrocería puede afectar a la suspensión, y una alteración en la ubicación de un elemento mecánico puede obligar a revisar la refrigeración o el reparto de espacios.

Esta coordinación es especialmente importante en un prototipo de competición, donde las mejoras rara vez dependen de una única pieza. El rendimiento final surge de la suma de decisiones que deben funcionar conjuntamente.

El departamento de dinámica se ocupa de componentes como las ruedas, los portamanguetas, los brazos de suspensión y los amortiguadores. Su geometría, resistencia y peso influyen directamente en el comportamiento del vehículo, desde la precisión de entrada en curva hasta la capacidad de mantener la estabilidad sobre un asfalto irregular.

Tanguy Lourdelet, ingeniero especializado en dinámica y suspensión, desarrolla estas soluciones teniendo en cuenta su integración con el monocasco de fibra de carbono y con el resto de los sistemas. Los modelos digitales permiten comprobar los espacios disponibles y estudiar cómo afectan a la suspensión los cambios introducidos por otras áreas de ingeniería.

La misma lógica se aplica al sistema de propulsión. Kendal Beykoylu, responsable de diseño en la interfaz entre el grupo propulsor y el chasis, coordina cuestiones como la instalación mecánica, la refrigeración y el escape. En un Hypercar sujeto al reglamento técnico de la categoría Le Mans Hypercar (LMH), la potencia debe convivir con unos requisitos exigentes de fiabilidad y eficiencia.

En resistencia no basta con alcanzar una cifra elevada de prestaciones durante unas pocas vueltas. El coche debe mantener un ritmo competitivo, controlar las temperaturas y permitir que los pilotos conduzcan con confianza durante relevos prolongados. Una mejora que aumente la velocidad, pero comprometa la durabilidad de un componente, puede acabar convirtiéndose en un problema durante la carrera.

Por eso, los cálculos se complementan con simulaciones que estudian las cargas aerodinámicas sobre la carrocería, las tensiones térmicas del sistema de propulsión y los esfuerzos mecánicos que soportan las ruedas y la suspensión.

“Nuestro trabajo consiste en ayudar a los diseñadores a dimensionar los componentes para que puedan soportar las cargas y los esfuerzos a los que estarán sometidos durante su funcionamiento”, explica Jean-Marie Lavoisier, ingeniero de simulación de Stellantis Motorsport.

Los resultados sirven para ajustar los modelos tridimensionales y repetir las comprobaciones hasta encontrar una solución adecuada. Después llegan las pruebas físicas, imprescindibles para verificar que los cálculos se corresponden con el comportamiento real, especialmente en los elementos relacionados con la seguridad del piloto y las prestaciones del vehículo.

El trabajo del departamento técnico tiene un objetivo deportivo concreto: situar al Peugeot 9X8 de forma sostenida entre los aspirantes a las primeras posiciones. La prueba de Barcelona, en la que la lluvia permitió a los dos prototipos franceses ganar posiciones, dejó señales positivas, aunque las condiciones cambiaron a medida que se secó la pista.

“Las condiciones iniciales nos ofrecieron buenas oportunidades de adelantamiento y nos permitieron mantenernos en la lucha. Cuando la pista se secó, volvimos a una posición más acorde con nuestro rendimiento actual”, explicó Emmanuel Esnault, director de Team Peugeot TotalEnergies. “El resultado es frustrante, porque los puntos estaban a nuestro alcance, pero nos quedamos con la dinámica positiva del fin de semana”.

La carrera catalana terminó con un doblete de Toyota y la victoria en el disputado campeonato con ocho fabricantes, mientras Peugeot puso el foco en la siguiente fase de su proyecto. La evolución técnica prevista para 2027 irá acompañada de cambios en la alineación de pilotos: Robert Shwartzman y Alex Quinn se incorporarán como titulares al equipo. El primero regresará al WEC tras competir en IndyCar, mientras que Quinn dará el salto desde el trabajo de pruebas y desarrollo después de incorporarse al programa en 2026.

Nationwide to slash 275 jobs in latest round of cuts since takeover of Virgin Money

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Exclusive: Bosses tell staff cuts part of effort to ‘simplify’ operations and ‘remove duplication’ across wider workforce

Nationwide building society is slashing another 275 jobs in the latest round of cuts since bank bosses pushed through a £2.9bn takeover of Virgin Money.

Bosses made the announcement to staff on Wednesday, saying it was part of efforts to simplify the bank’s operations and “remove duplication” after absorbing thousands of staff in to the group’s wider workforce.

The cuts are to fall across its retail division, including those working on mortgages, as well as in HR, the chief operating officer’s office, customer operations, communications and finance.

Nationwide said no customer-facing staff would be affected, which appears to be in line with its pledge to keep its 700 branches open until at least 2030.

It follows closely behind the last round of job losses, which were announced in June and affected 600 staff.

Commenting on the latest cuts, a spokesperson for the lender said: “As we integrate Virgin Money into Nationwide, we’re making a limited number of changes. No customer-facing colleagues are at risk and we’re supporting those affected, including through retraining and redeployment.”

The Virgin Money takeover was a boon for the building society sector when it was first announced two years ago, having marked a rare moment when a member-owned lender – traditionally focused on mortgages and savings – was snapping up a commercial high street bank. However, the move also proved controversial, with Nationwide having refused to give members a vote on the deal.

Virgin Money had more than 7,000 staff around the time Nationwide formalised its takeover in late 2024, when the building society had about 18,000 of its own employees, according to its 2025 annual report. Its total workforce now sits at roughly 27,000 today.

Executives had confirmed back in 2024 that there would be “limited workforce changes” to deal with overlapping roles but that they would undergo a formal review of its employee base in the years that followed.

A Nationwide spokesperson stressed that the cuts were not linked to any growing use of AI.

“They don’t need your password, they don’t trigger a login alert, and they can often bypass your multifactor authentication (MFA).”

It looks like a regular session on the web. At some point you will have logged in and given your password, and maybe used MFA to prove it’s you.

If it’s a social media or email account you may discover it’s been used by scammers to trick other people. If it’s your bank they could access your account, or they could use details they find to perpetrate an impersonation scam.

And if you were shopping online, the fraudsters could change and redirect your order and charge it to your saved payment details.

You may be alerted by a security alert from a site, for example, a message asking if you have tried to change any details.

Be really careful about the extensions and software you download, because that’s where infostealer malware will usually be hidden.

Look out for signs that your laptop or phone has been infected with malware. Briedis says red flags include your device suddenly struggling with simple tasks, a battery that drains faster than it should, frequent crashes, or a browser that starts behaving oddly with redirects, pop-ups, or a changed homepage.

He suggests moving passwords out of your browser and into a dedicated password manager.

If you suspect you have been compromised, run an antivirus scan. Also log out of your accounts on every device and change your passwords.

Use MFA on accounts, as it does still offer more protection than a simple password.

Zadie Smith tells Dua Lipa inequality has made culture clashes feel ‘existential’

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Novelist says demise of Britain’s ‘incredible’ welfare system and housing struggles have led to deeper divisions

The novelist Zadie Smith has said that inequality has made culture clashes feel “existential”, adding that it has become “natural to look to your left and think ‘who the fuck are you?’”

In an interview with Dua Lipa as part of the Southbank Centre’s London literature festival, Smith argued that the demise of Britain’s “incredible, historic” welfare system has led to greater divisions.

The pair discussed Smith’s debut novel, White Teeth, which was published in 2000 and primarily set in the last three decades of the 20th century.

Asked by Lipa about how attitudes towards multiculturalism have changed since the 80s and 90s, and the current “dividing line” on immigration in UK politics, Smith pointed to increased inequality and unemployment.

“When you’re living in basically decent housing, when you hurt yourself you know you can go to hospital and be treated just like the rich kid down the road – then the differences [with others] become interesting, sometimes annoying, but not existential,” said the novelist.

“Now, they’re existential. When you can’t get a job, when there’s no housing, when your schools are falling apart, when the hospitals are falling apart – now it feels existential.”

White Teeth traces the stories of three families – the Joneses, the Iqbals and the Chalfens – and is set in Kilburn and Willesden in north-west London, where Smith grew up. The writer praised her state education, saying it has stood her “in incredibly good stead” throughout her life. “I can talk to anyone. The posh kids up the hill? Not so much.

“Now, when you look at my neighbourhood, the same schools, the same situations – everything is harder, everybody is poorer, everybody is struggling more,” she continued. “It becomes very, very natural to look to your left and think ‘who the fuck are you?’ That is an economic and structural question.”

Smith encouraged developing “a nostalgia for the left”. While “you don’t want the LGBTQ+ politics of the 80s, you don’t want the racial prejudice” depicted in White Teeth, “do you want the healthcare and the schools and the education? I think you do. So it’s about looking back and seeing what was of value – and what was [of] value to me was a social democracy that basically functioned, and might function again if people cared about it, wanted it, believed it could happen again.”

On the writing process for White Teeth – which she began when she was 21 and was published when she was 24 – Smith said that she finished the acclaimed novel in the aftermath of being broken up with and moving back in with her mum.

“Everybody else was out clubbing, I was like, ‘I’m fucking stuck in this room, finishing a novel,’” she said. But Valentine’s Day was approaching, and “all my girlfriends were like ‘we gotta go out, go on the pull’.” She ended up finishing it on 14 February and then “literally ran straight out to the club” – so the ending of the novel is “a chaos”, she said, adding that this is “why young people shouldn’t write novels”.

Since White Teeth, Smith has written five novels – The Autograph Man, On Beauty, NW, Swing Time and The Fraud – along with short stories and nonfiction. Her most recent book was the essay collection Dead and Alive.

Lipa and Smith also discussed facing criticism for their works, with Lipa saying that “learning to live with reviews – bad reviews, good reviews” was “one of the hardest things for me to learn to deal with”. Smith said that literary criticism is “absolutely essential” because it “helps develop taste”.

“I’m very interested in criticism,” added Smith. “Even if it’s a bad review of me, if it’s well written, if it’s intelligent … I quite love ‘takedowns’.”

London literature festival runs until 1 November, with speakers selected by Lipa including Patti Smith, Malorie Blackman and Hisham Matar. Lipa runs a book club, Service95, through which she interviews authors on a monthly basis.

Pubs, festivals and workplaces: how cocaine has become an everyday habit

From pub toilets and private parties to football terraces and far-right marches, cocaine has become a familiar presence across modern Britain. Deaths linked to the drug have risen to record levels, while its supply supports a multibillion-pound criminal industry.

But who is using the drug, and how has the picture changed? The figures do not necessarily show millions of new users, but highlight an established market in which some people take more cocaine, more often – and face greater harm as they grow older.

In June this year the scientists at the Home Office published a new experimental estimate of cocaine consumption. Samples taken from sewage suggested that cocaine use rose by 26% between 2021 and 2025. They estimated that people in England consumed 123,000kg of cocaine in 2024-25: the equivalent of roughly 2.5bn lines, with an estimated street value of £9.8bn.

Yet crime survey data suggests the number of people reporting cocaine use in the past year has remained flat or fallen slightly since the pandemic. One explanation is that a similar or smaller number of people are using the drug more frequently. Another is that the survey excludes people who are homeless or living in institutions, and some respondents may be reluctant to admit to illegal drug use.

Dr Josh Torrance, a specialist in drug markets, says wastewater analysis can reveal use that surveys miss. Having surveyed festivalgoers, he said: “People are often not honest about their drug use with strangers.” Although sewage estimates have substantial margins of error, and the estimated street value should be taken with a pinch of salt, the direction of travel is broadly accurate.

For several years, the market offered cocaine at falling prices. The National Crime Agency (NCA) told the Guardian that wholesale prices dropped below pre-pandemic levels in 2022 and reached “historically low levels” in 2025. However, it has seen signs that wholesale prices are rising again in 2026.

Torrance tracks advertised prices for an ounce of cocaine from a pool of vendors. He says the price fell from about £1,300 in March 2022 to about £1,000 by August 2023. When he resumed collecting data in September 2025, it was about £700. That remained steady until June this year, when it rose to roughly £800; in July it reached £1,000. By September it had eased to about £950.

“I’ve never seen such a quick increase in the price of cocaine,” he says of the summer rise. He suspects a supply disruption, but says the cause is difficult to pin down in a market with many suppliers and routes.

High purity means that cheaper supply is more dangerous. Torrance says cocaine sold as “pure” is often about 70-80% pure, and a more diluted tier (sometimes marketed as “social cocaine”) may be about 60%. The NCA says wholesale purity has remained high in recent years. In Torrance’s view, “a reduced price drives up use. With increased use and increased purity, you end up with more deaths.”

Crime survey data – while subject to caveats – shows younger people are still the most likely to use the drug, but that use is falling among the young, and rising among older age groups.

Other statistics back this up. In England, 33,612 people started treatment for cocaine problems in 2024-25 – up 57% from 2019-20 – which also suggests an increase in frequent users.

Millennials seem to be the generation with the most problematic use. People in their 30s were the largest age group seeking help. Ten years earlier, it was those in their 20s. “We’ve seen a cohort of cocaine users age and continue to use cocaine,” says Torrance.

There has been a 10% rise in recorded crime rates mentioning cocaine since 2019, according to figures collected by the Guardian from 39 police forces.

Police recorded almost 30,000 crimes mentioning the word cocaine in 2025. Most were drug offences, but 18% were violent crimes, including stalking and harassment and one murder case. About 4% were sexual offences, including 344 rapes.

A police record mentioning cocaine does not by itself demonstrate cocaine use but the figures do give an indication of the direction of travel.

Home Office figures suggest that nearly six in 10 police drug tests carried out on people arrested on suspicion of domestic abuse were positive for cocaine between July 2025 and June this year. Those tested may be more likely to have used drugs than other domestic abuse suspects, but the figures highlight cocaine’s exacerbating role.

More older people taking cocaine, increased purity on the streets, and a possible increase in frequent users have coincided with mounting harms, and not just for the victims of crime. A record number of people are now dying from taking cocaine in England and Wales. This rise has mainly been driven by older people, with more than half of cocaine deaths registered last year involving someone over 40.

Torrance says the ageing of a group that continues to use cocaine is reflected in hospital presentations and deaths. Acute overdose deaths often involve cardiac problems, he says, while the health effects of long-term use add to the risks.

An independent report last year found that the UK lacked a coordinated drug prevention strategy with dedicated funding, despite international evidence showing drug prevention programmes work.

“One reason cocaine deaths have risen so dramatically over the last five to 10 years is that we didn’t invest in upstream prevention,” Torrance says. Earlier drug education and helping people at risk engage with services would help.

“The more we can do that, the fewer of these issues we’ll see decades down the line.”

Concerned about its workers losing brainpower as they outsource work to artificial intelligence, Dropbox is considering using cognitive training tools, like memory exercises and attention-span training, to keep them mentally sharp. 

The cloud storage pioneer has also experimented with “analog” offsites, where executives practice breathwork and put aside their electronic devices. 

“We’re incredibly optimistic about [AI’s] impact, but we also believe we have a responsibility to understand how it’s affecting the people who use it every day,” Melanie Rosenwasser, the cloud storage pioneer’s chief people officer, told Fast Company.  

“Brain rot” has emerged as a term to describe the cognitive decline caused by AI and social media, as studies have shown. “That’s why we’re beginning to explore training around brain health and cognitive well-being, while also experimenting with ways to foster critical thinking and human connection,” said Rosenwasser.

While evidence is limited that brain-training exercises improve general intelligence, some research suggests that targeted exercises can produce lasting improvements in specific mental skills. 

A National Institutes of Health-funded study found that training in reasoning and processing speed, for example, produced benefits that lasted up to a decade in older adults.

“We’re approaching this with curiosity and humility, recognizing that this is an emerging area where there is still a lot to learn,” Rosenwasser said. 

Dropbox would not be the first company to provide such exercises to workers. 

Last year, commercial real estate giant JLL set up a “brain gym” in its Singapore office featuring “lighted pods” and “vision training boards.” Workers who participated reported better focus and fewer distractions, according to Business Insider. 

In 2024, another commercial real estate firm, Fischer, partnered with the University of Texas at Dallas on a three-month trial program intended to “improve cognitive performance and support better work habits.” 

The workers showed measurable progress on a composite index measuring “cognitive capacity, sense of well-being, quality of social interactions and complexity of daily routines,” researchers said.

"Sa fille pensait qu'il ne marcherait plus" : comment cette méthode de soins a bouleversé la vie de cet Ehpad de 45 résidents dans ce village en Ariège

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Article rédigé par Clémentine Rivière

l'essentiel À Seix, en Ariège, la résidence Paul Ané ne veut pas seulement "prendre soin" de ses 45 résidents. Elle veut leur laisser une place, des habitudes, des choix et surtout le plus d’autonomie possible. Depuis dix ans, sa directrice Hélène Denat transforme peu à peu l'Ehpad, jusqu’à décrocher le label Humanitude en 2025. La Dépêche du Midi est allée à leur rencontre.

La musique résonne dans le séjour. Autour de l’enceinte, des résidents - ou plutôt des "habitants", comme préfère les appeler la directrice - discutent, jouent ou se retrouvent simplement autour d’un moment de vie. Il est un peu plus de 11 heures, ce lundi 28 septembre 2026, à la résidence Paul Ané de Seix, en Ariège.

La veille, ici, avait encore des airs de fête. Au mur, les chiffres "102 ans", en rose, sont toujours accrochés. L’anniversaire est passé, pas les décorations. Elles resteront encore quelques heures. Après tout, ici, c’est l’Ehpad qui s’adapte aux personnes, pas l’inverse.

C’est l’une des règles d’or qu’Hélène Denat s’applique à faire vivre depuis son arrivée à la tête de cet établissement de 45 lits, en 2015. Dix ans plus tard, cette philosophie a même reçu une forme de reconnaissance officielle. En juillet 2025, la résidence Paul Ané est devenue le premier Ehpad et Centre de ressources territorial (CRT) de France à obtenir le label Humanitude. Une distinction qui récompense une approche fondée sur la bientraitance, le respect, la douceur et l’accompagnement personnalisé des personnes âgées.

Hélène Denat, la directrice de l'établissement et Ghislaine Couderc, référente Humanitude de l’établissement et cadre de santé. Photo Llivia Schmitt

Pour Hélène Denat, pourtant, rien n’était vraiment écrit. Lorsqu’elle candidate, elle connaît peu le fonctionnement d’un Ehpad. Et ceux qui connaissent l’établissement sont plutôt là pour la mettre en garde. "On m’a dissuadée de venir !" raconte-t-elle aujourd’hui en riant. Ses prédécesseurs, les uns après les autres, avaient fini par jeter l’éponge. Elle, au contraire, y voit une occasion. Celle d’arriver avec un regard neuf, après plusieurs années passées à travailler en mairie. Sa question de départ est finalement assez simple : "Qu’est-ce que j’aimerais pour mes grands-mères ?"

À lire aussi : "Il faut se préparer à avoir plus d’aînés" : de 260 personnes à 600 d’ici 2050, comment l’Ariège devient peu à peu une terre de centenaires

Alors elle regarde. Les gestes. Les habitudes. La manière dont on entre dans une chambre, dont on parle à une personne âgée, dont on l’aide à se lever, à se laver, à manger. Et, peu à peu, une autre question apparaît : qu’est-ce qui pourrait être fait autrement ?

Un des "habitants" de l'Ehpad lors de la fête des voisins. Photo Llivia Schmitt

Deux ans après son arrivée, la directrice part se former dans un établissement qui applique déjà la méthode Humanitude. Le principe tient en quatre piliers. Le regard d’abord : se placer à hauteur de la personne, "pupille dans pupille", pour retrouver un regard de qualité. La parole ensuite, lente et douce. Le toucher, sécurisant et rassurant. Et enfin la verticalité : encourager autant que possible la position debout et la mobilité, pour préserver l’autonomie et éviter qu’une personne ne s’installe durablement dans un fauteuil ou un lit.

La méthode, développée dans les années 1980 par Yves Gineste et Rosette Marescotti, deux anciens professeurs d’éducation physique et sportive, Hélène Denat veut alors la proposer à ses salariés. Mais l’accueil est loin d’être chaleureux. "Il y a eu une très grosse résistance, souligne-t-elle, ce qui revenait, c’était : “On n’a pas le temps, c’est trop lent, on n’y arrivera jamais.” Et même : “C’est sectaire, votre truc.”" Il faudra en effet du temps. Et beaucoup d’énergie.

À lire aussi : Des clowns hospitaliers à l’Ehpad avec Un Moment de nez

La méthode a fini par descendre des murs de formation pour entrer dans les gestes du quotidien. La formation représente entre 5 000 et 10 000 euros par an mais, selon la directrice, certaines économies se font ailleurs : dans les protections ou la contention qui cessent d'être systématiques. Résultat : "Entre 2020 et 2024 on a divisé par trois le nombre d'anxiolytiques et par deux les antidouleurs", précise-t-elle.

Maia fait le bonheur des habitants de l'établissements. Photo Llivia Schmitt Un matin d'hiver, les habitants de Paul Ane se sont réunis sur la terrasse. Photo Llivia Schmitt

Et surtout, il y a cette obsession : ne pas laisser une personne devenir spectatrice de sa propre vie. Herenui, 42 ans, aide-soignante depuis dix-huit ans à Paul Ané, connaît bien le chemin parcouru. Depuis la mise en place de la méthode Humanitude, elle est devenue référente en manutention relationnelle. "J’ai vu la différence entre les formations où le professionnel fait à la place de la personne et celle-ci, où on apprend à la laisser faire. Ça lui permet de garder son autonomie et nous, par la suite, de gagner du temps."

Ici, même les murs racontent cette volonté de ne pas transformer l’Ehpad en simple lieu de soins. Si le bâtiment, construit à la fin des années 1960, conserve encore quelques codes hospitaliers, petit à petit, le décor change. Des boîtes aux lettres sont installées devant les portes. Un "quartier des cyclistes" a trouvé sa place. Des bibliothèques apparaissent ici et là.

"On estime qu’on est un grand village", explique Hélène Denat. Une manière aussi de rappeler qu’entrer ici ne devrait pas signifier quitter complètement sa vie d’avant. Dans sa chambre, chacun peut apporter ses objets, ses habitudes, ses souvenirs. L’objectif est simple : donner envie d’y rester… mais aussi d’en sortir. En participant notamment aux choix des activités et des sorties.

Aide à la marche d'une habitante de la résidence Paul Ane. Photo Llivia Schmitt "On estime qu’on est un grand village", explique Hélène Denat, la directrice. Une manière aussi de rappeler qu’entrer ici ne devrait pas signifier quitter complètement sa vie d’avant. Photo Llivia Schmitt

Et puis il y a les petites victoires. Celles qui ne figurent dans aucun dossier de labellisation. Ghislaine Couderc, référente Humanitude de l’établissement et cadre de santé, en garde une en tête. "Il y a ce monsieur qui était en fauteuil. Sa fille, médecin, pensait qu’il ne marcherait plus. Et puis, tout doucement, il s’est verticalisé."

Il y a aussi cet "habitant" retrouvé un jour dans sa chambre dans un état où l'équipe "ne savait pas s’il allait survivre". Depuis l’arrivée de Maïa, une chienne donnée à l’établissement par une association, il revit. Une présence qui, parfois, vaut bien plus que des mots...

Hélène Denat veut croire que les choses commencent enfin à bouger en France, notamment avec le film réalisé par Ryūsuke Hamaguchi, sorti le 12 août 2026, qui met en lumière l’Humanitude à travers l’histoire de Marie-Lou, directrice d’un Ehpad incarnée par Virginie Efira. Les résidents de Seix n'ont d'ailleurs pas manqué de le visionner.

Le label Humanitude est même devenu un argument pour recruter en faisant connaître cette autre manière de prendre soin au-delà des professionnels déjà en poste. Au lycée professionnel François-Camel de Saint-Girons, qui forme notamment aux métiers de l’accompagnement et du soin, des élèves ont demandé une "coloration Humanitude" après avoir découvert le fonctionnement de Paul Ane. Des journées sont également organisées avec France Travail pour faire connaître cette approche aux demandeurs d’emploi.

Un enjeu appelé à prendre de l’ampleur avec le vieillissement de la population. Comme l’expliquait déjà Hélène Denat dans un précédent article publié dans nos colonnes : "Il faut retarder au maximum l’entrée en Ehpad, parce que, d’ici 2030, la population des plus de 80 ans va doubler. On ne sera pas en mesure d’accueillir tout le monde".

Aujourd’hui, la résidence affiche un taux d’occupation de 100 %, quand d’autres établissements peinent à remplir leurs lits. Mais même ce précieux sésame qu’est Humanitude ne met pas l'établissement à l’abri des difficultés qui traversent les résidences. Car derrière le projet humain, il y a aussi une équation économique de plus en plus difficile à tenir.

"Dans les prochains mois, l'enjeu, c'est d'essayer de trouver des économies pour survivre", précise Hélène Denat. Pour elle, l’une des pistes passe par la mutualisation avec d'autres établissements, à condition de préserver "l’âme et la dimension familiale" qui font la particularité des petits établissements comme celui de Seix. "On estime aujourd’hui qu’il faut être autour de 100 lits pour être économiquement viable. Nous, on en a 45."

Autrement dit, à Seix, le défi n’est plus seulement de montrer qu’un autre Ehpad est possible. Il faut désormais trouver comment le faire durer...

This mysterious mushroom makes people see hundreds of tiny humans

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People living thousands of miles apart have reported an extraordinarily strange experience after eating certain wild mushrooms. They begin seeing tiny human figures that appear to move through the real world, walking around objects, climbing over surfaces, and interacting with their surroundings.

These visions are known as Lilliputian hallucinations, named after the miniature inhabitants of "Gulliver's Travels," who stand just 6 inches tall. For decades, researchers suspected that cultural beliefs and folklore might explain why people in different regions described such similar experiences.

Now, a new study provides evidence that the phenomenon has a biological explanation.

Researchers at the University of Utah (U) used DNA sequencing to identify Lanmaoa asiatica as the mushroom responsible for these unusual hallucinations in Southwest China and the northern Philippines. Surprisingly, their investigation found no evidence of any known psychoactive mushroom compounds, including psilocybin.

The findings suggest that the mushroom may produce an entirely unfamiliar hallucinogenic substance. Identifying that chemical could help scientists better understand neurological disorders, unusual visual experiences, and the processes through which the brain creates our perception of reality.

The research was conducted by Colin Domnauer, a doctoral student at the U, and Bryn Dentinger, a mycologist affiliated with the university and the Natural History Museum of Utah. Their findings were published in the journal Mycologia.

In an interview with @theU, Domnauer explained how researchers traced the mushroom's identity, what makes its effects so unusual, and why the discovery could have implications far beyond the study of fungi.

A Century-Old Mystery of Tiny Human Hallucinations

The scientific mystery dates back to 1934, when Western researchers learned that people in Papua New Guinea sometimes experienced vivid visions of miniature humans after eating wild mushrooms. The bizarre effect became known as "mushroom madness."

During the 1950s and 1960s, anthropologists and mushroom specialists traveled to Papua New Guinea to investigate. Their work pointed toward several possible species belonging to the Boletaceae (bolete) family.

However, chemical testing failed to reveal any substances that could explain the hallucinations. Samples were even examined by Albert Hofmann, the scientist who first isolated psilocybin and discovered LSD. He, too, was unable to identify an active ingredient.

With no chemical explanation available, some researchers began questioning whether the mushrooms were responsible at all. They suggested that the visions might instead arise from cultural expectations or traditional beliefs. By the end of the 1960s, scientific investigation of the phenomenon had largely stopped.

The mystery resurfaced during the 1990s, when people in Yunnan, Southwest China, reported similar hallucinations after eating bolete mushrooms. Although the precise species remained uncertain, researchers suspected a recently described mushroom called Lanmaoa asiatica.

Scientists Identify the Mushroom Behind the Strange Visions

Another important clue appeared in 2024, when Domnauer learned of comparable experiences among an Indigenous community in the remote Northern Cordillera region of the Philippines.

Community members described a wild edible mushroom called "Sedesdem" that could sometimes cause people to see miniature human figures. Similar mushrooms were known locally as "Nonda" in Papua New Guinea and "Jian shou qing" in Yunnan.

These mushrooms have an important place in local food traditions, but consuming them without thoroughly cooking them can trigger the unusual hallucinations.

The Philippine accounts represented a third independent report of wild mushrooms causing remarkably similar visions of tiny people.

To investigate, Domnauer traveled to Yunnan and the Philippines, interviewed local residents, and collected specimens of L. asiatica along with other mushrooms belonging to the Lanmaoa genus.

The fieldwork included the first scientific survey of fungi in the northern Philippines. Researchers then analyzed DNA from the collected mushrooms to establish their identities and evolutionary relationships.

The results revealed that the mushrooms called Jian shou qing in China and Sedesdem in the Philippines were both L. asiatica.

This was an unexpected finding because scientists had previously believed the species occurred only in China.

Dentinger also visited Papua New Guinea in hopes of collecting specimens from the location where the mystery originally began. Unfortunately, unusually dry weather made it impossible to obtain samples for genetic comparison.

Although the Papua New Guinea mushroom remains unverified, the evidence from China and the Philippines provides a compelling connection. Three geographically separated cultures have described the same highly specific hallucinations, and DNA testing has linked two of those cases to a single mushroom species.

That consistency strongly suggests a shared chemical and neurological explanation rather than unrelated cultural influences.

Hallucinations of Tiny Humans That Seem Remarkably Real

What makes these hallucinations especially unusual is their striking similarity across different accounts.

People typically describe seeing dozens or even hundreds of miniature figures, generally measuring between 3 and 30 centimeters tall. The figures appear with extraordinary clarity, often seeming as solid and convincing as actual people.

According to Domnauer, approximately 90% of those experiencing the visions describe figures resembling elves, clowns, or other fairy-like characters, often wearing brightly colored clothing.

Even more surprising is the way these imaginary figures seem to behave.

Rather than floating through walls or ignoring their surroundings, they appear to follow familiar physical rules. They walk around obstacles, move across ordinary objects, and sometimes tumble from the edges of tables.

Domnauer recalled one particularly unusual account involving someone eating soup. The person reported seeing tiny humans leap from a spoon into the bowl, where they began swimming. When the person took another spoonful, the miniature figures reportedly remained visible inside their mouth.

Such detailed interactions distinguish these visions from many other forms of drug-induced hallucination.

A Mysterious Psychedelic Compound Unlike Any Known Drug

Scientists also investigated whether L. asiatica contains any of the chemicals already known to cause psychedelic experiences.

Using both chemical testing and genetic analysis, the researchers searched for established psychoactive compounds found in other mushroom species.

The absence of familiar hallucinogens was particularly intriguing because the mushroom's effects differ so dramatically from those associated with known psychedelic drugs.

Researchers now suspect that L. asiatica produces an unidentified psychoactive substance responsible for the tiny human hallucinations.

The compound has not yet been isolated or chemically identified, so its exact nature and mechanism remain unknown.

Discovering it could open an entirely new avenue of research into how certain chemicals alter visual perception.

What the Mushroom Could Reveal About the Human Brain

Although L. asiatica provides an unusually consistent trigger for Lilliputian hallucinations, the phenomenon is not limited to mushroom consumption.

Stories involving miniature humanlike beings appear throughout folklore around the world. Similar hallucinations have also been documented in people experiencing alcohol withdrawal, dementia, macular degeneration, and other neurological conditions.

These connections suggest that the visions may arise from a fundamental feature of the way the human brain processes visual information.

However, scientists still do not fully understand the neurological mechanisms responsible for these experiences or how best to treat them when they occur in medical settings.

This is where the mysterious mushroom could become especially valuable.

Unlike the neurological and medical conditions in which Lilliputian hallucinations occur relatively rarely, L. asiatica can produce the phenomenon in a more consistent and recognizable way.

If researchers can isolate the chemical responsible, they may gain a useful tool for investigating the brain circuits and processes that create these unusually detailed visual experiences.

Such research could eventually improve scientific understanding of hallucinations associated with neurological diseases.

The research team's next major objective is to isolate and identify the suspected psychedelic compound.

DNA Sequencing Reveals Four Previously Unknown Mushroom Species

The investigation also produced important discoveries about fungal biodiversity.

Rather than studying L. asiatica alone, Domnauer and Dentinger conducted the first comprehensive genomic analysis of the Lanmaoa genus.

They examined 53 mushroom specimens collected from natural habitats and preserved in fungarium collections around the world.

Genetic sequencing revealed 17 species within the group, including four species previously unknown to science.

Remarkably, two of those newly identified species had already been preserved in existing collections in the United States. Their distinct identities had gone unrecognized until researchers examined their DNA.

The analysis also indicated that L. asiatica is the only species in the genus currently known to produce psychoactive effects.

Scientists are now working to reconstruct the group's evolutionary history. They hope to determine how the unusual chemical trait developed and whether closely related mushrooms might produce comparable substances.

The findings also demonstrate the scientific value of museum collections. Specimens preserved for years can contain biological discoveries that become apparent only when newer analytical techniques are applied.

For Domnauer, the hidden diversity found in these collections is a reminder that many species and potentially important biological characteristics remain undiscovered, even among organisms that scientists have already collected.

Exploring Fungi Could Lead to New Medicines and Discoveries

The broader implications extend well beyond unusual hallucinations.

Fungi remain one of the least thoroughly explored groups of organisms, and researchers believe their chemical diversity could provide valuable clues about biology, medicine, and the nervous system.

One of the most famous examples is penicillin, the antibiotic originally obtained from a fungus that transformed the treatment of bacterial infections and became one of the most consequential medical discoveries of the last century.

Scientists hope continued exploration of fungal biodiversity could reveal other medically useful compounds and previously unknown biological processes.

The mysterious effects of L. asiatica illustrate just how much remains to be learned about the fungal kingdom. A mushroom long consumed by communities in Asia may contain a substance capable of revealing new information about the human brain and how it creates conscious experience.
