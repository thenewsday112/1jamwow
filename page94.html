Tech firms should pay the creators whose work they use | Letter

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It could raise up to £300m a year, providing UK creators with additional income, write Barbara Hayes, Tayyiba Nasser, Christian Zimmermann and Paul Seheult

Anthropic’s proposal (AI firm Anthropic to commit 5% of shares to fund for ‘public good’, 19 October) raises an important question: who should benefit from technological progress? Before pledging money for the public good, AI companies such as Anthropic should first pay the creators whose work they use.

Device manufacturers have a separate responsibility under the same principle: creators should share in the value their work generates. Smartphones, computers and other devices enable creative work to be copied and stored, yet UK creators receive no private-copying payments, unlike their counterparts in more than 40 other countries.

The proposed Smart Fund would require manufacturers and importers to make modest contributions on devices used to copy or store creative work. It could raise up to £300m a year, providing creators with additional income and supporting skills, cultural programmes and affordable workspaces.

For creators on persistently low and falling incomes, these payments would make a tangible difference. Visual artists’ median annual earnings are just £12,500 – 47% lower in real terms than in 2010. Authors earn a median of only £7,000 from writing, down from £11,300, according to surveys published in 2022 and 2018 respectively. Audiovisual performers report median annual earnings from performing of £17,500. Yet it is creators’ work that attracts people to technology, sustains their engagement and drives innovation. Those creators should share in the value their work helps generate, so they can continue creating and powering that cycle. The government should turn this principle into action by introducing the Smart Fund. Labour MP said he 'feared the worst' during angry confrontation with constituent which left him thinking of murdered parliamentary colleagues including Jo Cox

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published: 17:10 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 17:42 GMT, 25 October 2026

A senior Labour MP said he 'feared the worst' and thought of murdered parliamentary colleagues including Jo Cox as he was confronted by an angry constituent in the street, a court heard.

Former minister Liam Byrne described coming 'under attack' during an outdoor surgery in his constituency.

A court heard Kevin Sullivan, 52, overturned the Labour Party gazebo and ranted at Mr Byrne and his colleagues about mosques and grooming gangs during the confrontation last September.

The Birmingham Hodge Hill and Solihull North MP said scaffolder Sullivan was 'tense and aggressive' at the 'community gazebo surgery' that Mr Byrne was hosting.

He told Walsall Magistrates' Court: 'We were obviously under attack as a team. We feared an attack, we were braced for an attack.

'Jo Cox was my friend, David Amess was my friend and Stephen Timms is a close friend.

'Two of those friends lost their lives in their constituencies in the course of their duty, and Mr Timms was very seriously injured.

'You can't help but fear the worst. I was fearing the worst.'

Liam Byrne MP, leaves court after giving evidence on Thursday afternoon. He said the incident left him 'deeply shaken'

Kevin Sullivan outside Walsall Magistrates' Court. He claimed 'a public servant has to expect some kind of criticism'

Sullivan was found guilty of using threatening, abusive or insulting words or disorderly behaviour to cause harassment, alarm or distress after a trial lasting less than two hours.

Following the case, Mr Byrne told the Daily Mail that 'disagreement is fundamental to a healthy democracy' but added: 'We just can’t have that cross the line into thuggish behaviour on the streets when democratically elected people are just trying to do their best, campaigning to improve the area and help people.'

The court heard on Thursday that the incident unfolded in the Kitts Green district of Birmingham.

Mr Byrne and two colleagues were packing away their equipment at the end of the event, where the MP had set up a gazebo so constituents could meet him and discuss matters of concern.

Sullivan then approached them and asked the MP whether a nearby parked Range Rover belonged to him.

Mr Byrne, the chair of the Commons business and trade committee, told the court: 'He was assertive but neutral. I said it was not my vehicle, to which he replied something to the effect of "Well, it will be gone in a few minutes".'

Mr Byrne said as his group continued packing up, he saw Sullivan 'grabbing one of the legs of the gazebo, basically upending it, throwing it into the road and walking off'.

Mr Byrne said he had 'never had an incident like this' in his 22 years as an MP

The 56-year-old MP said he ran towards the 'expensive' gazebo and 'righted it', at which point Sullivan crossed back over the road towards him.

He said: 'You have two thoughts – firstly you need to rescue the equipment, secondly you are anxious about what will happen next, could it become a violent incident?

'He was demanding in an aggressive tone what we were doing in the area. I said we were trying to help people and to help get new £20million funding for the area.

'Mr Sullivan asked what is that money for? I said among other things it was to fix the parking problems.

'He said, "Oh you mean mosque parking?" I didn't answer. Then he said, "Get out of our area and take your grooming gangs with you".

'I took it to mean there was a racialised element to his hostility and he was aiming to intimidate us into stopping what we were doing.'

Mr Byrne said he and his colleagues were left 'deeply shaken' but relieved the situation had not escalated.

'In 22 years we have never had an incident like this,' he added.

The court heard Parliamentary security services were alerted to the incident and Mr Byrne now needs two security officers with him at all surgeries.

Under cross-examination from Sullivan, who represented himself, the MP accepted he had not been sworn at.

But he said: 'Your demeanour felt threatening. It was the aggressive manner in which you presented yourself, plus you upended our gazebo.

'In our society it's completely unacceptable for people to pick up other people's property and throw it. We felt that you were about to attack us.'

Giving evidence, Sullivan said he turned the gazebo upside down because it had been 'negligently' left unattended and unsecured.

He said: 'Mr Byrne came storming towards me saying, "What gives you the right to behave like that in public?"

'I said, "What gives you the right to come here after all your promises turned to lies?" Then I said, "Take your grooming gangs and sling it".

'I was not aggressive in any way. He is a public servant and has to expect some kind of criticism.'

Asked why he took a dislike to Mr Byrne, Sullivan replied: 'I didn't take a dislike to Mr Byrne. I took a dislike to the Labour Party's agenda.

'I don't want to say he is a liar but he is a politician. He has got to expect some criticism.'

But in convicting Sullivan, of Kitts Green, Deputy Chief Magistrate Tan Ikram said he found Mr Byrne to be a 'reliable' witness.

He told the defendant: 'The fact is it was an act that was disorderly. You had no right to touch their gazebo. It's not yours. You had no right to tip it over, push it over.

'Judges and magistrates don't do politics. We are not here to decide political issues. I'm only here on the niceties of the law and whether you committed an offence

'What you did caused him to be shaken. He gave examples of other MPs and how he felt in the context of what happened to people he described as friends.'

Judge Ikram found Sullivan had no case to answer to a second identical charge relating to Mr Byrne's colleague Craig Rogers.

Sullivan was released on conditional bail and will be sentenced at Birmingham Magistrates' Court on November 26.

Mr Byrne entered parliament after winning a tightly contested by-election in July 2004. He had previously worked as a management consultant and also co-founded a tech business.

In 2010, as outgoing chief secretary to the Treasury under Gordon Brown, Mr Byrne famously left a note for his coalition successor David Laws saying: 'I'm afraid there is no money. Good luck!'

The note was later published by Mr Laws and was used as a stick to beat Labour over its handling of the economy by political opponents. It was also brandished by Prime Minister David Cameron as evidence of the party's financial irresponsibility throughout the 2015 general election campaign.

Writing in The Observer three days after the Tories won an unexpected majority at that election, Mr Byrne admitted he had 'burnt with shame' over the note, which he said had hampered his party's election campaign.

Labour MP Jo Cox was killed by a far-Right extremist in 2016, while Conservative Sir David Amess was fatally stabbed in 2021 at a constituency event by an attacker inspired by the Islamic State group.

Sir Stephen Timms, then Labour MP for East Ham, survived being stabbed by an Islamic extremist at a constituency surgery in 2010 over his backing of the Iraq war.

U.S. President Donald Trump. File | Photo Credit: Reuters

U.S. President Donald Trump announced on Saturday (October 24, 2026) that German automaker Audi planned to invest $4.2 billion to build its first factory in the United States, 10 days before midterm elections.

Audi confirmed the investment in a release, saying the plant would create about 4,500 jobs.

"I'm thrilled to announce a massive auto manufacturing investment in the great state of Georgia by a fantastic company," Mr. Trump said at a campaign rally ahead of the November 3 vote that will determine control of the U.S. Congress.

"Audi has just committed to invest $4.2 billion in nearby Macon, Georgia, to begin the construction next month on a massive plant that will produce 200,000 cars a year," Mr. Trump continued.

Audi, which is a unit of Volkswagen (VW), said in its release that construction was scheduled to begin next year and production in the early 2030s.

"The planned investment intends to mark a long-term commitment to the United States and to establish North America as a third major pillar of Audi's global production network alongside Europe and China," it said.

VW, long a titan of German industry, has struggled with U.S. tariffs, Chinese competition and patchy demand in Europe.

Increasing production in the United States represents an "opportunity", "provided that factories and employees in Germany also benefit from it", said Olaf Lies, president of the German region of Lower Saxony, where VW is headquartered.

In September, the 10-brand Volkswagen Group struck a deal with unions to axe up to 100,000 jobs by 2030, increasing by 50,000 the number of expected cuts across the group.

The cuts will be the largest restructuring ever carried out in the global automotive industry.

Mr. Trump has claimed a "roaring comeback" in American auto manufacturing in response to his aggressive trade policy.

Since returning to the White House in January 2025, Mr. Trump has wielded executive authority to slap tariffs on various products -- including autos -- and countries.

In response, carmakers including General Motors, Toyota, and Ford have announced plans to expand U.S. plants or shift production from overseas.

Grievances over Mr. Trump's trade policies are among fault lines that Democrats hope will work in their favor in the midterm elections.

Mr. Trump is putting all his efforts into campaigning ahead of the elections as he faces dismal polls and unexpectedly tight races in Republican strongholds.

Dafür schleust er einen Informanten in das IS-Milieu ein: den Ex-Kriminellen Murat Cem (Tamer Trasoglu). Bald lernt Cem dort einen jungen Mann kennen, den er für den Idealtypen eines künftigen Attentäters hält: Anis Amri (Wael Alkhatib). Cems Hinweisen geht auch die junge Polizeibeamtin Isra (Bayan Layla) nach, die selbst mit ihrer Familie aus dem Irak geflohen ist. The truth about getting Invisalign in your 50s: I've developed a lisp and mental anguish. But I lost 7lbs in a month. It took me years to take the leap at 59 - here's how I feel about it now

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Four weeks ago, I was fitted with Invisalign retainers – those bespoke plastic aligners or ‘trays’ that midlife women, clearly too old for braces, use to straighten their teeth.

It was the culmination of at least six years of thinking about it, planning it and then endlessly changing my mind.

I used to have perfectly good teeth, but as we get older our jaws shift because our bone density decreases and, like sinking gravestones, our teeth become crooked and loose.

My once-straight pearly whites were getting jagged and ugly. And, after years of drinking black coffee and red wine, I could see they were discoloured.

My dream was to have straight white beautiful, perfect teeth. But every time I went to sign up for the trays, the cost (£3,900) and the rigmarole of it put me off. It would involve X-rays, my dentist told me, in order to make a series of complex moulds.

While I was deciding, I had my teeth scanned by a sophisticated machine, which showed me quite how horrible they were. I was told some would need filling and that I’d also need to treat my receding gums before being fitted for retainers because exposed root surface is softer than enamel and when it’s encased in trays for hours on end it runs a greater risk of decay. I’d need my overbite corrected too, she said.

On and on it went. I’d be paying thousands for days – months – of faff and pain, and yes the teeth at the end might look better, but as a 59-year-old mother of four, could I really be bothered? To paraphrase Jennifer Aniston and all the other L’Oreal models, was I worth it?

Two months ago, I decided that I was, and that the best policy – which probably applies to all dental treatment – is not to listen too hard when it’s described and to close your eyes when you sign on the dotted line. Blind trust was what I went with.

And then there I was, lying on her reclining chair, having strange, huge, clunky wedges of plastic put in my mouth. ‘It might feel a bit odd,’ my dentist Dr Shah said, forcing these plastic monstrosities into my mouth. Odd? It felt as if my mouth had been attacked. My first reaction was to gag. Once they had been ‘clicked’ in, all I wanted was for the dentist to take them out again.

‘Are you sure you want to do this?’ she asked.

‘Of courshhh,’ I replied. I’d just put down a deposit of £2,000. I was hardly going to back out now.

‘How long have I got to wear them for?’ I squeaked.

‘Twenty seven weeks,’ she replied, and then added a ruthless: ‘Twenty two hours out of every 24.’

The trays are moulds that fit my teeth very closely, and at first they are very difficult to put in. As their purpose is to shift your teeth into the ‘correct’ position you have to align them correctly in your mouth and ‘click’ them in so you know they are in the right position.

I have 27 retainers to wear; one a week for 27 weeks, each specifically designed to keep on gradually changing my teeth until – at the end of number 27 – my teeth will be perfect.

‘I don’t think I can speak any more,’ I said to the dentist as I was leaving. Every word felt like a ginormous effort using a whole new configuration of tongue and lips around the big plastic trays.

‘Don’t worry,’ she said brightly, ‘everyone feels that way but they adjust pretty quickly. It’ll feel totally normal in a matter of days.’ Only it didn’t. Four weeks in and I still haven’t ‘adjusted’. My brain has not computed in any way whatsoever that my mouth is full of plastic.

I am supposed to change them to the next smallest size every week but only if I wear them long enough each day, which I don’t. (Instead of 22 hours a day, I’m averaging about 18.) Besides, I get a headache every time I change them, so I beg for an extension and am allowed to downsize every ten days instead.

Still I find it excruciatingly difficult. The two hours ‘off’ – in my case four – are supposed to be for eating, which means taking the trays out and putting them back in again three times a day, but this takes me ages. I have to put most of my hand into my mouth and rummage around trying to ‘unhook’ the trays. It is painful and clumsy.

How does anyone have a life when they have retainers? I spend all my time obsessed by the things. If I’m not taking them out and putting them in, I’m running my tongue over them uncomprehendingly or worrying about what they look like.

My teeth feel restrained and also more tender, so that even free of the retainers I can’t eat anything hard. All I manage is soup, yogurt and soft eggs.

I used to love melon, radishes, bread, but it is impossible to eat any of that. It just hurts too much. I don’t know if I’ll ever eat an apple again. I am also not sleeping well, always grinding my plasticised teeth together. Plus, I’m convinced everyone’s staring at my mouth – the moulds seem so huge, so visible.

Obviously it is impossible to date wearing them. I look swollen around the mouth area as if I’ve been in a fight, and I still lisp all the time. Kissing is out of the question. Imagine having to take out your trays in front of someone else, hooking your fingers into your mouth and forcibly hoicking them out? Then dribbling everywhere.

I feel like Jaws from the James Bond movies – my fear is that someone might unexpectedly lunge in for a kiss to find themselves enmeshed in my trays.

So instead I date without them in, but then spend the entire evening anxiously counting the hours until I can get back to the bathroom to put them back in. All of it is very clearly the opposite of sexy.

I am assuming that people who live together and/or are in a relationship either don’t kiss or plan their intimacy to the very minute around a schedule entirely devoted to the retainers. Eating three meals and having sex in just two hours a day will test the organisational capabilities of even the most efficient midlife woman.

The only upside is that you drink gallons of water and you barely eat because it’s such a faff. Most people on retainers report losing weight. I recently got down to my ideal weight of 10st 7lb using Mounjaro, but now I’ve lost another half stone because of my retainers.

All this has made me regret my teeth obsession, but also become even more obsessed. I realise that for years, every time I went on a date all I could really focus on was how bad everyone’s teeth were. Every man I met had teeth that looked like piano keys, the colour of Werther’s Original. Most were crooked, jagged, sticking out or retrograding back into their mouths.

The more I noticed it, the worse it became. I then began to look at everyone’s teeth and it soon became obvious to me that some people had great teeth and some didn’t. I realised I was in the latter camp.

I know in the end my teeth will look brilliant – or so my dentist says – even and white with no overbite. However, I will have to wear a retainer every night forever more to keep them in place or they will start creeping back. And the thought of it makes me feel very tired indeed.

It is a big commitment to make, and all in the name, essentially, of vanity. Only time will tell if it’s worth it.

Hull’s unbeaten home record remains intact but the Tigers have not tasted victory for seven games after a 1-1 draw with Brentford at the MKM Stadium.

The opening goal came courtesy of an 24th-minute Oli McBurnie penalty, awarded when Aaron Hickey hooked his leg through Tim Iroegbunam’s to flatten him inside the area.

McBurnie picked out the bottom right of the net and though Brentford’s Caoimhin Kelleher guessed right, he could not get in the way of his opponent’s first Premier League goal for over 900 days.

After the break Hull were left to rue some inattentive defending as Igor Thiago found his way to Dango Ouattara, who slotted the ball home under very little pressure at all to make it 1-1 in the 63rd minute.

Jaidon Anthony pushed for another Brentford goal and saw an initial shot land just wide before a second chance forced a save from Konstantinos Tzolakis, but there was a quick counter as Mohamed Belloumi raced in the other direction before firing over the bar at a distance.

An 88th-minute chance was Hull’s last to find the winning goal, but Mohamed Ali-Cho’s cross to Joe Gelhardt resulted in an off-target shot that ensured the Tigers took no more than a point as their wait for a win continues.

Both clubs were required to pick through the detritus of the pre-match display in the opening minutes as balloons and litter were carried by the strong winds of Storm Boelo onto the pitch.

The first substantial chance was for the Bees, with City faltering in midfield after 11 minutes and allowing Hickey to unleash a shot on goal that was comfortably managed by Tzolakis.

Ten minutes later it was Belloumi who loomed and found McBurnie inside the box, but as the Scot was preparing to strike, Brentford’s Mathias Jensen rushed forward to foil the attempt.

After 22 minutes the visitors gifted their hosts a golden chance when Hickey, having failed to collect a ball from Kelleher, lunged at the legs of Iroegbunam and floored him to give away a penalty.

McBurnie stepped up to deal with the spot kick and made no mistake, finding the bottom right of the net and putting the ball beyond Kelleher despite the goalkeeper leaping in the correct direction.

After the break Brentford broke through, and in the 63rd minute it was Ouattara who made the most of lax Hull defending to collect a Thiago ball and tap home.

Belloumi then reacted with a sprint towards the Brentford goal with substitute Cho in pursuit as back up, but the long-range shot was too high and flew into the stand.

The final roll of the dice was Cho’s cross in to Gelhardt, but he was wide of the mark and the whistle blew on a 1-1 verdict in East Yorkshire.

The Crkd Nitro Deck was a saving grace for the original Nintendo Switch. The slide-on $59.99 accessory brought Hall effect sticks to drift-plagued consoles — no need to open up your Joy-Con for a complicated repair. The Nitro Deck 2, made for both the original Switch and the larger Switch 2, continues the tradition of having better sticks than what Nintendo puts in even the newest Joy-Con (though these aren’t Hall effect or TMR). But it doesn’t feel as essential, and there’s one thing I really can’t stand about it.

It’s too difficult to use the Nitro Deck 2’s ABXY face buttons without the right stick getting in the way. The stick-to-button arrangement is a big step down from the original Nitro Deck (and especially bad compared with the symmetrical Steam Deck-like stick layout in the video output-ready Nitro Deck Plus). I’m surprised Crkd’s team thought this was an improvement. My patience with the run-based gameplay in Echo Weaver is already tested enough, and trying to quickly hammer inputs on the Deck 2 only adds to the frustration.

Your preference may differ, but I like having a little breathing room between the right stick and the face buttons. Some companies choose to space them out or, in the case of the Switch 2’s Joy-Con, make the stick small enough to allow my thumb to hover over it so I can press the buttons. The Deck 2 makes me adjust my thumb’s angle of approach outside of what’s comfortable. It’s just a few millimeters off.

The original’s superior slide-in design was traded for a vice-grip design to ensure compatibility with both the newer and older Switch consoles. The Deck 2 has a twisting mechanism on the back that you can loosen, tighten, or lock. It’s clever, though the tradeoff for broader compatibility is a design that feels inherently less secure. Crkd is asking Switch 2 owners to put a ton of faith in this mechanism staying tight around their $500 consoles. My Switch 2 didn’t feel wobbly during testing, but I’d rather not have to think about its safety at all when I’m gaming. I would have preferred a slide-in design for the Deck 2 made expressly for the Switch 2.

It’s disappointing that Crkd’s Joy-Con-replacing solution isn’t as good because I like some things about it more than the original. For instance, the accessory has adjustable tension on both sticks, so they can snap back to their original position faster or slower than the default speed. Also, the accessory’s bulkier design makes the console more comfortable in my medium-size hands to use in handheld mode. It also has a bunch of customizable buttons: four on its back and an extra near the triggers.

Not many similar accessories are out there yet, and the other one I tried (the abxylute N6) wasn’t all that good, either. I plan to test Mobapad’s latest take on Joy-Con soon, and it seems promising. Alternatively, you can add a bunch of comfort to your Switch 2 in handheld mode with either the Dbrand Killswitch 2 or the Mechanism Basecase. And with the money left over, you could buy a good wireless Switch 2 controller with long-lasting sticks and better rumble.
