Just two drinks a day may be far riskier than you think

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Even alcohol consumption that many Americans would describe as moderate may carry significant health risks, including a greater likelihood of premature death, disability, cancer, and heart disease. That is the conclusion of a new study published in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, a journal published at Rutgers University.

The research, known as the Alcohol Intake and Health Study, was originally commissioned by the U.S. federal government to help shape updated national dietary recommendations. Researchers estimated that people who consume an average of 14 alcoholic drinks per week face a 1 in 25 lifetime risk of dying from alcohol-related causes. By comparison, consuming up to seven drinks weekly was linked to only small increases in risk for most health conditions.

"Even low levels of alcohol use come with health risks," says lead study author Kevin Shield, Ph.D., an associate professor at the University of Toronto and a senior scientist who leads the World Health Organization (WHO)/Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) Collaborating Centre in Addiction and Mental Health. "And that risk continues to increase the more someone drinks."

How Scientists Measured the Health Risks of Alcohol

To better understand the long-term consequences of drinking, Shield and his colleagues in the United States and Canada investigated how alcohol consumption over a lifetime contributes to disease and premature death among Americans.

Medical experts first examined more than 7,200 scientific publications covering illnesses and injuries associated with alcohol. Using this evidence, they estimated the risks for individual health conditions and combined those findings with large national health data sets. Statistical models then allowed the team to calculate how different levels of alcohol consumption affect the likelihood of developing serious health problems or dying prematurely.

The findings provide specific estimates that are missing from the latest U.S. Dietary Guidelines. The updated recommendations simply encourage Americans to "limit alcoholic beverages" without identifying a particular consumption threshold. Earlier versions advised men to drink no more than two alcoholic beverages per day and women no more than one.

"While the new U.S. Dietary Guidelines contain a useful 'less-is-best' message, they provide no quantitative framework. Our study was designed to do just that across the drinking spectrum," says study co-author Timothy Naimi, M.D., M.P.H., director of the University of Victoria's Canadian Institute for Substance Use Research and an adjunct professor at Boston University.

"It turns out that two drinks per day, which might be considered 'moderate' from a social standpoint, is associated with a substantially elevated risk of a premature death caused by alcohol," explains Naimi.

Moderate Drinking Offers No Significant Overall Health Benefits

The researchers looked beyond the risk of premature death to examine alcohol's connection to a wide range of immediate and long-term health problems. These included cancers (e.g., esophageal, oral, and breast), cardiovascular disease, liver disease, and injuries.

One of the study's most important findings challenges the longstanding belief that consuming small amounts of alcohol can improve health, particularly heart health.

"We did not observe a significant protective effect of alcohol on health at any level of consumption," says Shield. "At low levels, alcohol may be associated with a reduced risk of ischemic heart disease and stroke. But when you look across the full range of health outcomes, including cancer and other chronic diseases, those potential benefits are outweighed by the risks even at 7 drinks per week."

In other words, while some studies have suggested that light drinking may benefit certain aspects of cardiovascular health, those possible advantages do not translate into an overall health benefit once alcohol's links to cancer and other diseases are taken into account.

Although the analysis drew on extensive scientific evidence, the researchers emphasize that its estimates describe risks across populations rather than predicting precisely what will happen to any particular person.

Shield says the statistical calculations relied on "the best possible data." However, he cautions that individual circumstances can substantially influence the results.

"However, we can't assume that means one person's individual health risk is the same as what is reported here -- that depends on other factors like lifestyle, genetics, drinking patterns, and other choices that differ person to person."

To estimate alcohol's overall impact, the researchers calculated risks for health conditions already known to be caused by alcohol and combined them into a broader assessment. However, scientists are continuing to investigate additional possible consequences of drinking, including emerging evidence connecting alcohol consumption to pancreatic cancer.

"Understanding those relationships, and how much alcohol contributes to those risks, is an area that still needs further work," says Shield.

What the Findings Mean for U.S. Drinking Guidelines

The results suggest that alcohol-related health risks become more pronounced when consumption exceeds one drink per day, regardless of whether the person is male or female. This provides a more specific reference point for people trying to understand how their drinking habits might affect their long-term health.

Rather than suggesting that any particular amount is completely safe, the findings show how risks increase with greater consumption and why drinking less generally reduces exposure to alcohol-related harm.

"Having a clearer threshold helps people better understand what level of drinking is associated with increased risk and make more informed decisions when drinking," says Shield.

The study has also raised questions about how scientific evidence was incorporated into federal dietary recommendations.

In an accompanying editorial, Robert M. Vincent, a former Associate Administrator for the U.S. Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, offers his perspective on the circumstances surrounding the research and its role in the development of national guidance.

"The Alcohol Intake and Health report was explicitly invited to inform alcohol guidance during development of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030," he writes. "Despite the study's adherence to its mandate, its findings were sidelined."

Look up tonight: October’s full moon is for hunters

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

This evening, the night sky is helping to set a spooky mood for the upcoming Halloween festivities later this week.

Just after midnight (12:12 a.m. ET on Monday, October 26), the Hunter’s Moon will reach peak illumination. Here’s everything you need to know about this luminous orb so you can be the most informed person at your office.

What exactly is a full moon anyways?

Much like Picasso or moody teenagers, the Moon goes through phases. Unlike the artist and hormonal youths, the Moon’s are more predictable and reliable. The Moon itself doesn’t change, but our view of the orb shifts because of its orbit.

Moonlight is actually sunlight reflected by the satellite. When the Earth is sandwiched between the Sun and Moon, a full moon occurs. (When all three line up perfectly, it’s called a lunar eclipse, but that’s another story entirely.) During a full moon, the half of the orb facing Earth is completely lit up by the Sun.

Why is it called the Hunter’s Moon?

Full moon names are based on Native American and historical tradition. The Old Farmer’s Almanac recorded many of these monikers, further cementing their place in history.

The Hunter’s Moon describes the time of year after the harvest where people prepared for the upcoming cold winter by hunting game.

Why isn’t it called the Harvest Moon?

Sometimes the October full moon can be known as the Harvest Moon. This is not the case for 2026. The Harvest Moon is the closest full moon to the fall equinox, so this year September’s offering won out.

When will the next full moon land on Halloween?

According to NASA, a full moon on Halloween happens around every 19 years. The last time this occurred was 2020, which means we will have to wait until 2039.

Halloween full moons are also blue moons, which means two full moons occur in the same month.

Are there any other celestial events to look forward to?

The night sky always has something up its sleeve. According to NASA, you can look forward to the Geminids Meteor Shower on December 13 through December 14, thanks to asteroid 3200 Phaethon. Additionally, December’s full moon will be a “supermoon” and will illuminate the Christmas holiday.

How can you best view the Hunter’s Moon?

Peak illumination for the Hunter’s Moon occurs on Monday, October 26, at 12:12 a.m. ET. You don’t need to stay up that late. The Old Farmer’s Almanac recommends viewing it at moonrise on Sunday evening.

Three giant black holes discovered in a galaxy from the dawn of time

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Astronomers have discovered three actively feeding supermassive black holes in a single galaxy more than 12.5 billion light-years from Earth. The finding, made by an international research team led by the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics (MPE), offers a rare glimpse of how enormous black holes grew and interacted during the universe's earliest stages.

The black holes are located in a galaxy known as J0148-4214. All three are actively accumulating matter, which spirals toward them through surrounding disks of gas and other material. Because the galaxy is so distant, its light has traveled through space for approximately 12.5 billion years before reaching Earth. This means astronomers are observing it as it existed just 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang.

Over such vast distances, the expansion of the universe stretches light to longer, redder wavelengths. Astronomers measure this effect through a property called redshift. For J0148-4214, the researchers measured a redshift of z=5.02, allowing them to determine its remarkable distance.

"This is the first evidence of three active black holes in a single galaxy in the distant Universe," says Hannah Übler, research group leader at MPE and lead author of the study.

Two of the black holes lie near the galaxy's center, separated by a projected distance of just 620 light-years. The third is much farther out, approximately 5,500 light-years from the center.

The discovery provides important evidence about how massive black holes came together in the young universe.

"It suggests that processes in the early Universe were efficient at bringing massive black holes together, setting the stage for the massive black hole mergers we expect to detect with future gravitational wave observatories," says Übler.

Astronomers believe that galaxy collisions and mergers were common during the universe's early history. As galaxies combined, their central black holes could also move closer together and eventually merge. These mergers would produce increasingly massive black holes at the centers of the newly formed galaxies, potentially helping explain how some grew to enormous sizes so soon after the Big Bang.

How Astronomers Detected the Hidden Black Holes

The researchers identified the three black holes by examining distinctive patterns in the light emitted by hydrogen gas. Near a black hole, powerful gravitational forces cause gas to move at extremely high speeds. These movements leave recognizable signatures in the spectrum of light, allowing astronomers to detect black hole activity even when the objects themselves cannot be seen directly.

In the center of J0148-4214, the team found a particularly complicated hydrogen emission pattern. The best explanation was the presence of two closely spaced black holes rather than a single source.

To distinguish between them, the scientists used a precise measurement technique called spectro-astrometry. By tracking subtle changes in the positions of spectral emissions across the galaxy, they were able to identify the locations of both central black holes, even though the telescope could not resolve them as two separate points of light.

The observations also revealed the third active black hole in the galaxy's outer region.

Three Black Holes With Remarkably Different Masses

The three black holes vary dramatically in size. According to the researchers' calculations, their masses are approximately 80 million, 600,000, and 2 million times that of the Sun.

Surprisingly, the largest black hole is not the one feeding most aggressively. Its smaller neighbor, with a mass of roughly 600,000 suns, is accumulating matter at a much higher rate. In fact, its estimated feeding rate exceeds the maximum predicted by basic models of black hole growth (the Eddington limit).

This unusually rapid growth may offer clues to how some black holes became extraordinarily massive during the relatively short time available in the early universe.

"The JWST data allowed us not only to identify the three black holes, but also to estimate their masses, accretion rates, and the stellar mass of the galaxy," says Dr. Giovanni Mazzolari, second author of the study and researcher at MPE. "We find a total stellar mass of about 1.3 billion suns, and the black holes represent a significant fraction of that."

An Ancient Black Hole Merger in the Making

The two black holes near the galaxy's center are expected to merge within the next few hundred million years. Their eventual collision could help produce an even more massive black hole, illustrating a process that may have been essential to the growth of the universe's earliest cosmic giants.

"These results are extremely exciting," adds Roberto Maiolino, professor at the University of Cambridge and co-author of the study. "They suggest that black hole merging may be an additional, fast route for their rapid growth in the early Universe."

The third black hole presents another intriguing puzzle. Located well outside the galaxy's center, it could be a remnant of an earlier merger that was pushed outward by a powerful gravitational recoil. Such a kick can occur when merging black holes emit gravitational waves unevenly, sending the resulting black hole in the opposite direction.

Another possibility is that the third black hole is gradually moving toward the galaxy's center, where it may eventually interact with the other two.

James Webb Reveals Black Holes That Might Otherwise Remain Hidden

The discovery highlights the remarkable capabilities of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) for studying black holes in distant galaxies.

In particular, the findings demonstrate the importance of integral field spectroscopy, which allows astronomers to examine how light and its spectral signatures vary across different parts of a galaxy.

Using Webb's Near Infrared Spectrograph in its integral field spectroscopy mode (NIRSpec-IFS), the researchers were able to separate signals that would otherwise have appeared to come from a single black hole.

Without that detailed spatial information, astronomers likely would have identified only one of the galaxy's three active black holes.

The results suggest that other distant galaxies may contain multiple growing black holes that have gone undetected. Finding more of these hidden systems could help scientists reconstruct the history of black hole mergers and better understand how the universe produced its most massive black holes so early in cosmic history.

Cuando el célebre naturalista Charles Darwin lo observó por primera vez en las Islas Cocos durante su histórico viaje a bordo del Beagle, no dudó en calificarlo como una criatura verdaderamente monstruosa. Este singular crustáceo habita en las boscosas islas tropicales de los océanos Índico y Pacífico occidental, sorprendiendo a los investigadores por sus dimensiones gigantescas, su extraordinaria adaptabilidad y sus hábitos de vida singulares.

A pesar de pertenecer a la familia de los decápodos acuáticos, el cangrejo cocotero adulto es forma parte de la fauna terrestre y de hecho es incapaz de nadar en el mar. Si un ejemplar fuese sumergido en las profundidades del agua marina, moriría ahogado en muy poco tiempo al haber perdido la capacidad de respirar bajo la superficie. Su respiración depende exclusivamente de un pulmón branquiostegal humedecido que capta con eficiencia el oxígeno del aire exterior. En su etapa juvenil, este animal se comporta como un cangrejo ermitaño convencional, resguardando su blando abdomen dentro de conchas abandonadas de moluscos o pequeñas cáscaras de coco. Sin embargo, al alcanzar la etapa adulta, abandona cualquier cubierta externa y desarrolla un resistente exoesqueleto mediante la secreción continua y calcificación de su propio caparazón corporal. Durante los delicados períodos de muda necesarios para crecer, el nuevo exoesqueleto permanece blando durante un mes entero, obligando al gigantesco crustáceo a permanecer oculto e inmóvil en el interior de profundas guaridas rocosas.

La anatomía del Birgus latro destaca por la descomunal fuerza que concentran sus pinzas asimétricas delanteras, capaces de ejercer una gran presión de cierre. Esta potencia, cien veces superior a su propia masa corporal, le permite levantar objetos pesados de hasta treinta kilos y perforar sin dificultad los duros frutos de las palmeras. Para alimentarse, el cangrejo golpea de forma constante y calculada los poros de germinación del coco con sus pinzas principales hasta traspasar la cubierta leñosa y acceder finalmente a la nutritiva pulpa blanca.

Socialism's growing appeal meets an awkward reality check on college campus

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As socialism gains popularity among young people, confusion over what it actually means persists, as Fox News Digital discovered while speaking with students in Michigan.

ANN ARBOR, Mich. — Socialism is gaining popularity among young Americans, but Fox News Digital found strikingly different understandings of the ideology among college students, some of whom acknowledged that their generation may not fully grasp what they're embracing.

A recent Fox News poll found that 48% of Americans ages 18 to 34 viewed socialism favorably, compared with 32% of Americans overall. When Fox News Digital asked more than a dozen University of Michigan students to define socialism, their answers ranged from government-funded safety nets and affordable health care to collective ownership of industry.

"If the definition was more widely known, people would support it less honestly," one student, Ahnika, told Fox News Digital.

Other students acknowledged the confusion, while at least a dozen declined to be interviewed on camera because they said they did not know enough about socialism to give an informed answer.

Sam, Reese and Samantha, three students who spoke to Fox News Digital about socialism. (Robert Schmad/Fox News Digital) ((Robert Schmad/Fox News Digital))

"Absolutely. I feel like many people don't really understand, I feel, the full grasp or scope of what socialism really means," a student named Owen told Fox News Digital. "I think they kind of have a more idealistic image of it."

The divide over what socialism actually means comes as the ideology becomes an increasingly prominent issue in American politics, including Michigan's closely watched Senate contest, where Republicans have sought to tie Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed to socialism – despite his rejection of the label.

Some of the students interviewed used the word socialism as a way to describe a government that spends more money on subsidizing healthcare, housing or other basic necessities.

"It's an economic system in which the resources of the government … are allocated into public services such as housing, affordable housing, food like food banks, education, things like that," James, one student, told Fox News Digital.

"Socialism to me is a concept in politics where everybody gets a little bit of everything. Everybody benefits and everybody is really taken care of. Kind of that social net that you see in other types of politics," Kayla, another student, said.

"It's a political system where less social goods are delivered by the market than in capitalism," said her fellow student, Sam.

Republicans and centrist Democrats have both raised concerns about the rising tide of socialism, while young people turn to it as a solution to the cost of living.

"Does the socialism thing scare me? Yes. Is it the wrong solution? Yes," Vice President JD Vance said in a July interview. "But one thing I try to persuade my fellow Republicans of is socialism is the alternative if we don’t have a pathway to give people a sense that the system is not rigged and that the American dream is attainable."

While the precise definition of socialism is debated by economists and philosophers, the ideology generally calls for replacing private ownership of major productive assets with some form of public, collective or worker ownership, though socialists differ sharply over whether that control should be exercised through the government, workers themselves or other institutions.

Modern socialism emerged as a major political and economic movement in 19th-century Europe, fueled by concerns over wealth inequality, industrial working conditions and the concentration of economic power in private hands.

A fair number of the students who spoke with Fox News Digital had understandings of socialism more in line with this traditional definition.

"It's an economic system where certain aspects [of the economy] are controlled by the government such as health care, possibly housing, agriculture, general necessities," Owen said. "It's distinct from communism in that the government does not have free rein over every aspect of the market, but it's not the same as capitalism where the free market determines everything."

How far government control should extend has long divided socialists. Some advocate government ownership of nearly all major industries, while others favor worker-owned businesses amid a mix of public and private enterprise.

"I guess I would define it as an economic system where resources are shared in a more, in a somewhat equitable sense, and there's some level of, you know, public control of that," Zack, also a student at the university, told Fox News Digital. "It’s not really any more specific than that, socialism is a broad term, you know?"

While some young Americans associate socialism with government control of private businesses, an idea that has long faced resistance in American politics, the ideology has gained support among younger voters, even as they differ over what it would mean in practice. The Democratic Socialists of America, which advocates replacing capitalism with a more collectively controlled economy, has seen its ranks swell with members and notched a series of electoral victories during the second Trump administration.

"I'd say I define socialism by the government taking collective ownership of companies’ production and distribution of goods," student Ahnika told Fox News Digital.

"As far as I’m aware, it’s about like workers and members of society owning the product … as opposed to capitalism where you make the product, if you work on an assembly line, the product goes to your manager or whoever runs the factory and then they make most of the profit off of your work," Noah, also a student, told Fox News Digital.

Others defined socialism in relation to countries or politicians.

"Mostly I think of Canada when I think of socialism," Samantha, another student, told Fox News Digital. Elyse, her peer, said that New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani comes to mind when thinking of a definition for socialism.

The University of Michigan’s student body isn’t alone in its division over the definition of socialism.

Gallup found in 2018 that 23% of Americans thought the word meant "equality, including equal rights, standing or distribution," while 10% defined it as "provision of benefits and services, such as health care," 6% said it was "communism or a modified version of it" and further 6% understood it to be "being social or getting along with others."

Roughly a quarter of respondents had no opinion on the definition of socialism, and just 17% described it as "government ownership or control of businesses and the economy," the definition typically favored by economists.

Michigan, where many of the students interviewed vote, could easily decide control of Congress following November’s midterm elections. Socialism is a major issue in the race, with Republicans attempting to portray Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed as a socialist — a label he rejects despite a long-running association with the Democratic Socialists of America.

At least a dozen students told Fox News Digital that they were not comfortable filming an interview because they did not feel they knew enough about socialism to give an informed response, further highlighting the degree of confusion surrounding the term among young people.

While the students couldn’t agree on what socialism meant, they did agree that their peers were confused.

"Yeah, I mean there's sort of a general idea of an equitable distribution of resources, but other than that, in terms of how it should be done, um like what it actually looks like, how power is distributed, there's a lot of open questions there," said Zach.

"Yeah, absolutely. There's a huge misconception about socialism, even capitalism as well," a student named Jeff told Fox News Digital. "So I think these terms in the broader space of political thought are probably allegories depending on your political orientation for good or bad … that's probably the most ubiquitous use of that term. ‘Socialism bad, capitalism good,’ or vice versa if you're a progressive."

Other students felt their peers were too quick to define socialism in relation to other political systems.

"I would say some people would compare socialism to communism as being practically identical, whereas others would just understand it as having a stronger social safety net like in like Nordic countries," Sam said, noting that he doesn’t feel there is a right or wrong definition for the word.

"Yeah, I feel like a lot of people don't really understand what socialism is," Tommy, another student, said. "I feel like they think that sometimes it's communism and they conflate the two. So I feel like people need to educate themselves on what the concept actually is."

One student felt that, if more people understood socialism to imply government ownership of industry, the system would be significantly less popular.

"I honestly think there’s a bit of confusion … If the definition was more widely known, people would support it less honestly," Ahnika said. "Rather than having a good understanding of what socialism is, people often try to antagonize the opposite end of the spectrum, which is private ownership."

Not everyone felt that the ambiguity surrounding the term was necessarily a bad thing, however.

US military gives Elon Musk’s SpaceX access to sensitive tracking data

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This gives the company an advantage in tracking objects in orbit, raising national security concerns and fears of greater control over the space industry

The US military has given Elon Musk’s SpaceX access to restricted space tracking data, raising a new round of questions about whether the Trump administration is attempting to hand over traffic control in Earth’s orbit to a controversial donor.

The access also raises new national security questions, industry observers say.

The move comes after Musk’s Doge in 2025 fired many of the federal government’s nascent space traffic control program’s staff, and the administration has twice attempted to financially gut the department, called the Traffic Coordination System for Space (Traccs).

The moves were widely viewed in the industry as an attempt by Musk to privatize space traffic control. The defense department has now granted SpaceX access to its most precise data on potential collisions among satellites and other space objects, which will give Musk’s companies an advantage over competition that offers a similar service.

SpaceX and the Space Force signed the new contract. The commercial US space market is expected to grow to a value of nearly $2tn in value over the next decade, and the unprecedented access to military data appears to be another step toward Musk asserting monopoly control over commercial space activity more broadly, said Cole Donovan, a former Traccs employee now with the Stand Up for Science non-profit.

“It’s making a company the de facto source for space traffic coordination within the US,” Donovan added. “It’s like giving a trucking company control of all the traffic lights, which they can switch to red whenever it’s their competition’s turn to go.”

Traccs manages thousands of satellites, debris and spacecraft, and helps prevent up to 50,000 near-collisions, called conjunctions, daily.

SpaceX and Space Force did not respond to requests for comment from the Guardian, but in a statement to Breaking Defense acknowledged the move.

“The Space Force is engaging with several companies to exchange commercial data sources for access to sections of the controlled, unclassified information, high accuracy catalogue, where it provides benefit to the US government,” a spokesperson told the outlet.

SpaceX has been expanding its power over launch and other space services, while growing closer links with the US government. Multiple industry observers have said since the Trump administration took over that they fear a “vendor lock” scenario in which the government is overly reliant on SpaceX because it has such immense influence and is so technologically advanced.

The first Trump administration established Traccs to take over commercial space traffic control from the defense department. But Traccs has never been fully funded or staffed, and has struggled to take root. The latest move by the military comes at a crossroads for Traccs as the administration, Congress, and industry debate what to do next.

The administration in fiscal year 2026 tried to largely defund the $65m program during the budgetary process, but Congress re-inserted the funding. The administration has again proposed slashing the budget to nearly zero in fiscal year 2027, but the House again reinserted the funding.

Several employees of commercial space companies have told the Guardian since early 2025 that there is wide support in the industry for Traccs because it would level the playing field. But the administration’s apparent move to privatize and consolidate power with a Trump ally is consistent with its broader approach to governance, Donovan said.

However, a former National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (Noaa) official who spoke on the condition of anonymity said SpaceX has largely been “remarkably responsive to space safety concerns” and they do not fault Starlink for stepping into a space that the government has failed to adequately address.

“My greater disappointment is with the government’s failure to fulfil its responsibility to establish a coherent space governance framework that provides guidance, transparency, and a level playing field for all operators,” the official said.

Musk’s growing lower Earth orbit empire includes a massive satellite constellation belonging to Starlink, a broadband internet company that is a subsidiary of SpaceX. It operates about 11,000 satellites in orbit, and could have as many as 20,000 in the coming years.

For the first time, the Space Force has given a commercial company “enhanced” access to the US Space Command’s most detailed, secure version of its High Accuracy Catalog (Hac) of on-orbit objects.

This includes Vector Covariance Messages (VCMs) made up of tracking data collected from military sensors, telescopes and radars on the ground that track the paths of satellites and other space objects in lower orbit that are down to about 10cm (3.93in) in size. The military largely uses the radars and data for monitoring other countries’ satellite positions, but it is also utilized to track commercial spacecraft and objects to avoid collisions.

The latter is part of the responsibility that the military no longer wants, and was supposed to hand off to Traccs, Donovan said. Instead, SpaceX has been given access to the data. Market-implied interest rates are calculated from the prices of a range of financial instruments, including derivatives such as “swaps”, to establish where traders think interest rates will be in future.

Economists cite several reasons why market-implied interest rates expectations are high. One is that these expectations have been closely correlated with oil prices since the Iran war began.

Back then, that motto was a neat representation of his personal brand of cultural mischief. But the culture always takes unexpected twists and turns – and 50 years on, an almost-identical maxim seems to be defining the fate of an increasing chunk of the British retail economy. While Andy Burnham pledges to restore life to town centres, a private equity outfit is busy buying up national retail chains and then shutting down scores of their outlets, while seemingly operating in line with its boss’s most celebrated quotation: “Where there’s chaos, there’s opportunity.”

Those words came from Jamie Constable, the owner of a set of businesses that includes Modella Capital, which specialises in “distressed investing” and “aggressive restructuring”. In 2024, Modella bought the craft supplies chain Hobbycraft – insisting on its “real commitment to bricks-and-mortar retail”, but then shutting 18 shops and triggering 600 job losses, while leaving redundancy costs to the taxpayer. Months later, it acquired the discount outlets branded as The Original Factory Shop, only for the entire chain to collapse. But its most ambitious move was its recent purchase of the 480 high-street shops that previously traded as WH Smith, rebranded by Modella as TG Jones (a completely made-up name, which it said would “keep that sense of a family business”), and the rapidly announced closure of up to 150 of them.

In September, 19 branches breathed their last, in such varied locations as Bath, Southend, Cardigan, Redhill and the Essex new town of Basildon. This month, the shutters are permanently falling in – among 23 places – Pinner, Truro, Inverness and Leek in Staffordshire. It will inevitably accelerate the decline of printed newspapers. I say that as someone who fell in love with this form of publishing as a child, via a weekly educational magazine called Look and Learn – stuffed, as I recall, with comic-strip versions of Dickens stories, and stuff about space exploration. By my early teens, I was an avid reader of Sounds, the Melody Maker and NME, the so-called weekly music papers that were really magazines whose ink came off on your fingers. Much to my amazement, between my 19th and 26th birthdays, I ended up successively working for all three – until, in 1995, I joined the now defunct company Emap Metro, which set enormous store by its mastery of glossy fortnightlies and monthlies.

Some of these names may mean precious little to younger readers, but spark a Proustian rush among older ones: Q, Smash Hits, Select (which I edited for three years), Sky, the metal and hard rock title Kerrang!, Big, Neon, FHM. Most have now closed, but three of the old stable are still standing, under different ownership: Kerrang!, which mostly lives online; the film bible Empire; and Mojo, the lovingly produced music magazine that I am thrilled to still write for.

Thirty years ago, we all knew that there was no better medium for in-depth interviews, or high-end photography. The management constantly asked us who our intended reader was, and what the future was likely to entail. Even the smallest cover-lines were pored over, as if the mere mention of Placebo or DJ Shadow might attract scores of newbies. And though a lot of us didn’t listen, we were told to never, ever put our magazines in sealed plastic bags (which, in line with a longstanding convention, would contain “free gifts”), for fear of losing the kind of punter who liked to browse before they bought.

And therein lies the TG Jones tragedy. The business has rightly acquired an utterly grim reputation, but is – or was – one reliable place to still find shelves full of printed content worthy of endless curiosity. And now? The culturally omnivorous twentysomething in Rickmansworth will no longer chance upon the latest edition of the cutting-edge music monthly the Wire, and thereby be inducted into a world of esoteric electronica and contemporary avant-garde jazz. The student-age customer in Ormskirk tiring of production-line pop may know nothing of the more substantial music covered by Mojo’s excellent competitor Uncut.

Would-be radicals will be unable to browse the printed iteration of Tribune; film freaks may be completely unaware of the in-depth, art-house wonders of Sight and Sound. Publishers will fall over themselves to alert us to subscription offers, but for a lot of people, they will be prohibitively expensive.

As George Orwell once put it, the UK is “a nation of stamp-collectors, pigeon-fanciers, amateur carpenters, coupon-snippers, darts-players, crossword-puzzle fans”. Products that reflect that enduring aspect of our national character are still at the core of magazine publishing, and why some titles still sell enviably: at the last count, for example, BBC Gardeners’ World sold a monthly average of 164,807 copies, while Country Living shifted 107,596. The broad women’s and fashion sector is also still here. It isn’t all over, by any means.

Still, one can easily imagine a Mark Zuckerberg-esque voice scoffing at all this as so much stone-age nonsense, and claiming that what printed magazines deliver can easily be either replicated or superseded on the internet. Experience often suggests otherwise: witness the fate of the once-independent online music magazine Pitchfork, bought out by the corporate publisher Condé Nast in 2015 and bizarrely folded into the workings of the men’s title GQ nine years later: a classic case of what we now know as enshittification. But there is an even bigger point here about how we now engage with the things we like and love, summarised in a forthcoming book titled Circulation: How Culture Moves in the Age of Algorithm by the Nashville-based US music insider David Macias, with a foreword written by Dan Clancy, the CEO of the livestreaming platform Twitch.

His argument is about music, but its logic applies across the cultural, recreational and sporting board. “The algorithm is great at finding me new music in the limited style I listen to,” he writes. “But it is really bad at showing me something new and different. My listening has become homogeneous, even though I can pick any album ever created.” This is the tedious prospect that the demise of the magazine will only accelerate – so after the high-street chaos has died down, we could be faced with an awful silence, broken only by the leakage from headphones playing the same things over and over again. And to speak the language of the insatiable capitalist, where will anyone find money in that?

Scientists Trapped an Antimatter Particle for Months to Study Its Secrets

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Antimatter is not known for being easy to get ahold of. That’s part of why these particles remain so cryptic. But recently, a team of physicists at Northwestern University managed to trap a single antimatter particle for months. Cooling it to temperatures near absolute zero, they could control its quantum states with great precision without even touching it. The experiment, published in Physical Review Letters, represents a breakthrough in our understanding of one of the great mysteries of physics.

In theory, antimatter should be everywhere. Mathematics predicts that every particle of matter has an equivalent antiparticle, a fact that scientists have verified experimentally. The universe should therefore contain similar amounts of both, but almost everything we observe is made up of ordinary matter, and we still don’t know why.

It is possible to produce antimatter particles in laboratories, but studying them is still incredibly difficult. When an antiparticle comes into contact with ordinary matter, the two annihilate each other and release energy. To prevent research facilities from exploding, scientists use vacuum chambers and electromagnetic fields that suspend the antiparticles in isolation. But this only partially solves the problem. Studying these particles, especially experimenting with their behavior on a quantum scale, remains challenging.

For this experiment, researchers at Northwestern trapped a positron, the antimatter counterpart of electrons. The team, led by Gerald Gabrielse, developed a device known as a single-positron quantum cyclotron, which used electric and magnetic fields to hold the positron in a vacuum at a temperature close to absolute zero. This means the antimatter particle was at its lowest energy state, allowing the researchers to induce controlled changes in its quantum behavior. Unlike other methods in particle physics, this approach didn’t destroy the particle. The researchers were therefore able to keep the single antiparticle trapped for months, allowing them to repeat measurements.

So what can taming antimatter particles actually teach us? Scientists know that matter and antimatter have very similar properties. For example, an electron and a positron have exactly the same mass, although their electric charges are opposite. However, questions still remain about exactly how similar these two forms of matter are and whether there are small differences that have gone unnoticed in experiments conducted to date.

To this end, researchers want to compare the magnetic properties of both particles. Physicists have already made very precise measurements with electrons, but achieving the same level of precision with positrons has proven more difficult. According to the American Physical Society, the new device could enable measurements of antimatter particles about 30 times more precise than scientists were capable of before.

So far, the experiment has not revealed any unexpected differences between the two particles. What the researchers have achieved is an improvement in their ability to detect these differences in antimatter particles. If future measurements find that a positron responds to magnetic fields differently than an electron, scientists would have to revise some of the laws they use to explain the behavior of matter and antimatter.
