Theme parks attractions are never really finished, especially those intended to be built to last. And so it is with Disneyland’s Indiana Jones Adventure, which officially reopens Friday after a six-week refurbishment. The ride is looking as fresh as it has in years. There were minor changes — more large spiders and other creepy critters were added to one foreboding tunnel — but also some substantial ones designed to increase the attraction’s capacity and reliability.

While some ride refurbishment closures result in very noticeable changes to the guest — for instance, the recent addition of a face-shifting buccaneer on Pirates of the Caribbean, or the temple-shattering projections that were added to Indiana Jones Adventure back in 2023 — other updates are less visible yet enhance the experience, such as helping to eliminate downtime.

That is the case for Indiana Jones Adventure. Behind-the-scenes engineering modifications over the last year have boosted not only the attraction’s reliability, but allowed its capacity to increase by about 125 people per hour. Ultimately, that allows for about 1,000 more guests per day, says Nicholas DiBernardo, an area manager for Attractions Engineering Services and eight-year veteran of the resort.

A yearly rehab of the ride is required, says DiBernardo, in part because it is one of the resort’s most complex, with many moving show elements. To ensure that it continues to meet the theatrical and safety standards required, it may appear to Disneyland regulars that Indiana Jones Adventure receives tune-ups with more regularity than other rides at the resort.

When asked whether that’s the reality, DiBernardo answers: “Yes and no.”

“Every attraction is different,” he says. “Indiana Jones typically has a refurbishment every year just because of the size of the attraction and the amount of work that goes into keeping this attraction up. It all depends on the needs for improving show quality or maintenance reliability.”

Two key changes were made to the attraction in 2026. The most recent — and the reason for the ride’s downtime this fall — was something of a wishlist item, and that was changing the machinations that drive its first major show scene, the doors that lead guests face-to-face with Mara, the deity within the Temple of the Forbidden Eye that drives the ride’s action. Prior to the refurbishment, the doors were controlled by hydraulic actuators. Now they’re fully electric. In simple terms, DiBernardo says the doors are now easier to maintain, there’s no longer a risk of a hydraulic spill, and they should properly open and close with significantly more dependability.

That may not sound like a sexy ride enhancement, but it’s one that DiBernardo says will ensure Indiana Jones Adventure runs more efficiently. “In the past, when the old actuators would fail, the doors would have to remain open, ruining the guest’s show experience,” he explains. Not just that, but it slowed down the ride, as fewer cars could be dispatched so riders weren’t seeing the vehicle in front of them.

Elsewhere, DiBernardo says capacity was able to be increased by improving the loading and unloading process, again a change that will be invisible to guests. Essentially, the vehicles park more accurately.

DiBernardo, who works Disneyland’s third shift from 2 a.m. to 11 a.m., says this adjustment, a mix of hardware and software changes, was made over the course of the past year. The problem, oversimplified: Vehicles did not always park in their precise loading and unloading position. Because a theme park attraction needs to operate with extreme exactness to ensure guest safety, DiBernardo says that before this fix, operators would have to send vehicles through the temple sans guests to reset them.

“That was a major win for us,” says the lifelong San Pedro resident. The parking issue was completed in May, and it’s changed the ride’s capacity.

Of course, a huge cause of downtime for a Disneyland attraction is often largely out of the park’s control. It’s us, the guests, who are the problem.

“Our No. 1 downtime driver is guest items,” DiBernardo says. “Guests will be on the attraction, and they’ll have a large water bottle or a backpack. All the vehicles have a pouch for them to store everything, but if they don’t do that, there’s many twists and turns throughout the ride, and those articles can fly out. We’ll have to stop the ride. Not only are we working on ways we can improve maintenance reliability, we’re also working with our operations to help notify guests to store their items.”

To that end, each Indiana Jones Adventure vehicle has been outfitted with new, bigger pouches that should make it easier for riders to dump in their items. All of that should ensure more guests throughout the day can enjoy the attraction, and right now they’ll want to, as all the figures have been dusted, blacklight paint has been reapplied, and the Indiana Jones audio-animatronics have been disassembled and rebuilt.

Walking through the attraction on Thursday, DiBernardo pointed out how all the figures are currently moving with full mobility. The first time we see Indy he’s attempting to force shut a door, one that moves with the figure in front of vibrant animations. Later, he’s hanging over a guest in one of the ride’s grandest illusions, one designed to trick guests into believing the vehicle is reversing (it doesn’t move backward, but rather the walls shift) as a crater of a ball closes in on us.

Walking the track, I noticed many a detail I hadn’t seen on my hundreds of rides on the attraction over the decades. As Indy hovers above us, for instance, his legs and hips subtly shift. Elsewhere in the ride, when a vehicle dips down a hill, it’s actually going down steps. The latter are painted on the track, but DiBernardo notes that over time the cars eventually strip away the paint. I had never seen the stairs. Of course, it’s easy to be distracted by the skull in front of us hovering over a pit of lava, where many of the lights have now been refreshed.

Indiana Jones Adventure has changed over the years. Some opening day effects are long gone, such as the illusion created by actual shifting doors at the start of the attraction, or visions of tumbling rubble near its beginning. But Indiana Jones Adventure remains one of the park’s most detailed attractions, one in which I’m still discovering artistic minutiae more than 30 years after it opened in 1995. And as the recent refurbishment proves, it’s an attraction that remains an ever-evolving work of art.

“The sky is the limit on what we can also add to this attraction,” DiBernardo teases. But for now, it looks good as new.

Despite years of rising rents, Los Angeles County still has hundreds of thousands of affordable apartments.

But the region’s cheapest rentals are also the oldest, and they’ll probably need costly upgrades in the years ahead. Some units are crumbling to the point of being unlivable. Others are at risk of being torn down to make way for more profitable projects.

This marks a major challenge at a time when developers aren’t building enough new housing to keep up with demand.

“It underscores a massive slowdown in construction over the past several decades,” said USC research professor Jared Schachner, who co-wrote a Lusk Center for Real Estate study on aging affordable housing. “When you’re not bringing in a large influx of units, that’s the result: a large share of old buildings with a higher probability of being dilapidated.”

As the L.A. area grapples with an affordability crisis, local governments have a tightrope to walk. Should they enact policies to preserve crumbling homes and keep rents down? Or should they encourage upgrades to the aging housing stock, bringing in more comfortable and energy-efficient, yet more expensive, properties?

As a metropolis, L.A. is relatively young compared with other urban areas, but its housing stock is old. The average L.A. County home is 58 years old — 11 years older than the California average and 16 years older than the national average.

That’s largely due to a building boom in the 20th century that has since slowed significantly. In the 1950s, more than 70,000 housing units were built in L.A. County. In the 2010s, fewer than 15,000 were added, according to the report.

The USC study focused on “naturally occurring affordable homes” that aren’t subsidized by the government in order to be affordable. In the midst of a housing crisis, when governments have tight budgets and limited power to influence rents, these naturally occurring affordable homes are crucial for lower-income residents.

According to the study, the most affordable apartments tend to be in five- to 19-unit buildings built before 2000. These make up 43% of L.A. County’s multifamily housing stock with five units or more — a sizable chunk.

“That segment is a promising place for intervention,” Schachner said. “What types of resources need to be provided to ensure they remain affordable?”

L.A. needs more multifamily housing, but developers say it doesn’t make sense to build apartments in the current market.

Fixing up aging affordable apartments can be part of the solution, but it depends on who’s financing the fixes. The government can subsidize renovations and dictate that rents remain affordable. But private developers and homeowners will want higher rents to offset their investments, especially because construction and labor costs have skyrocketed since the COVID-19 pandemic.

Schachner suggested that local governments should concentrate their efforts on areas with a lot of naturally occurring affordable housing. The Koreatown-Mid-City area, where 62% of rentals are naturally occurring affordable homes, has the highest rate in L.A. County, followed by Hollywood-Studio City at 61% and Burbank-Glendale at 57%, according to the USC study.

“Policymakers could look to preserve units in these areas because the affordable housing is so densely concentrated,” he said.

Another approach would be to preserve affordable housing in neighborhoods with the oldest homes, which tend to be in East L.A. and South L.A. According to a 2020 map by Abundant Housing L.A., the oldest neighborhoods include Cypress Park, Vermont Square, South Park and Harvard Park, all with average home ages of at least 90 years.

“Solving the housing crisis that has been ignored for decades will not be fixed by one approach — it will take both preservation and the acceleration of affordable housing,” said Paige Sterling, a spokesperson for Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass. “Since taking office, Mayor Bass has cut red tape and eliminated the bureaucratic barriers that have slowed housing production for decades.”

Donovan Rypkema, principal of consulting firm PlaceEconomics, said it’s unrealistic to rely on the private sector, including developers and landlords, to prioritize affordable apartments, because building luxury projects is more profitable. Therefore, he said, governments need to intervene.

“We tend to think of housing as a private good, but on the affordable side, there’s a public good aspect,” he said. “Waiters, teachers and janitors can’t afford the housing that exists. But the city needs that workforce, so there’s a public benefit to having houses that are affordable to them.”

Rypkema said the region could look to San Antonio’s Under 1 Roof program, which provides low- to moderate-income homeowners with one-time grants of up to $19,000 to fix worn or damaged roofs. Local governments could enact similar programs to help landlords fix up run-down rentals and keep them from being replaced with more expensive housing.

“It’s tricky to invest public resources in private homes, but what’s the cost if we don’t?” Rypkema said. “Houses fall apart, and developers tear them down.”

He added that preservation should be the top priority. To address the affordability crisis, the region needs new homes, but not at the expense of the ones it already has, because new construction won’t command cheap rents, he said.

Another possible option? Build our way to affordability.

Azeen Khanmalek, executive director of the nonprofit YIMBY (“yes in my backyard”) group Abundant Housing L.A., said the city’s problem is that 75% of its residential land is zoned for single-family homes.

“We’re limiting the map of where new housing is able to be built, so there’s more economic pressure on neighborhoods like Echo Park and Silver Lake that have aging affordable housing stock,” Khanmalek said. “An easy way to reduce that development pressure is to expand the map of where we can build new dense housing.”

New laws such as state Senate Bill 79, which increases zoning density near transit stops statewide, could be key to building that housing. L.A. delayed SB 79’s implementation until 2030, but as part of that delay is allowing projects of up to 16 units in 55 single-family zones throughout the city.

L.A. City Council approved $466.6 million in affordable housing funding on Tuesday. It’ll go toward developers, nonprofits and land trusts developing housing projects.

As for the private sector, Khanmalek said many small landlords are being pushed underwater by deferred maintenance costs, with some selling to larger companies that have the money for repairs. Smaller-scale landlords tend to be more flexible with tenants on issues such as late rent, data show, so the city should award grants or subsidies to help those landlords keep their properties habitable, Khanmalek said.

But ultimately, he argues, building new homes will help stabilize the market.

“If we have an adequate supply of new housing, it’s not as desperate of a situation when old properties get to a place where they need to be torn down,” he said.

The city of L.A. is on a tight budget, but it does have a relatively new tool to help save affordable apartments: Measure ULA. The so-called mansion tax, levied on high-end property sales, has raised more than $1.4 billion for affordable housing initiatives since 2023, including the preservation of at-risk apartments.

The City Council approved a $466.6-million round of funding in September, which developers, nonprofits and other organizations can apply for through Dec. 4. In addition, Sterling said Measure ULA’s Small NOAH program will have $102 million in funding available over the next four years.

“Preservation has been on everyone’s mind,” Khanmalek said. “There are pots of money out there to be utilized.”

Even as recently as a decade ago, Las Vegas hotel concierges and plenty of locals would have described the gap between the Strip and Fremont Street as something of a dead zone. This was a ragged corridor of old warehouses and auto shops, with more boarded-up storefronts than open doors.

“You couldn’t park your car and not worry,” remembers artist Gem Jaxx, who moved to Vegas in 2006 from Monterey County, Calif. “You couldn’t even walk the streets.”

Yet the idea of an arts district had been taking shape since the early ’90s, when the city identified an 18-block area south of downtown as ripe for creative development. Loosely framed by Hoover Avenue to the north and Colorado Avenue to the south, with Commerce Street and Fourth Street marking its west and east edges, this region became known as the 18b Arts District. First Friday, launched by a small group of artists and gallery owners in 2002, started pulling crowds into the neighborhood. Sometimes tens of thousands at a time, if only once a month.

The regular influx of visitors (combined with cheap rents and plenty of retail space) drew a wave of interesting tenants: antique dealers, secondhand clothing boutiques, microbreweries, cocktail bars. As the mix of businesses grew, 18b began to matter less as a set of boundaries and more as an identity.

“Very quickly it changed,” says Josh Kellman, president of the Arts District board. “Businesses here were saying, ‘Hey, I want to be in 18b.’ No one wants to feel like they’re outside the neighborhood.”

Today, the Las Vegas Arts District is a low-key alternative to the Strip for dining, drinks, art, shopping and beyond. Centered on Main Street, but with plenty to explore on neighboring Commerce Street and Casino Center Boulevard, it’s easy to spend an afternoon browsing vintage shops and gallery spaces, catch live music or a stage show, grab dinner, coffee and craft beer — and still have money left for tomorrow.

But the neighborhood is at a crossroads. After a tragic shooting happened earlier this month during First Friday, the monthly arts festival is now on hold as organizers and city officials consider how to move forward. Meanwhile, a proposed Business Improvement District intended to fund additional safety measures and neighborhood services has stirred debate over how to improve the area while preserving the independent spirit that made it a destination in the first place.

As questions swirl, fans of the Arts District hope it holds on to what makes it such a distinctive place. At a time when Las Vegas can feel increasingly engineered around sky-high bottle service, celebrity-chef tasting menus and cocktails edging toward $30, the neighborhood remains one of the places locals actually hang out and where tourists can experience Sin City beyond the slot machines. Nearly 20% of Switzerland’s glacier ice has vanished in just five years

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Switzerland's glaciers lost more than 5% of their total ice volume in 2026, marking another devastating year for the country's rapidly shrinking glaciers. Although the melting did not quite match the record losses of 2022, glaciers thinned by an average of 2.5 to 4 meters. In some areas, the lower sections of glaciers lost as much as 10 meters of ice.

Several of Switzerland's major glaciers, including the Rhône, Aletsch, and Clariden glaciers, experienced their most severe melting on record in 2026. The losses were even more dramatic for some smaller glaciers, which disappeared entirely. Among them were Bella Tola in the canton of Valais and Griessfirn in the canton of Glarus, both of which have now vanished permanently.

The scale of the decline is particularly striking over the past five years. During that relatively short period, Switzerland has lost nearly 20% of its total glacier ice volume, illustrating just how quickly the country's mountain landscapes are being transformed.

These findings come from the annual report of the Swiss Academy of Sciences (SCNAT), which draws on long-term glacier observations collected by the Glacier Monitoring Network, known as GLAMOS. The monitoring program is based at ETH Zurich and the universities of Fribourg and Zurich in Switzerland.

Extreme Summer Heat and Low Snowfall Accelerate Ice Loss

A combination of unusually little snowfall and prolonged summer heat contributed to the extensive melting. The winter of 2025 to 2026 ranked among Switzerland's ten least snowy winters since records began, leaving glaciers with less snow to protect them from warmer temperatures.

Conditions became even more unfavorable between May and September 2026, when persistent heat and dry weather accelerated ice loss, even at the highest elevations.

During the summer, the elevation at which temperatures reached freezing climbed above 4,000 meters on 76 days. That was more than twice the usual number of days and established a new Swiss record. By September, snow had disappeared even at elevations of 3,500 meters, exposing glacier ice that would normally remain protected beneath a layer of snow.

Melting Glaciers Provide Water, but the Supply Is Shrinking

The enormous amount of melting also temporarily helped ease Switzerland's summer water shortages. Between July and September, the country's glaciers released approximately 2.2 trillion liters of water, an amount exceeding four times the drinking water consumed by Swiss households in an entire year.

However, this additional water comes from a resource that is steadily disappearing. As Switzerland's glaciers continue to shrink irreversibly, the amount of meltwater they can provide will also decline, reducing a source of water that currently helps offset shortages during the warmer months.

America's largest banks are working to prevent AI worst-case scenarios. But they're also preparing for the "day after" a catastrophe, Axios has learned.

State of play: Banks are experiencing unvetted AI agent activity, primarily driven by customers providing login credentials to agents, which in some cases appear to have taken actions independent of user prompts, Axios reported Thursday.

  • The activity is distinct from the rogue behavior involved in a summer attack on a startup called Hugging Face, where an AI agent swarm hacked its platform in an episode that kicked off an industry security crisis.
  • Instead, banks fear the ripple effects and risks that extend from users allowing agents to log in to their accounts and take action, especially since agents could take steps users haven't sanctioned.
  • Concerns about frontier AI systems, not AI agents, have led banks to invest in prevention and to plan for the worst.

Zoom in: The Axios report came amid growing concerns about a burgeoning cybersecurity crisis.

  • Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff posted "code red" on X, quoting an Anthropic security employee who said "as bad as things have gotten in the past 4 days, they are going to get a lot worse before they get better."
  • The Anthropic employee was referring to security researchers finding major vulnerabilities in a programming language used to run millions of websites, marking one of the most consequential cybersecurity discoveries yet made with AI.
  • The Axios report on banks, which referred to agentic AI, not frontier AI systems, sparked calls for regulation from across the political spectrum, including Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.), Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis and Sen. Mark Warner (D-Va.).
  • Former White House AI czar David Sacks said banks should support user-directed agents that allow users to "grant, limit, and revoke access," with read-only as an "interim step."
  • Sacks said conflating "user-authorized agentic behavior and cyberattacks is irresponsible."

Flashback: The major banks were part of early testing of Anthropic's Mythos, the most cyber-capable model from the AI company.

  • That was part of an effort from AI companies and major banks to use this technology to better understand and prepare for cyberattacks or other risks.
  • Since then, banks have worked on plans not just to avoid AI-related issues, but to protect accounts, reassure customers and keep operations running in case a large-scale attack hits.

Reality check: This is not just standard, it's required for critical industries that have to answer to the government, shareholders and customers.

  • Scenario planning is a requirement for critical infrastructure providers, including in the financial sector. Avoiding it would be malpractice.
  • The highest-risk scenarios involve fear: Speaking in Chicago earlier this month, JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon warned about the potential for cyberattacks or the possibility AI agents could take steps that users have not approved.

  • The extent of federal planning for a crisis spanning both remains unclear.

“NAZA” and Israel’s History of Automated Warfare

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As violent technologies like spyware, predictive targeting algorithms, and 2,000-pound warheads become ordinary instruments of military rule, once-abhorred practices become routine.

IDF intelligence illustrating warfare tactics in Naza.

On July 22, 2002, an Israeli Air Force pilot dropped a one-ton bomb on a block of apartments, four stories tall, in the Karkash neighborhood of Gaza City. The target was Sheikh Salah Shehadeh, one of the founders of Hamas’s Izzedine al-Qassem brigades. The operation flattened the residential building he called home, killing him, his aide, his wife, three sons, and nine other civilians; 150 more were injured. It was the latest outcome of a relatively new Israeli military strategy adopted at the height of the second intifada: aerial assassinations.

In Israel, the carnage set off a wave of dissent among Air Force pilots. Some had begun to question what, exactly, the air strikes were achieving. For months, Palestinian testimonies from the West Bank and Gaza, reprinted in major global newspapers, had telegraphed horrific scenes: shrapnel slicing through the torsos of bystanders; students taken out while exiting their school. The operation that day in June was, however, a tipping point. Twenty-seven IDF pilots penned a letter refusing to play a part in operations they deemed “illegal and immoral.” Thousands more rallied in support. It was the first coordinated protest among Israeli pilots against such policies in the occupied Palestinian territories. It was also the last.

NAZA, a new documentary by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, the Israeli half of the team behind the Oscar-winning No Other Land, helps elucidate why, offering a portrait of the technological war machine that developed in the years since. Composed of interviews with anonymous reservists and officers from Israeli units responsible for planning and coordinating air strikes, the film digs into how Israel’s military carried out one of the most destructive air campaigns in modern history in the weeks following October 7, 2023. The documentary’s name is not a combination of “Nazi” and “Gaza” as some reviews have insinuated, but rather taken from the Hebrew acronym for collateral damage. Previous reporting on the film has focused on the dystopian array of machine-learning technologies used to kill Palestinians—one officer calls Israel’s air war the “perfect use of AI.” Less remarked upon, however, though no less consequential, is how NAZA showcases this arsenal’s political effects on the country’s liberal camps: how Israel’s embrace of automated warfare has made policies that once seemed exceptionally cruel, like intentionally killing dozens of innocents as they sleep in their beds, feel routine to the intelligence officers who helped coordinate Israeli air strikes in Gaza. And with them, how ideologies once relegated to the margins of political discourse have become mainstream.

For those who have been paying attention to reporting on Israeli military operations in Gaza for the last three years, NAZA’s revelations are not exactly new. Palestinians in Gaza have been documenting the astronomical toll of these air strikes since October 2023. Abraham and others have also reported on the operational details, like how the army hacks children’s phones with spyware to kill a Hamas operative and his family and military lawyers sign off on these operations. What is new with NAZA, though, is the way the film articulates these points: through testimonies from secular, ostensibly liberal young Israelis who served in elite military units. The film’s subjects sit on Tel Aviv rooftops, describing how they helped destroy Gaza from a distance, with the click of a button. “Fun” is how one officer describes the work of coordinating air strikes that kill families sleeping in their homes. “I understand how a man can blow someone up and go home to his degree in clinical psychology,” another officer admits. “It was like a video game,” still another tells Abraham. “Everything was done remotely.”

It would be amiss to view these technological developments in a vacuum; they are part of Israel’s shift to the far right over the last few decades. The IDF began to adapt these systems en masse in the 2000s. The land, sea, and air blockade that began after Hamas was voted into power in 2006 spurred a dramatic change in the form of Israel’s military rule over the Gaza Strip. Israel’s army poured resources into expanding the use of drones, cell phone tracking systems, and wiretaps prototyped and refined during the Second Intifada. The idea was to leverage new military technologies to maintain Israeli control without deploying boots on the ground. Intelligence units—whose conscripts were often plucked from Israel’s affluent urban centers—tracked down members of Hamas and Islamic Jihad by listening to their cell phones, hacking into their e-mails, or poring over aerial images of Palestinian cities, compiling them into long lists of military targets for drone or fighter jet pilots to bomb.

The effects on the ground were gruesome and bloody, but the technology at the disposal of airforce and intelligence units meant that many involved—unlike their compatriots patrolling the West Bank—were increasingly shielded from its effects. The names of targets were displayed in spreadsheets, the coordinates of their mobile phones as blinking dots on a virtual map, and they were killed by munitions launched from armed drones, hovering hundreds of feet in the air. “It reminds me of a Play Station game,” one intelligence operative told a Haaretz reporter after Operation Cast Lead back in 2009, a 22-day assault on the Gaza strip that killed 1,400 Palestinians, an unprecedented number. “You hear cheers in the war room after you see on the screens that the missile hit a target.”

Though Operation Cast Lead was roundly condemned by institutions like the United Nations and International Criminal Court, following the event the Israeli military only set about making its surveillance and targeting arsenal more potent. Israeli conscripts developed predictive algorithms to synthesize the reams of data taken from the Palestinian territories, where Israel’s control of the telecommunications infrastructure and airspace give soldiers panoptic powers. NAZA describes one named Lavender, which culls through automatically translated and transcribed calls, text messages, social-media content, and location history stored on phones to assign Palestinians in Gaza scores, which, in turn, determine whether someone is a military “target” or a member of Hamas or Islamic Jihad’s armed wings.

Technological innovations often go hand in hand with political ones. With time, long lists of algorithmically generated military targets helped make wars on Gaza more frequent and gruesome: By 2021 they had become an annual event. Israel’s growing right-wing camps were galvanized by these spectacular displays of military force. Politicians like Benjamin Netanuyahu and Itamar Ben Gvir boasted to their supporters of expanding lists of Palestinians killed, who had taken to shouting “death to Arabs” at rallies. Meanwhile, the daily violence necessary to maintain Israel’s occupation in the West Bank, with settler vigilantes killing Palestinians outside their homes and burning their agricultural lands, armed soldiers searching high school students’ phones at makeshift checkpoints or raiding homes in the dead of night, allowed a militant strain of Jewish supremacy to creep from the margins of Israeli political discourse into the mainstream.

Among Israel’s shrinking left-wing camp, the same arsenal helped the possibility of unending military rule disappear from view. Soldiers from Israel’s more liberal constituencies circulated from elite technological units into high-paying jobs at multinational technology firms setting up shop in central Israel. They might have gotten their start using spyware to hack into Palestinians’ phones or tracking a reaper drone as it zeroed in on a house in Gaza city, but on LinkedIn the work was reframed as “user experience research” or “development operations.” Though violence gestated a few miles away, as settlers torched Palestinian agricultural lands in the West Bank and air strikes pummeled Gaza, an influx of foreign capital transformed cities like Tel Aviv into hermetically sealed environments.

That seemed to change on October 7, 2023. The atrocities Hamas inflicted were an unprecedented national trauma, one that Israelis from across its ever-narrowing political spectrum desired to avenge. The technological arsenal detailed in NAZA allowed the army to act with unrestrained force on that day. One officer in the film describes how, though Lavender had a 15 percent error rate, soldiers were instructed to use it to mark even the lowest-ranking Hamas operatives out for assassination. Another describes using a program called “Where’s Daddy?” that alerted him when targets returned to their family homes by automatically flagging messages from their children. A third explains how this arsenal allowed the army to systematically kill hundreds of Hamas operatives as they slept alongside their wives and children at night.

On camera, the officers Abraham interviews sketch out the user-friendly interfaces they used to coordinate destruction. Some had no qualms about the unprecedented bloodshed, while others explain how specific military policies helped rationalize skyrocketing civilian casualties. As various investigations have shown, directives from the upper echelons of Israel’s government to wage war without restraint meant that military lawyers authorized strikes that could kill anywhere from tens to hundreds at a time, all in accordance with the army’s flexible interpretations of laws of war.

Reporting by Palestinians in Gaza has long evinced the cost these weapons, and the policies that come with them, exact on their victims. If that was not enough, NAZA now makes the consequences for their perpetrators impossible to ignore. When extraordinarily violent technologies—like spyware, predictive targeting algorithms, and 2,000 pound warheads—become ordinary instruments of military rule and occupation, practices that once seemed abhorrent, like dropping a bomb on a house full of children, become routine. The result is the Israel featured in the documentary: a place where 18-year-old soldiers dispatch drones to blow up houses with families inside of them in between espresso breaks at a shopping mall.

Books & the Arts / Emily Wilson

NAZA should be a warning to other countries—if it is not already too late. The United States military forged its AI arsenal through two decades of drone warfare in Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Syria, and beyond, where the killing of unarmed civilians by pilots sitting on airbases in Nevada was routine. It was released in full force in Iran, as large language models offered up long lists of targets for drones and fighter pilots to strike in operations that left hundreds of civilians dead. Today, many Americans rally behind politicians who champion unrestrained force and expansionist policies, parroting Israel’s own brand of hawkish nationalism. Others simply do not have to think about the gruesome outcomes of their country’s military adventurism, even those who engineer the algorithms now being used to determine where the Pentagon drops bombs.

The protests that rocked Israel back in 2002 were summarily condemned by IDF officials in the months to follow. Speaking to reporters later on that year, Dan Halutz, then the commander of the Israeli Air Force, famously told his soldiers they could “sleep well at night” whilst carrying out aerial strikes in Gaza or the West Bank, even when the mission might entail bombing civilians. They should trust the chain of command, from intelligence analysts who hunted down purported militants to commanders who reviewed the data and dispatched a pilot to kill. “When I release a bomb,” Halutz said, “I feel a light bump to the plane as a result of the bomb’s release. A second later, it’s gone. That’s all. That is what I feel.” NAZA showcases the technical arsenal that helped generations of soldiers heed his advice.

The story so far: As Serbians go to polls on Sunday (October 26, 2026) in an election to the 250-member parliament, speculation and forecasts have converged on two entities. On one side is Aleksandar Vucic, a radical nationalist who has held power for more than 12 years moving from Prime Minister to President and heads the prominent Serbian Progressive Party (Srpska Napredna Stranka or SNS). On the other side stands a leaderless group of students, academics, and other professionals seeking to take down what it alleges to be a corrupt regime which opposition members and international groups have flagged for taking an authoritarian turn. While Mr. Vucic has dismissed the group as a tool of ‘foreign powers’ and appealed for Serbian unity ahead of the polls, the ‘Students Win’ movement draws encouragement from the mass dissatisfaction towards the government and the recent fall of Mr. Vucic’s ally Victor Orban in neighbouring Hungary and is driven by the momentum built with nearly 2 years of protests and campaigns.

While Mr. Vucic’s term, as Prime Minister from 2014 to 2017 followed by two terms as President, saw allegations of authoritarianism with widespread censorship and crackdowns on dissent, the immediate cause behind the students’ movement was the collapse of a concrete canopy of a railway station in the city of Novi Sad in November 2024 killing 16 people. Improper modifications and unsound construction were blamed for the incident along with allegations of severe corruption prompting protests around the country.

An escalation occurred weeks later when students and professors of the Faculty of Dramatic Arts in capital Belgrade were attacked during a demonstration allegedly by an SNS mob. Students and academics across the country came in solidarity with their peers after the physical assault on peaceful protesters and launched mass blockaded over the absence of proper police action against the attackers. Over the following months, the movement grew with protests, marches, and blockades as sporadic violence and police action on the protesters earned more supporters for the cause with over 3 lakh citizens taking to the streets in Belgrade in March 2025.

The movement put forward a set of six demands, including the publication of documents regarding the construction of Novi Sad station and action against assaulters of the Belgrade academics. The movement says authorities had made multiple promises to fulfil the demands but failed to follow through.

The lack of accountability from the elected representatives and executives led to the group entering electoral politics. While the movement had called for snap parliamentary protests during the protests in 2025, it was Mr. Vucic’s desire to hold on to power that ultimately led to the coming election. With just months to go in his second term as President, the head of the Serbian state, and the possibility for a third term denied by the constitution, Mr. Vucic called snap elections to the parliament in hopes of switching back to being the Prime Minister and heading the government in an equally powerful role.

The Students Win, however, is not contesting the election as a typical political party. The movement has always opted to keep distance from the traditional parties and claims to be a decentralised, leaderless system with democratic principles depending on plenums, consultations, debates, and votes for decision making. Election to the Serbian parliament is held in a single national constituency with a proportional representation system where contesting parties get seats allocated based on their vote share after laying out a list of 250 candidates.

Students Win has come up with the ‘Students List’ with 250 names for parliament drawn up from the plena with many lesser-known names and no clear Prime Ministerial candidate. The movement’s website lays open for any and all volunteers to take up any role they feel fit in the run up to the elections, with tasks including door-to-door canvassing, operating call centres, observing polling station, transporting campaign materials, and delivering food and water. It has also put forward an 11-point reform programme for the country which includes eradicating corruption and freeing the media among plans for the economy and healthcare.

The only poll published ahead of the election, by New Serbian Political Thought, gave the Students List a minor advantage with 44.5% votes in favour against 42.7% for Mr. Vucic’s SNS. However, analysts have expressed concerns over a leaderless party forming a government. Sympathisers of Mr. Vucic, meanwhile, have come down hard on the students’ movement, opting to delegitimise the support the group claims to have garnered with its intensive and personalised campaign by calling it baseless and questioning the political and administrative experience of the new entrant. Mr. Vucic has gone all guns blaring against the movement, terming it an attempt by nameless external actors to destabilise the country in a ‘foreign-funded coup’, doubling down on the claims towards the end of the election campaign.

“They tried to destroy our country on an external order”, he said at the final pre-election meeting in Belgrade on Thursday even while making a call for reconciliation to his political opponents. Opposition parties, however, have acknowledged the momentum generated by the movement, as some parties chose to stay away from the election while others put out a smaller list for polls.

With youth movements making a wave around the world, including in India, the outcome of the Serbian election will be keenly observed by optimistic youngsters and ruling powers alike. A victory for the Students List will open possibilities for movements that do not require a popular face that could end up becoming a personality cult. But the challenge for the group, if victorious, would be to keep its decentralised and democratic identity intact in a parliamentary system that requires fixed roles and the inevitable positions of power.

Long After Maui Fire, Residents Found With High Levels of Toxic Metals in Their Bodies

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

This article was originally published by Grist, in partnership with The Intercept, and is reproduced here as part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

Damage from the 2023 fire that destroyed the Hawaiian town of Lahaina. Matt McClain / The Washington Post via Getty Images via Grist

More than a year after a devastating wildfire destroyed the Hawaiian town of Lahaina, residents were found to have high levels of arsenic and other toxic metals in their bodies.

In August of 2023, a series of wildfires broke out across Maui, killing more than 100 people, destroying thousands of homes, and causing billions of dollars in damage. Noelani Ahia, who is Indigenous Kanaka Maoli and cofounder of Maui Medic Healers Hui, helped mobilize nearly 600 volunteers in Lahaina, including doctors, nurses, and therapists.

Three years later, the housing and health crises remain acute. Maui Medic Healers Hui runs a free clinic, but Ahia wishes it had 10 more to address the community’s needs. “The fire may be out, but the catastrophe is not over by any stretch of the imagination,” she said.

The devastation extended well beyond the lives lost and buildings destroyed. Research published last year by researchers with the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa’s Maui Wildfire Exposure Study, or MauiWES, found that more than 1 in 5 residents had reduced lung function.

Now, new research from the same project has found elevated levels of toxic metals in the island’s residents months after the fires, with higher exposure associated with impaired lung function. The findings offer further evidence that the health consequences of catastrophic wildfires can persist long after the flames are extinguished and the smoke clears.

“What we consider and sometimes look at as a one-time event isn’t really one-time anymore,” Alika Maunakea, who is Kanaka Maoli and codirector of MauiWES, told Grist.

Researchers don’t yet know how much of the contamination came from the fires and how much was already present in the environment. That distinction matters as wildfires increasingly race through towns like Lahaina and cities like Los Angeles, where burning of homes, cars, and infrastructure can leave contaminants that people encounter for months or years afterward.

“We have traditionally thought about wildfire exposure primarily through the lens of smoke,” Maunakea said in a press release. “But when an entire community burns, we are dealing with a much more complicated exposure environment.” Researchers must also understand what contaminants were already present, what the fire may have released, and what entered residents’ bodies, he said.

The research, which studied 1,400 adults across Maui six to 18 months after the fires, measured 24 metals in urine samples. It found that participants had higher concentrations of several metals than people in a nationwide health survey conducted before the fires. That included 2.2 times as much arsenic, 2.3 times as much barium, and 3.8 times as much manganese. Antimony, which is used in flame retardant materials and has been previously linked to lung irritation, stomach pain, and other symptoms, was found at levels nearly 40 times higher.

Because the researchers lack comparable data from before the fires, they could not determine how much of the metal exposure resulted from the disaster. Maunakea told Grist that answering that question is the next step for the project, which has received a $3.6 million grant from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences. That work will include investigating whether and to what degree past agricultural and industrial activity contributed to the contamination.

“It’s important to realize that these are all part of settler colonialism,” Ahia said. “So what we’re looking at is confounding factors that are to the detriment of the health of our local people.”

Decades of plantation agriculture altered water systems, introduced herbicides, and displaced native plants, leaving a legacy on the islands that is still felt today. Disasters like fires and storms can compound those ongoing environmental problems.

“If we begin the story when the fire starts, we miss the decisions that shaped the landscape and the community’s ability to respond,” said Katie Kamelamela, who is Indigenous Kanaka Maoli and an assistant professor at Arizona State University based in Hawaii. “Micro climate change acts on places already shaped by histories of land ownership, water diversion, development, and unequal access to resources.”

Kamelamela said true recovery must look beyond the fire. “I want recovery to be measured by whether people can remain in their communities, care for ʻāina, and pass on their knowledge and practices in place,” she said, using the Hawaiian word for land, which carries deeper meaning and relationship than property or mere place. “Rebuilding should not recreate the same conditions that left people at risk. Caring for ʻāina and caring for people need to be funded and planned together.”

Maunakea said the multitude of intersecting pressures is one reason why he wanted to pursue this research. As climate change continues to accelerate, longstanding issues facing Native Hawaiians like housing, the harms of tourism, and the legacy of plantations can amplify problems. “We worry that disasters like this that we experience on Maui, as well as the floods and other storms that we’re experiencing now, is just going to continue widening the gaps in health disparities that already exist in those populations,” he said.

Moving forward, the project will not just identify health risks but also help residents respond to them. Study participants receive their results, discuss them, and meet with health professionals. “People now know that there’s these types of potential hazards, and what to do about them is a big concern,” Maunakea said. “We still don’t have all the answers for sure, but we are taking the next steps to get a better picture and inform our community around it.”
