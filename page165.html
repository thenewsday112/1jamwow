Abdul El-Sayed's America Tastes Like Meatloaf and Ful Medames

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In Person of Interest we talk to the people catching our eye right now about their projects past and present. Next up, we chat with Michigan US Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed, who is in the closing weeks of his campaign, about food affordability and Detroit's food scene.

Sharia law? Abdul El-Sayed has a different proposition: shawarma law.

In an April cooking video published on TikTok, Egyptian American US Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed, wearing a Detroit Lions apron, joined Lebanese American food creator Summer Homayed to prepare beef shawarma, a staple of metro Detroit's Arab food scene. As they grilled marinated flank steak, drained potatoes, and mixed garlic and herbs, Homayed peppered him with political questions. (“We don't usually grill shawarma,” he said in the video, alluding to the use of a traditional spit.)

El-Sayed responded with food-adjacent commentary. The steak was purchased without corporate money, just like his campaign. He’ll compromise on how to prepare shawarma, but never on Medicare for All.

El-Sayed is campaigning on bread-and-butter issues: getting money out of politics, putting money in people's pockets, and Medicare for All. His Republican opponent, former congressman Mike Rogers, has sought to cast suspicion on El-Sayed’s Muslim identity by invoking alleged ties to extremist groups, claims challenged by independent fact-checkers.

“People are worried about that Sharia law, especially Michiganders. What do you have to say about that?” Homayed asked in the video.

“Look, I was born and raised in America. I believe deeply in the U.S. Constitution. So Sharia law? No, I'm here for Shawarma law,” El-Sayed quipped.

The Nov. 3 election could determine control of the Senate and make El-Sayed the country's first Muslim senator. Born to Egyptian immigrants, he was raised in suburban Detroit by his father and stepmother, a white woman born in Michigan. El-Sayed grew up eating his stepmother's meatloaf for dinner and his father's ful medames, an Egyptian fava bean dish, for breakfast.

In an interview with Bon Appétit, the former Detroit health director chatted about Michigan's food culture, grocery affordability, and the foods that shaped him.

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

Bon Appétit: If we were able to meet for an interview over a meal, where would you want to meet, and why?

Abdul El-Sayed: I'll give you four spots. There's a place near my house called Ricewood, a barbecue joint. They have a Friday and Saturday burger special, and they always rotate it. It's one of the best burgers I've ever had. Then, there’s Dearborn Meat Market, a butcher shop with a grill in the back with these two guys grilling up some of the best kebabs you've ever had. Third would be Sicily’s Pizza in Detroit. I think it's the best Detroit-style pizza. [It’s a] three-ingredient, aged fermented sourdough, but they figured out how to make Detroit-style pizza without making it oily or too heavy. And then a fourth one would probably be going out for ice cream. I love Midwestern-style ice cream, just the joy of eating it outside on a summer day. It's the joy of childhood and one of my favorite things to do with my kids.

BA: Can you share some early childhood food memories?

AE: As a child, some of my favorite memories were going down to the Eastern Market and picking up our groceries. We’d go to the cheese counter, sample everything, and pick one to take home. My dad would spend Saturday afternoon cooking, and we’d have a meal together as a family. Food is how my parents said I love you, and it's a profoundly universal human language.

My mom would make traditional Midwest meatloaf or tuna noodle casserole. On Sunday mornings my dad cooked ful, a fava bean paste, which is a traditional Middle Eastern breakfast, especially in Egyptian families.

When we made burgers, it was always kofta in a bun. But you know, as a kid, I wanted the authentic thing. So now, I make traditional pizza, traditional burgers, traditional steak. My wife is Indian American, and so my kids always prefer the spiced food.

BA: Metro Detroit has become a food destination, with an abundance of halal restaurants. What do you make of how the region's food scene has evolved?

AE: When I was a kid, that whole ecosystem of halal burger shops and pizza didn't exist. It was a rare treat to be able to have a straight-up burger. A lot of that's changed and is catering to all kinds of different taste buds now.

The beautiful thing about food, art, and music is that you can fuse all kinds of different emotions and backgrounds into them. You have the most traditional burger or the fusion restaurants, like Warda Patisserie, which is just phenomenal. I love that so much of our history and culture in Michigan is about bringing together different kinds of people.

The job of a campaign is to communicate about shared values, and food is a tool for communication. When we share food, we’re also sharing history, culture, and traditions with each other. It’s an amazing way to gather community and talk about where we’ve been, and where we’re headed.

BA: You talk regularly about affordability and the cost of groceries in your campaign. If you're elected to the Senate, what are some things you could do to provide relief to Michiganders who are struggling to pay for groceries?

AE: There are a couple of reasons why groceries are so expensive right now that we should put an end to immediately: the war in Iran and the tariffs. Diesel is at an all-time high because of the war; every item in your grocery store was shipped there by truck and needed diesel. Our local farmers need potash fertilizer. A lot of it comes from Canada, which is now being tariffed.

Additionally, a few corporations dominate our food space. Eighty-five percent of our meat supply comes from four meatpackers who are able to suppress the prices they pay farmers and raise the prices you pay at the grocery store. We need to stand up to monopolies who use their power to suppress our markets.

We also need to invest in local agriculture, create opportunities for local farmers to sell their products to local school districts, or fight for their right to repair their own tractor. We can take on ways that their prices have gone up.

It is becoming far harder to operate a profitable small business or restaurant in America, in large part because of the prices of inputs and the ability of people to afford it.

BA: I'm a longtime journalist who lost my job last year and had to apply for food stamps. As more of the workforce turns to freelancing and contract work, documenting income and employment can make it difficult to meet new SNAP work requirements and impending Medicaid requirements. How would you ensure these workers can access the safety net when they need it?

AE: It's a crazy thing that we've put so much time and effort into telling people what they can't have, rather than just creating good universal programs for everybody. Medicare for All would address all of the time that people have to put into figuring out how to provide paperwork, to then give that paperwork to somebody employed specifically to tell people they can't have the service.

Think about the way they've raised work requirements in place for Medicaid and SNAP. People are really going to face a bigger burden next year. We need to be building out a much broader safety net. Things like childcare should be free. Education should be free. Healthcare should be free. Think of the waste, fraud, and abuse being created to keep people off these programs, rather than making sure folks have the food they need—it’s backwards. All to cut taxes for the richest people and fight wars we have no business fighting.

Kritik wegen Abholzung – Google rodet illegal Wald für Rechenzentrum in Finnland

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Einige Elemente auf SRF.ch funktionieren nur mit aktiviertem JavaScript.

Nach der Rodung von 250’000 Bäumen stehen die Bauarbeiten still. Die Regierung plant nun strengere Gesetze zum Waldschutz.

Wo vor Kurzem noch 250’000 Bäume wuchsen, liegen heute Schlamm, Erde, einzelne Wurzeln und Äste. Google hat in Finnland illegal ein Waldstück abgeholzt, um ein Rechenzentrum zu bauen. Die gerodete Fläche entspricht rund 460 Fussballfeldern oder der gesamten Zürcher Altstadt.

Die Empörung in Finnland ist gross: 93’000 Personen haben eine Forderung unterschrieben, um entsprechende Gesetze zum Schutz des Waldes zu verschärfen.

Google schreibt in einer Stellungnahme, man habe «bona fide», also in gutem Glauben, gehandelt und sei davon ausgegangen, alle Gesetze einzuhalten. Gleichzeitig begründet der Techkonzern die vorzeitigen Abholzungen mit der Dringlichkeit, möglichst schnell zusätzliche Rechenkapazität zu schaffen.

«Es geht darum, im internationalen Wettkampf um die künstliche Intelligenz mithalten zu können», erklärt SRF-Nordeuropa-Korrespondent Bruno Kaufmann die Argumentation des Unternehmens.

Es ist nicht das erste Mal, dass Google mit Abholzungen in Finnland für Kritik sorgt. Bereits vor sieben Jahren wurden auf dem Gelände des heutigen Rechenzentrums in Hamina, unweit der finnisch-russischen Grenze, Hunderttausende Bäume gefällt.

Schon damals habe das Vorgehen viel Kritik ausgelöst und der Umwelt nachweislich geschadet, sagt Kaufmann. Die Behörden hätten Google angesichts der Milliardeninvestitionen jedoch weitgehend gewähren lassen: «Sie haben damals eigentlich noch beide Augen zugemacht.»

Im aktuellen Fall wird Google zwar nicht bestraft. Jedoch wurde jetzt jegliche Bautätigkeit auf dem Gelände gestoppt. Zudem wird laut Kaufmann eine Gesetzesänderung vorbereitet, damit solche Verstösse gegen die Umweltgesetze künftig bestraft werden können.

Finnland bietet günstige Voraussetzungen für Rechenzentren: Das Land ist politisch sicher, geologisch stabil und hat ein kühles Klima, das die Kühlung der Anlagen erleichtert. Insgesamt hätte Finnland 145 Rechenzentren geplant.

Der Streit um Google könnte diese Pläne nun beeinflussen. Im kommenden April stehen Parlamentswahlen an. Die bürgerliche Regierung von Ministerpräsident Petteri Orpo kommt laut Meinungsumfragen auf weniger als 40 Prozent der Stimmen, die rot-grüne Opposition hingegen auf über 50 Prozent.

«Deshalb wird jetzt erwartet, dass dieser Streit um die Rechenzentren amerikanischer Techfirmen diesen Trend hin zur Opposition noch verstärken dürfte», sagt Bruno Kaufmann. Auch deshalb stehe die Regierung unter Zugzwang.

La pregunta “¿dónde vives?”, que antes servía para llenar silencios o ubicar a una persona, es hoy un interrogante afilado sobre tu situación económica y tu salud mental. Germán Aranda repasa cada jueves las principales noticias y debates sobre la crisis de la vivienda.

Ya estamos con la cantinela electoral de precampaña y, por ahora, en clave vivienda, el PSOE bastante tiene con que le aprueben el decreto y empiece a dar resultado, con numerosas medidas que van de la protección al inquilino al impulso al parque público, aunque se quedarán cortas a la hora de atajar de lleno la crisis que vivimos. La ministra no quiso dar aún pistas en su último encuentro con los medios sobre qué otras medidas llevarán a la campaña. En cuanto al Frente Amplio y Podemos, aún falta que se asienten sus candidaturas y sus rifirrafes para que empecemos a escuchar sus medidas.

Al otro lado, ante la amenaza de que gane la derecha, sí que empiezan a sonar algunas medidas. Y no hay buenas noticias. Mientras que Vox ha insistido con vincular la inmigración a la crisis de vivienda, como hace con todos los problemas de España, el PP de Feijóo se ha sentido cómodo agitando bulos sobre ese miedo a la okupación que tanto espanta a algunos propietarios aunque el riesgo es ínfimo.

Que el PP haya encargado a Albiol un plan antiokupación y que ese sea el titular de sus propuestas de vivienda es una pésima noticia para las casi tres millones de personas que viven ahogadas por el alquiler.

Las medidas en materia de vivienda del PP se centran básicamente en tres patas que el PSOE ya ha ido avanzando a un ritmo mucho más lento del que necesitaba el país: las ayudas fiscales, subvenciones a la compra y construcción de “un millón de viviendas” en una nueva “España de los propietarios”. Ya lo dijo el franquismo: “No queremos una España de proletarios, sino de propietarios”. Aunque por mucho que bajen los precios o se moderen, el alquiler nunca va a desaparecer y debería ser un modelo de vida viable y estable.

Que ninguna de estas medidas va a tener un impacto a corto plazo es algo que reconoce él mismo, tal y como destacó en su cobertura el compañero Aitor Riveiro. Hasta que se construya el millón de viviendas que prometió, “vamos a pasar años difíciles”, dijo el propio Feijóo.

Sin contar con que la construcción de un millón de viviendas (no detalló cuántas serán públicas) puede moderar los precios pero no hacerlos bajar en picado y es difícil imaginar que los tres millones de personas que viven asfixiadas por el alquiler vayan a comprar una vivienda. En un mercado especulativo al alza en que la finalización de un contrato de alquiler permite subidas desorbitadas, no atacar este modelo contractual a corto plazo o los precios de mercado (todas las Comunidades del PP salvo Galicia se han negado) condena a los inquilinos al ahogo económico y les complica el ahorro para la compra de vivienda, cuyo precio también debería ser intervenido a estas alturas mientras no haya vivienda pública suficiente para competir.

Cuando el PP defiende que liderará una política de vivienda opuesta a la del Gobierno y le acusa del fracaso, esconde una realidad: que las principales competencias en vivienda las tienen las Comunidades Autónomas, donde el PP tiene un dominio absoluto, ya que gobierna en 13 de las 19 comunidades. Y no son, precisamente, las que tienen la mejor situación: Madrid, Baleares, Comunidad Valenciana, Murcia o Andalucía no son precisamente los mejores ejemplos.

Por otra parte, aunque la construcción, sobre todo de vivienda pública, debe jugar un papel clave en la recuperación de la crisis de vivienda, hay que andar con ojo. Una reforma de la Ley de Suelo que permita atrocidades en plena crisis climática puede ser contraproducente para la calidad de vida en una España que necesita espacios naturales. Pero es que además, los reparos de Podemos y Greenpeace a la propuesta del PSOE se veían avalados por la amenaza de pelotazos urbanísticos ilegales. Una reforma de la Ley de Suelo aún más permisiva con las empresas como la que puede proponer el PP puede ser nefasta. El pretexto de la vivienda, por urgente que sea, no puede servir para destrozar el medio ambiente o para alimentar fiebres especuladoras.

Las propuestas de unos y otros partidos se irán dirimiendo en las próximas semanas y PSOE y PP deben batirse en un debate de vivienda en breve. Pero mientras tanto, las calles y los sindicatos preparan la huelga general del próximo 11 de noviembre, motivada precisamente por la crisis de la vivienda. El Sindicato de Inquilinas tiene una web activa muy intuitiva donde te puedes apuntar a hasta 240 actividades (por ahora) alrededor de toda España, tal y como contó Guillermo Hormigo este jueves. Las protestas del sindicato, además, se han contagiado a Portugal, donde la policía desalojó la principal plaza con acampada, pero los manifestantes desafían al poder, contó desde Lisboa Ruben Martins.

No pasa un día, entretanto, sin que salga algún informe o estudio que ponga cifras a la magnitud de cómo nos atraviesa esta crisis. A principios de semana, Oxfam publicaba un artículo que explicitaba que 770.000 trabajadores se vuelven pobres tras pagar la vivienda, una situación que afecta tres veces más a inquilinos que a hipotecados.

Otro estudio de la misma entidad citado por Gemma Barra en un reportaje de esta semana detallaba que la incidencia de problemas de salud mental se duplica entre quienes destinan más del 50% del alquiler. “Nos quitamos de medicinas para pagar la vivienda”, resumía el doctor en psicología y presidente de la Fundación Psicólogos sin fronteras en el texto.

Y este jueves, una encuesta de GAD3 apuntaba a que el 65% de los menores de 30 años necesitó ayuda para emanciparse, mientras que el 70% de las personas de la misma franja de edad renunciaron en alguna ocasión a una vivienda por no cumplir los requisitos.

Son las personas con trabajo que pasan a ser pobres después de pagar un alquiler, según un informe publicado esta semana por Oxfam.

El Ministerio de Consumo ha exigido a dos plataformas que retiren 8.300 anuncios de pisos turísticos que se ofertan como alquileres de temporada, algo que contradice expresamente lo aprobado en el último decreto de vivienda.

Por otra parte, el Gobierno vasco ha comprado 510 viviendas protegidas a Blackstone en San Sebastián por 100 millones de euros, contó Iker Rioja. A un precio medio de 200.000 euros la vivienda en una ciudad donde un piso de 80 metros cuadrados cuesta de media 417.000, parece una medida de lo más razonable. Y, mientras no acelera la construcción, esa debería ser la fórmula a aplicar por todas las administraciones: comprar vivienda usada a empresas con muchos pisos para destinarla al alquiler asequible. Justo lo que no hizo Madrid con los pisos de Blackstone que perdieron su protección cuando se los vendió Ana Botella, y que ahora viven en vilo pendientes de cuándo el nuevo propietario, Brookfield, decida venderlos.

Mientras la pobreza de inquilinos sigue apretando, al otro lado se demuestra que los caseros con varias propiedades suben más el precio que los que alquilan solo una vivienda, según un estudio de EsadeEcpol.

Un ejemplo de mala praxis entre propietarios de varios pisos de alquiler, en este caso de la empresa BCN Housing, propiedad de Azora, lo encontramos en Barcelona y lo contó Pau Rodríguez. Los inquilinos denunciaban precios por encima de los topes de alquiler que se disparaban con los precios del IBI y de comunidad. Los pisos parecían de lujo, pronto se encontró otra realidad.

From the Palme d’Or-winning abortion drama 4 Months, Three Weeks, and Two Days (2007), to the institutional corruption-themed Graduation (2016) and beyond, Romanian auteur Cristian Mungiu’s body of work has always concerned itself with the human cost of systemic oppression and bureaucratic faultlines. With the searing legal and familial drama Fjord, which earned Mungiu his second Palme at the 2026 Cannes Film Festival in May, the filmmaker once again brings his signature sense of slow-burning tension to the clash between individual and societal codes of morality. Except this time, he shrewdly flips the script. With the story of a religious immigrant family of seven facing an uphill battle against Norway’s child protection services (known as Barnevernet), Fjord dares to imagine a scenario where well-intentioned progressive values can backfire as severely as rigidly conservative ones if they disregard basic human principles like empathy, compassion, and respect.

Written by Mungiu, Fjord follows Mihai and Lisbet Gheorghiu, a Romanian engineer and Norwegian nurse played by Sebastian Stan and Renate Reinsve, respectively. Set in their modest ways, the couple tries to settle into their lives in Norway, having moved there after the death of Mihai’s parents to be closer to Lisbet’s family. Raising their five children with a conservative ethos, they don’t quite mesh with the liberal community that surrounds them. But their eager kids still build close friendships with the children of their progressive neighbors, Mats and Mia Halberg, a development that further underscores the two families’—and consequently, the two belief systems’—political and practical differences.

Tensions rise in town when the kids’ school raises child-abuse allegations against the Gheorghius after teachers notice some mysterious bruising on one of their kids. While the film never spells out their precise origins, Barnevernet steps in at once, separating their five kids (including a baby, still breastfeeding) from Mihai and Lisbet, placing them in foster care after the couple admits to some physical discipline that would be considered mild in Mihai’s culture, but is against the law in Norway. Claiming that they are being harshly discriminated against due to their religion and conservative beliefs,  the couple asks help from Mia, an attorney, to represent their case in the Norwegian court.

Stan and Reinsve as Mihai and Lisbet Gheorghiu Courtesy of Neon

Joining TIME on Zoom recently, Mungiu emphasizes that “the film is fictional. It’s not a reenactment associated with any one particular case.” Still, there were a number of real-life court cases that intrigued him as a storyteller with a journalism background. Chief among them was that of the Bodnariu family, who occupied international headlines for several months after they were separated from their five children in November 2015. Pentecostal parents Ruth and Marius were accused of domestic violence, with worldwide protests criticizing what dissenters called harsh and hasty steps taken by Norwegian social workers without proper evidence. In Romania, one of the indirect results of the wildly popular case went as far as a constitutional referendum in 2018 that aimed to alter the definition of a traditional family and prevent same-sex marriages. (The referendum eventually failed due to low turnout.)

“So you couldn’t avoid the Bodnariu case,” recalls Mungiu. He followed the story as it remained in the papers, and during that time and the years that followed, Mungiu began to notice other cases that displayed a similar pattern. “Whenever there was this encounter between, let's say, families coming to Scandinavia from more conservative parts of the world, there was a cultural clash.” What eventually convinced him to pursue the topic through a film was a recent-enough realization that the world somehow became more illiberal than it used to be. “I thought that through this story, I could talk about the [confrontational] state of the world today. People are not listening to the other group. I hope that the world can be a bit more rational than this.”

Upon deciding to write Fjord, Mungiu read everything he could online, moving to Norway for several extended periods to investigate the country’s child-protection laws and relevant cases on the ground. His process involved talking to several families involved with child protection cases and their relatives, as well as journalists who covered cases like the Bodnarius’, court interpreters, police officers, NGO representatives, and various different religious groups. “Finally, I talked to Barnevernet about these cases in general,” he adds. “They were willing to also give details about how things have changed.”

One key tool Mungiu didn’t have access to was the recording and transcription of the Bodnariu trial, which is among the reasons why Fjord had to be largely fictional. “Norwegian authorities said only the family could have access to the recording if they came over. But the family knew that they couldn’t come over. Because if they did, they could be arrested,” he explains. “The family was trying to get their justice in the European Court of Justice, but they first had to utilize all the legal procedures [in Norway]. And because they left Norway before having a final decision about their proceedings, they couldn't get a positive answer from the European Court of Justice.”

Vanessa Ceban, at the piano, plays eldest daughter Elia Courtesy of Neon

Mungiu draws a direct connection between Fjord and his previous film, R.M.N. “In that film, I was trying to investigate how a conservative society reacts when someone who’s different from them comes into their world. We know that they’re not very welcoming or willing to accept differences. They don't understand that there's a bigger world out there. In Fjord, I really wanted to reverse this paradigm and to understand what happens when the conservatives are the minority in a progressive, liberal society.” According to Mungiu, we might expect that type of society to be more empathetic and inclusive, treating minorities with more compassion. But through his investigations, he got the sense that whoever’s in the majority feels entitled to impose their own point of view onto others. “This is not a good example that we wish to set for the conservative part of the society,” Mungiu reflects. “We shouldn't be behaving the same way they behave.”

On these grounds, the central question of Fjord is whether anyone can be truly objective toward people whose values they don’t share. The Gheorghius’ conservatism, for example, means they oppose same-sex marriage. So can viewers both disagree with the couple on those grounds, while also feel that they are being treated unfairly by Barnevernet?   It is a challenge that is essentially at the heart of the American justice system, which asks potential jurors to set all their biases aside and only assess the case in hand through proven facts. “I noticed that saying, ‘I'm here to [only] judge if they are guilty of what they are charged with’ isn’t easy, because you are biased by your own point of view of the world. But I hope that this is what the audience is going to do. The reason why I wanted to tell this story from this neutral perspective is that it is a rare opportunity of inviting not just one bubble that you sympathize with in your regular life. The other bubble exists and dialogue is needed to make the society a little bit more peaceful again. So I took this position of a mediator, if you will.”

To accentuate the connection between Fjord’s searching themes and lean visuals, Mungiu purposefully embraced long shots and continuous takes beginning during the scripting phase, in order to fully immerse the audience in the conflicting world of the characters in a neutral fashion. Indicating that he avoids many of the mainstream filmmaking methods that he finds manipulative, “I don't use anything [distracting] within the scene,” Mungiu says. “I don't use coverage. I don't use music. I don't have funny angles. The camera doesn't move unless it follows a movement in the shot. I try to do this in a sober way, just with the actors and their emotions. I think it's a respectful way of telling the audience, ‘You are adults like we are, you are as smart as I am, so feel free to see [for yourself] what's more important in this shot, or who the main character is.’”

Similarly, he carefully scouted locations in sync with his ideas around isolation and lack of communication. “It was important for us to set this story in a small community, with two houses at the end of this fjord in a lovely but somehow curled world,” he explains. “It’s very majestic, and [it’s a location where] people will have to talk to one another eventually because there's nobody else around.”

He remembers an anecdote about the real inhabitants of the two houses he uses in the film for the Gheorghius and Halbergs, who were in fact not communicating with one another. That all changed shortly after the production convinced one of them to allow the film crew to shoot in his house. Mungiu recalls the homeowner saying, “I will let you, but my neighbor is very grumpy. He hasn’t talked to me in the 10 years that we have been neighbors, so you are going to have problems with him." But when the crew contacted the owner of the other house, they found that he was quite open-minded. “We asked him, ‘Do you have a conflict with your neighbor?’ He said, ‘No, but none of us have started a conversation, so I let him enjoy his privacy.’ At the end, they were communicating normally, and we had a very good relationship with both of them.”

No matter how neutral Mungiu tried to stay throughout his research process, there were some moments that made him face his own biases. “I went [to Norway] with this idea that I will meet a society that is not at all religious. This is the idea that we project onto the Protestant north.” As such, he was surprised to notice deeply rooted religious leanings there, especially among older people with a connection to the Norwegian church. He also learned about the younger population’s attraction to new neo-cult movements. “I watched at least a couple of documentaries about this phenomenon. People are really worried that the younger generations are [drawn to] these new cults. So I noticed that the people there are just as inclined to have religious beliefs as any other.”

With this discovery, Mungiu eventually eliminated various scenes in Fjord which would have insinuated that Norwegian people saw the world in a more rational way only because they weren’t religious. “Making decisions on rational grounds is of course a very good thing to do,” Mungiu explains. “But [there is also] this need of belonging to something bigger than just [rationality] that we all feel. Some people still long for the discomfort that there's something bigger than them. And as long as this belief places them on the right side of the world through [tolerance] and [inclusivity], I think we should be tolerant to it.”

His time in Norway also led Mungiu to learn more about the Sami people. “I didn’t know much about them before, but the Sami population sets a good [historical] example about how bigger communities and countries were not acting in an ethical way towards minorities. You can see this in the U.S. as well, [in the way] Native Americans were treated,” he adds. “And I think that we should learn from this [that] maybe this ‘we want to civilize you’ idea is not the most ethical one,” he reflects, clarifying that while everyone should obey the laws and respect the culture of the country they live in, real integration cannot happen by enforcing one’s own values onto others. “That won't change [the way] someone thinks. You will just frustrate them, [reinforcing] that there is a hypocrisy in the world.”

Mungiu cautions against the ongoing ideological war that he says is moving in the wrong direction across the globe. “I don't want to be preachy, but hopefully there's something that stays with you after you watch a film like Fjord,” he reflects. “I took the risk of talking about things that people need to listen to even if they’re not nice. Don't kill the messenger. It's time to start to have a dialogue and this is what cinema can do.”

En tiempos recientes marcados por la incertidumbre y la creciente competencia de las marcas chinas, el grupo Stellantis ha recibido sus mayores alegrías de su división de vehículos comerciales y, especialmente después de la pandemia, de su posición de dominio en el mercado de campers y autocaravanas. Como decimos, éste vive todo un boom desde 2020 por la promesa de libertad de movimientos que encierra tras aquellos meses de confinamiento y restricciones a la circulación de toda la población.

Si en vehículos comerciales Stellantis disfruta de una cuota de mercado que ronda el 33%, en el de campers supera el 50% (ésta en el 53% concretamente), lo que significa que uno de cada dos modelos de este tipo que encontramos en la carretera proviene del consorcio italofrancés. El Fiat Ducato es líder con 2.244 unidades entregadas este año -hasta el 1 de octubre-, y tanto Citroën como Peugeot figuran en el top 5 de un nicho que no para de crecer. El grupo anuncia planes para que Opel también escale puestos en esta clasificación, con propuestas basadas en el Zafira y el Movano.

En los últimos 20 años, ha puesto en el mercado dos millones de bases camper, y siete de cada 10 vehículos de esta clase que circulan ahora mismo por Europa están construidos sobre dichas estructuras.

El negocio de la compañía, centralizado en la división Stellantis Pro One, abarca vehículos transformados en fábrica, transformados para que los carroceros los adapten a su vez y personalizados en fábrica, todo ello en el mercado de empresas. Para el de particulares reserva su oferta de vehículos de recreo sobre los que tratamos hoy, que aúna camperizados, que son modelos estándar más o menos modificados, y las autocaravanas propiamente dichas, en los que el nombre del fabricante llega a desaparecer y quien estampa su logo es el especialista en la transformación.

En los trabajos más profundos, el carrocero acostumbra a cambiar incluso el frontal del vehículo, razón por la que en ocasiones no reconocemos qué modelo concreto -en este caso de Stellantis- se esconde tras la silueta de las autocaravanas de mayor tamaño.

En el ecosistema montado por Pro One, sus colaboradores más asiduos en trabajos de transformación son las firmas nacionales Tinkervan, Furgok, Ilusion, Nomade Nation, Bunkervan y Benimar, esta última parte del grupo francés Trigano, líder europeo indiscutible del sector.

El grupo dispone de un catálogo que comprende cuatro modalidades de carrocería, cuatro longitudes, tres alturas y cinco opciones de motor, incluidas una nueva variante de gasolina de 160 caballos y otra 100% eléctrica. Esta última, de 270 caballos, incorpora una batería de 110 kW que otorga hasta 420 kilómetros de autonomía WLTP y se encarga de surtir de energía a los consumibles que pueda embarcar el vehículo, desde la calefacción estacionaria hasta una nevera, por ejemplo. Las otras opciones de gasolina son de 120, 140 y 180 CV.

La oferta abarca modelos basados en la plataforma K9, la del Citroën Berlingo o el Peugeot Rifter, la K0 de Expert o Jumpy y la X250 de la que es máximo representante la Ducato. En el tamaño intermedio, Stellantis ofrece un modelo, el Citroën Holidays, que puede considerarse una “camper de fábrica” porque no necesita de transformación posterior. Basado en el SpaceTourer, tiene un precio de partida de 57.800 euros.

Para todos los demás modelos, que sí necesitan de trabajo ulterior por parte del carrocero -y con vistas a facilitar el trabajo de éste y reducir costes-, Stellantis suele suministrar chasis con bastidor rebajado, espalda y techo de la cabina recortados de fábrica para favorecer la instalación de la parte de vivienda del vehículo y suspensiones traseras de material compuesto, que soportan mejor el peso extra. El eje trasero también suele ensancharse a fin de que el motorhome sea lo más habitable posible.

Nos ha llamado mucho la atención una ingeniosa solución logística bautizada Back-to-Back. Hablamos de una configuración industrial ideada para optimizar el transporte logístico de fábricas a carroceros y facilitar la camperización de vehículos recreativos, y que consiste básicamente en unir temporalmente dos cabinas motrices (cabezas tractoras) de espaldas entre sí, conectadas por una estructura provisional que luego corta el carrocero.

Entre las campers más asequibles que hemos podido conocer en un acto organizado en Valencia, destacan los Peugeot Rifter y Opel Zafira by Tinkervan, disponibles a partir de 32.000 y 39.000 euros, respectivamente. Unos 15 días aproximadamente emplea en la preparación esta empresa oscense que toma su nombre de los carromatos cíngaros tradicionales -símbolo por antonomasia de la vida nómada-, y más específicamente de la raza de caballo que tiraba de ellos, el famoso Gypsy Tinker.

En el extremo más lujoso de la gama encontramos el Fiat Ducato transformado por la firma catalana Nomade Nation, a la venta desde 73.500 euros, que puede incorporar toda clase de comodidades, desde ducha, cocina y salón hasta una cama con colchón de primera calidad que sube y baja apretando un botón.

Para la cantidad creciente de usuarios de campers que se desplazan sobre todo en periodos vacacionales, Stellantis Pro One ha puesto en marcha en el mes de marzo un centro de atención al cliente exclusivo para este tipo de vehículos. Centralizado en Italia, el servicio incluye asistencia en carretera todos los días del año en Europa y dispone de un número de teléfono único y gratuito para todas las marcas del grupo Stellantis.

Amandeep Kaur, who is among those charged, has lived in the country for eight years and is in the process of becoming a naturalised U.S. citizen, said her husband, Kulwinder Singh.

Mr. Singh said she got her driver's license in 2024 but took the written test at the DMV in Hindi because she struggles with English. He doesn't know if she registered to vote then. He said she received and returned a ballot in 2024 but they both thought it was a registration form.

Now, he said his wife is distraught with the news that she may have done something illegal.

“All she does is cry. It's heartbreaking,” said Mr. Singh, who is a U.S. citizen.

One of them is José Antonio Bañuelos-Díaz, 42, who applied for citizenship in October 2025 after living in the country for 40 years and spending his entire adult life as a legal permanent resident. The federal government has alleged in charging documents that he improperly claimed to be a citizen in 2007 when registering to vote, then later lied about it on his immigration paperwork. The government hasn't accused him of casting a ballot.

Bañuelos-Díaz told The Associated Press he had no idea he had registered to vote until he was arrested on Monday (October 19, 2026) outside his Los Angeles home, though he recalls getting a driver's license around 2007. In California, people are prompted to register to vote when applying for a driver's license.

“I have never voted because I know I can't vote,” he said. “If there was a mistake, they should have called me out right away, not 20 years later.”

He was released from custody after his arrest but faces up to five years in prison if convicted.

Federal authorities on Monday (October 19, 2026) charged more than two dozen legal permanent residents in California in a crackdown on people accused of illegally voting or registering to do so. As of Thursday (October 22, 2026) morning, the government had filed charging documents in 10 of the 28 cases, offering additional details about how authorities relied on voter registration data to pursue broader criminal charges around immigration status.

Most of the immigrants charged in California have lived in the country for at least five years, and some of them for decades. They could face deportation if they lied on their citizenship application.

Immigrants convicted of illegally voting in the United States face up to a year in prison. Those convicted of lying on applications to become American citizens face up to a decade.

The Justice Department said in a statement that six people would be charged with voting as a noncitizen, a misdemeanor. The 22 others were facing a combination of felony charges including lying on their naturalisation application during interviews or on voter registration forms.

In one case, Homeland Security agents arrested Pamele Arbues Line immediately following her October 15 naturalisation interview in San Diego.

In charging documents, the federal government said Ms. Line had allegedly registered to vote five times and had submitted ballots in several elections but said in her paperwork and in the interview that she had never voted or claimed to be a U.S. citizen. She later admitted to registering to vote while getting her driver's license renewed last year and said she voted once, according to the charging documents.

Ms. Line, who is from the Philippines, told authorities she thought she was a U.S. citizen because her mother had become naturalised after they moved to the United States more than four decades ago. But Ms. Line, now 56, was 19 at the time and not covered by her mother's citizenship, according to the government. She learned she was not a citizen in June when she went to get a renewed Social Security card, and she told agents she applied for naturalisation the same day, according to the documents.

She faces at least 10 years in prison if convicted of two charges.

The cases are part of a Trump administration effort to root out noncitizen voting ahead of the midterm elections, even though data shows that the problem is incredibly rare, difficult to prove and has not been shown to change the outcome of races.

The Department of Justice has sued California for not complying with a request to verify the citizenship of roughly 20,000 registered voters it says may be noncitizens. California, which has more than 23 million registered voters, has argued the state complies with federal law and that the Trump administration's numbers are likely significantly inflated.

State election officials also noted once a ballot is mailed in, it is separated from any individual voter identification, making it impossible to separate based on the information provided by the federal government.

Beyond voting, the Trump administration has also ramped up immigration enforcement nationwide and slowed down the approval of naturalisation applications. Government data shows officials approved 58,000 naturalisation applications between April and June 2026, less than a quarter of the number approved in the same period in 2025, according to the data.

Immigrants can apply to become U.S. citizens after holding green cards for at least three years. They must answer extensive questions about activities related to voting, political activities and crime, pass background checks and a civics test and meet English language requirements.

The Department of Homeland Security did not say what initially drew attention to the immigrants charged but said in an email that the agency reviewed naturalisation and other records in its investigation. Some of the court documents in the cases describe a benefits fraud audit performed by Homeland Security in August that started the process of identifying those charged this week.

“The voting rights of American citizens must be protected,” First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli said in a statement announcing the charges this week.

Psychologist found the first corporate psychopath—a 17th century Dutch mutineer

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:48 PM EDT

The concept of a corporate psychopath is eerily recognizable, depending on your work history. It’s also notoriously misunderstood. The archetype of the manipulative, self-serving manager has regularly resurfaced in popular culture for decades, but concrete evidence supporting the wider concept is debatable. For example, a research study went viral in 2016 after estimating upwards of 1-in-5 business employees fit the “psychopath” bill, only to be retracted two years later. Additionally, psychiatrists widely agree that only about 1 percent of the general population truly can be considered clinically psychopathic.

That said, many of the people determined to claw their way to the top of the company ladder display at least some of the trademark psychopathic behaviors. Many theorists have convincingly argued this has a lot to do with corporate capitalism’s foundational principles, which leave it easily exploited by the most ruthless and manipulative among us.

Clive Boddy, one of the world’s foremost investigators of corporate psychopathy, recently took this idea to its logical historical conclusion. In a study recently published in the Asian Journal of Business Ethics, Boddy reviewed one of toxic corporate leadership’s earliest documented examples—and he makes today’s nightmare coworkers sound tame in comparison.

Meet Jeronimus Cornelisz, an all-time contender for World’s Worst Boss. According to archival accounts, the 17th century Dutchman Cornelisz embodied every core corporate psychopath characteristic over 400 years ago while working for the conglomerate that started it all: the Dutch East India Company.

Established in Amsterdam in 1602, the Dutch East India Company is arguably the world’s first multinational, shareholder-owned company. It quickly amassed enough funds and power to rival most sovereign countries through the import and export of spices, tea, luxury items, coffee, wine, and enslaved populations. The Dutch East India Company eventually exerted enough influence to wage its own wars, mint its own currency, form colonies, as well as arrest and execute convicts.

All of this was made possible through a vast fleet of ships,but even the best outfitted vessels sometimes fell afoul of nature. In June 1629, a Dutch East India Company’s flagship called Batavia collided with a reef off the Western Australian coast. Nearly 300 survivors made it to the nearby island shores, but their remote location ensured they wouldn’t survive without help. The ship’s senior officers soon set off in an open longboat on a 1,900-mile journey to present-day Jakarta, then the Dutch East Indies’ capital. Before leaving, they appointed under-merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz to the top corporate position in their absence.

Unbeknownst to Batavia’s departing senior crew, Cornelisz had already spent weeks scheming on ways to incite a mutiny. The plan was to take control of the vessel then steer its copious stores of gold and silver to a new life. Cornelisz apparently viewed getting marooned as an inconvenient detour, and quickly got to work on his hostile takeover.

According to firsthand accounts, Cornelisz immediately consolidated power by swapping fellow council members with loyalists and requiring them to pledge an oath of allegiance to him. Next, he tricked suspected opponents under the direction of a soldier named Wiebbe Hayes to search a nearby island for supplies, then left them to die.

Although his revised plan required any impending rescue ship, Cornelisz’s ultimate goal remained the same—get as far away as possible, then start his own kingdom using riches plundered from Batavia. Over the ensuing months, Cornelisz directed his gang to kill more than 100 of the shipwrecked men, women, and children. Cornelisz often justified the murders under the pretense of conserving food and water, when in actuality he was continuing to eliminate perceived threats. Meanwhile, some of the surviving women were subjected to sexual abuse.

However, Cornelisz underestimated his abandoned compatriots. As the operatically psychopathic Cornelisz terrorized his group of survivors and scrounged for supplies, Hayes’ team reportedly discovered freshwater wells and hunted local wallabies, seals, and fish. The two sides subsequently squared off on multiple occasions after learning of each others’ continued existence until a rescue ship finally arrived in October 1629. After nearly three months, only about a third of Batavia’s original passengers and crew were left.

Management ultimately decided Cornelisz wasn’t internal promotion material, even by Dutch East India Company standards. Nearly everyone involved in his reign of terror was executed. As for Cornelisz and his closest loyalists, authorities eventually hanged them, but not before cutting off their hands.

The tragedy of the Batavia is an admittedly extreme case study, but Boddy argued that it illustrates how corporate structures have facilitated and encouraged psychopathic behaviors since the invention of the corporation itself.

“The same playbook we see in toxic workplaces today appears in this case from 1629,” he said. “He removed opponents, rewarded loyalists, created fear, manipulated followers and pursued power at any cost.”

In recent years, some historians have expressed doubts about details in the Batavia saga after reviewing available evidence and recontextualizing cultural dynamics. Instead of focusing on the machinations of a single ruthless corporate psychopath, they contend Batavia is more accurately a case study in group desperation. But even when viewed under this lens, the chilling event is still a cautionary tale about corporate capitalism’s darker underpinnings.

“Perhaps the most striking finding is that another leader facing the same circumstances achieved completely different outcomes,” Boddy said. “This was effectively a natural experiment in leadership. One group prospered while the other descended into murder and abuse.”

Authorities investigating whether first Kenya and Nigeria Ebola cases crossed paths

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Kenya Red Cross personnel during a simulation of the evacuation of remains of an Ebola victim in the Kenyan capital Nairobi on October 22. Kenyan authorities detected a ninth case of Ebola in the country, the health ministry said Tuesday. Tony Karumba/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

JOHANNESBURG – The first Ebola patient confirmed in Nigeria may have crossed paths with the first Kenyan Ebola patient at a Ugandan airport, health officials said Thursday.

Nigeria announced its first case of Ebola in more than a decade on Tuesday. Nigerian health authorities said the patient is the Ugandan-born wife of a diplomat based in Abuja who had vacationed in Uganda in September and returned to Nigeria earlier this month.

She is being treated and 44 people she had close contact with have been quarantined, Dr. Wessam Mankoula, head of emergency preparedness and response at the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, told a press briefing on Thursday.

Authorities are now investigating what the source of her exposure was.

Nigeria, Africa's most populous country, is the fourth on the continent to record an Ebola case during the current outbreak, but it's the first in West Africa and the furthest away from the epicenter in the Democratic Republic of Congo.

Congo's current Ebola outbreak began in May in the country's east. It has become the fastest spreading on record. At least 4,600 people have died and about 9,700 have been infected in Congo.

Neighboring Uganda initially recorded about 20 cases but was declared Ebola-free in August.

The first case in Kenya was recorded in early October and since then eight other cases have been confirmed in the east African nation.

The first Kenyan patient, who has since died, travelled from Congo to Kenya, via Uganda.

Officials are now investigating whether that person crossed paths with the Nigeria case at the Entebbe International Airport in Uganda – as they were there on the same day.

Dr. Mankoula said the center was probing whether there had been "some sort of interaction between the two cases at the airport."

Ebola is spread through direct contact with an infected person's bodily fluids or contaminated items. It causes severe fever and can be deadly. However, generally people are not considered infectious until they start showing symptoms.
