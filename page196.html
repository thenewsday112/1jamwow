10 Desayunos Frescos y Saludables para Empezar el Día con Energía

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mucha gente lleva años escuchando que la primera comida del día es la más relevante de todas, pero la realidad es que no sirve de nada centrarse solo en el desayuno si luego descuidamos la alimentación el resto de la jornada. Para que nuestro cuerpo funcione a tope, necesitamos un equilibrio constante, aunque admitamos que arrancar la mañana con el combustible adecuado es vital para evitar esos bajones de energía o los típicos dolores de cabeza que nos arruinan la productividad.

Llevar un ritmo de vida tan frenético hace que a veces nos conformemos con un café rápido y una galleta, pero eso es un error garrafal. Si queremos evitar la somnolencia y mejorar nuestro estado de ánimo, debemos apostar por combinaciones nutritivas que nos mantengan saciados y alertas. No se trata de complicarse la vida en la cocina, sino de saber elegir ingredientes que realmente nos aporten beneficios y no solo calorías vacías.

Para que un menú matutino sea realmente completo, lo ideal es seguir una estructura sencilla: lácteo, fruta y cereal integral. Es fundamental huir de los productos industriales, como la bollería o los cereales azucarados, que están cargados de grasas trans. Ojo con los llamados falsos integrales; para que un pan sea verdaderamente sano, el grano debe aparecer como entero en la etiqueta, y no ser simplemente harina refinada con salvado añadido.

Una técnica muy útil es el método del plato, dividiéndolo en cuatro partes iguales. El primer cuarto puede dedicarse a los frutos secos (como nueces o almendras) o incluso a verduras como el tomate o la zanahoria. El segundo espacio es para la fruta de temporada, priorizando aquellas con bajo índice glucémico. El tercer cuadrante se reserva para lácteos bajos en grasa, y el último para farináceos como la avena o el pan de centeno.

No podemos olvidar la proteína, que es la que nos da la energía de calidad. El huevo es el rey indiscutible por su versatilidad, ya sea cocido, poché o revuelto con verduras. También podemos optar por embutidos magros que tengan más del 90% de carne fresca, como el pavo asado. Para quienes prefieren opciones vegetales, el hummus sobre pan integral es una excelente alternativa ya que proporciona todos los aminoácidos esenciales.

Si vas siempre con el tiempo justo, aquí tienes opciones que se preparan en un abrir y cerrar de ojos:

Lograr que los más pequeños coman sano puede ser una odisea, ya que suelen preferir galletas y leche. Para combatir esto, podemos usar estrategias visuales. Por ejemplo, convertir el pan integral en sándwiches calentitos o crear una especie de pizza saludable con tomate casero, pavo y orégano sobre una base de pan integral.

Otras ideas ganadoras son el banana bread casero, las barritas de avena con crema de cacahuete o los smoothies de fruta con yogur y avena, que son una alternativa fantástica a los zumos. Incluso podemos introducir las legumbres mediante tostadas de hummus con trozos de plátano por encima, logrando que el desayuno sea divertido y nutritivo a la vez.

El aguacate es un aliado increíble por sus grasas saludables. Podemos usarlo en láminas sobre pan de centeno con pavo, o machacarlo con AOVE y pimienta para ponerlo sobre un tomate jugoso. Mezclarlo con atún también crea una combinación potente y deliciosa.

En cuanto al huevo, aunque no siempre sea el protagonista en España, es ideal para empezar el día. Desde huevos a la plancha para emergencias de tiempo hasta tortillas con tomate, el aporte proteico es insuperable. Si buscamos algo más gourmet, el huevo poché sobre aguacate es una opción que gusta a todo el mundo.

Si tenemos un poco más de tiempo, podemos preparar tortitas integrales mezclando harina de espelta, huevos, yogur natural, nueces, manzana asada, leche vegetal, aceite de oliva y levadura. Solo hay que batirlo todo y dorarlas en la sartén.

Para los que prefieren algo dulce y frío, el pudding de chía es ideal: basta con dejar 40g de semillas de chía en remojo con leche vegetal toda la noche y añadir frutos rojos al despertar. También es muy recomendable el yogur con pera asada, cocinando la fruta con canela en el horno durante 15 minutos y sirviéndola con nueces.

Para los amantes de lo salado y elaborado, unos huevos al horno con salsa de tomatillo y acelgas suizas son un manjar. Se asan tomatillos, se sofríe cebolla, ajo y jalapeño con los tallos de la acelga, se tritura todo para hacer una salsa y se hornean los huevos creando huecos en la mezcla, terminando con aguacate y queso Cotija.

Priorizar alimentos naturales, evitar los azúcares ocultos y variar los ingredientes cada día es la clave para mantener una salud óptima. Al combinar correctamente las proteínas, las grasas saludables y la fibra de los cereales integrales, aseguramos que tanto adultos como niños tengan la energía necesaria para afrontar su rutina sin hambre y con el ánimo elevado.

Im Gefängnis brät man seine Bernerwürstel auf der Kaffeemaschine: Aber warum kochen Häftlinge überhaupt? [premium]

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Eigentlich bekommt jeder Häftling drei Mal täglich etwas zu essen. Trotzdem kochen viele auch selbst. Warum sie das tun – sogar unter erschwerten Bedingungen.

„Drei Mahlzeiten pro Tag und dennoch koche ich? Da fragt sich vermutlich manch Kochmuffel, Kantinenessenbegeisterte oder Lieferserviceexpertin, warum“, sagt Shelly W.. Die Frau heißt eigentlich anders. Sie verbringt ihr Leben derzeit hinter Gittern.

Und sie gehört zu jenen Häftlingen der Justizanstalt Josefstadt, die im neuen Buch „Taste of Prison“ erzählen, was sie in ihren Zellen kochen und warum – trotz Essensversorgung aus der Gefängnisküche und mit sehr beschränkten Möglichkeiten. Denn die meisten Insassen in der Josefstadt haben weder Kochplatte noch Kühlschrank, es gibt weder Mehl noch Ei noch Pfeffer – und scharfe Messer sind im Gefängnis sowieso tabu.

Wer eine Haft antritt, bekommt eine kulinarische Grundausstattung, die das Nötigste abdeckt: ein Buttermesser, eine Gabel, einen Löffel, einen Kaffeelöffel. Dazu kommen ein Schneidbrett, ein Teller, zwei kleine und eine mittelgroße Glasschüssel. In dieses Geschirr wird das Essen gefüllt, wenn der Speisewagen durch die Gänge rollt. Denn anders als man es etwa aus US-amerikanischen Filmen kennt, wird in heimischen Gefängnissen üblicherweise nicht in einer Kantine gegessen, sondern in der Zelle.

Ein Viertel Brot, eine kleine Margarine sowie ein kleiner Aufstrich und ungesüßter Tee oder ein „stark gewässerter“ Löskaffee sind das klassische Frühstück, wie Shelly W. schreibt. Mittags gibt es eine klare Suppe mit Einlage und oft ein Fleischgericht: Das können Cevapcici mit Reis und Gurkensalat sein, gebratenes Huhn mit Nudeln und Sauce, Spaghetti Bolognese. Freitags gibt es Fischlaibchen, manchmal Bröselnudeln oder Topfenstrudel („aus dem Großküchenbedarf“).

Das wiederholt sich im Monatstakt. Rettung vor der ultimativen kulinarischen Monotonie verspricht da einzig die sogenannte Ausspeisliste: Einmal pro Woche können Insassen darüber auf eigene Kosten (unter anderem) Lebensmittel bestellen. Was sie daraus machen, hängt von ihrem Status ab: Wer im Gefängnis arbeitet – auch wegen Jobmangels ist das in der Josefstadt nur die Minderheit der Häftlinge –, hat in seiner Zelle eine Induktionsplatte und einen Kühlschrank. Andere werden zwangsläufig kreativ.

„Oft pimpen sich Insassen das Essen aus der Anstaltsküche mit den Lebensmitteln von der Ausspeisliste nach ihrem eigenen Geschmack auf“, sagt Daniela Schwimbersky, evangelische Gefängnisseelsorgerin, die das Buch gemeinsam mit Bischöfin Cornelia Richter herausgegeben hat. „Sie schneiden sich eine Sucuk-Wurst hinein, eine Paprika oder geben ein Löfferl Rahm dazu.“ Es geht aber auch ohne die Minimal-Küchenausstattung noch viel weiter.

„Die Kaffeemaschine ist die Kochplatte der U-Haft“, sagt Schwimbersky. Das zeigt etwa das Rezept für Bernerwürstel: Frankfurter werden mit Käse gefüllt, mit Speck umwickelt und – eingewickelt in Alufolie – auf der Kaffeemaschinenplatte gebraten. Auf dieselbe Art und Weise wird Toast getoastet, ebenfalls in der Kaffeemaschine kochen manche Häftlinge ihren eigenen Reis oder Nudeln. „Ich finde das wahnsinnig faszinierend.“

Dabei sind auch die Zutaten stark limitiert: Fertige Bernerwürstel gibt es nicht, auch kein Mehl und keinen Pfeffer („Das ist wohl sicherheitsrelevant, die Menschen sind zurzeit sehr unzufrieden in der Josefstadt, auch mit Mehl kann man viel Unsinn anstellen“), als Gewürz gibt es ausschließlich Salz und Suppenwürfel; Curry, Paprika und dergleichen sucht man vergeblich. Trotzdem gelingt es den Häftlingen, aus den verfügbaren Lebensmitteln sogar philippinische oder nigerianische Gerichte nachzubauen.

Oder ein Tiramisu – im Buch „Häfn-Tiramisu“ genannt: ohne Biskotten, Ei und Mascarpone, stattdessen mit Striezelscheiben, Sauerrahm, Vanillejoghurt und Nutella. „Kochen ist meine kleine Freiheit zu entscheiden, was auf den Teller kommt“, zitiert Schwimbersky den Insassen, der sie auf die Idee gebracht hat, die Rezepte zu sammeln: zunächst für den internen Gebrauch gedacht, später dann (mit Unterstützung von Anstaltsleitung und Justizressort) fürs Buch.

Nach einem Stück Autonomie klingt das Selber-Kochen auch bei Shelly W., die das Gefängnis als einen Ort beschreibt, „an dem sogar für Sie entschieden wird, wann Sie duschen gehen oder abends die Beleuchtung Ihres Raumes ausgeschaltet wird“. Das Kochen sei außerdem eine Erinnerung an zu Hause, es fördere die Gemeinschaft und biete Insassen die Möglichkeit, „Normalität zu leben“.

Man kann es auch als einen Schritt in Richtung Resozialisierung sehen. Wenn es darum gehe, Menschen nach der Haft wieder teilhaben zu lassen, müsse man ihnen darauf Lust machen, schreibt der katholische Gefängnisseelsorger Jonathan Werner im Buch: Da könne auch Essen eine wichtige Rolle spielen. „Diese Lebensfreude“, sagt auch Schwimbersky: „Das positiv Konnotierte, das wird mit dem Kochen ganz oft transportiert.“

Taste of Prison heißt das Buch, das Daniela Schwimbersky (evangelische Theologin und Gefängnisseelsorgerin) und Cornelia Richter (evangelische Bischöfin) herausgegeben haben. Es versammelt Rezepte und Geschichten aus der Justizanstalt Josefstadt. Verlag Berger, 176 Seiten, 24,50 Euro

Billionaire Mukesh Ambani's Jio launches India's biggest share sale

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Jio Platforms IPO is expected to be the biggest in the country

India's second richest man Mukesh Ambani's digital and telecommunications venture is tapping public investors for the first time in what's expected to be the country's largest share sale.

Jio Platforms, a unit of conglomerate Reliance Industries Ltd, aims to raise 302bn rupees ($3.2bn; Â£2.8bn) in the initial public offering (IPO) of freshly issued shares from Wednesday.

The company will use the money to repay loans it took to build and expand its unit Reliance Jio Infocomm Ltd, India's largest private-sector telecoms operator.

This is the second mega IPO in a month in India after the National Stock Exchange's $2.4bn-issue despite the weakness in the broader equity market.

Arun Kejriwal of Kejriwal Research and Investment Services told the BBC that while the share market has remained subdued, the IPO market has been flush with issues and has seen interest from institutions and retail investors driven by attractive valuations and interest in new and scalable technology businesses.

He said that IPOs are being priced to reflect lower valuations and price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios among listed companies, making them attractive. The P/E ratio measures how much investors are willing to pay for each unit of a company's earnings.

Jio Platforms is offering shares in the price band of 1,065 to 1,119 rupees in the issue that closes Friday.

The share issue comes as the Nifty 50, the NSE's benchmark index of 50 leading Indian companies, has fallen by over 12% this year.

According to a Reuters report, institutional investors on Monday placed bids worth $3.2bn, which is 30 times the shares exclusively reserved for them and offered ahead of the IPO opening.

The response from institutional investors aligns with analysts' view that investors will be drawn to the IPO for Jio's scale, network infrastructure and established customer relationships.

Centrum Institutional Research, part of an Indian brokerage, said in a note that "with strong network leadership, large scale infrastructure, and a deepening digital ecosystem, Jio is well positioned to sustain industry-leading growth over the medium-to-long term".

But analysts also flag that competition is likely to remain intense for the company with price wars and potential churn in customer base.

Recently, technology billionaire Elon Musk publicly took on the low-cost Reliance Jio declaring that his Starlink's satellite internet service will be priced affordably in India.

Musk criticised Ambani on social media platform X as his satellite internet company struggles to get a final approval for a commercial launch in India.

The criticisms escalated with Musk questioning if Ambani was the "real boss of India". Ambani or Reliance did not respond directly to the attacks.

The IPO roadshows were led by Ambani's twins Isha and Akash who hold key positions in Jio Platforms

Jio also faces regulatory uncertainty even as the federal government has emerged as the largest stakeholder in rival Vodafone Idea after it converted its dues into equity as part of a relief package.

Equity experts flag that Jio Platforms remains exposed to regulatory risks as the telecom sector remains subject to extensive government oversight in India.

Retail investors have long viewed Reliance group companies as wealth creators and looked forward to IPOs from the conglomerate.

Founder Dhirubhai Ambani famously referred to shareholders as part of the "Reliance family", a tradition that has continued and created a loyal retail investor base.

The Jio Platforms IPO is the group's first public offering in two decades and signals a transition in leadership to the next generation of Ambanis.

Investor roadshows were led by Jio Platforms Managing Director Akash Ambani and Reliance Retail Ventures Executive Director Isha Ambani, the twin children of Reliance Chairman Mukesh Ambani who stayed away from the investors and shareholders this time.

“Del faro rojo al faro verde. Del faro verde, al faro rojo. ¡He abierto la madrugada, caminando de faro a faro!”, escribía Carmen Conde. Con sus versos, la poetisa nacida en Cartagena en 1907 evocaba la luz del sol poniéndose y bañando el puerto de Cartagena, una particular luz que hace brillar lo que podemos ver desde los diques de Curra (con su faro verde) hasta Navidad (con el faro rojo). Sus palabras siguen vivas en pregones de Semana Santa y viajes literarios.

Lo cierto es que, ante el estrés del día a día y el agobio que contamina el espíritu de las grandes ciudades, Conde y otros muchos antes y después, se enamoraron de su tierra y defendieron que la Región de Murcia esconde tesoros que son un bálsamo de paz para quien los ha conocido.

Solo hay que animarse a dejar a un lado la rutina y desconectar con un city break. Y la Región de Murcia se presenta como una opción ideal gracias a una propuesta turística que combina cultura y gastronomía, articulando una oferta patrimonial distribuida en cuatro ciudades principales: Murcia, Cartagena, Lorca y Caravaca de la Cruz.

Si no sabemos por dónde empezar, empecemos como Conde algunos de sus texto: con una capital. La ciudad de Murcia se descubre cómodamente a pie visitando la Catedral de Santa María, ubicada en la Plaza del Cardenal Belluga y poseedora de la segunda torre más alta de España. Muy cerca se encuentra el Real Casino de Murcia, uno de sus edificios más emblemáticos, que cuenta con un salón de baile de estilo Luis XV considerado un auténtica joya para los enamorados del arte. Tras recorrer el centro histórico, la costumbre de ir de tapeo por la Plaza de las Flores invita a probar una marinera.

En la zona litoral, la historia cobra vida en Cartagena, la ciudad de poetas como Carmen Conde, caracterizada por su pasado púnico, romano y militar. Para transportarse a la época romana, el plan destacado es visitar el Teatro Romano, cuya grandiosidad sorprende teniendo en cuenta que no fue descubierto hasta el año 1990. Y para ver con nuestros ojos aquellos mares, el Puerto de Cartagena, en cuyo paseo marítimo podemos visitar el Museo Naval y al Museo Nacional de Arqueología Subacuática (ARQUA).

Por su parte, Lorca es la Ciudad del Sol y brilla con luz propia gracias a su arquitectura barroca. Su castillo, el conocido como la Fortaleza del Sol, se ha acondicionado como espacio temático medieval que alberga aljibes tradicionales y exposiciones permanentes en sus torres, ofreciendo visitas guiadas, incluso pases teatralizados durante todo el año. En este complejo, de hecho, se conserva una sinagoga judía única en España: la única que no ha sido utilizada por otras religiones.

La ruta patrimonial concluye en Caravaca de la Cruz, meta para miles de peregrinos durante todo el año y ciudad jubilar habilitada para celebrar el Jubileo in Perpetuum. Este estatus permite obtener la indulgencia plenaria cumpliendo con los requisitos exigidos. Una alternativa idónea para llegar a la ciudad es recorrer el Camino de la Cruz que consta de 8 itinerarios distintos entre los que destaca el conocido como Camino de Levante, cuyo trazado comienza en Orihuela y combina cultura y naturaleza.

La oferta gastronómica de la Región de Murcia es mucho más de lo que creemos. Los 1.001 Sabores, la marca gastronómica de la Región de Murcia, ponen de relieve los contrastes de una zona, conocida como la Huerta de Europa, exuberante en diversidad y abundancia de productores de frutas y verduras de calidad.

La cocina murciana refleja el legado cultural de las diversas civilizaciones que la han habitado. La época romana dejó la elaboración de conservas y salazones como la hueva y la mojama, mientras que la etapa musulmana aportó el cultivo de los cítricos y el arroz. Por eso, en la mesa, uno de los platos estrella es el arroz caldero, un arroz caldoso característico de Cartagena y del Mar Menor, que destaca por su intenso sabor a mar combinado con el aroma de la huerta que le aportan las ñoras y el majado. ¿Y para la hora del aperitivo? Sin duda, la ya mencionada marinera, una tapa imprescindible de la región. El almuerzo puede concluir con un típico café asiático, elaborado con el icónico Licor 43, cediendo el toque dulce final al paparajote, elaborado con hojas de limonero y una deliciosa masa. Se trata de un postre tradicional sobre todo en la ciudad de Murcia y su huerta.

No hay mejor momento para disfrutar de lo que propone la Región de Murcia, pues ahora los visitantes pueden beneficiarse del Bono Turístico. Un programa que permite obtener hasta un 50% de descuento en el viaje, si bien requiere una estancia mínima de dos noches en la Región de Murcia. Se trata de un programa que puede solicitar cualquier persona mayor de edad con residencia legal y estable en España, y que está diseñado para disfrutar el doble y pagar la mitad. Toda la información necesaria sobre los requisitos y los trámites para solicitar la ayuda se encuentra disponible en el portal oficial.

Hamilton steel-maker delays toxic cleanup, again — this time, the reason is U.S. tariffs

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Rajpreet Sahota has been reporting on provincial environmental policy in Ontario with a focus on how it impacts racialized communities. She is based in Toronto.

From the surface, a strip of water contained by docks at the southeastern edge of the Hamilton Harbour appears much like any other in the industrial centre, once known as “Steeltown.” Cargo ships bound for the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence Seaway line the boat slip’s western edge. On the eastern side are piles of black slag — the hard, clumpy by-product of steel processing.

But beneath the water lies a legacy of pollution, one which The Narwhal has learned is a decade behind in being cleaned up, in part due to the economic fallout of U.S. tariffs on the steel industry.

The sediment in the Kenilworth Avenue boat slip is contaminated with chemicals, including polychlorinated biphenyls, or PCBs, and heavy metals that are toxic to fish and other aquatic organisms; exposure can cause reproductive problems, reduced growth and developmental effects.

The steel-maker Dofasco, which has operated on the shore since 1912 and was purchased by Luxembourg-based ArcelorMittal in 2006, owns the boat slip and has publicly pledged to contain the contaminated sediment. Decades of industrial activity contributed to the contamination: a 1999 scientific report published by Environment Canada’s Water Science and Technology Directorate identified runoff from the steel-maker’s coal piles as one source of metals and other toxins entering the water, along with industrial cooling water, stormwater and sewer overflows from various sources.

According to a 2015 project description the company submitted to the federal environmental assessment office, some concentrations of chemicals found in the area were high enough to be considered hazardous waste under federal criteria. That means the waste would have to be transferred off-site to a landfill or contained within an engineered cap.

ArcelorMittal Dofasco proposed covering one contaminated area with such a cap. A separate containment structure along the eastern edge of the boat slip would lock in additional contamination, including some dredged up and pumped in from other areas.

Work was scheduled to begin in August 2016 and, aside from long-term monitoring, finish by June 2017. But more than nine years after that deadline, the cleanup remains unfinished, with no containment structures in place. According to company records reviewed by The Narwhal, as of 2025 the anticipated cleanup cost was US$28 million. By May 2026, a company statement given to a group coordinating efforts to restore Hamilton Harbour, and shared with The Narwhal, showed progress had stalled due to the economic pressure U.S. tariffs have put on Canada’s steel industry.

ArcelorMittal Dofasco did not respond to detailed questions or multiple requests for comment by publication time.

The Narwhal reviewed annual reports, meeting notes and project updates going back to 1999. The documents lay out early efforts — and then years of delays — in removing the contaminated sediment and working toward the broader project of cleaning up Hamilton Harbour.

A former senior executive at the steel-maker, who requested their identity be kept confidential for fear of retribution, said the remediation was taken seriously while they worked there, before retiring in the past decade. “We had hired people to do the engineering work. We had put money aside in a special account, so everything was being set up,” they told The Narwhal, adding approximately $5 million had been allocated for the project at the time. (The company’s 2018 financial report notes it expected to spend US$5 million on the cleanup the following year.)

Although the original 2017 deadline passed, the former executive said the project continued advancing internally. Upon retirement, they believed it was close to moving into construction.

“It was just a matter of hiring a general contractor to do the work.”

But records show that work is far from done.

Kristin O’Connor, who coordinates efforts to remediate Hamilton Harbour at Conservation Halton, says the waterway’s transformation began as Hamilton expanded during the 1800s and 1900s.

“It was consciously decided that the Hamilton Harbour would be the dumping ground for industry and for sewage,” O’Connor said of her belief about how the contamination problem began across the harbour.

Over time, natural shorelines were filled in, wetlands disappeared and large sections of the waterfront were converted into industrial lands. The result was decades of deteriorating water quality, algae blooms, contaminated sediment and damage to fish and wildlife habitat. O’Connor points out a visiting urban planner described the harbour as the world’s largest and most beautiful septic tank in 1958.

In 1972, seeing a number of similarly degraded shorelines around the Great Lakes, Canada and the United States agreed to work together to clean up some of the most deeply polluted sites. The two countries signed the Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement in 1987, which listed 43 official areas of concern, where industrial pollution was the most deleterious. Hamilton Harbour was one of them.

The harbour ultimately had 11 of 14 possible categories of environmental impairment identified through the Great Lakes restoration framework. Among them were restrictions on eating fish and the degradation of benthos — small organisms that live in or on sediment at the bottom of waterways. Those two concerns are particularly relevant to the Kenilworth boat slip.

The contamination in the Kenilworth Avenue boat slip didn’t come from a single incident. For generations, Hamilton Harbour, extending off of Lake Ontario, was shaped by steel-making, shipping, urban development and the discharge of industrial waste and sewage.

The 1999 scientific report examined contaminant levels in 1996 and found run-off from Dofasco’s coal piles was heavily contaminated with metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, or PAHs. The group of chemicals is associated with sources including petroleum products, steel-making and coking operations — the process of heating coal at high temperatures to produce fuel for steel-making. Some are considered to be cancer-causing and toxic for aquatic life. After the study came out, Dofasco contracted work to capture and treat run-off from its coal piles.

Also found within the sediment, PCBs, once widely used in industrial equipment, can remain in the environment for long periods and accumulate in animal tissue. Environment and Climate Change Canada identifies the human-made chemicals as potential human carcinogens that are toxic to fish at low levels. The United Nations Environment Programme has reported they accumulate in the tissues of plants and animals, becoming more concentrated and harmful as they move up the food chain.

Dofasco acknowledged the sediment problem in its 2002 annual report, saying, “The company’s boat slip contains contaminated sediment from a time that pre-dates environmental awareness and control technologies.”

At the time, the company said it was working with the federal and provincial environment departments and other stakeholders to examine options for addressing the contamination. Neither Environment and Climate Change Canada nor Ontario’s Ministry of the Environment, Conservation and Parks responded to requests for comment by publication time.

ArcelorMittal Dofasco’s 2015 project description acknowledged the pollution could negatively affect local aquatic organisms and noted the cleanup was intended to prevent them and other wildlife from coming into contact with the most heavily contaminated material.

The Canada Water Agency, the federal agency responsible for freshwater governance, said in a statement to The Narwhal that cleaning up the Kenilworth boat slip, along with a nearby contaminated site known as the Strathearne boat slip, remains necessary to reduce contamination from PCBs, PAHs and metals.

After Hamilton Harbour was designated an area of concern, restoration efforts kicked off, starting with the development of a remedial action plan to address decades of environmental damage.

The Hamilton Harbour remedial action plan brings together governments, industry, conservation authorities, researchers and community organizations to restore the harbour. Dofasco and Stelco, Hamilton’s two major steel producers, have been involved in the process since its early years. O’Connor, the remedial action plan coordinator with Halton Conservation, says the Kenilworth boat slip was one of many environmental problems contributing to the harbour’s designation as an area of concern.

Over the decades, the partnership has worked on projects ranging from wastewater treatment improvements to shoreline restoration and fish habitat rehabilitation. One of its largest undertakings — and one that looks to be a model for success — is the cleanup of Randle Reef.

Located near the property of Stelco, Randle Reef was the largest contaminated sediment site on the Canadian side of the Great Lakes. A $138.9-million cleanup began in 2015, funded by the federal and provincial governments, municipalities, the port authority and industry. While Stelco was not deemed solely responsible for the pollution at Randle Reef, it supplied $14 million in steel to build a box to contain it. The sediment there was contaminated with the same toxins found at Kenilworth, and had to be dredged and moved into the steel container. The final phase, which involves installing a cap over the box, is scheduled for completion in 2027.

Kenilworth, however, has followed a different path.

ArcelorMittal Dofasco’s 2015 remediation plan outlines how it would contain the most contaminated sediment in the two proposed structures: one approximately 66 metres wide and 120 metres long and the other roughly 264 metres long and 44 metres wide. Less than 40,000 cubic metres of contaminated sediment was expected to be dredged from other areas of the boat slip and relocated into the containment structures.

Sediment containing naphthalene — the most volatile member of the PAH family — would be tested and, where required, transported to a licensed landfill for hazardous waste disposal. Other areas would be covered with sand to isolate contamination remaining after dredging. A temporary water treatment system would manage wastewater generated during the work, while long-term monitoring would assess the integrity of the containment structures. The approach was designed to minimize disruption to shipping operations while reducing exposure to contaminated sediment.

Unlike the multi-partner Randle Reef project, ArcelorMittal Dofasco was identified as the “sole proponent” for its boat slip remediation. O’Connor says the remedial action plan identifies the environmental work that needs to be completed, but individual partners are responsible for carrying out their own projects. “We don’t fund it. We don’t monitor it,” she said.

In its 2023 annual report, ArcelorMittal Dofasco listed a US$31-million environmental provision for the expected cost of remediating toxic sediment at the Kenilworth boat slip. The company’s 2025 annual report included a US$28-million provision for the project. These provisions are accounting estimates of expected environmental costs, not proof that money has been spent on remediation or that those amounts are held in a dedicated account.

In 2020, the International Joint Commission, a Canada-U.S. organization that investigates and mediates transboundary issues involving fresh water, reported that ArcelorMittal Dofasco was expected to clean up contaminated sediment near the Kenilworth boat slip over two years beginning in 2021.

A Hamilton Harbour remedial action plan fact sheet originally published in 2018 and updated in 2023 continued to describe ArcelorMittal Dofasco as obtaining provincial approval to manage PCB, PAH and metal-contaminated sediment in the boat slip. Meanwhile, meeting records from the Bay Area Implementation Team, the group of partners responsible for implementing the remedial action plan, show the project remained under development.

At a June 13, 2023, meeting, ArcelorMittal Dofasco reported conducting additional sediment sampling and working with an engineering consultant to review the results. The company was preparing an updated environmental summary report as part of the permitting process and reviewing the engineering design.

A year later, the project still had not moved into construction. Meeting notes from June 20, 2024, state a tender was being prepared for proponents to bid on the remediation work. The notes state that technical options and logistics were being reviewed as well as further consultations with the Indigenous community.

By November 2025, the project timeline was still being reviewed, according to notes, but ArcelorMittal Dofasco remained committed to the cleanup.

The most recent update provided to the Bay Area Implementation Team came from ArcelorMittal Dofasco on May 29, 2026: “The current market context and threat to the Canadian steel industry as a result of the U.S. tariffs creates a very difficult environment for the company and the industry. So much so that every investment must be carefully reviewed and managed in order to continue to be a sustainable business. Given this, there is no further update or progress since the last meeting.”

The statement comes amid substantial uncertainty facing Canadian steel producers.

U.S. President Donald Trump imposed 25 per cent tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminum in March 2025, before doubling them to 50 per cent that June and escalating further with additional trade pressures through 2026. In steel-making communities, including Hamilton, workers have faced job losses as a result. That includes at Stelco, which has laid off hundreds of workers in the past weeks.

But that doesn’t account for the past decade of delays on remediating the Kenilworth boat slip.

Chris McLaughlin, executive director of the Bay Area Restoration Council, said the precise circumstances behind the years of delays remain unclear. He called for greater leadership from the federal and provincial government in addressing the project’s technical and economic obstacles, and said the public deserves clearer commitments and timelines from the company for completing the work.

“There is a moral imperative to reconcile with our destructive industrial past,” he said. “Effective cleanups and restorative actions with public engagement and community connections create meaningful improvements and sustain hope.”

UNEP: Opportunity has ‘been lost’ to meet 1.5C warming limit without ‘overshoot’

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A temporary breach of the 1.5C global-warming limit is now “inevitable” following “decades of insufficient climate action”, says the UN Environment Programme (UNEP).

In its “sobering” annual assessment of the global “emissions gap”, UNEP says that exceeding the Paris Agreement’s 1.5C limit is “imminent and unavoidable”.

This year’s report, titled “The point of return”, says the “task now” is to minimise the size and scale of a temperature “overshoot” and to “establish a credible return path to 1.5C by the end of the century”.

Even the “lowest possible” overshoot scenario for 1.5C would first see global warming peak at around 1.7-1.8C in the middle of the century before declining, says the report.

This scenario – which is “still aligned with the long-term temperature goal of the Paris Agreement” – would require global emissions cuts of 18% by 2030 and 45% by 2035, while ramping up “removals” of carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere, says UNEP.

In contrast, it notes that the current climate pledges by countries around the world – even if fully implemented – would only reduce emissions by 5-8% by 2030 and 11-15 % by 2035, relative to 2025 levels.

Nonetheless, the report identifies “ample opportunity to accelerate action”, concluding that “countries can still limit peak warming, shorten the duration of overshoot and keep the goals of the Paris Agreement within reach”.

(See Carbon Brief’s detailed coverage of previous reports in 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025.)

The report says that emissions in 2025 were “comparable” to those of 2024.

Global greenhouse gas emissions in 2025 totalled 58.4bn tonnes of CO2 equivalent (GtCO2e), according to the report. This is around 0.16GtCO2e lower than 2024 levels – albeit, with high levels of uncertainty around the numbers.

In addition, because the figures presented in the 2025 report are “provisional”, the report says, it is “not possible to conclude on the overall direction of emissions in 2025”.

The emissions growth rate has been slowing down in recent decades: the growth rate over 1990-2015 was, on average, 1.7% per year, while emissions have increased by an average of 0.8% per year since 2016.

The chart below shows the total human-driven emissions between 1990 and 2025, separated by greenhouse gas: CO2 from fossil-fuel combustion (dark grey), methane (light grey), nitrous oxide (blue), fluorinated gases (orange) and CO2 from land-use change (green).

Compared to 2024, emissions from fossil fuels increased slightly, by between 0.4-1.1%, says the report. Emissions of both nitrous oxide and fluorinated gases increased by even higher rates, but since these comprise such a small part of total greenhouse gas emissions, “their contribution to the overall trend is relatively less significant”, it adds. Methane emissions remained steady over that time.

A reduction in the CO2 emissions from land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF) “slightly overcompensates” for the rise in emissions of fossil-fuel CO2 and other gases, according to the report. (For more on falling land-use emissions, see Carbon Brief’s explainer.)

The decrease in LULUCF emissions was “partially driven” by a decline in global wildfire emissions, particularly in Africa, South America and parts of south-east Asia. The report notes that these decreases were “likely connected to the ceasing of El Niño conditions”. The ongoing “super” El Niño is likely to cause LULUCF emissions to increase in 2026.

The report also examines countries’ contributions to the increase in greenhouse gas emissions – although here, it excludes LULUCF due to a lag in national-level emissions inventories for the sector.

Among the top six emitters, only the US saw a notable increase in 2025, says the report, whereas emissions in China, the EU, India, Russia and Indonesia were relatively flat. Emissions in the US grew by 133m tonnes of CO2e (MtCO2e) in 2025, due to increased electricity demand and a “rebound” of coal power. This growth in US emissions is likely due to a combination of market factors, including increased heating demand due to a colder winter, higher gas prices and overall increased electricity demand.

Across most sectors, progress towards decarbonisation has been “modest”, says the report. The most notable decreases have occurred in the power sector and the transport sector, due to the growth of solar power and electric vehicles. The report notes:

“There are emerging signs of transport sector electrification and decarbonisation, but at rates and levels that are not yet sufficient to drive emissions reductions at the global level.”

However, emissions from the transport sector have continued to grow, due to increased demand for international aviation and shipping.

The report notes that while electricity demand from data centres is “projected to double” by 2030, data centres at present account for less than 1% of total emissions.

All countries were meant to submit new UN climate pledges for 2035, known as the third nationally determined contributions (NDCs) to the Paris Agreement, by February 2025. However, 95% of nations missed this deadline.

Many countries came forward with their third NDCs at a UN climate summit in New York in September 2025 and at the COP30 climate summit in Brazil in November 2025.

By the end of COP30, 122 – or 63% – of countries had produced their third NDCs.

The report says that figure had risen to 124 by 1 September 2026, with pledges now covering 75% of global emissions.

This is more than double the number of third NDCs available at the time of the 2025 emissions gap report – allowing a “more robust basis for assessing collective progress and ambition toward 2035”, says the report.

Although the first global stocktake of action towards the goals of the Paris Agreement called for countries to submit targets “covering all greenhouse gases”, there has only been a “modest” increase in the number of nations to do so, according to the report.

It says that eight countries included greenhouse gas targets for the first time in their third NDCs, bringing the total number parties with such targets up to 155.

The third NDCs “increasingly use more robust and verifiable target formulations”, such as standardised baselines, says the report.

However, they have not “meaningfully broadened the scope of sectors and gases covered by the targets”, it adds.

If the third NDCs were implemented in full, it would lower global emissions in 2035 by 3.5GtCO2e, compared to 2030 targets, says the report.

It says this is “far from the global reductions needed to get on track to achieving the long-term temperature goal of the Paris Agreement”.

Compared to the 2025 report, the projections for global emissions in 2035 as a result the third NDCs are about 3GtCO2e higher.

This is down to the formal withdrawal of the US from the Paris Agreement, as well as from “harmonising” the projections with the latest historical emissions data, says the report.

The global “implementation gap” for 2030 – defined as the difference between countries’ projected emissions under current policies and the emissions expected if they implemented their NDCs in full – has narrowed compared to last year, says the report.

However, this is mainly due to the withdrawal of the US from the Paris Agreement. In other words, the narrowing of the gap is due to “policy changes, not policy progress”.

The implementation gap for both 2030 and 2035 are of a similar size – suggesting “enhanced collective ambition of 2035 NDCs is weak”, continues the report.

Some 14 of the 17 G20 nations, counting the EU as one, have submitted new NDCs with emissions targets for 2035, says the report.

However, the emissions trajectories of the majority of G20 nations remain “misaligned” with their net-zero targets, says the report.

A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea and unleashed a 330-foot tsunami

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea roughly 43 to 46 million years ago, triggering a tsunami more than 100 meters (330 feet) high. Now, after more than two decades of scientific disagreement, researchers have uncovered compelling evidence confirming the ancient collision.

The impact created what is now known as the Silverpit Crater, a massive structure buried deep beneath the seafloor. Although scientists first discovered it in 2002, its origins remained fiercely debated, with some researchers arguing that geological processes, rather than an object from space, were responsible.

A research team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson of Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh has now provided the strongest evidence yet that Silverpit was formed by an asteroid or comet. By combining advanced seismic imaging, microscopic examination of rock samples, and computer simulations, the scientists have reconstructed the violent event and identified minerals that could only have formed under extraordinary pressure.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, establish Silverpit as one of the relatively few confirmed impact craters on Earth.

A Mysterious Crater Beneath the North Sea

The Silverpit Crater lies approximately 700 meters beneath the seabed in the southern North Sea, about 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England. The crater itself measures roughly three kilometers across and is surrounded by a much larger, 20-kilometer-wide region of circular faults, which are fractures in the rock where movement has occurred.

When scientists first identified the formation in 2002, several features suggested that it had been created by a high-speed collision with an asteroid or comet.

Its nearly circular outline, elevated central peak, and surrounding rings of faults resembled structures found at other impact sites. Central peaks can develop when rocks compressed by a powerful collision rebound upward, somewhat like the way water rises in the center after an object splashes into a pool.

Some geologists proposed that the unusual formation resulted from the movement of underground salt deposits, which can deform the rock layers above them. Others suggested that volcanic activity had caused part of the seabed to collapse, producing a structure that only appeared to be an impact crater.

The disagreement persisted for years. In 2009, geologists even voted on the competing explanations, as documented in Geoscientist magazine. Most participants rejected the asteroid impact hypothesis.

The latest findings show that the impact explanation was correct.

Scientists Find the Smoking Gun for an Ancient Asteroid Impact

The breakthrough came from combining newly available seismic images with microscopic evidence recovered from beneath the seabed.

Seismic imaging allows geologists to investigate structures hidden underground by analyzing how sound waves travel through different layers of rock. This technique can reveal buried faults, craters, and other formations that cannot be observed directly.

Using the improved data, Nicholson and his colleagues were able to examine Silverpit's internal structure in unprecedented detail.

But the most decisive evidence came from tiny mineral crystals discovered in rock cuttings collected from an oil well near the crater.

Dr. Uisdean Nicholson, a sedimentologist in Heriot-Watt University's School of Energy, Geoscience, Infrastructure and Society, said: "New seismic imaging has given us an unprecedented look at the crater.

"Samples from an oil well in the area also revealed rare 'shocked' quartz and feldspar crystals at the same depth as the crater floor.

"We were exceptionally lucky to find these -- a real 'needle-in-a-haystack' effort. These prove the impact crater hypothesis beyond doubt, because they have a fabric that can only be created by extreme shock pressures."

Shocked minerals are particularly important because their internal crystal structures have been altered by immense pressure. Such changes can occur when an asteroid strikes at tremendous speed, generating powerful shock waves that pass through the surrounding rock.

Ordinary geological processes generally cannot produce these distinctive microscopic features. Their discovery at the same depth as the crater floor provides strong physical evidence that an extremely violent impact occurred.

A 160-Meter Asteroid Unleashed a Monster Tsunami

With the crater's origins established, researchers used numerical models, or computer simulations based on physical laws, to reconstruct what happened during the collision.

Their results suggest that an asteroid approximately 160 meters across approached from the west and struck the seabed at a shallow angle.

Although much smaller than the asteroid associated with the extinction of the dinosaurs, an object of this size could still release an enormous amount of energy when traveling at cosmic speeds.

According to the simulations, the impact violently displaced vast quantities of rock and seawater, producing a towering curtain of debris before the material collapsed back into the ocean.

Dr. Nicholson said: "Our evidence shows that a 160-meter-wide asteroid hit the seabed at a low angle from the west.

"Within minutes, it created a 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water that then collapsed into the sea, creating a tsunami over 100 meters high."

That means the collision sent material nearly a mile into the air before it fell back into the sea, generating a tsunami taller than a 30-story building.

Unlike ordinary wind-driven waves, tsunamis involve the movement of enormous volumes of water. They are typically generated when a sudden disturbance, such as an earthquake, underwater landslide, or asteroid impact, rapidly displaces the water above it.

The estimated height of Silverpit's tsunami comes from computer modeling of the ancient impact rather than direct observation. Nevertheless, the reconstruction illustrates just how destructive even a relatively modest asteroid can be when it strikes the ocean.

A Scientific Debate Finally Settled After Decades

For Professor Gareth Collins of Imperial College London, the discovery brings a satisfying conclusion to a controversy he witnessed firsthand.

Collins participated in the 2009 debate over Silverpit's origins and contributed the numerical simulations used in the new research.

Professor Collins said: "I always thought that the impact hypothesis was the simplest explanation and most consistent with the observations.

"It is very rewarding to have finally found the silver bullet. We can now get on with the exciting job of using the amazing new data to learn more about how impacts shape planets below the surface, which is really hard to do on other planets. "

The findings do more than settle an old argument. They also give scientists a valuable opportunity to study the underground damage caused by high-speed asteroid impacts.

Because much of Silverpit's structure remains buried and well preserved, researchers can investigate how the collision fractured, displaced, and reshaped the surrounding geological layers.

These observations could help scientists interpret impact structures on other worlds, where the subsurface effects of collisions are often much more difficult to examine.

Earth has experienced countless asteroid and comet impacts throughout its history, but relatively few recognizable craters remain.

Weathering, erosion, sediment deposition, and plate tectonics continually reshape the planet's surface. Over millions of years, these processes can wear down, bury, or completely destroy evidence of ancient collisions.

As a result, exceptionally preserved impact sites like Silverpit are scientifically valuable.

Dr. Nicholson said, "Silverpit is a rare and exceptionally preserved hypervelocity impact crater.

"These are rare because the Earth is such a dynamic planet -- plate tectonics and erosion destroy almost all traces of most of these events.

"Around 200 confirmed impact craters exist on land, and only about 33 have been identified beneath the ocean.

"We can use these findings to understand how asteroid impacts shaped our planet throughout history, as well as predict what could happen should we have an asteroid collision in future."

Hypervelocity impacts occur when objects strike at extremely high speeds, typically several kilometers per second. At those velocities, even an asteroid measuring only a few hundred meters across can deliver a devastating amount of energy.

With its origins now confirmed, Silverpit joins a small group of important underwater and buried impact structures, including the Chicxulub Crater in Mexico, associated with the mass extinction that wiped out the nonavian dinosaurs, and the Nadir Crater off the coast of West Africa, another recently confirmed asteroid impact site.

Together, these craters provide evidence of the powerful role cosmic collisions have played in shaping Earth's geological and biological history.

By studying Silverpit's remarkably preserved features, scientists hope to better understand what happens when an asteroid strikes the ocean, how the surrounding rock responds, and what such an event might mean for Earth in the future.

A newly identified freshwater cave snail in China is offering scientists a rare glimpse into the little-known aquatic ecosystems hidden beneath East Asia. Published in the journal Subterranean Biology, the discovery represents an important step forward in understanding the region's underground wildlife. Until now, no freshwater cave snails had been documented in China, and the country's subterranean freshwater mollusks had received relatively little scientific attention.

The newly discovered species, Erhaia dijiang, has evolved remarkable features that allow it to live in complete darkness. As a true cave-dwelling animal, it has entirely lost its eyes, with the structures normally associated with vision having fully degenerated. The tiny snail also has a cone-shaped, nearly transparent shell, a delicate body without pigmentation, and long, slender tentacles.

Its unusual appearance inspired a name drawn from ancient Chinese mythology. Researchers chose dijiang in honor of "Di Jiang," a mountain patron god portrayed as a faceless beast in the ancient text Classic of Mountains and Seas (山海经). The connection is particularly fitting because the snail is extremely sensitive to light. During laboratory observations, individuals consistently retreated into dark spaces beneath rocks whenever they were exposed to illumination.

The location of the discovery is just as remarkable as the animal itself. Scientists found Erhaia dijiang at an elevation of 2,062 meters, making it the highest-altitude cave snail yet recorded within the family Erhaiidae. The finding demonstrates that freshwater cave snails can inhabit limestone cave systems at elevations above 2,000 meters.

Life in the Dark Waters of a Mountain Cave

Within its underground habitat, Erhaia dijiang lives among submerged gravel and broken rock in water less than one meter deep. The cave's water temperature stays close to 20°C, providing a relatively stable environment for the tiny mollusks.

Even when heavy seasonal rains increase the flow of water and occasionally wash a few snails out through the cave opening, they have not been observed in nearby streams. Researchers found that the animals remain confined to the cave environment, staying within two meters of its entrance.

Laboratory observations also provided clues about what these elusive creatures eat. The snails were seen feeding on diatoms, microscopic algae that grow on rock surfaces, and they also consumed fish food supplied by the researchers.

Only Three Living Snails Found, Raising Conservation Concerns

Although the discovery sheds new light on China's largely unexplored underground aquatic biodiversity, researchers are concerned about the species' future. They describe its conservation outlook as "far from optimistic."

During the first field investigation in August 2025, the team encountered an exceptionally small number of snails. Despite searching the shallow cave waters, researchers located only three living adults and one empty shell. They found no juveniles, suggesting that the known population is extremely sparse.

The scientists are now urging more comprehensive investigations of cave ecosystems throughout China to uncover additional species and better understand these fragile underground habitats. They also emphasize the need to develop conservation protections quickly for these vulnerable mollusks, which are native to highly restricted environments and could face serious threats before their populations are fully understood.

Scientists find 10 different diets linked to slower aging

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A large study involving approximately 7,500 people suggests that eating a healthy diet may help slow biological aging, and there is no single eating plan that stands above all others. Scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) investigated 10 different dietary patterns, including the Mediterranean, Nordic, plant-based, and DASH diets. The DASH diet was specifically developed to help reduce blood pressure.

Every dietary pattern examined was associated with slower biological aging, as measured by chemical changes in DNA that reflect the body's aging process. The findings were first identified using data from DZNE's Rhineland Study and then independently confirmed in another population. The research was published in Nature Communications.

"Our study suggests that healthy eating goes hand in hand with slower biological aging. The effects are not massive, but they are measurable and relevant for prevention. By slowing the aging process, the risk of age-related diseases such as dementia or cardiovascular disorders can be reduced. Thus, healthy eating contributes to healthy aging," says Prof. Monique Breteler, Director of Population Health Sciences at DZNE and head of the Rhineland Study. "Importantly, there is probably no single 'correct' diet. According to our data, various dietary patterns are associated with slower aging, some more, some less. That is an encouraging finding, because it leaves room to tailor healthy eating to personal and cultural preferences, budget and taste."

To investigate the connection between diet and aging, the scientists analyzed dietary information and blood samples from roughly 7,500 men and women. The initial analysis included 6,470 participants in DZNE's Rhineland Study in Bonn, Germany. The researchers then tested their findings using data from another 1,034 people enrolled in the EPIC-Potsdam Study, conducted by the German Institute of Human Nutrition Potsdam-Rehbrücke (DIfE).

Rather than relying only on participants' chronological ages, the team looked for molecular signs of aging in their blood. Specifically, they measured DNA methylation, a process involving chemical modifications to DNA that help regulate gene activity and influence how cells function.

These chemical patterns change in relatively predictable ways as people grow older, allowing scientists to estimate biological age. Unlike chronological age, which simply measures how long someone has been alive, biological age offers clues about how the body is aging at the molecular level.

"We applied three different approaches, so-called epigenetic clocks, to read biological aging from DNA methylation," explains Juliana Tavares, a doctoral researcher at DZNE and lead author of the current publication. "To this end, we leveraged state-of-the-art technology, which allowed us to cover about 850,000 sites in the DNA. This is roughly twice as many as in most previous studies."

The next step was to determine how closely each participant followed the 10 dietary patterns. Researchers compared reported food consumption with the recommendations for each diet and assigned scores reflecting how well individuals followed those guidelines.

Because the diets emphasize different foods and nutritional goals, a person could receive a high score for one eating pattern while scoring poorly on another. In fact, participants who met one definition of healthy eating often did not meet the requirements of other diets.

"One and the same person could therefore rank high on one diet score and low on another. In other words: People who ate healthily by one standard were often not the same people who ate healthily by another. According to our data, hardly anyone counts as a healthy eater by every standard at once," says Tavares.

Despite those differences, the overall pattern was remarkably consistent. Better adherence to every one of the 10 diets was associated with slower biological aging. Some eating patterns showed stronger associations than others, but the absolute differences were relatively small.

"While there are measurable differences between the diets, they are modest in absolute terms. It's the big picture what matters," says Tavares. "In summary: When it comes to slowing down the aging process, there isn't just one healthy diet, but indeed a whole repertoire of possible diets with a positive effect."

One unexpected result emerged when researchers examined the association between diet and aging among smokers. The link between healthier eating and slower biological aging was stronger in smokers than in nonsmokers, even though smokers did not generally have healthier diets.

The researchers have not yet determined what biological processes might explain this difference. One possibility is that the extensive damage caused by smoking leaves greater room for measurable improvements associated with healthier eating. However, the finding does not lessen the well-established dangers of tobacco use.

"Smoking is very harmful to health," says Tavares, "One explanation for our findings might be that there is just more health benefit to gain for smokers by eating healthy because of the enormous detrimental effects of smoking."

Different Diets Share Common Aging Pathways

The scientists also investigated which biological processes might connect the different diets to slower aging. Although each eating pattern was associated with changes at a distinct collection of DNA methylation sites, the underlying biological pathways were surprisingly similar.

More than 70% of the affected pathways were shared across all 10 diets. These processes help regulate essential functions such as cellular structure, communication between cells, and metabolism. They also play important roles in aging and the development of chronic diseases.

The researchers identified some differences that reflected the specific goals of individual diets. For example, the DASH diet was associated with pathways related to heart health, while the MIND diet, which was developed to support brain health, showed distinct associations with pathways involved in memory and cognitive function.

"More than 70 percent of the affected biological pathways were shared across all diets. These involve cell structure, cell signaling and metabolism -- processes central to aging and chronic disease. Therefore, it is understandable that the dietary patterns we examined were all linked with a slowing of biological aging," says Tavares. "A few pathways were diet-specific, and they matched each diet's main objectives: heart-related pathways were unique to the DASH diet, and pathways related to cognition and memory were unique to the so-called MIND diet, which aims to support brain health. You don't need a perfect diet -- that may be the most practical message here. But moving forward, the diet-specific pathways point to future research on targeted dietary interventions. This could be helpful in designing targeted health recommendations."
