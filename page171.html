Why soaring prices aren't breaking America's diesel habit

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The quickest and most affordable response to historically high diesel prices is also the simplest: Use less of it.

Why it matters: Diesel is so entrenched in farming, trucking and other industries that even soaring prices aren't enough — at least not yet — to prompt a wholesale shift to alternatives from electric semi trucks to manure-powered tractors.

Catch up fast: Diesel prices have climbed to record highs since the U.S. war with Iran began in February, rising faster than gasoline amid global oil supply disruptions.

  • Diesel averaged $6.31 a gallon Thursday, up 74% from a year ago, according to AAA. Regular gasoline averaged $4.27, up 39%.

The big picture: Gasoline gets the attention, but diesel is the workhorse of the American economy. It powers grocery trucks, tractors, freight trains, construction equipment, hospital backup generators and even school buses.

Driving the news: The price spike is hitting farmers during the fall harvest.

  • "Farmers buy diesel by the truckload, so we're talking about hundreds of thousands of dollars in diesel, and when it goes up the way it has spiked, that's the difference between profit and loss in a year," said Curt Blades, senior vice president at the Association of Equipment Manufacturers, a trade group representing tractor makers and other heavy-equipment manufacturers.
  • The financial pain varies: Trucking companies can often pass higher fuel costs to customers through surcharges. Farmers, who have little control over crop prices, generally can't.

The economic pressure has become political ahead of the midterm elections.

  • President Trump announced $8 billion in one-time aid for farmers at a campaign rally in Kansas on Monday, where Republicans face a competitive Senate race.
  • "It's a bit too soon to tell the impact," Blades said of the aid package, though he welcomed support during harvest.

State of play: Trucking and agricultural equipment experts say the most immediate savings come from using existing diesel engines more efficiently.

  • Truckers can slow down and reduce idling.
  • Farmers are increasingly using GPS-guided tractors to avoid covering the same ground twice, a practice known as precision agriculture.
  • Biodiesel and renewable diesel can reduce petroleum dependence without replacing existing engines, but government blending requirements — rather than high diesel prices alone — are driving growth, according to Clean Fuels Alliance America, an industry trade group.

Zoom in: Batteries that power truck drivers' sleeping quarters without idling the engine are gaining traction.

  • Nevada-based Dragonfly Energy sells systems that recharge through the truck's diesel-powered engine while driving, then power air conditioning and other appliances while parked.
  • The company projected a 160% increase in third-quarter trucking revenue, a bright spot amid otherwise sluggish financial performance.
  • Texas-based Stevens Transport says 400 of its trucks use Dragonfly's batteries, cutting diesel consumption by about 4% compared with trucks using smaller diesel engines to power their sleeping quarters.

The approach illustrates a fundamental tension in energy transitions: Does technology incrementally change an industry or revolutionize it?

  • "We haven't even mastered leaving the truck off when it's not moving, let alone moving it with electricity," said Wade Seaburg, Dragonfly's chief commercial officer. "Let's master the little things first, and that's been a well-received message."
  • "Think evolutionary rather than revolutionary," said Mike Roeth, executive director of the North American Council for Freight Efficiency, a nonprofit researching fuel-saving trucking technologies.

Zoom out: The same logic applies across diesel-dependent industries. Expensive equipment lasts for years, and switching fuels often requires new infrastructure and vehicles.

  • "The fact diesel fuel prices are higher matters in the calculations, but it doesn't make the challenges of these alternatives any easier," Roeth said.

Yes, but: More dramatic changes are still percolating, particularly in electric trucking.

  • Forum Mobility, which builds shared charging depots and leases electric trucks, says electric trucks have a roughly 40% fueling cost advantage over diesel in California, where diesel prices have topped $8 a gallon.
  • The company has commitments from 25 carriers for 330 Tesla Semis and is building four additional charging depots in California.

Meanwhile, on the farm, equipment maker New Holland has spent more than a decade bringing methane-powered tractors to market, including ones that can run on fuel made from livestock manure, after abandoning an expensive hydrogen tractor project.

  • "The increase in diesel prices has generated a little more interest," said Sean Dorosz, a product marketing manager at New Holland. But "I don't think it's going to be the one thing that makes them write a check to buy a methane tractor."
  • Access to manure-derived fuel and state incentives also shape the economics.

The bottom line: High diesel prices are strengthening the case for alternatives. Making them practical is a much slower process.

8 Things to Know About the iPhone Duo

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The iPhone Duo is finally here. Apple's first folding smartphone arrives seven years after Samsung pioneered the category with the Galaxy Fold. That gave Apple plenty of time to refine the hardware behind closed doors rather than in public view—and the results show: The Duo is a pretty dang good foldable.

Whether your Duo just landed on your doorstep or you're still weighing that $1,999 price, here's what you need to know before unfolding it.

The iPhone Duo is a new shape and size for Apple, so many of your apps likely aren't optimized to show up properly on the unique 5.4-inch outer screen or the 7.6-inch inner display. Apple also changed how navigational elements appear on the Duo, aligning them on the right rail instead of centering them on the bottom like every other iPhone.

Apple apps, redesigned for the Duo, look great. Many third-party apps look like the Google Home app in the picture above: a strip down the middle with thick, black bars on the sides. Several major app developers have already optimized their apps for iOS 27.1—Slack, Instagram, DoorDash, and WhatsApp, for example—but I've noticed that even some optimized apps like Instagram can still have broken user-interface elements. It will take time for every app in your library to work perfectly across these new screens.

It might be a good thing that the Duo is back-ordered for several weeks. By the time you snag yours, app developers will likely have caught up to the new device, and you might enjoy a richer folding experience.

Nearly a decade ago, Apple introduced Face ID as the future of the iPhone, eventually replacing the fingerprint-based Touch ID. So it might be confusing to hear that Apple's first folding phone doesn't have Face ID at all and instead relies on the older biometric authentication method. (Likely because Face ID tech takes up too much space in the thin iPhone Duo.) Touch ID never disappeared—Apple has used it on the iPhone SE and still features it on iPads and MacBooks—but this is the first time it has returned on a flagship iPhone.

Using your thumb again will take some getting used to, but it's not a deal-breaker. Some folks will miss the convenience and speed of Face ID, where all you needed to do was stare at the screen, instead of now reaching for the Touch ID sensor on the right side. Others who hated facial authentication might actually like the return of the fingerprint sensor.

Apple also introduced the Dynamic Island a few years ago—the pill-shaped island around the Face ID camera sensors that shows live app activities, like the next turn on your navigation or music currently playing, giving a second job to the camera cutout. Without Face ID, Dynamic Island hasn't disappeared from the iPhone Duo. Instead, Apple moved it to the top-right edge around the outer selfie camera; on the inner display, it appears at the top-right edge when a live activity is in progress.

The iPhone Duo supports MagSafe just like the rest of the iPhone lineup, so you can magnetically stick your favorite phone grip on the back, or attach the phone to a magnetic car dock, bike mount, or wireless charger. One thing to note: If you unfold the Duo and attach it to a car dock or tripod, the phone will tip to one side because the magnet can’t keep it held straight given the uneven weight distribution. Third-party accessory makers could address this with anti-rotation magnets, but it might mean upgrading your current accessories.

Wallets, unfortunately, are the one accessory that won't really play well with the Duo. Yes, you can still attach a MagSafe wallet, but because the Duo is shorter than other iPhones, the wallet sticks out awkwardly past the lower edge. Apple doesn't have an official solution, so if you want to use a magnetic wallet with your iPhone, you'll have to look at third-party accessories.

Everyone knows the rear cameras on your smartphone are better than the selfie camera on the front. One of the best features on a folding phone is the ability to take a selfie with those superior rear cameras and still be able to see a preview with the outer screen.

Don't confuse this with “Duo Preview” as I did, though. That's a feature that lets you show the person you're photographing the camera's viewfinder, so they can make sure they look picture-perfect before you tap the shutter. The setting to enable this is on the left edge of the camera app, where you can also turn on “Kids Cue” to have Snoopy animations play on the outer screen to get the attention of a kid for the perfect photo.

But if you're taking a selfie yourself and want the camera controls on the front screen, all you need to do is launch the camera and flip the phone around—you'll see a slider that says, “Slide to show camera.” Slide it, and the full camera app will show up on the outer screen, so you can switch modes and access Photographic Styles right there and enjoy the higher-quality results from the superior cameras. Doing this also crucially disables the inner screen, so your fingers won't accidentally switch modes.

Filling the inner 7.6-inch screen with two different apps running side by side is incredibly handy and one of the cornerstone features of any folding phone. Whether you're on a phone call and want to pull up a confirmation email, or want to browse two different Safari webpages, you can drag an app from the bottom and pull it to the left or right side to enter the split-screen state.

One trick that isn't immediately obvious is that you can create app pairs. Just tap the middle bar between the two apps once, and it'll glow white, meaning that those two apps are now locked and can be accessed at any moment from the Recent apps menu. You can't save that as an app icon combo, though. This is still very handy if you know you'll always want to open two apps simultaneously on the big screen.

I get it, you're going to want to protect the fragile screens of your $1,999 iPhone. A case is smart—folding phone cases aren't like normal cases, as they usually come in two shells that attach to each side of the phone. You can also totally add a screen protector to the outer 5.4-inch screen.

But for the 7.6-inch matte inner screen, exercise some caution. You might see chatter online that you can “remove” the Duo's nano-texture finish, but it's an “integral” component of the inner display—as Apple says on this support page. “Don't attempt to remove it,” Apple writes. (Apple also has a page on cleaning the nano-texture display properly here.) Attempting to remove the finish is likely a surefire way to void your warranty. Apple doesn't say anything about using third-party screen protectors, but using one could compromise the display performance.

If you scratch or damage the nano-texture finish, Apple says you can take it to any Apple Store or an Apple Authorized Service Provider and have it replaced for $19 (or $49 if the phone is out of warranty). That's not the same as if there's an actual crack in the inner display—that'll run you a cool $999 if you don't have AppleCare+.

The iPhone Duo is a wide phone. If you've complained about the size of modern smartphones and how it's hard to reach the top of the screen, well, you might still have some issues reaching the sides of the Duo. There's some good news, though. Apple lets you split the keyboard to make typing on the inner display a little easier.

Later this year, Apple will also issue a software update bringing Reachability to the Duo. On other iPhones, this brings the top of an app down, making it easier to reach. On the Duo, it will move an app to the left or right so you won't have to stretch those fingers as much.

Have you always wanted to use a stylus on an iPhone? Soon! Steve Jobs is probably rolling in his grave.

Apple will bring support for the USB-C Apple Pencil (and only that Apple Pencil model, not others) in a software update later this year. Paired with the 7.6-inch screen's nano-texture finish, it should make writing and drawing on the iPhone Duo feel paper-like. Sadly, there's no place to store the stylus on the iPhone like you can on an iPad. I'm sure someone will make an accessory for it.

Benjamin Netanyahu : le « Monsieur Sécurité » d'Israël parviendra-t-il à remporter un septième mandat de Premier ministre ?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Benjamin Netanyahu, le Premier ministre israélien ayant exercé le plus longtemps, espère remporter un septième mandat lors des élections législatives qui se tiendront en Israël le 27 octobre.

Depuis des décennies, M. Netanyahu, âgé de 77 ans, se présente comme le « Monsieur Sécurité » d'Israël.

Mais sa candidature à un nouveau mandat intervient après ce que de nombreux Israéliens considèrent comme le plus grand échec en matière de sécurité de l'histoire du pays : les attaques menées par le Hamas le 7 octobre 2023.

Depuis lors, il s'est attaché à mener des guerres contre le Hamas, ainsi que contre ses alliés, le Hezbollah et l'Iran, tout en faisant l'objet d'un procès pénal pour corruption, accusations qu'il nie.

Les sondages laissent entrevoir une course serrée entre le bloc de droite de Netanyahou et un bloc d'opposition composé de partis qui s'unissent pour le chasser du pouvoir. Parmi eux figurent l'ancien Premier ministre Naftali Bennett et l'ancien chef d'état-major Gadi Eisenkot.

L'héritage du 7 octobre devrait jouer un rôle central dans la décision de nombreux électeurs.

Malgré les polémiques et les crises politiques qui ont menacé son emprise sur le pouvoir, Netanyahou s'est forgé une réputation de survivant politique.

Réélu en novembre 2022 pour ce qui était alors son sixième mandat de Premier ministre, il dirige la coalition la plus à droite de l'histoire d'Israël.

Son retour au pouvoir est intervenu après une période relativement brève dans l'opposition, qui a suivi douze années consécutives à la tête du gouvernement, un retour qui a renforcé la conviction de ses partisans selon laquelle le « roi Bibi » était politiquement invincible.

Il reste toujours populaire auprès de nombreux électeurs de droite et religieux, qui le considèrent comme un leader fort en matière de sécurité, et dont le gouvernement a fait pression pour étendre les dispenses de service militaire aux juifs ultra-orthodoxes poursuivant des études religieuses à temps plein.

Mais son apparente invincibilité est désormais menacée, l'ombre des attentats du 7 octobre planant sur les élections.

Netanyahu a reconnu une part de responsabilité pour ne pas avoir empêché ce qui s'est passé le 7 octobre 2023, lorsque Israël – sous son mandat – a subi l'attaque la plus catastrophique de son histoire, au cours de laquelle des milliers de tireurs dirigés par le Hamas ont franchi la frontière depuis Gaza, tuant environ 1 200 personnes et emmenant 251 autres à Gaza en otages.

Mais il affirme que de nombreuses autres personnes portent également une part de responsabilité, notamment de hauts responsables de la sécurité, dans les défaillances qui ont conduit à ces attaques. Il les a accusés d'avoir délibérément choisi de ne pas le réveiller dans les heures qui ont précédé les attaques, par crainte qu'il n'ordonne un déploiement massif de l'armée israélienne et n'aggrave la situation.

Au moins 74 000 personnes ont été tuées lors de l'offensive israélienne à Gaza, selon le ministère de la Santé de Gaza, dirigé par le Hamas, dont les chiffres sont considérés comme fiables par l'ONU. Les actions d'Israël à Gaza ont suscité une condamnation généralisée de la part de la communauté internationale.

Au cours des années qui ont suivi l'attaque, de plus en plus de rapports font état d'avertissements qui auraient été adressés à Netanyahou – tant par son propre chef de la sécurité que par des voisins de la région –, ce qu'il nie.

Le mois dernier, le journal israélien *Haaretz* a publié des extraits d'un nouvel ouvrage, affirmant que le dirigeant des Émirats arabes unis avait appelé Netanyahou dix jours avant l'attaque pour l'avertir des projets du Hamas visant à mener une opération de grande envergure.

Le bureau de Netanyahou a qualifié cette information de « mensonge absolu » et a déclaré qu'il allait poursuivre le journal en justice.

En novembre 2024, Netanyahou est devenu le quatrième dirigeant mondial à faire l'objet d'un mandat d'arrêt délivré par la Cour pénale internationale, aux côtés du Russe Vladimir Poutine, du Soudanais Omar el-Béchir et du Libyen Mouammar Kadhafi.

Cette décision, ainsi que le mandat d'arrêt délivré à l'encontre de l'ancien ministre israélien de la Défense Yoav Gallant, a suscité une vive réaction dans l'ensemble du spectre politique israélien.

Ces mandats sont liés aux actions militaires menées par Israël à Gaza à la suite des attaques du Hamas du 7 octobre et portent notamment sur des allégations d'utilisation de « la famine comme méthode de guerre » et de « crimes contre l'humanité, notamment des meurtres, des persécutions et d'autres actes inhumains ».

Les deux hommes rejettent ces accusations.

Benjamin Netanyahu est né à Tel-Aviv en 1949. En 1963, sa famille s'est installée aux États-Unis lorsque son père, Benzion, éminent historien et militant sioniste, s'est vu proposer un poste universitaire.

À l'âge de 18 ans, il est retourné en Israël, où il a passé cinq années remarquables dans l'armée, servant en tant que capitaine au sein d'une unité de commandos d'élite, le Sayeret Matkal. Il a été blessé lors d'un raid contre un avion de ligne belge détourné par des militants palestiniens qui avait atterri en Israël en 1972, et a combattu pendant la guerre du Moyen-Orient de 1973.

En 1976, le frère de Netanyahu, Jonathan, a trouvé la mort alors qu'il menait une opération visant à libérer des otages d'un avion détourné à Entebbe, en Ouganda. Sa mort a profondément marqué la famille Netanyahu, et son nom est entré dans la légende en Israël.

Netanyahu a créé un institut de lutte contre le terrorisme en mémoire de son frère et, en 1982, il est devenu chef de mission adjoint d'Israël à Washington.

Du jour au lendemain, la carrière publique de Netanyahu a pris son envol. Parlant anglais avec aisance et arborant un accent américain caractéristique, il est devenu un visage familier de la télévision américaine et un défenseur efficace d'Israël.

Il a été nommé représentant permanent d'Israël auprès des Nations unies à New York en 1984.

Netanyahu s'est lancé dans la politique à son retour en Israël en 1988 : il a remporté un siège pour le Likoud à la Knesset (le Parlement) et est devenu ministre délégué aux Affaires étrangères.

Il a ensuite pris la tête du parti, puis, en 1996, est devenu Premier ministre d'Israël à l'issue d'élections anticipées organisées à la suite de l'assassinat d'Yitzhak Rabin.

Netanyahu était également le plus jeune dirigeant d'Israël et le premier à être né après la création de l'État en 1948.

Bien qu'il ait vivement critiqué les accords de paix d'Oslo de 1993 entre Israël et les Palestiniens, Netanyahu a signé en 1997 un accord cédant 80 % d'Hébron au contrôle de l'Autorité palestinienne et a accepté de nouveaux retraits de la Cisjordanie occupée, suscitant de vives critiques de la part de la droite (même si le plan a échoué avant que la plupart des retraits israéliens n'aient eu lieu).

Il a perdu son poste en 1999 après avoir convoqué des élections 17 mois avant la date prévue ; il a été battu par le chef du Parti travailliste Ehud Barak, son ancien commandant.

Netanyahu a démissionné de son poste de président du Likoud et a été remplacé par Ariel Sharon.

Après l'élection d'Ariel Sharon au poste de Premier ministre en 2001, Netanyahu a réintégré le gouvernement, d'abord en tant que ministre des Affaires étrangères, puis en tant que ministre des Finances. En 2005, il a démissionné pour protester contre le retrait israélien de la bande de Gaza occupée.

Sa chance s'est présentée à nouveau en 2005, lorsque Sharon – juste avant d'être victime d'un grave accident vasculaire cérébral qui l'a plongé dans le coma – a quitté le Likoud pour fonder un nouveau parti centriste, Kadima.

Netanyahu a de nouveau remporté la direction du Likoud et a été élu Premier ministre pour la deuxième fois en mars 2009.

Il a accepté un gel sans précédent de dix mois de la construction de colonies (avec diverses exceptions) en Cisjordanie, permettant ainsi l'ouverture de pourparlers de paix avec les Palestiniens, mais les négociations ont échoué fin 2010.

Bien qu'il ait annoncé publiquement en 2009 qu'il acceptait sous certaines conditions la création d'un État palestinien aux côtés d'Israël, il a par la suite durci sa position. « Un État palestinien ne verra pas le jour, pas tel que les gens l'envisagent. Cela n'arrivera pas », a-t-il déclaré à une station de radio israélienne en 2019.

Plus tôt cette année, il a déclaré que tout le monde le connaissait comme l'homme qui « a bloqué pendant des décennies la création d'un État palestinien qui mettrait en danger notre existence », ajoutant qu'Israël maintiendrait un contrôle total en matière de sécurité sur l'ensemble du territoire situé à l'ouest du Jourdain, y compris Gaza et la Cisjordanie, « quoi qu'il arrive ».

Son mandat a également été marqué par une forte intensification de l'expansion des colonies israéliennes en Cisjordanie occupée, ce qui, selon ses détracteurs, a compliqué les efforts visant à parvenir à une solution à deux États.

Son gouvernement actuel a autorisé la création de plus de 100 nouvelles colonies depuis son arrivée au pouvoir en 2022 et a supervisé une expansion rapide des avant-postes et fermes illégaux situés au sommet des collines.

L'organisation israélienne « Peace Now », qui surveille les colonies et milite en faveur de la solution à deux États, a déclaré que le gouvernement avait « fait progresser l'annexion de facto de la Cisjordanie à un rythme sans précédent ».

Toutes les colonies et tous les avant-postes israéliens situés en Cisjordanie occupée sont illégaux au regard du droit international. Les avant-postes sont également illégaux au regard du droit israélien, bien que nombre d'entre eux bénéficient d'un soutien de l'État.

Les attaques palestiniennes et les interventions militaires israéliennes ont à plusieurs reprises plongé Israël dans des affrontements au sein et aux abords de la bande de Gaza, avant et après le retour au pouvoir de Netanyahou en 2009.

Tout au long de sa carrière politique, Netanyahou n'a cessé d'alerter sur le fait que l'Iran représentait la plus grande menace pour Israël. Pendant plus de trois décennies, il a affirmé que Téhéran cherchait à se doter d'armes nucléaires et a appelé à maintes reprises les dirigeants mondiaux à adopter une position plus ferme à l'égard du gouvernement iranien.

Cette campagne de longue haleine a atteint son paroxysme en février 2026, lorsque Israël et les États-Unis ont lancé des frappes contre l'Iran qui ont coûté la vie au Guide suprême Ali Khamenei. L'Iran a riposté par des attaques contre Israël, des bases militaires américaines et des États du Golfe, tandis qu'une attaque du Hezbollah a dégénéré en une nouvelle guerre au Liban.

Un cessez-le-feu négocié par le Pakistan est entré en vigueur en avril, suivi en juin d'un protocole d'accord entre les États-Unis et l'Iran, mais celui-ci a volé en éclats en l'espace de quelques semaines. Depuis, les États-Unis et l'Iran se sont livrés à des frappes réciproques, et aucun nouvel accord n'est en vigueur.

Après 2016, Netanyahou a fait l'objet d'une enquête pour corruption, qui a abouti, en novembre 2019, à sa mise en examen pour corruption, fraude et abus de confiance dans le cadre de trois affaires distinctes.

Il nie toute malversation et affirme être victime d'une « chasse aux sorcières » à motivation politique orchestrée par ses adversaires. Son procès s'est ouvert en mai 2020, faisant de lui le premier Premier ministre en exercice à comparaître devant un tribunal.

Il a demandé la grâce au président Isaac Herzog, mais ce dernier a déclaré qu'il fallait d'abord s'efforcer de parvenir à un accord sur le plaidoyer. L'affaire n'est toujours pas résolue, plus de six ans après les faits, et le procès se poursuit.

Les projets controversés de réforme du système judiciaire ont également déclenché d'importantes manifestations, mettant en évidence de profondes divisions concernant l'identité nationale et la démocratie en Israël, avant les attentats du 7 octobre — des divisions qui restent sans solution alors qu'Israël s'apprête à se rendre à nouveau aux urnes.

L'arrivée au pouvoir de Donald Trump en 2017 a conduit à un rapprochement entre les politiques des gouvernements américain et israélien, et moins d'un an plus tard, Trump a annoncé qu'il reconnaissait Jérusalem comme capitale d'Israël.

Cette décision a déclenché une vague d'indignation dans tout le monde arabe – qui soutient la revendication des Palestiniens sur la partie orientale de Jérusalem, occupée par Israël depuis la guerre du Moyen-Orient de 1967 ; cette revendication est appuyée par l'ONU, qui considère Jérusalem-Est comme un territoire palestinien occupé –, mais elle a permis à Netanyahou de remporter un grand succès politique et diplomatique sur le plan national.

Et en janvier 2020, Netanyahou a salué le plan de paix de Trump entre Israël et les Palestiniens comme « l'opportunité du siècle », bien qu'il ait été rejeté par les Palestiniens, qui l'ont jugé partial, et qu'il soit resté lettre morte.

Netanyahou était également sur la même longueur d'onde que Trump concernant l'Iran, saluant le retrait du président en 2018 de l'accord sur le nucléaire iranien et le rétablissement des sanctions économiques.

Trump a toutefois tenu des propos cinglants à l'égard du dirigeant israélien, l'accusant de déloyauté, après que celui-ci eut félicité Joe Biden pour sa victoire à l'élection présidentielle de novembre 2020.

Les relations entre les deux dirigeants ont depuis été rétablies, puis mises à nouveau à l'épreuve.

Au cours de son deuxième mandat, Trump a soutenu Israël dans la guerre contre l'Iran, a fait pression pour que Netanyahu soit gracié dans une affaire de corruption et l'a reçu à la Maison Blanche à plusieurs reprises, la dernière fois en juillet. Netanyahu a rencontré Trump à huit reprises depuis le retour au pouvoir du président américain en janvier 2025, soit plus que tout autre dirigeant mondial.

Il a réaffirmé ce soutien quelques jours seulement avant les élections. Dans un message publié sur son compte Truth Social, Trump a apporté son soutien à Netanyahou, le qualifiant de « grand Premier ministre guerrier » et le décrivant comme « fort, courageux et brillant face à une opposition farouche ».

Avant tout, cette élection est considérée comme un référendum sur l'une des figures politiques les plus controversées du pays. Pour ses partisans, Benjamin Netanyahu reste indispensable à la sécurité d'Israël. Ses détracteurs affirment quant à eux que sa survie politique met Israël en danger.

The ‘Exotic Physics’ Behind a Mysterious 1,000-Mile Cloud on Mars

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every morning during the Martian spring and summer, a thin and extremely long cloud emerges, only to disappear again. The wispy formation appears near the volcano Arsia Mons and gradually elongates until it reaches over 1,000 miles—about the distance from New York to Kansas City as the crow flies—before dissipating. This cycle then repeats for several months.

Since it was first recorded by a probe in 2018, scientists have been unable to find a plausible explanation for the existence of this cloud, often referred to as the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC). Until now. Recently, researchers successfully modeled the cloud’s formation for the first time, their results suggesting that it may be driven by a physical process that has never been directly observed in any planet's atmosphere.

"We found that we needed to include some exotic physics … physics that, while included in textbooks, is treated as theoretical and usually thought not to happen in nature. It certainly hasn’t been seen in action before," Jorge Hernández-Bernal, a planetary scientist at Paris’ Sorbonne Université and lead author of the study, said in a press release. "Once we included this physics in our simulations, the AMEC emerged just as we hoped.”

On Earth, clouds usually form when water vapor creates droplets or ice crystals in the presence of tiny particles, such as dust or salt. The new study, published in Nature Geoscience, instead suggests AMEC could be forming through so-called homogeneous nucleation, where vapor changes directly into ice crystals on its own without any help from debris.

“It’s akin to droplets of condensation appearing in the middle of a room, rather than on a window," Hernández-Bernal explained. “It’s wholly unexpected.”

This phenomenon requires extreme conditions—a sharp temperature drop of 30 degrees Celsius within 10 minutes and high relative humidity. The Arsia Mons volcano also plays a key role. When wind current hits the 65,000-foot-tall volcano, it creates a powerful wave that launches moist air high in the atmosphere in a matter of minutes.

Scientists have assumed a similar phenomenon could occur in the upper atmospheres of other planets, including Venus and even Earth, but it has never been observed. That’s not too surprising given the extreme air humidity required for this phenomenon, which is more than 100,000 times greater than any value measured on Earth.

Before this modeling, it was not known that these humidity levels existed on Mars either. “We don’t have nearly as much information about Mars’ atmosphere as we do about Earth's, so reproducing the AMEC to this degree is a big success,” said Hernández-Bernal, adding, “the result is remarkable.”

Tom Hayden’s Legacy and the New American Left

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Standing in front of protesters on a misty day in Seattle, a 59-year-old Tom Hayden spoke into a bullhorn, declaring his hope. “I never thought the time would come that a new generation of activists would part the waters, the waters in which your idealism is supposed to be drowned.” The venerable activist, cofounder of Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), had spent almost two decades in the California State Legislature. But like other progressive legislators, he, too, was stymied by the conservative politics of the late 20th century. Earlier that day, tens of thousands of people—a historic coalition of union members, environmental activists, and anarchists—overwhelmed city officials and shut down the World Trade Organization (WTO) Ministerial Conference on November 30, 1999. It was the first major blow against the Clinton-era free-trade consensus.

The crowd repeated Hayden’s words through the “people’s mic” method and mirrored the then–state Senator’s visible giddiness toward the end of his remarks. “You have slowed the machinery of destruction down. But it can’t be about slowing the rate of destruction. It has to be about speeding the rate of creation of a new world, a better place.” Decades after he organized the landmark anti-war protests at the 1968 Democratic National Convention, Hayden was inspired by what he witnessed in Seattle. He could once again imagine a left that could win.

Tom Hayden passed away 10 years ago, on October 23, 2016, just weeks before Trump’s unexpected victory. As a result, his worst fears of an authoritarian right’s limiting the gains of the 1960s came true. Still, his life has become an even more relevant case study for those trying to figure out the most effective way to resist the current administration’s assault on democracy. As a lifelong insider-outsider, a Freedom Rider who later advised presidential candidates, Hayden was a passionate proponent of both radical direct action and electoral campaigns. Whether in the streets of American cities fighting back against ICE or in the offices of activist politicians like Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez or Mayor Zohran Mamdani, today’s progressives are finding creative ways to build on the work of Hayden’s generation. As memory of the Vietnam War era fades, a reexamination of ecumenical organizers like him will strengthen our understanding of the links between the victories and defeats of the past and today’s attempts to build a better democracy.

Born in 1939, Thomas Emmet Hayden was a product of middle-class America. Coming of age as the American left was decimated during the Red Scare, he did not consider himself political until he learned about the sit-in movement in 1960. He was first arrested as a Freedom Rider in Albany, Georgia, on December 10, 1961, the moment he began work on the first draft of SDS’s manifesto, the Port Huron Statement. Completed by Hayden and other founders of the organization the following June, the document promoted participatory democracy, offered a powerful rebuke of the era’s Cold War consensus, and soon became required reading for organizers for decades to come. What began as a modest network of student leaders would grow dramatically following the expansion of the war in Vietnam, with approximately 100,000 members at its peak in the late 1960s.

Hayden became one of the leading figures of the New Left, a figure whom many reporters treated as an entry point to a new age of radicalism. Together with other SDS leaders, he helped establish community organizing projects in several cities, traveled to Vietnam, where he once facilitated the release of three American POWs, and was put on trial for conspiracy to riot as part of the Chicago Eight. Over time, the times increasingly radicalized Hayden, who vacillated between calling for “Two, Three, Many Columbias” and clinging to his battered but lingering hope for mainstream politicians like Robert F. Kennedy during his presidential campaign.

However, Hayden’s work stretched far beyond the 1960s. Like other New Left leaders, he is often tied to a narrative of decline about the New Left’s rise and fall, centered on the well-documented implosion of SDS in 1969. The truth is that Hayden’s life tells a far more complicated and representative story of the decade’s activism, its ongoing influence on today’s progressives, and the porous relationship between radicals and reformers in America.

Unlike some high-profile leaders of his era, Hayden remained dedicated to movement work. Instead of burning out, he gradually shifted toward more conventional tactics in the early 1970s. Together with his then-wife Jane Fonda, he led the Indochina Peace Campaign, which helped sustain the anti-war movement’s political power and cut off funding for the Vietnam War. Following an insurgent Senate campaign, he and Fonda established the Campaign for Economic Democracy in California in 1977. By the early 1980s, CED had become a statewide organization that helped pass rent control initiatives and elect dozens of local and statewide officials. Hayden won a State Assembly seat in Santa Monica in 1982, serving a decade as a state representative, then another eight years as a state senator. Despite Republican governors’ limiting him for most of his time in Sacramento, Hayden still championed environmental reforms, including the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986, and pushed for peace among street gangs in Los Angeles.

After he was termed out of the State Legislature, Hayden taught classes on social movements, joined The Nation as an editorial board member, and once again became a fixture at anti-war rallies and other meetings in progressive spaces. During his brief stint as a fellow at Harvard’s Institute of Politics, he led a series of workshops, “Activism Now!: Students, Sweatshops and Globalization,” that culminated in a trip where four students were arrested while documenting planned protests against the Free Trade Area of the Americas. After Occupy Wall Street, Hayden was in regular contact with new movement figures such as Debt Collective cofounder Astra Taylor after the two appeared on a panel together in 2014. “Good luck to Strike Debt. As your generation’s prospects decline and a new Dark Age rises, I do think you have a window of glory ahead though,” he wrote to Taylor. It was one of many interactions where Hayden cheered on a new generation shaped by the WTO protests, the post-9/11 wars, and the 2008 financial collapse.

In his final years, Hayden tried to situate the Obama presidency within a longer history of protest that contributed to his 2008 victory. He encouraged progressives to celebrate the best elements of the 44th president’s foreign policy (Cuba, Iran) while remaining critical of the war in Afghanistan and the administration’s tepid domestic agenda. He drew links between the Black Lives Matter movement and his work in Newark and lauded the lobbying efforts of the Dreamers. In 2016, Hayden infuriated many progressives when he backed Hillary Clinton over Bernie Sanders just days before the New York primary. He saw it as a pragmatic choice, rooted in fears of a Trump administration and the future of the Supreme Court. Those fears were legitimate and have proven prescient, but his endorsement underestimated the Sanders campaign’s potential impact.

If he were alive, he would celebrate that 2016 was a real turning point for the American left, when countless young organizers began to seriously engage in electoral politics. While he never would have predicted that the Democratic Socialists of America would become the post-2016 left’s primary beneficiary (even though he spoke at their annual convention in 2013), he would praise its electoral work, especially in cities like New York City, Chicago, and Los Angeles.

Much as he felt witnessing the Battle of Seattle in 1999, Hayden would have been ecstatic to see how a broad cross section of ordinary people has stood up to masked ICE agents occupying American cities over the past two years. The Minneapolis protests and general strike were, in many ways, the model many others from the 1960s generation had dreamed of for decades. The former community organizer would have been moved by how Minnesotans courageously faced down the president and won.

It is, in fact, a new dark age, but it has also benefited from those who built on the work of figures like Tom Hayden. He recognized the long battle but was also often driven by his desire to take urgent action to confront injustice. His story reflects that of many from his era who spent decades searching for different channels for progressives to win political power. Hayden had a searching mind, open to experimentation and adapting to history. Throughout it all, he thought that radical grassroots movements could merge with conventional politics to create a new democracy.

The Decades-Long Battle Within the Democratic Party Establishment Between the Heirs of Dean Acheson and Franklin Roosevelt

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The calamitous history of America’s wars of choice.

As Trump’s disastrous war against Iran continues to grind on, it might benefit the incoming class of Democratic policymakers who seem poised to take over not just the House but possibly the Senate to reflect on the calamitous history of Washington’s wars of choice. The first such war was President Harry Truman’s, in Korea, begun by North Korean forces in June 1950. As Nation contributing editor James W. Carden shows in The Great Betrayal: How the Democrats Became the Party of War, Truman and his hawkish secretary of state, Dean Acheson, steered the party in a direction not intended by Truman’s predecessor, Franklin D. Roosevelt. Truman and Acheson’s embrace of the burgeoning national security state and their adoption of the paranoiac anti-communism that heretofore had been characteristic of the American right led them headlong into an unnecessary war of choice in Korea. Korea set the precedent for Vietnam, for Iraq, and for Iran—it was the first of many wars waged in the absence of constitutional authorization on the flimsiest of pretexts.

The invasion of South Korea by North Korea’s Kim Il Sung provided Washington’s Cold War planners with just the story they needed at just the right time. As President Harry Truman reached the midpoint of his first elected term, he surprised some of his Defense and State Department planners by not immediately accepting his national security staff’s more hawkish recommendations. “We were sweating over it,” one Truman adviser admitted later, “then thank god Korea came along.”

The idea that South Korea represented a core US national security interest was a novel one. In fact, there was, in the years and months leading up to the conflict, broad agreement among US policymakers that it represented no such thing. At Yalta, a four-power trusteeship was created to oversee the former Japanese colony. In April 1948, Truman and the Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) admitted that Korea was “of little strategic value to the United States.” In a speech on January 12, 1950, Acheson famously left both Korea and Formosa (Taiwan) outside the boundaries of the US defense perimeter in East Asia. North Korea launched its attack on June 24, and even then the Joint Chiefs of Staff warned that it would be “militarily unsound” for Washington to commit large forces “in an area of slight strategic importance.”

But once the war was joined, Truman made the ever-dubious historical parallel with Neville Chamberlain’s decision to “appease” Hitler at Munich. “The free nations have learned the fateful lesson of the 1930’s. That lesson is that aggression must be met firmly. Appeasement leads only to further aggression and ultimately to war,” declared Truman on July 19. Truman later claimed that upon learning of North Korea’s invasion, he “remembered how each time that the democracies failed to act, it had encouraged the aggressors to keep going ahead. Communism was acting in Korea just as Hitler, Mussolini and the Japanese had acted ten, fifteen and twenty years earlier.”

“If this was allowed to go unchallenged,” he continued, “it would mean a third world war, just as similar incidents had brought on a second world war.” In his seminal study on the “Origins of the Cold War,” the realist scholar Hans Morgenthau noted, “The misinterpretation of the North Korean aggression as part of a grand design at world conquest originating in and controlled by Moscow resulted in a drastic militarization of the cold war.” Indeed, Truman and Acheson viewed Asia solely through the prism of the Cold War. Not surprisingly, Acheson blamed the Russians for starting the war, but there is no evidence the project originated with Stalin.

Dean Rusk, an Acheson protégé then serving as assistant secretary of state for the Far East, blithely dismissed China as a puppet of the Soviets. China, in Rusk’s view, was merely a “colonial Russian government, a Slavic Manchukuo on a large-scale.” Acheson also thought he saw Moscow’s hand behind Ho Chi Minh’s insurgency in Vietnam. Acheson worried that Ho might establish a “communist-dominated, Moscow-oriented” regime. For Acheson and his disciples like Rusk, Moscow’s was the not-so-hidden hand behind every problem everywhere.

Paranoia is a poor substitute for strategy, but driven by their fears of a monolithic communist movement, Truman and Acheson provided $15 million to France to bolster its efforts against Ho’s insurgency. Truman also created a US Military Assistance Advisory Group, Indochina, to provide material support and advisers to the French forces fighting the Viet Minh. On October 9, Army Brigadier General Francis G. Brink arrived in Saigon with a contingent of 65 American troops—the first of many more to come. As a recent, and sympathetic, Acheson biographer admits, Acheson “was in the vanguard in leading the United States into Indochina’s Big Muddy.” As with many of his Cold War policies, Truman’s intervention in Vietnam was diametrically opposed to the intentions of his predecessor.

At the Tehran Conference, Roosevelt said the Allies should not fight to restore Vietnam to the French—in his view, after 100 years of French colonialism, the Vietnamese were worse off than ever. In Bohlen’s judgment, Roosevelt’s views on Vietnam reflected the president’s “strong dislike of colonialism,” which Roosevelt frequently expressed. Yet by the time Truman and Acheson left office, Washington was funding nearly half of France’s costs in Vietnam. Ultimately, these decisions set in motion a war that would eventually kill upwards of 3.5 million Vietnamese and 58,000 American soldiers, sailors, and airmen.

By 1952, Truman was a deeply unpopular and polarizing figure, so much so that he was defeated in the New Hampshire primary by a margin of 11 points (55 percent to 44 percent) by Tennessee Senator Estes Kefauver. By the end of March, he had abandoned his bid for reelection. Truman left office with a 32 percent approval rating, the second-lowest approval rating for a departing president after Richard Nixon, who had sunk to 24 percent in 1974. Yet in the three-quarters of a century since leaving office, Truman’s reputation has witnessed a remarkable turnaround. By the time the new century dawned, Truman’s decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki; his headlong rush into a Cold War with the Soviet Union; and his unconstitutional war of choice in Korea were all treated as acts of statesmanship by both popular historians and the Democratic elite. In September 2000, President Bill Clinton presided over a ceremony naming the headquarters of the State Department in honor of Truman. “Thanks, in no small measure to President Truman, we have won the Cold War,” said Clinton.

Truman’s stock has only risen further in the quarter-century since Clinton spoke those words. A Siena College survey released in 2022 ranked Truman as the seventh-greatest president. The 2024 Presidential Greatness Project at the University of Houston ranked Truman as the sixth-greatest. There are precious few dissenters from the pro-Truman consensus. Among the most astringent was the novelist and critic Gore Vidal, who noted, “The inexorable beatification of Harry Truman is now an important aspect of our evolving imperial system.”

But it was Acheson who lent an intellectual sheen and respectability to Truman’s parochialism. It was he, not Truman, who, as the main driver behind institutions like NATO and programs like the ERA, spawned generations of imitators among the Democratic foreign policy cognoscenti. Kennedy’s secretary of state, Dean Rusk, learned at his knee; Clinton’s secretary of state, Madeleine Albright, said Acheson was her model. Republicans also now line up to praise him. Henry Kissinger observed in 2006—and perhaps with not a little envy—that “Accolades for him have become bipartisan.” Later in life, working to defeat congressional opposition to President Nixon’s anti-ballistic missile program, Acheson served as an early mentor to the young neoconservative operatives Paul Wolfowitz, Richard Perle, and Edward Luttwak. Acheson spent his years out of office crafting one of the most enduring and pernicious of Cold War myths: that of Roosevelt’s credulousness in the face of Stalin. Acheson succeeded in selling the story that the Cold War was unavoidable, and the course he and Truman chose could not have been otherwise. Yet others, like Harriman, who knew the Soviets, Stalin, and Roosevelt far better than Acheson, thought otherwise. “FDR was basically right in thinking he could make progress by personal relations with Stalin,” said Harriman some years later. Harriman, like Bohlen, was at Tehran and Yalta and remembered that “Stalin was very much impressed with Roosevelt; you could almost say he was in awe of Roosevelt.” “I don’t know what would have happened if FDR had lived,” said Harriman toward the end of his long, eventful life. “I only know things would not have been the same.”

The militarization of the Cold War that took place under Truman and Acheson was a conscious choice. But it was not a choice with which everyone agreed; and so began a decades-long battle within the Democratic Party establishment between the heirs of Acheson and the heirs of Roosevelt.

The strain of bird flu that’s ripped through American barns for the last four years has killed two people who caught it from sick animals. But as far as anyone can tell, humans can’t spread it. The virus has remained a true “bird” flu, tailored to attach to avian cells, not invade human throats and noses, where coughing and sneezing reliably spew droplets.

Under the right conditions, however, that could change. A single mutation could give the bird flu a foothold in people and spark a pandemic that experts fear could be deadlier than COVID-19.

For that to happen, this bird flu would likely need to find an intermediate host — an animal vulnerable to viruses that infect both birds and humans. These animals would be crowded together, each infection raising the odds that a new strain could arise through mutation. Then, for that strain to jump across species, it would have to have access to humans and time to spread among them undetected.

That perfect petri dish exists right now, a ProPublica investigation found. For over two months, an outbreak of bird flu has raged uncontrolled on a Utah farm that raises thousands of mink for fur. Mink have lungs and airways that on a cellular level are remarkably similar to those of people; they have caught and spread other flus and infected some humans with COVID-19.

Instead of trying to stop the outbreak and prevent it from spilling into humans, state and federal officials have done little except try to keep its existence quiet, our investigation shows. The failure to act amounts to a cascade of missteps flu experts called “irresponsible” and “puzzling.” One called it “playing with fire.”

Utah agriculture officials learned in mid-August that mink were dying of bird flu on C&T Fenn Farms, about an hour’s drive south from Salt Lake City, according to communications released under the state’s public records law.

Had poultry been infected, the playbook would have been clear: The strain is so virulent that if a single chicken tests positive, the U.S. government requires farmers to kill the whole flock to protect other farms as well as public health. Poultry farmers are reimbursed for the loss.

But there are no federal rules for how to deal with sick mink.

Handling Utah’s response was state veterinarian Amanda Price, who sought help from the U.S. Department of Agriculture, which takes the lead on bird flu outbreaks involving poultry and cows.

USDA officials said they didn’t regulate mink or have a vaccine that was approved to give them. And though officials with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention spoke with Price’s health department counterparts who were less involved with the outbreak, they never talked to her, and she never reached out.

The threat of spillover has been far from the minds of Utah officials, records and interviews show. Instead, front and center have been the interests of a business raising animals to skin for luxury pelts to ship overseas.

“It has been devastating,” Price told ProPublica of the outbreak and its impact on the farm’s owner. “This is his life’s work that he’s seeing be destroyed. I really hope that you understand the humanity behind this situation.”

Flu experts were unequivocal that the government should have ordered the farm to cull its mink, as other countries have done.

But Utah never seriously considered it. A fur trade representative warned Price that doing so would kill the business and, ultimately, the industry. Farms spend decades breeding mink to develop unique genetic lineages.

“There’s just not a lot of options out there other than kind of sitting and waiting and seeing what happens,” Price said, noting that USDA officials did not urge her to kill the mink or make her feel like bird flu in mink was any riskier than infections in other animals that have caught it, like ducks and cows.

Flu experts found that response surprising.

“Every other country that I’m aware of that has had H5N1 in fur farms, depopulation has happened pretty quickly,” said Tom Peacock, an avian influenza researcher at the Pirbright Institute. “The fact that the first instinct wasn’t just to depopulate and figure stuff out later is very concerning to me.”

And all the while, officials didn’t monitor the mink workers for symptoms, as guidance from the CDC urges.

Their exposure is significant: Mink sneeze. They escape. They attack. “The mink are fierce,” said a Spanish-speaking “minkero” who works on a nearby farm, showing the scars he got from the aggressive, solitary creatures. “You get scared because the fucking thing bites, and it claws at you.”

Right now in Washington state, officials are working to track down the contacts of a symptomatic person exposed to dairy cattle infected with bird flu. The patient is isolating at home. Utah public health officials used to do similar, proactive monitoring. But they stopped after complaints from their colleagues in the agriculture department that farmers were annoyed by it.

“Us doing active monitoring was making it so that farms didn’t want to work with them,” said Pamela Gomez Pinedo, a respiratory disease epidemiologist for Utah’s health department. As a result, public health workers had a “pretty difficult time” getting details from agriculture officials, she said.

As part of a 2025 agreement, the Utah Department of Agriculture and Food agreed to share information about bird flu outbreaks on farms — so long as the health officials didn’t do anything to track workers’ health.

“We will only do active monitoring in the case of evidence of human-to-human transmission,” Pinedo said in emails with a CDC epidemiologist. As the outbreak stretched to a month, CDC officials expressed concerns, asking how anyone would know if a worker had been infected if they weren’t getting tested.

Price said the state’s policy not to monitor workers is meant to allow farmers to decide whether to contact the health department, rather than “forcing it on them.” “If we started seeing people actively dying from this, obviously that would change how we respond to this,” she said.

A telltale sign of bird flu infection in humans is eye redness. It is present in over 90% of cases and is often the sole symptom a person displays.

Last Saturday, a ProPublica reporter tracked down two people who worked at the Utah mink farm. One had a visibly red eye.

The CDC explicitly urges testing for the symptom.

After ProPublica reached out to the farm, its owner, Chad Fenn, contacted the Utah County Health Department about the red eye, which had cleared up by Monday, he told ProPublica. He said the worker got dust in his eye; the department didn’t recommend testing.

“They’re not going to do anything,” Fenn said. “They said they don’t see that there’s a need.” He said the department recommended testing only if other symptoms, like a cough or sore throat, developed.

A half dozen flu experts had no shortage of adjectives to describe their disbelief at the government decisions not to cull the mink or monitor and test the workers.

“It’s amazing to me,” said Gail Hansen, a former Kansas state public health veterinarian and epidemiologist. “Utah doesn’t want to be the epicenter of the next human flu outbreak, right?”

Most of the new viruses that have shaken humanity in the past century have spilled over from animals. Ebolas likely came from fruit bats; HIV, from chimpanzees. The leading theory for COVID-19 is that it jumped from bats to humans via an intermediate host mammal such as a raccoon dog. And all the modern flu pandemics have been bird flu viruses that mutated to spread in humans.

ProPublica reported extensively on spillover in a 2023 series called “Roots of an Outbreak.” To prevent more deadly spillovers, experts worldwide urge governments to take a “one health” approach to viral threats, treating dangers to animals as dangers to humans.

That’s not happening in Utah, experts said after reviewing ProPublica’s findings. Both the state and federal government are to blame.

“There’s no leader of this ‘one health’ team to take charge,” said Erin Sorrell, a senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security. “That is going to allow the virus to continue to circulate, potentially adapt and potentially spill over into another host, whether it’s avian, mammalian or human.”

This strain of bird flu, which began ripping through American poultry farms in early 2022, has proved adept at jumping to other species. It took hold in cows in 2024 and since then has run rampant through dairies.

Yet federal guidance to prevent the spread into mink farms hasn’t considered cows a threat. Farmers are encouraged to keep wild birds from infiltrating their barns and avoid feeding them raw poultry. It has said nothing about feeding beef parts to mink, which farmers commonly do in summer months to encourage the animals to put on weight and grow.

Dairy cows, which are not “depopulated” if exposed to bird flu, can still be slaughtered with the meat sold to farms and humans even though researchers have found the virus in the tissues of sick cows. USDA officials have insisted that beef for human consumption is free of bird flu, in part because the virus concentrates itself in sections of the cow people don’t usually eat. Fenn said that the mink are routinely fed raw organs of livestock, including cows.

The genomic data from the samples of mink lungs and brains sent from Utah to a USDA lab shows that the mink were stricken with the same strain of the virus that has spread in dairy herds. Officials said they didn’t know whether contaminated beef was the way the mink got infected.

In mink, the dairy strain of bird flu may have found a much better host for mutating into a human flu, because mink airways have an abundance of the type of cell receptors in people’s throats and noses that are a common pathway for infection.

A 2024 study of the dairy strain of the bird flu found that a single mutation could switch the virus from preferring the bird-type receptors to human-type receptors. And in studies using ferrets, a close relative of mink, the virus acquired mutations that let it attack human-type cell receptors.

It’s not clear to scientists just how much of an evolutionary leap would be needed for this strain of bird flu to spread among humans, or whether mink are the host that would allow that leap to happen.

How this ultimately plays out “really depends on what happens with the virus,” said Meghan Davis, an epidemiologist at Johns Hopkins University.

“We might get lucky, and a response that is not robust may not have dire consequences,” she said. “But we may get unlucky.”

There’s also a risk of something called “reassortment,” where another flu virus could infect the same cell as a bird flu virus and swap wide swaths of genetic code already adapted to humans.

When the outbreak in the mink farm started, there was very little seasonal flu it could mix with, but September saw one of the earliest starts to flu season on record, compared with November, when flu normally takes off.

“The most likely scenario for a pandemic is that we have a virus that reassorts with another virus,” said Jennifer Nuzzo, director of the Pandemic Center at the Brown University School of Public Health. “Obviously, having this circulate amid human flu season is not great.”

Utah officials were far more responsive to the threat of spillover in 2020, when outbreaks of COVID-19 spread explosively on mink farms – including this very farm. They invited federal officials to the state to research how the virus adapted and moved.

Given mink’s reputation as escape artists, wildlife experts set up traps around infected farms. The traps frequently caught mink carrying COVID-19 that had originated in the nearby barns. The virus infected both escaped mink and wild mink they had come in contact with. Mink and farmworkers were frequently tested, tracking the way that the virus was spreading and mutating.

As other states and countries took the same measures, they identified humans infected by the mink with COVID-19 and, ultimately, human-to-human transmission.

There are no similar efforts now in Utah with bird flu.

A USDA spokesperson told ProPublica that it was more involved in 2020 because the virus that causes COVID-19 had “significant” potential to transmit between humans and animals and vice versa.

Scientists with the USDA sequenced and published bird flu genomic data from the first samples of dead mink. Those sequences didn’t show any particularly concerning mutations, experts and the CDC told ProPublica.

But in the past two months, experts said, the virus is sure to have continued mutating as it spread – it’s only a question of whether those mutations could confer some advantage in infecting humans as well.

“As long as it continues to go mink to mink, then it becomes more and more likely that it could jump to a human,” Hansen said.

Fenn said new samples were taken two weeks ago. Price said she is still waiting for the results.

John Korslund, a retired USDA official who helped coordinate the response to COVID-19 on mink farms several years prior, was one of several experts who said officials need to look closely at newer genetic samples to see how the virus is evolving.

“I really want to know what the virus has done, from when it started to today,” he said. “Are we getting accumulated mutations making the virus more dangerous?”

ProPublica began reporting on the outbreak late this summer after the USDA updated a bird flu dashboard that showed seven captive mink had tested positive in Utah. But neither the USDA nor state officials had said anything publicly, and, when asked, they wouldn’t discuss what was actually happening on the farm.

Communications obtained under the state’s public records law — first published by the Animal Welfare Institute and reported on by the Salt Lake Tribune — show Utah officials took pains to protect the farm’s privacy amid the outbreak. The state did not declare an emergency, because doing so, officials said, “may alert animal rights groups,” according to the messages.

Willy Lanier, a public health veterinarian with the state health department, told colleagues to keep the outbreak under “closer hold” than outbreaks of bird flu on poultry and dairy farms. Keeping the information confidential was consistent with state protocol, a spokesperson in his department said, adding that it was still shared with local health officials. But when ProPublica reached out to the spokesperson for the Utah County Health Department about a possible bird flu outbreak, she said, “That would be news to me.”

Price is so deferential to the family that owns the farm that she has not set foot on the property.

“We might get lucky, and a response that is not robust may not have dire consequences. But we may get unlucky.”

“I’m really the only one here at this facility that’s been dealing with the producer. They’re a very private bunch,” Price said. “A lot of what I’m going off of is what I’ve seen from Google Maps, what I’ve talked to the producer about, what I’ve talked to the vet about. I haven’t felt the need to go out there based on those conversations.”

She said she “became very comfortable” in conversations with the owner that the virus wouldn’t spread off the farm. It doesn’t have animals moving on and off over the course of the year like on a dairy or poultry farm; mink leave only when they’re slaughtered and skinned for pelts at the end of the year. She also said that she was confident the owner would recapture any escaped mink.

Short of a formal quarantine, Price gave the farm a “verbal hold” to not move any mink, or manure or carcasses, off the farm. “We haven’t seen spread off of this farm. We have not had additional cases in Utah that are linked to this, so thus far the verbal hold orders and the measures that have been taken look to be successful: There is not spread happening from this farm from this case.”

Price’s assertion didn’t allay the concerns of experts that ProPublica spoke with. When poultry test positive for this strain of bird flu, all nearby farms are quarantined and tested for the virus. But the approach in Utah relies on farms reporting cases or the public calling in reports of dead birds in the area.

“If you’re not looking for something, you won’t find it,” Sorrell said. “Unless you’re doing active surveillance, you can’t claim that you haven’t had cases.”

Fenn told ProPublica his conversations with state officials made him less worried about any kind of spread. When the outbreak started, Fenn said, he asked whether the bird flu could infect people.

“Right off the bat, the state people we reported to, they said you don’t need to worry too much,” Fenn said. “My guys wear masks every day. They have glasses they can wear,” Fenn said. “ Me? I don’t wear it at all. Never have, never will, because I’ve been doing it for 40 years.”

Fenn said he wished he could vaccinate the mink for bird flu to keep the rest of his animals from catching it and dying; Price asked the USDA about a vaccine that had been used to save endangered wild species but said she got nowhere with that request. The USDA told ProPublica its division responsible for protecting animal health has no “species-specific regulatory authority or dedicated programs” for vaccinating mink, depopulating them or paying owners back for such a culling.

Fenn told ProPublica he thinks concerns about mink as a particularly risky bird flu host are trumped up by anti-fur activists, the same forces that he believed were behind the reactions in Spain and Finland, which depopulated mink farms hit with bird flu. “Europeans are a little scared of everything,” he said. “They’re going to do what they’re going to do, and here in the United States, we’re going to do what we’re going to do.”

Price said she was aware that Finland and Spain had depopulated their fur farms when the same strain infected them. “I didn’t really look much further into it,” she said, explaining that she was interested in those cases only for lessons about how to handle the outbreak, but not the particular reasons those countries chose to depopulate the farms.

A USDA spokesperson said Congress would have to specifically fund a program to depopulate mink. (The program to cull poultry to stop the spread of bird flu has cost well over $1 billion since 2022.)

The events in Utah are a “clear warning sign” about the dangers of mink farms, said Neil Vora, executive director for the Preventing Pandemics at the Source Coalition.

“Fur farms are to produce pelts purely for luxury goods, to make fancy coats,” he said. “It’s absurd that we’re willing to gamble with the lives of millions of people just to keep an industry alive.”

The industry has struggled as American consumers increasingly reject fur. Though domestic farms peaked about a decade ago, selling luxury pelts that often ended up in China, foreign farms have sprung up, threatening the business.

While animal rights advocates have pushed for Congress to adopt legislation that would phase out the fur industry, buying out farmers over the course of a year to end the practice once and for all, winds in Congress are blowing the other direction. The Senate will soon weigh in on the latest Farm Bill, which now seeks to expand market opportunities for mink farmers by undoing a prohibition in federal law on subsidizing the fur trade.

The virus continues to spread on the Utah farm, said Fenn, though at a slower pace. Experts told ProPublica it still poses a risk.

“I would want a full on hazmat suit if I were on that farm,” Peacock said. “Or, preferably, I’d rather not to be on that farm.”

Price’s assertion didn’t allay the concerns of experts that ProPublica spoke with. When poultry test positive for this strain of bird flu, all nearby farms are quarantined and tested for the virus. But the approach in Utah relies on farms reporting cases or the public calling in reports of dead birds in the area.

“If you’re not looking for something, you won’t find it,” Sorrell said. “Unless you’re doing active surveillance, you can’t claim that you haven’t had cases.”

Fenn told ProPublica his conversations with state officials made him less worried about any kind of spread. When the outbreak started, Fenn said, he asked whether the bird flu could infect people.

“Right off the bat, the state people we reported to, they said you don’t need to worry too much,” Fenn said. “My guys wear masks every day. They have glasses they can wear,” Fenn said. “ Me? I don’t wear it at all. Never have, never will, because I’ve been doing it for 40 years.”

Fenn said he wished he could vaccinate the mink for bird flu to keep the rest of his animals from catching it and dying; Price asked the USDA about a vaccine that had been used to save endangered wild species but said she got nowhere with that request. The USDA told ProPublica its division responsible for protecting animal health has no “species-specific regulatory authority or dedicated programs” for vaccinating mink, depopulating them or paying owners back for such a culling.

Fenn told ProPublica he thinks concerns about mink as a particularly risky bird flu host are trumped up by anti-fur activists, the same forces that he believed were behind the reactions in Spain and Finland, which depopulated mink farms hit with bird flu. “Europeans are a little scared of everything,” he said. “They’re going to do what they’re going to do, and here in the United States, we’re going to do what we’re going to do.”

Price said she was aware that Finland and Spain had depopulated their fur farms when the same strain infected them. “I didn’t really look much further into it,” she said, explaining that she was interested in those cases only for lessons about how to handle the outbreak, but not the particular reasons those countries chose to depopulate the farms.

A USDA spokesperson said Congress would have to specifically fund a program to depopulate mink. (The program to cull poultry to stop the spread of bird flu has cost well over $1 billion since 2022.)

The events in Utah are a “clear warning sign” about the dangers of mink farms, said Neil Vora, executive director for the Preventing Pandemics at the Source Coalition.

“Fur farms are to produce pelts purely for luxury goods, to make fancy coats,” he said. “It’s absurd that we’re willing to gamble with the lives of millions of people just to keep an industry alive.”

The industry has struggled as American consumers increasingly reject fur. Though domestic farms peaked about a decade ago, selling luxury pelts that often ended up in China, foreign farms have sprung up, threatening the business.

While animal rights advocates have pushed for Congress to adopt legislation that would phase out the fur industry, buying out farmers over the course of a year to end the practice once and for all, winds in Congress are blowing the other direction. The Senate will soon weigh in on the latest Farm Bill, which now seeks to expand market opportunities for mink farmers by undoing a prohibition in federal law on subsidizing the fur trade.

The virus continues to spread on the Utah farm, said Fenn, though at a slower pace. Experts told ProPublica it still poses a risk.

“I would want a full on hazmat suit if I were on that farm,” Peacock said. “Or, preferably, I’d rather not to be on that farm.”

Why going on a 'say no' retreat is a gift to your future self

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

To free up more time in your life, writer Caroline Sylge proposes a creative idea: Give yourself a "say no" retreat. It's a day to look at your calendar and thoughtfully decline obligations in the next few weeks and months. Juan Moyano/ Moment RF/ Getty Images hide caption

This story is adapted from Life Kit's weekly newsletter. Sign up here to get expert advice on lifestyle topics like health, money, relationships and more.

How is your to-do list looking for the next few weeks? If it's already full of obligations and tasks you've been dreading, and ramping up toward the holidays, you might need a "say no" retreat.

That's one of dozens of creative ideas in Caroline Sylge's book, How to Retreat: A Guide to Stepping Away from the Everyday to Find Space for Yourself, published this spring.

A "say no" retreat is a day to brainstorm all the things you want to say no to in your life in the coming weeks and months, she says. Then, you'll spend the last part of your day saying no to a few of those things.

Sylge, who has spent the last 30 years attending and organizing health and wellness retreats around the world, developed this exercise to help people reclaim their time.

"We often say yes for reasons that have nothing to do with what's good for us, because we feel like we should, don't want to seem difficult or fear missing out," Sylge says. "Saying 'no' is uncomfortable, but is a brave act of self-respect."

Devoting a whole day to saying "no" may seem like a wild idea, but with the holidays on the horizon, it may make room in your life for the things that matter. Here's how to do it.

Before you start: Pick a day on your calendar to do your "say no" retreat. That might mean calling out of work or moving obligations. Then find a comfy place in your home to reflect.

Right when you wake up, make a list of things you want to say no to in your life. What immediately comes to mind? Name anything obvious you don't want to do. For example, coordinating a big potluck for your book club or making a complicated dish for that dinner party on Saturday.

Then eat some breakfast and get yourself into nature to give yourself some time to think about your list. Maybe take a bike ride or a walk.

After your break, come back to your designated retreat space and build on your list. Add to that initial "say no" list by thinking about your upcoming days, weeks and months. Look at your digital calendar if you need to, but make sure your notifications are turned off, Sylge writes in her book.

Review all your requests, appointments, volunteer work, scheduled phone calls, work and social commitments, and memberships.

Is there anything you've planned that you're dreading? Are there tasks you've given yourself that you don't actually have to do or even want to do?

That might be sitting at a time-consuming hair appointment, hosting a playdate at your house, traveling to spend Thanksgiving with your husband's family or moderating a panel discussion for a work event.

Jot down notes — and if you'd like, reasons why you are saying no to a task.

Take lunch, then spend the afternoon reviewing your list — and take action. It's time to start saying no. You probably won't be able to get to all the nos on your list today, but you can pick a small, easy win, Sylge says. Maybe you just cross the arduous task of planting tulip bulbs off your to-do list … forever. You can just enjoy your neighbor's tulips next spring.

Now, you'll also need to start thinking about the harder nos — the ones that involve saying no to someone you care about or work with.

Sylge says instead of saying no outright, you can try delaying your yes. For example, "I'd love to, but can we plan it for a few weeks later?" You could also suggest alternatives. Instead of cooking for your friends, could you order takeout?

Don't underestimate the power of "crafting a kind decline," Sylge says. It "takes effort, but it saves time in the long run."

In the biotech world, you can’t see everything

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

A reporter’s unsettling takeaway from You Can See Everything.

Earlier this week, I attended a screening of You Can See Everything, the buzzy new documentary about the disgraced former biotech CEO Elizabeth Holmes.

I went in not really knowing what to expect. I’d avoided spoilers (and won’t be giving anything away here, either!). I was wondering what new information this film could reveal, given how familiar many of us are with the Theranos story thanks to years of media coverage, the best-selling book Bad Blood, and the Hulu series The Dropout, starring Amanda Seyfried.

Like many others who have seen the film, I came away feeling unsettled. But not just about Holmes’s fraud and the somewhat disturbing interactions between the subjects. The film reignited a concern I’ve had about covering science for most of my career: Despite our best efforts, we never can actually see everything.

In case you’re rusty on the details, Elizabeth Holmes dropped out of Stanford University in 2003 to run Theranos, a diagnostics company she founded, when she was just 19. She went on to claim that her company had invented a way to run over 100 diagnostic tests on just a few drops of blood.

Holmes raised hundreds of millions of dollars for Theranos, which was valued at $9 billion in 2014. Forbes named her the youngest self-made female billionaire—she was worth $4.5 billion when she was just 30 years old.

Things started to unravel in 2015, when an investigation by the Wall Street Journal raised concerns by former Theranos employees that the tests didn’t work. Some also said that the company’s device had not been tested properly, and that blood samples had been analyzed on machines from other companies instead. MIT Technology Review named it one of the biggest technology failures of 2015.

Forbes had revised Holmes’s worth to $0 by 2016. That year, a damning government report said that the company’s lab was struggling with basic procedures and gave patients results even when they knew they could be wrong.

In 2018, Theranos, Holmes, and former company president Ramesh Balwani were charged with “massive fraud.” In 2022, she was sentenced to a little over 11 years in prison for defrauding investors. She began serving her sentence in May 2023.

It was around a month before this that filmmakers Nathan Fielder, Lance Oppenheim, and their colleagues began documenting Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, at home with their two young children. I’m not giving anything away when I say it’s an extremely intimate portrayal—at least of how the two adults interact with Fielder, who is also a central character in the film.

As you might know if you’ve seen the trailer, there are plenty of super close-ups. I felt as if I spent most of the film staring straight into the faces of those people. And almost the whole way through, they all seemed to be trying to figure each other out. Holmes is known for her dishonesty, but she invited cameras into her home in an apparent bid to reframe the narrative and position herself as an honest person with an unusual mind. Will anyone believe her?

I couldn’t help feeling some empathy for Holmes, a mother we see preparing to leave her two kids for a lengthy prison sentence. And even though I have long known the facts, Holmes is clearly an effective manipulator. You can see her plant the seeds of doubt in Fielder’s mind. At times, I felt she was convincing me, sitting in my cinema seat. Maybe she really didn’t mean to lie?

But then I remembered how other journalists felt after being taken in by her claims. “I blew it,” Fortune’s Roger Parloff reflected in 2015, a year after profiling Holmes and her company. “I’m pretty sure she lied to me,” STAT’s Matthew Herper wrote recently, having interviewed Holmes in 2015. Even the former employee turned whistleblower Tyler Shultz, who knew the product didn’t work, said that he could “have a five-minute conversation with her and walk away feeling like my work was changing the world,” he said in 2022.

I didn’t cover Theranos in the 2010s. If I’d interviewed Holmes, would I have fallen for it all?

One of the early red flags in the Theranos case was the lack of published, peer-reviewed data. Much of the company’s work was classified as a “trade secret.” Scientists tend to publish their work in journals—a slow process that involves having the work evaluated by often anonymous experts in the field, who provide feedback, highlight questionable conclusions, and often ask for clarification. Peer review is meant to add rigor to the publication process. You should be able to trust published scientific papers.

Scientists aren’t perfect. Some will exaggerate their results or inflate their importance. Some manipulate data to serve a hypothesis. Sometimes these infractions are caught during peer review, but not always. There are plenty of examples of scientific findings that could never be replicated by other labs and ended up collapsing under the weight of real evidence. Many papers ultimately end up with corrections. Some are withdrawn—10,000 research papers were retracted in 2023.

At any rate, there’s been a shift to sharing research in other ways. Today, many scientists opt to publish their work at preprint servers like arXiv, medRxiv, and bioRxiv before they submit them to established journals. Those outlets bypass peer review. So do company press releases, social media posts, and many conference presentations. And I get a lot of my stories from conversations and interviews.

I’ll admit I’ve looked at a scientific finding and thought: A scientist has just said they’ve done that. How can I be sure they really have?

I like to think that I hold myself to high journalistic standards here, as does everyone at MIT Technology Review. I would never publish a story about some new scientific development without doing my own form of peer review, and asking scientists—ideally people I know and trust—what they think of a new claim.

But the film is a stark reminder of how easy it can be to fall for an appealing narrative. While most scientists are, I hope, pretty honest, and Holmes is certainly an outlier, we should all continue to interrogate the claims being fed to us. If you’ve seen the film, let me know what you think!
