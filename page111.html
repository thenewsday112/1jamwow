Former Tory candidate suspended over contract killer claims was adviser in Kemi Badenoch’s office

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sources close to Tory leader say Jack Ross, who faces party investigation after undercover report, worked as social media consultant

A former Conservative election candidate who was suspended after he was filmed discussing the cost of hiring contract killers worked as a consultant in Kemi Badenoch’s office as recently as last year.

Jack Ross is facing an investigation by the Tories after he was filmed apparently asking an undercover reporter: “What do you want done?” and apparently offering to “broker” the services of an alleged far-right paramilitary group.

Police in Scotland say they will be reviewing an Al Jazeera documentary in which Ross was filmed making a range comments about what a contract killer could be employed to do, and talked about using far-right groups to create disruption on the streets.

It has now emerged that he was working in 2025 in the leader of the opposition’s office, which can draw on taxpayer funds to pay staff. By that point, Ross was CEO of Turning Point UK, which was already involving itself in anti-migrant street protests, and listed its chief operating officer as Nick Tenconi, the leader of Ukip.

Sources close to Badenoch said Ross was contracted as a junior social media external consultant in her office. However, a Tory staffer familiar with the role of Ross at that time said: “Jack would come and go in the office and a few people raised their eyebrows, but it was brushed under the carpet because he got the party clicks on socials.

“He would have been in the same room as Kemi and she would have been aware of him. Some MPs were uncomfortable about it, but senior figures were clear that social media clicks were more important.”

Ross was reported by Al Jazeera to have introduced an undercover reporter to a violent criminal from Glasgow with links to the paramilitary Ulster Volunteer Force, who offered to have people killed for money.

Al Jazeera approached Ross with a fictitious family problem where a deceased uncle’s business was at risk of being taken over by a gang, whose members were supposedly warned they would “get fucked” if anything happened.

Ross replied to this saying: “Yeah, you have to deliver on that as well. You’ve got to explain to me what’s needed … I can then go and broker from there. But I’ve got to say, even to myself … if there’s lashback I’ve got to keep my name out of it.”

On another occasion, Ross was filmed apparently asking an undercover reporter “What do you want done?” before listing the type of violent options that could be bought.

Ross appeared to tell the Al Jazeera journalist: “They can just kick the fuck out of someone. That’s a certain amount. They can start cutting people’s faces up, that’s another amount. And they can stab someone. They can shoot someone. They can kill someone.”

Ross, who stood unsuccessfully as a Conservative local election candidate, became CEO of Turning Point UK (TPUK) in 2021, the British offshoot of the US campaign group established by the late rightwing American activist Charlie Kirk. Ross became a fixture of the British rightwing scene, appearing regularly on GB News and joining the conservative thinktank the Bow Group.

TPUK was intended to be a campus-based offshoot of the US group and initially endorsed by leading Tory figures such as Priti Patel and Jacob Rees-Mogg. Early members included younger rightwingers such as Tom Harwood, who went on to become a GB News anchor.

Chain-reaction chaos threatens to unleash new risks on the world

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Eye-opening revelations this past week have solidified my personal fears about the year ahead: As a nation and as a globe, we're facing a record number of wild risks that are way outside our control.

  • My growing hunch about this scenario has been bolstered by daily conversations with CEOs, government insiders and top investors.

Why it matters: It feels likely — not certain — that between now and 2028, we'll face society-level disruptions, possibly including cyberattacks and fast-twitch economic shocks.

What we're facing: We've navigated single-shot crises like the financial meltdown of 2008 and the pandemic of 2020. This next year will be different. The real risk is a chain reaction: One shock sets off others — faster than we can process, and caused by AI dynamics we don't fully understand.

  • Call it chain-reaction chaos.
  • And that's against the backdrop of this age of post-processable velocity: The speed of change exceeds the ability of any individual, company, market or government to fully absorb, understand and respond.

The reason this scenario looks so likely is that so many obvious risks, all with relatively high probabilities, keep piling up. Any one of them will rock already highly volatile markets and politics:

  1. Cyber chaos. This past week was a big flashing siren. AI-detected flaws in PHP, the code behind millions of websites, knocked government sites offline worldwide and prompted the National Security Agency to warn organizations to patch immediately or go dark. Major banks found unauthorized AI agents inside customer accounts, some trying to take actions customers never asked for. And Anthropic's deputy chief information security officer, Jason Clinton, warned that as bad as the past several days had been, cyber threats will get "a lot worse before they get better." Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff quickly agreed, writing on X: "Code red." As Madison Mills reported for Axios, the AI companies and the banks are scrambling for "day after" planning, fully anticipating a bigger cyber hit in coming months. People at top AI companies tell me the chances are far north of 50%.
  2. AI bubble bursts (partially). This doesn't feel like 1999, since giant companies with huge balance sheets are underwriting the AI boom. But that's also the problem. They're increasingly doing it with borrowed money: Goldman Sachs expects about a third of Big Tech's AI capital expenditure to be debt-financed this year. A bubble doesn't need to burst spectacularly to cause widespread economic and political pain. These companies are deeply intertwined, investing in, selling to and buying from each other. Look what happened with Meta this past week: It crushed its earnings, but then spooked the market by detailing the difficulties of raising capital to fund AI. Any sign of overbuilding … or companies underusing AI … or China catching up … or AI prices collapsing … or credit costs spiking … or the political backlash intensifying, and markets could tumble fast. A sharp correction feels like a 50%+ scenario in the short term.
  3. AI hits true RSI. AI could get better, faster than almost anyone anticipates via recursive self-improvement (RSI). People inside Anthropic, OpenAI and Google see this unfolding now. This could be awesome long-term if we steer the technology smartly. But it could also prove wildly disruptive in 2027: More advanced AI would pose a more urgent threat to industries, companies and jobs. You could easily see this enticing Democrats to join Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) in demanding a slowdown or full pause, which would rattle markets.
  4. War expands, oil spikes. President Trump's aides believe he's likely to resume the Iran war postelection. The Iranians warn they would expand their targets in retaliation. The U.S. reduced staffing at embassies throughout the Middle East amid increased threats. Escalation feels more likely than not at the very moment oil supplies are tight and gas and diesel prices are very high. Imagine the economic pain of sustained $120-a-barrel oil.
  5. An aging, unrestrained president. I want to be clear here: This isn't a political statement or Trump health appraisal. I'd write the same for Joe Biden. It's a simple, fact-based observation based on actuarial tables. Trump, age 80, heads into the next two years as the oldest person ever to be president. It's a medical certainty that the chances of mental decline and incapacitation rise dramatically in your 80s. Trump is much like Biden in that he doesn't have people around him who can level with him. The risk stretches from health episodes to judgments about war and the limits of presidential power.

What you can do: I spell this out not to scare you or cause you to throw up your hands and retreat from reality.

  • You can only make wise decisions for your career, your life and your family if you're living in the future — understanding what's coming, and living these historic times with your head in the game.
  • The expert journalists of Axios are working hour by hour to be your clinical, useful guide to this head-spinning era.

Three very practical steps:

  1. Be aware: This is an exciting time to be alive. So learn about AI — the good, bad and ugly. And think more deeply about what you expect from your employer and elected representatives.
  2. Be vigilant: This past week's Axios reporting about AI agents showed the importance of locking down your passwords. Put every account in a secure password manager with a unique password. Use passkeys where offered. Protect your email and phone with an authenticator app, since whoever controls those can reset everything else. And don't hand a password or login code to anyone who contacts you, human or AI agent.
  3. Be ready: This past week also showed how AI is the market. So if you have a 401(k) or other investments, make sure to understand how concentrated you are in different sectors. Seek out smart diversification strategies. And find a few news sources, like Axios, that you can trust to make sense of all the craziness.

The bottom line: It's easy to argue any one of these risks won't materialize. But when you add up this many, the overall chance of a crisis outside our direct control is spiking by the day.

Saudi Arabia says it intercepted missiles and drones from Iran-backed Houthis

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Saudi Arabia intercepted drones and missiles headed for several cities, including the capital Riyadh, fired by Iran-aligned Houthis on Sunday, the Saudi-led coalition in Yemen said.

The ongoing fighting between Saudi Arabia and the Yemen-based Houthis, combined with remarks by President Donald Trump last week suggesting that the U.S. and Israel are planning fresh strikes on Iran after the Nov. 3 midterm elections, drove oil prices to a six-month high Friday.

The official Saudi Press Agency quoted the spokesperson of the Saudi-led Coalition to Restore Legitimacy in Yemen, Turki Al-Maliki, as saying two drones and a ballistic missile were "intercepted and destroyed" as they headed toward Riyadh.

The agency quoted Al-Maliki as saying that 10 ballistic missiles launched by the Houthis toward the southern region of Saudi Arabia were also intercepted, as was a cruise missile headed for the city of Khamis Mushait.

The Houthis said in a Telegram post that they "carried out seven qualitative military operations using a number of ballistic missiles and drones."

The targets included Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport and oil giant Saudi Aramco's facilities in Yanbu on Saudi Arabia's west coast as well as other airports and energy installations around the country, the Houthis said.

King Khalid International Airport said in a notice to travelers that it was resuming operations Sunday, using its Terminal 1 for domestic flights and Terminal 2 for overseas routes.

The airport suspended operations on Oct. 10 following a strike that killed at least 13 people and injured around 300 others, making it the deadliest attack on Saudi Arabia in more than a decade.

Saudi Aramco did not immediately respond to CNBC's request for comment.

While the Houthis and Saudi Arabia have been exchanging strikes in recent weeks, concerns have been mounting among investors that the U.S. could soon resume its attacks on Iran.

The U.S. ordered non-emergency government employees and their families to leave Saudi Arabia last week amid the Houthi attacks.

Trump said Wednesday that the U.S. was not negotiating with Iran and something would happen "fairly soon."

The president's cryptic remarks came as U.S. officials told The New York Times that the U.S. and Israel have plans for fresh strikes on Iran after the midterm elections.

"I think there's more and more fears of escalation in the Middle East," Amrita Sen, Founder and Director of Energy Aspects, told CNBC's Squawk Box Europe on Friday.

"We keep getting asked by clients: 'Is it going to be before the midterms? Is it after the midterms?' So it's not a question of 'if.' People are asking if it's a question of 'when.' Our view remains that it's more likely after the midterms than before."

Rising tensions, worries about supply disruptions and concerns about possible fresh U.S.-Israeli strikes on Iran helped push Brent crude to a six-month high Friday.

Brent futures gained 3.1% to close at $116.72 a barrel, the highest international benchmark settlement since April 29. U.S. West Texas Intermediate added 3.8% to settle at $103.27 a barrel. Brent added 6.2% for the week, the third consecutive week of gains. WTI advanced 2.3% for the week.

I love spending time with my parents and siblings. Our partners and kids aren't invited.

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When my mom was younger and my siblings and I moved out, she really struggled with empty-nest syndrome. Now that I'm a mom of three myself, I can totally appreciate how she must have felt.

As a parent, you pour so much of your love, time, and energy into your kids. And then one day, they leave. I imagine it must feel incredibly strange. I already dread the day my babies leave the nest, to the point where I sometimes cry to my husband about it.

The other day, I was having one of those moments, sobbing on the bed, when my husband found me. I told him how I felt, and he said, "For God's sake, honey, our youngest is four! You have at least another 14 years with her at home." Fair point.

Now that my siblings and I all have families of our own, the time we have with my folks is often chaotic. Inevitably, one of the grandkids is throwing a tantrum, complaining about being hungry, or announcing they need the toilet, and conversations are often start-stop affairs.

But a few years ago, I had an epiphany. It was my sister's 50th birthday, and we were in Bali with all the extended family. To celebrate, we went out for dinner, just the original six of us — Mom, Dad, and the four of us siblings.

It was one of the most incredible nights. We told stories from childhood, talked about getting older, and shared what actually makes us happy and what we love about each other. There were tears and plenty of laughter.

Afterwards, I realized how much I'd missed my original people. The ones who've known me the longest. The people who remember what I was like as a naughty four-year-old who would scream the F-word in the supermarket if I didn't get lollies, or as a pimply teenager going through puberty. The people who knew me before I had a husband, children, a career, a mortgage, responsibilities, or any real idea of who I was or where life would take me.

As adults, we inevitably morph into different versions of our younger selves. We form new identities, build our own lives and our own ideas.

But there's something so incredibly beautiful about being able to return to those younger versions of yourself, if only for a few hours. For me, I've always been the baby of the family, and somehow, if I'm alone with my parents and siblings, I revert straight back into that role. Goo goo, ga ga. Even at 41, I'll always be the baby of the family.

l'essentiel Catherine et Jacques Saint-Martin, deux artistes plasticiens, présentent une exposition joyeuse et poétique à la boutique Minibout jusqu'à la fin du mois de décembre à Foix.

Pour la quatrième année consécutive et pour les trois derniers mois de l’année, le couple d’artistes, Catherine et Jacques Saint-Martin, expose ses œuvres. Le public pourra à nouveau apprécier la richesse et la beauté de leurs créations. Les talents de chacun se mêlent de façon harmonieuse avec les tableaux en tissu de Catherine ainsi que les peintures brodées et les sculptures en verre de Jacques avec, comme trait d’union, une flamboyance de couleurs, de légèreté et de gaieté pour l’ensemble de leurs œuvres.

Ces deux retraités habitent à Ganac et se consacrent désormais totalement à leur art. Catherine, qui était psychologue, a toujours aimé le textile, qu’elle a sublimé en créant des tableaux. Avec des tissus de différentes couleurs et matières, elle compose et crée ce qu’elle nomme des architextures, qui sont souvent des maisons imprégnées de cultures orientales. Les morceaux de tissus sont assemblés minutieusement de façon harmonieuse.

Elle les coud en y insérant des perles, des pièces de verre, des sequins pour ensuite les fixer sur des formes découpées en bois de différents formats. Il en ressort beaucoup de poésie, d’exotisme et d’invitation au voyage. Les créations de Jacques se distinguent par leur diversité. Toujours imprégné par son premier métier de chercheur, il en a gardé l’attrait pour la découverte. Ainsi, ses créations artistiques se sont ensuite orientées vers des peintures brodées sur des tissus en coton peints auparavant. Puis, après un stage auprès d’un vitrailliste aux Forges de Pyrène, il s’est tourné vers l’art du vitrail pour réaliser des montages à partir de plaques de verre colorées, aux formes multiples, soit à accrocher au mur, soit à suspendre pour en révéler la dimension spatiale.

Très inspiré et même fasciné par les artistes tels Paul Klee, Mondrian et Miro, il se dit nourri de leur art et l’exprime dans ses œuvres. "Tout est lié en jouant autant avec la lumière, le volume, la fluidité, la légèreté et la couleur".

Scientists reveal the hidden danger of missing blood pressure medication

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Missing doses of blood pressure medication could significantly reduce its ability to protect against heart attacks, strokes, and other serious cardiovascular problems, according to findings presented at ESC Congress 2026.

An estimated 1.4 billion adults worldwide between the ages of 30 and 79 have high blood pressure (hypertension), a major contributor to illness and death. Although medications that lower blood pressure are proven to reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications, approximately half of patients do not take their prescriptions as directed.

"We have evidence that poor adherence to antihypertensive medication is associated with worse outcomes," explained presenter, Miss Qianqian Yang from the University of Oxford, UK. "However, previous data has come from observational studies and could be confounded by other factors. We undertook an analysis of randomized trial data to assess the true impact of adherence on the benefits of antihypertensive treatment."

Major Study Examines the Impact of Missed Blood Pressure Medication

To better understand how consistently taking medication affects its benefits, researchers analyzed individual patient data from 91,339 participants across nine clinical trials. Each trial compared a blood pressure treatment (intervention group) with an alternative approach (comparator group: either placebo or a less-intensive regimen).

The researchers divided participants into two categories based on how closely they followed their prescribed treatment. Those who took at least 80% of their assigned medication were considered to have higher adherence, while those who took less than 80% were placed in the lower adherence group.

Even with the regular monitoring and structured conditions of clinical trials, patients became less consistent about taking their medication as time passed.

"A decline in adherence over time was apparent, even in the structured setting of trials," noted Miss Yang. Overall, 88% of participants met the higher adherence threshold during the first year, but that figure had dropped to just 79% by the fifth year.

Skipping Blood Pressure Pills Reduces Treatment Benefits

The difference in medication adherence was reflected in how much patients' blood pressure improved.

Among participants with higher adherence, systolic blood pressure, the top number in a blood pressure reading, was 5.2 mmHg lower in the intervention group than in the comparator group. For those with lower adherence, the difference was considerably smaller at just 3.0 mmHg.

Researchers then examined whether these differences translated into protection against major cardiovascular problems. The outcomes included strokes, heart attacks (myocardial infarction), ischemic heart disease, and heart failure that resulted in hospitalization or death.

The findings revealed a striking contrast. Among patients who consistently followed their treatment plans, the intervention group experienced an 11% lower incidence of major cardiovascular events compared with the comparator group. However, among patients with lower adherence, there was no statistically significant reduction in these events.

"Notably, our results reinforce the saying that 'drugs don't work in patients who don't take them.' Patients with lower adherence derived little or no cardiovascular benefit, whereas those with higher adherence had greater reductions in blood pressure and prevention of cardiovascular events," said Miss Yang.

Helping Patients Get Lifesaving Heart Protection

The researchers believe that helping people take their medication consistently should become a greater priority in the treatment of high blood pressure. They recommend more routine assessments of medication adherence, along with better patient education and practical strategies to make treatment easier to follow.

Possible approaches include simplifying medication schedules, combining multiple treatments into a single pill, and prescribing longer-acting drugs that can help limit the consequences of an occasional missed dose.

Professor Felix Mahfoud, Chair of the ESC Communication Committee, emphasized that missed medication has received too little attention as a contributor to poorly controlled blood pressure.

"For years, we have neglected non-adherence to medication as a cause for uncontrolled hypertension. Assessment of adherence should become a routine part of hypertension care so patients do not miss out on life-saving benefits. As healthcare providers, we are here to have open, non-judgmental conversations with our patients about any barriers they may have to taking medication, to help improve adherence."

She voted for Trump every time. Then came cancer and the bills.

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

McALLEN, Texas (AP) — The Ford Expedition rumbled to a stop and Cindy Banda's bedazzled flip-flops landed on the asphalt of the Houston cancer center's vast parking lot. She threaded through the rows of cars, a tally of patients who gather here to sit with chemotherapy needles or lie on radiation machines.

The elevator dinged open. A year ago in this building, surgeons removed Banda's right breast, and she still didn't know whether treatment had expunged the cancer from her body. Doctors had told her it would be tough to beat.

"I'm more of a positive person myself," Banda said, now sitting with a white wristband in a dim waiting room. But the bills are harder to dismiss, and the single mother with six children in her care repeated something like a mantra. "Keep the house. Keep the cars. And just keep the kids stable."

That's true for many Americans, as rising healthcare costs leave more families with a sinking feeling after seeing the bill for surgery or medication. Heading into the Nov. 3 midterm elections, about half of registered voters said healthcare costs are a major problem for them and their families, up from a little more than one-third four years ago, according to a Fox News poll from September.

With party control of Congress at stake, registered voters are nearly twice as likely to say they trust Democrats over Republicans to handle healthcare, according to an Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research poll from the same month. Those frustrations, combined with broader concerns over rising prices, have sapped support for President Donald Trump and his Republican Party.

Banda knows how that feels. She backed Trump in each of his three campaigns, motivated by her opposition to abortion. But when cancer came, the ensuing scramble to make ends meet was painful enough to rearrange her priorities, and she started to think about voting Democratic for the first time.

The 46-year-old was ushered into a sterile room for her appointment, her phone lit up with a text. "Please contact TD Auto Finance."

"This one's $1,300," she said of the car payment bill. "I have to go back to work."

She had been on sick leave from her job selling internet service for one year, and she needed the income to support her three kids and three more she had taken in after her sister's death.

The doctor said her reconstruction surgery could be scheduled early next year. "I was hoping for maybe December," Banda said, "only because I've met my deductibles." Come January, she would have to pay down the $7,500 again. Earlier may be possible, the doctor replied, if the skin around her surgical scar loosens.

The evening had cooled slightly by the time she walked back through the rows of parked cars, their ebb and flow marking the tide of sickness, survival and death in a small corner of a colossal healthcare industry that saves lives while taking in over $5 trillion every year.

Two weeks later, Banda was back at work, making door-to-door rounds to sell Spectrum's Wi-Fi in McAllen, her city along the U.S.-Mexico border in the Rio Grande Valley. Banda, holding blue flyers, walked along a row of storefronts, some that shuttered since she last was in the area.

She hadn't had a sale and commission since October of last year, after the biopsy and diagnosis.

The doctor who gave her the news had hugged Banda, and she sobbed even though she never really sobs in front of other people. During chemo, her children took turns buzzing off her wispy hair. When her 9-year-old daughter's hand quivered, a teenage cousin helped guide the clipper over her mother's scalp.

Banda's first impulse was to maintain normalcy, stability, for the children.

But that requires roughly $7,000 a month for utilities, mortgage, car payments and groceries, along with the two water leaks. Then, even with insurance through her job, the surgeries, chemo and radiation added up to over $25,000 that she's been paying off, making Banda one of tens of millions of other people in the United States in medical debt.

Banda tries not to dwell on death and debt.

"It'll consume you," she says. She smothers those thoughts beneath her bubbly demeanor and avoids being alone. But that truce, between her insistence on positivity and the fears that it covers, is brittle.

Certain thoughts still arrive uninvited. Banda's late sister, Melissa, didn't have life insurance. Banda makes sure it's a payment she never misses.

"Sometimes I think, and I shouldn't think like this, but I'm worth more gone than here," she said one day. "Obviously, the goal is not that, the goal is to beat it," she continued, "to keep the house and keep the cars."

On top of child support, the father of Banda's 9-year-old often pays $400 a week for a nanny to help with the kids. Banda's own father sent her $700 before for her recent Houston appointment, one of his many cash infusions. Her high school classmates, who she hadn't been in close touch with in decades, fundraised by selling grilled chicken, rice and beans from a roadside tent. Two GoFundMe fundraisers raised nearly $30,000 and Banda rented her late sister's house while putting her own in forbearance.

But months of insecurity takes its own, sleepless toll. She needed to sell some internet services to catch up.

After sticking the Spectrum Wi-Fi flyers onto empty storefronts, she drove to pick up her kids from school. The following day she had a morning meeting at a barbershop for what would be her first sale back at work.

A lifelong Christian, Banda had voted her anti-abortion stance in past elections. When her sister was kidnapped and murdered by her ex-husband in 2020, Banda retreated from religion. The cancer brought her back to church with an apology to God, but it also made her rethink her politics.

She voted for Trump in 2016, and his first four years seemed steady, so she voted for him again in 2020. Democratic President Joe Biden's tenure, including the tail end of a global pandemic and rising inflation, felt like bedlam, so she voted for Trump to return to the White House in 2024.

But two years later and fighting an expensive disease, she had found no respite from high prices.

So when James Talarico, a Presbyterian seminary student running for U.S. Senate as a Democrat, visited one of Banda's fundraisers — speaking about his diabetes and his work in the Texas Statehouse to lower the price of insulin — it moved her. "Imagine if he can do that for the cancer," she said.

Talarico and his Republican opponent, state Attorney General Ken Paxton, have different approaches to healthcare. Talarico wants all Americans to be able to join Medicare; Paxton proposed a $25,000 tax deduction for out-of-pocket medical expenses.

Talarico also supports a law guaranteeing abortion access nationwide, which Banda has voted against for years. But after she slid into debt to stay alive, after her grocery bills mounted and the high school had to comp the kids' soccer gear she couldn't afford, Banda's anti-abortion concerns have been superseded by costs.

"Because it's what I'm living," she said. "I'm living it."

Banda cast an early ballot for Talarico. "Never voted Democrat before today!!" she wrote in a text with blue heart emoji.

Banda had just started a new cancer-targeting medication that cost $16,000 a month, reduced to $500 by insurance. By the afternoon, she was nauseous and fatigued, unable to eat. The malaise would keep her home the next day. She would miss the meeting with the barbershop to close her first deal since getting sick, though she felt confident it was in the bag.

But Banda pushed through the discomfort one evening to join her family as they replaced the flowers at her sister's tomb.

She drove past the headstones and oak trees in the evening's slanting light, and the family gathered around Melissa Banda's tomb in an open-air mausoleum. There was no speech. Their eulogy was her father, Fidel Banda, methodically clipping and arranging the flowers for his daughter.

Banda walked along the wall of tombs. She will find out on Election Day whether the cancer is in remission or whether it's spread to her organs.

"This one's for me," she said, pointing to a blank tomb next to her sister's.

"Mom!" said her daughter with the huffiness of a 9-year-old. "Why would you say that?"

Banda smiled and added, "in a hundred years."
