Warum der digitale Schulalltag die Lehrer-Schüler-Beziehung gefährdet

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Der Artikel kritisiert die Gefährdung der Lehrer-Schüler-Beziehung durch den digitalen Schulalltag. Er kontrastiert die Befürwortung digitaler Werkzeuge mit Belegen für den Verlust kritischen Denkens und schlechteren internationalen...

Die stärkste Einwände gegen ihre Position kommen von Entscheidungsträgern, die das neue Curriculum als notwendige Anpassung an einen digitalisierten Arbeitsmarkt darstellen. Sie argumentieren, dass digitale Werkzeuge, wie adaptive Lernsoftware, in Pilotprogrammen über alle deutschen Sprachregionen hinweg die Schülerbeteiligung um bis zu 30 Prozent steigern konnten und dass Widerstand gegen Innovation Bildungsrückständigkeit gleichkäme. Allerdings weist das Beweismaterial auf ein differenzierteres Bild hin: Zwar mögen kurzfristig die Engagement-Metriken steigen, Langzeitstudien der letzten Dekade zeigen jedoch, dass Schüler in Systemen, die auf routinierte digitale Übungen setzen, um 15 Prozent schwächer in kritischen Denkvermögen abschneiden als jene in lehrerorientierten, diskussionsbasierten Umgebungen. Die Warnung der ehemaligen Lehrerin, "wir tauschen Tiefe gegen Dashboard-Metriken", gewinnt an Glaubwürdigkeit, wenn internationale Vergleichsdaten zeigen, dass finnische Schulen – bekannt für geringe Tech-Dichte – konsequent besser in Problemlöseaufgaben abschneiden als Systeme mit viel Bildschirmzeit. Das "Gegenargument" bricht unter dem Gewicht dieser vergleichenden Ergebnisse zusammen, was darauf hindeutet, dass der Trend eher ideologisch als pädagogisch fundiert ist.

Der strukturelle Preis: Lehrermangel und Klassengrössen

Der strukturelle Preis dieses Trends wird in Schweizer Klassenzimmer zunehmend sichtbar, wo Lehrermangel durchschnittliche Klassengrößen auf 28 Schüler anwachsen ließ, von 22 vor fünf Jahren. Diese Inflation untergräbt direkt das Versprechen personalisierter digitaler Unterrichtung, da eine Lehrkraft 28 individuelle Bildschirme nicht effektiv überwachen kann und gleichzeitig Klassenführung aufrechterhalten muss. Ein konkretes Beispiel taucht aus einer Grundschule in Zürich auf, wo das Verhältnis funktionaler Tablets zu Schülerinnen und Schülern im letzten Monat auf 1:4 sank und somit gemeinsame Geräte und fragmentierte Lernmomente erforderte. Die Erfahrung der ehemaligen Lehrerin veranschaulicht den Mechanismus: Wenn Technologie eingeführt wird, ohne die Personaldecke zu reduzieren, wird die versprochene "Individualisierung" zum Mythos, und Disziplinarprobleme steigen, da Schüler von impersonalen Bildschirmen ablenken lassen werden. Der Blick in die Zukunft offenbart, dass der wahre Kostenfaktor nicht das Hardware-Budget ist, sondern der Zerfall der Lehrer-Schüler-Beziehung, das Fundament für bedeutsames Lernen.

Dieser pädagogische Wandel spiegelt einen historischen Präzedenzfall wider, der Entscheidungsträgern Besorgnis bereiten sollte: die "Neu-Math"-Reform der 1960er Jahre, die abstrakte Mengenlehre über rechnerische Fluency stellte und letztlich nach einer Generation von Schülern, die mit grundlegender Rechenkunst kämpften, zurückgerollt wurde. Genauso wie diese Reform die kognitive Belastung entwickelnder Gehirne nicht berücksichtigte, riskiert der aktuelle Trend, eine Generation zu schaffen, die gut im Navigieren von Apps ist, aber defizitär in nachhaltiger Analyse. Der Vergleich ist angebracht, weil beide Bewegungen modernisieren wollten, indem sie etablierte Methoden verworfen, ohne eine robuste Evidenzbasis zu haben, und stattdessen auf Buzzwords und Investorenenthusiasmus setzten. Der Unterschied heute ist die Geschwindigkeit des Rollouts – getrieben von Risikokapital statt akademischem Konsens – was bedeutet, dass das Potenzial für eine schnelle Korrektur kleiner ist. Die Hartnäckigkeit der ehemaligen Lehrerin bezüglich "bester Methoden" ist keine Nostalgie; es ist ein Schutzmechanismus gegen das Wiederholen eines Zyklus, in dem Innovation für Fortschritt gehalten wird und die verletzlichsten Schüler den Preis zahlen.

Für Leser in der Schweiz sind die Risiken besonders hoch, bedingt durch das duale System und die Abhängigkeit von einem hochqualifizierten Arbeitskräftebestand, um den wirtschaftlichen Vorsprung zu erhalten. Das OECD hatte bereits 2023 festgestellt, dass 20 Prozent der 15-Jährigen in der Schweiz die Mindestkompetenz in Mathematik nicht erreichen – eine Zahl, die sich stetig erhöht hat, seitdem digital-first-Politiken adoptiert wurden. Der lokale Aspekt ist klar: Wenn der Trend ungehindert weitergeht, wird der qualifizierte Arbeitspool, der die schweizerische Exportmaschine antreibt, dünner und gefährdet damit sogar den Lebensstandard, den die Nation stolz ist. Darüber hinaus spüren kantonale Budgets die Belastung, da Schulen Geräte einkaufen und Software-Lizenzen warten müssen, während Lehrergehälter stagnieren. Die Belege deuten auf einen einfachen Schluss hin: Die effizienteste Nutzung von Ressourcen ist die Investition in Lehrerfortbildung und kleinere Klassengrößen, nicht in unprobierte Tech-Trends. Die Langfristigkeit verlangt, dass Schweiz ihre Bildungsgrundlagen schützt, denn sie sind das unsichtbare Fundament ihres Wohlstands.

European energy giants declare record profits

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As European households struggled to pay their energy bills, the continent’s oil companies more than doubled their profits over the last quarter, reaping the benefits of the US-Israeli war on Iran.

Eight months to the day since American and Israeli forces began bombarding Iran, Brent crude prices sat at $115 on Thursday, up from $72 pre-war. Dated Brent, the actual on-the-spot price that purchasers are paying for Brent cargoes in the North Sea, was even higher, at $120.

This price spike has driven the cost of petrol, diesel, and heating oil to record highs across Europe. However, it’s been a windfall for Europe’s largest oil companies, according to quarterly profit statements compiled by Germany’s Der Spiegel on Thursday.

British oil and gas giant Shell recorded a profit of $12.1 billion between July and September, a more than twofold increase on the previous quarter, and on the same quarter last year. Between July and September 2025, Shell made $5.43 billion in profit.

France’s TotalEnergies increased profits by 127% to $9.1 billion in the same period, while Spain’s Repsol recorded an almost fivefold increase, from $640.7 million to $3.1 billion. Austria’s OMV, meanwhile, increased profits by 68% to $2.36 billion.

German Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil and his counterparts in Austria, Italy, Poland, Portugal, and Spain, have called for an EU-wide windfall tax on the oil giants, similar to the “solidarity levy” imposed by Brussels after the escalation of the Russia/Ukraine conflict in 2022.

“The Commission has been very reserved on this issue, although several member states have been calling for models to ​be presented for a long time,” Klingbeil said after a meeting of EU finance ministers in Ireland last month. “This must happen by the next [finance ministers’] meeting in ​October at the latest,” he added.

The European Commission shot down Klingbeil’s proposal, and October passed with no action from Brussels. “It is in the hands of member states,” EU Economic Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis told reporters. “Member states can go ahead and take those decisions on the taxing of windfall profits.”

Despite Gulf exports reportedly recovering to 80% of pre-war levels, Houthi attacks on Saudi energy facilities and shipping, the threat of naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and warnings of an “intense” new US military campaign against Iran have all played a role in keeping oil prices nearly twice as high as they were at the beginning of February.

Quebec Premier fires opening salvo at Ottawa with refusal to attend Canada-EU summit

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Quebec Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon called the federal government’s perceived snub a rupture with the Gérin-Lajoie Doctrine, a policy in which Quebec conducts its own foreign policy on matters touching its areas of jurisdiction.Christinne Muschi/The Canadian Press

By refusing to attend a Canada-European Union summit and declaring that his province was being turned into a “vassal,” Quebec Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon has fired an opening salvo in what promises to be a combative relationship with the federal government.

The Premier’s comments marked a new tone of conflict between Quebec and Ottawa weeks after the election of the separatist Parti Québécois, which is seeking to abolish the monarchy in the province and hold a referendum on independence.

After days of back-and-forth between the Premier and Prime Minister Mark Carney, Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon refused to attend the Canada-EU summit in Montreal on Thursday in protest of Ottawa’s refusal to let him speak publicly at the event.

The federal government denied the province’s request to have its leader deliver a speech and hold its own reception, instead offering a seat at the head table for the opening supper and a role greeting visiting dignitaries at the airport.

“The federal government would have us believe that the Premier of Quebec being granted permission to serve shrimp cocktails at a reception constitutes an acceptable and sufficient form of influence for representing Quebec’s interests,” said Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon in a social-media post on Tuesday.

Andrew Coyne: The speech Paul St-Pierre Plamondon never gave (or at least the Q&A)

The PQ government, which has promised to hold a referendum on Quebec independence, was elected with a minority of seats and just 28 per cent of the popular vote on Oct. 5. Though Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon has said he will not organize a vote on secession before U.S. President Donald Trump leaves the White House in 2029, he has already taken steps to demonstrate his continued commitment to sovereignty, including by naming a minister responsible for independence to his cabinet.

This week, the Premier called the federal government’s perceived snub a rupture with the Gérin-Lajoie Doctrine, a policy in which Quebec conducts its own foreign policy on matters touching its areas of jurisdiction, such as education and culture.

Announcing his decision to pull out of the summit on Wednesday, Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon said on social media that the invitation to sit at the head table was an effective request for Quebec “to get back in line, with the vassalization and invisibilization of Quebec which that implies, in exchange for obtaining better seats to watch the party.”

Mr. Carney said earlier this week that it was the Premier’s decision to leave an “empty chair.”

“He was invited to the dinner and he refused,” the Prime Minister said.

Konrad Yakabuski: Quebec’s new PQ cabinet is shaped with a referendum in mind

Asked about the prospect of a more challenging relationship with Quebec under a PQ government, Mr. Carney said Thursday that he would continue to practise “co-operative federalism” with the provinces.

“That means working together on the priorities of Canadians,” he told reporters at a news conference in Montreal. “I’ll continue to practise that and be available to work with Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon for the interests of Quebeckers.”

Quebec Liberal Leader Charles Milliard criticized the Premier’s absence from the summit and the provincial government’s choice of replacement, newly appointed chief diplomat and former PQ deputy Pascal Paradis. Sending someone unelected to represent Quebec is insufficient, Mr. Milliard told reporters Thursday.

Every opposition party agreed that Quebec should have been allowed to speak at the summit, however. Quebec Conservative Leader Éric Duhaime said Ottawa’s refusal constituted “toxic federalism.”

Even Quebec Liberal luminaries such as former deputy premier Nathalie Normandeau and the party’s former parliamentary leader Marwah Rizqy called Mr. Carney’s decision “a serious mistake” and an “enormous lack of respect,” respectively, this week.

Paul St-Pierre Plamondon sworn in as Quebec premier, adds independence minister to cabinet

There are precedents for inviting Quebec premiers to speak at international events held in Quebec, said the public policy scholar Stéphane Paquin, such as the UN biodiversity conference in 2022.

Many of the subjects likely to be discussed between Canada and the EU this week fall under provincial jurisdiction, such as the Erasmus+ program and critical minerals, said Prof. Paquin. Quebec could still refuse to implement aspects of any treaty touching on its constitutional powers, he added.

Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon seemed to threaten as much this week when he alluded to the “risks” involved in negotiating an international agreement without Quebec’s participation.

“Politically, it would have been easier to let him speak,” Prof. Paquin said.

The Quebec Premier gave another indication of his posture toward the federal government earlier this week, when he chose not to attend a first ministers’ meeting with First Nations leaders in Ottawa.

Simon-Pierre Savard-Tremblay, Quebec’s Minister for First Nations and Inuit Relations, said in a social-media statement that the Quebec government meets Indigenous nations “on a nation-to-nation” basis and “not at a table organized by Ottawa.”

Sophie Villeneuve, a political analyst and former Parti Québécois staffer, said Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon is setting the tone for future discussions with the federal government.

“Few premiers in the last 20 years have taken such a firm and clear position,” she said.

“Paul St-Pierre Plamondon intends to speak out more forcefully and to do so in a different way. We’ll see what kind of results that yields.”

Violent home invasion in affluent Toronto neighbourhood sends four to hospital

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Police work the scene of a crime at a home on Hyde Park Circle in Toronto, on Thursday, where three people were shot and one person was injured with a hammer.Sammy Kogan/The Globe and Mail

An attack that police said began as a random home invasion in Toronto’s exclusive Bridle Path neighbourhood left three family members with gunshot wounds and another with injuries from a hammer.

Toronto police said that shortly after 3 a.m. on Thursday, three masked gunmen stormed into the Hyde Park Circle house through its back entrance while a family of four were inside.

A confrontation erupted in the house as the assailants demanded the family give them money or other valuables.

Police told reporters near the scene Thursday morning the assailants fired their weapons and shot three family members. Each suspect was armed with a handgun, and one also had a hammer.

“There was a demand made for valuables within the home,” Toronto Police Inspector Kristy Smith of the Hold Up Squad told reporters. “The suspects fired their guns, multiple times, and one victim was struck with a hammer. Three members of the family, the father and his two daughters, sustained gunshot wounds.”

Inspector Kristy Smith of Toronto Police Services' Hold Up Squad, left, leaves after speaking to media outside the scene of a home invasion and shooting incident at a residence on Hyde Park Circle.Sammy Kogan/The Globe and Mail

The family members are all adults. Two remain in hospital. Police said they do not know what the attackers took and are hoping to recover bullet casings that may help identify the suspects.

Police said they will not publicly identify the victims, two of whom are now said to have “life-altering” injuries.

Shaken by break-ins, an affluent Toronto neighbourhood takes action

The Bridle Path is one of Canada’s richest neighbourhoods, where large houses are often hidden behind high walls and wrought-iron gates, and flanked by vehicles belonging to private security companies. Security cameras are almost everywhere. Home invasions in the neighbourhood remain rare.

Authorities said they suspect the motive in the current attack was simply greed. “Typically, in an affluent neighbourhood comes nice things – and there’s always people out there that want to acquire nice things,” Insp. Smith said.

“We can’t go around always being paranoid about having nice things,” she said. “But I would suggest that people be ultra aware if they are being followed and potentially, if they feel like they are being followed, calling 911. ”

The Bridle Path is considered one of Canada’s richest neighbourhoods, yet home invasions remain rare.Sammy Kogan/The Globe and Mail

Shootings and homicides have dramatically declined in Toronto in 2025 and 2026. But police say one crime category is increasing: The city has had 119 home invasions in 2026, up 35 per cent from the same time last year.

“Across the city we have seen the rise of carjackings decline and the home invasions increase,” Insp. Smith said.

Police have yet to identify any of the suspects in the Bridle Path attack. Insp. Smith said she had no information indicating the family was targeted for any reason other than that they live in one of Canada’s wealthiest neighbourhoods.

The Decibel: Is it legal to defend yourself if someone breaks into your home?

Earlier this year, The Globe and Mail reported that a rash of break-ins and home invasions are affecting another wealthy Toronto neighbourhood – Rosedale, where the rates for these crimes more than doubled between 2015 and 2025.

In that neighbourhood and others, residents have been hiring more security to meet an evolving threat where masked men show up at front entrances in the early morning, bodychecking doors until they give way, or smashing windows in their searches for jewellery and keys to luxury automobiles.

Thieves are also mounting battery-powered trail cameras to remotely monitor homeowners’ comings and goings, police have warned some residents.

So far in 2026, there have been 119 home invasions in Toronto.Sammy Kogan/The Globe and Mail

Toronto Mayor Olivia Chow released a statement condemning the latest attack.

“I am deeply troubled by the violent home invasion early this morning in the Bridle Path neighbourhood,” she said.

“This attack has understandably shaken the neighbourhood and many residents across Toronto. This violence is not acceptable in our city. People must be safe in their own homes.”

Von der Leyen’s Canada power play: Words fail the EU boss

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By RT newsroom, a team of multi-lingual journalists with over a decade of experience in Russian and international reporting, delivering original research and insights often missing from mainstream coverage

EU Commission President Ursula von der Leyen is stirring up trouble at home during a controversial trip to Montreal, turning what should have been a straightforward trade mission into a petty argument over Canada’s fictional “membership” in the bloc.

The EU mandarin traveled to Montreal for the 21st annual EU-Canada summit on Thursday, with her tail between her legs. A month after unilaterally promising Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney “associate member” status of the bloc, she was reportedly forced to scrub all mentions of the term and downgrade her offer to one of an “ever closer association” between the EU and Canada.

When the summit closes on Friday, both sides will issue a revised joint statement that reads: “We will create an even closer association between Canada and the EU... and work to define by the 2027 EU-Canada Summit the concept of an ever closer association of Canada to the EU, short of full membership itself,” according to a draft seen by Policito.

Von der Leyen reportedly gave neither the EU’s vast bureaucracy, nor its members’ wary leaders, any warning before she told Carney in front of the European Parliament that she “would like to work with you on opening the door for Canada to being the first associate member of the European Union.”

Unilateral decisions and boundary-overstepping are von der Leyen’s calling cards, as her fellow Eurocrats have repeatedly complained to the press. “Nobody was consulted about associate membership apart from [von der Leyen’s] closest staff, it would seem,” an unnamed diplomat told The Guardian on Wednesday. The widespread anonymous briefing to various mainstream European media made it clear that France, Germany, Greece and Italy had forced von der Leyen to adopt the new, watered-down terminology.

In her first speech in Montreal on Thursday, von der Leyen refused to adopt the new, sanitized wording. After noting that “Canada cannot become a full-fledged member of the EU because of geography,” she insisted that “we are discussing the idea of Canada becoming the first associate member of the European Union. Our idea is simple: it is an ‘association without accession’.”

Whatever form Friday’s communique will take, von der Leyen has evidently chosen to disregard the concerns of the EU’s largest member states for the sake of her own personal project. However, the difference between both statements is purely rhetorical.

Neither “Associate member” nor “ever closer association” are defined anywhere in EU law.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz attempted to define “associate membership” in May when he suggested that it be granted to Ukraine. Under his proposal, Ukraine would participate in European Council meetings without voting rights, send representatives to the European Parliament, receive more EU funding, and become party to the EU’s mutual defense clause.

The proposal was rejected by Kiev, with Ukrainian leader Vladimir Zelensky arguing that this kind of ‘membership-lite’ would leave his country “present in the European Union, but voiceless.”

“Ever closer association” is an even more meaningless term, lifted straight from ‘Ever Closer Union’, a hagiography on EU integration by the bloc’s court historian, Desmond Dinan. Writing in 1994, Dinan argued that “European integration is a good thing,” and the phrases he used – “ever closer union” and “pooling sovereignty,” for example – have since become staples of EU speeches and press releases.

US President Donald Trump ridiculed von der Leyen’s word games last month, calling the concept of associate membership “laughable,” and threatening to “put very serious tariffs or stop trading with Europe on many things” if the EU-Canada proto-alliance ever harmed US interests.

Carney – who plainly insisted last month that Canada is “not looking to become a member of the European Union” – responded diplomatically to the intra-EU squabbling. “The good news is it doesn’t matter about ⁠specific names or nomenclature,” he said ahead of the summit. “What matters is the difference it will ​make to the lives ​of Canadians.”

However, Von der Leyen’s “membership” promise and the subsequent retraction has undermined Carney in front of his opponents. In a social media post on Thursday, Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre described the concept of “associate membership” as “theater,” and dismissed the summit as “yet another grand theatrical meeting to sign a bunch of meaningless declarations filled with big words (catalyze, anyone?) and promises to meet again to talk more."

How long will this theatre go on? While Canadians can’t afford food and homes, Mr. Carney has distracted for months with big talk about “Associate Membership” with Europe. Now, Europe has rejected it. For the next two days, we will be distracted again from the cost-of-living crisis with wall-to-wall media coverage on yet another grand theatrical meeting to sign a bunch of meaningless declarations filled with big words (catalyze, anyone?) and promises to meet again to talk more. No one’s mortgage or grocery bill will come down. We already have a free trade deal with Europe, Mr. Carney. Get the 9 countries who have not ratified it to do so. Von der Leyen has far more riding on this summit than Carney. While the EU needs energy, minerals, and Canada’s geographic clout in the Arctic, Canada only needs to diversify some of its trade away from the US – which accounts for around 72% of its exports and 46% of its imports – in order to hedge against Trump’s tariffs and trade restrictions. At present, the EU accounts for less than 6% of Canada’s exports and 12% of its imports.

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A Russian network of brokers has trafficked North Korean laborers into Russia under exploitative conditions and placed some at factories supplying the war effort against Ukraine, the Hague-based group Global Rights Compliance said in a new report.

According to the report, North Korea reaps funds from the operation, taking much of the wages earned by its workers who are sent to Russia and made to stay under threat of retribution.

Global Rights Compliance documented the network run by a Russian businessman using open-source intelligence, facial recognition technology to identify North Korean officials and undercover operatives posing as prospective employers.

Legal adviser Elise Lauriot Prevost called the findings evidence of a dangerous alignment between Pyongyang's use of forced labor to fund its weapons programs and Moscow's demand for workers to power its war economy.

Neither the Russian Foreign Ministry nor the North Korean Embassy in Moscow responded to requests for comment.

The rights group said it contacted two Russians identified as key figures in the network. Only one replied and denied the accusations. Global Rights Compliance did not say if the other responded.

Russia and North Korea have boosted military and political ties since pledging renewed cooperation at a 2023 summit between leaders Vladimir Putin and Kim Jong Un, while a mutual defense pact serves as a cornerstone of a partnership treaty signed the following year.

To circumvent UN sanctions prohibiting member states from employing North Korean workers, laborers enter Russia on student visas under the pretense of university enrollment, Global Rights Compliance said.

Middlemen then contract the workers out to Russian employers, collecting commissions as high as 150,000 rubles ($1,860) per worker.

Posing as potential employers, investigators from the rights group documented North Koreans working at production facilities, including a military rations factory.

Employers force the workers to stay on the job through coercion and threats of retribution against them and their families.

One job listing assured prospective employers that workers would not "run away," the Global Rights Compliance report said. Workers are forced to undergo 12-hour shifts with just two days off per month, it added, citing the listings.

After North Korea's sixth and last nuclear test in 2017, a UN Security Council resolution banned the country's citizens from being able to work overseas.

While Russia and China, both permanent members of the Security Council, voted for that ban, Western and South Korean officials accuse them of ignoring enforcement.

With Europe’s Doors Closed, Russians Are Flocking to Vyborg for a Taste of the West

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

VYBORG, Russia — With its storybook architecture, Swedish-built castle, English landscape park and medieval town walls, you could be forgiven for thinking you were in a Nordic country.

In reality, this is Vyborg, a small city northwest of St. Petersburg whose Swedish and Finnish heritage has long drawn visitors seeking a taste of life across the border.

Now, as visa and entry restrictions imposed following Moscow’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine make it harder for Russians to travel to Europe, Vyborg is enjoying a tourism boom.

According to local authorities, annual visitor numbers rose from 1.3 million in 2022 to 1.9 million in 2025, and are projected to reach 2.1 million in 2026.

Hotel bookings for the winter holidays are up 22% from a year earlier, the Russian Union of Travel Industry said. Jousting tournaments, fairs and medieval-style treats offer visitors the atmosphere of a European Christmas without leaving Russia.

But reminders of the war in Ukraine are never far away, and tensions between Russia and NATO member Finland — whose border lies just 30 kilometers (18 miles) away — loom over life in this otherwise picturesque city.

Just a few blocks from the train station, a military recruitment billboard greets visitors with images of Soviet soldiers from World War II alongside Russian troops fighting in Ukraine.

In March, Vyborg came under Ukrainian drone attack for the first time. The roof of a historic Finnish-era building and a patrol icebreaker being built for the Federal Security Service (FSB)'s Coast Guard were reportedly damaged in the attack.

“Lately, the mobile internet sometimes goes down during [drone] attacks. But otherwise, things are pretty normal,” said a young woman working as a barista at the Penka coffee shop.

Vyborg has repeatedly changed hands over the centuries. Founded by Swedish crusaders in 1293 as a fortress on the Gulf of Finland, it was captured by Peter the Great’s forces in 1710 during the Great Northern War.

In 1812, it became part of the Grand Duchy of Finland, an autonomous territory within the Russian Empire. When Finland declared independence in 1917, Vyborg, known in Finnish as Viipuri, stayed within its borders and became the country’s second-largest city after Helsinki.

Finland ceded Vyborg to the Soviet Union in 1940 after the Winter War, which began with a Soviet invasion the previous year.

Finnish troops retook it in 1941 as Finland fought alongside Nazi Germany against the Soviet Union, but Soviet forces captured the city again in 1944. Its Finnish population was evacuated and forced to abandon their homes for good.

The displacement bears resemblance to scenes in eastern Ukraine today, where advancing Russian forces have driven tens of thousands of people from their homes.

The Soviet invasion of Finland is often downplayed in Russia’s official historical narrative.

“When Finland … handed this city over to us,” a guide leading a group of schoolchildren said, pausing as she searched for the right word, “they took away many precious artifacts from the museums. Absolutely nothing was left here.”

Traces of Vyborg’s Finnish past can still be found on its streets. A faded advertisement for the Finnish company Uuttaus Oy survives on the brick wall of an old factory.

The city’s library, designed by Finnish architect Alvar Aalto and a landmark of 1930s Nordic functionalism, was restored by Russian and Finnish specialists in a joint project completed in 2013. Its local history section contains many jointly published Russian-Finnish books.

Finnish soldiers’ graves lie in an old cemetery, while some shops still sell Finnish products brought into Russia despite sanctions.

As recently as a few years ago, it wasn’t uncommon to hear people speaking Finnish on Vyborg’s narrow streets.

Tourist cruises from Lappeenranta, Finland, to Vyborg were popular, as Finns with Vyborg roots arrived to see their ancestors’ homeland. Many Finns would also drive across the border for cheaper gasoline than they could get at home.

“We used to go to Finland to buy fish and other goods on one-day bus tours from St. Petersburg,” said Anna, 26, who asked that her surname be withheld for safety reasons. “It wasn’t hard to get a visa before 2022. The Finns welcomed us warmly.”

“It was a different world, more open and friendly,” she said.

Maria, 23, also lamented the loss of these cross-border ties.

“There are a lot of Finnish-Russian families in Lappeenranta, just across the border. And there used to be so many cars with Finnish license plates in Vyborg itself,” she said, also withholding her surname.

Maria’s parents bought a plot of land near Vyborg shortly before the full-scale invasion.

“Dad thought he’d be going to Finland to get croissants for Mom. Then the war started,” she said.

Today, Vyborg’s tourist crowd is overwhelmingly Russian. Chinese tourists arriving on day trips from St. Petersburg make up the bulk of the foreigners who do visit.

Still, some of the city’s Finnish connections remain. Anna said it’s still possible to find Finnish glassware, ceramics and other antiques in Vyborg.

This summer, she also encountered a Finnish man who was visiting to trace his family’s roots.

“He bought himself a beer, waited for the bus and headed off to look for his family home,” she said. “It was very sweet.”

These kinds of interactions have become increasingly rare as border closures and deteriorating relations have severed many connections between the two countries.

The full-scale invasion of Ukraine prompted Finland to join NATO in 2023, and Helsinki later closed its land borders with Russia over a surge in migrant arrivals that the Nordic country blamed on Moscow.

Restrictions on Russian tourists have further curtailed travel between the two countries.

Since then, tensions between the two countries have only escalated.

Four Ukrainian drones targeting Russia strayed off course and crashed in Finland in March and April. And in May, a drone alert temporarily halted flights at Helsinki Airport.

More than 5,000 troops from seven NATO countries took part in the Northern Star exercise in Finland’s Kainuu region that month, while Finland held its own Karelian Sword drills.

In September, Finland announced that it had finished constructing 200 kilometers (125 miles) of fencing along sections of its border with Russia.

Another security scare came this month, when Finnish authorities disclosed suspected intrusions at the homes of several members of parliament.

While the incidents prompted speculation about possible Russian intimidation of Finnish politicians who support military aid to Ukraine, authorities had not publicly identified those responsible or established a motive.

Despite the estrangement, Anna said she hopes the border will eventually become a place of exchange again.

“I believe that sooner or later relations will warm up, and we’ll start traveling to Finland again,” she said.

For Maria, the rupture is felt in the loss of ordinary family visits.

“We bought groceries and household appliances there. Everything was good quality. Mom also has a friend there who’s married to a Finn. But since the war started, we haven’t been able to go,” she said.

“Will I ever be able to go there again? That depends on one person whose last name starts with ‘P’,” she said.

Bangladesh nuclear plant gets power generation nod

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Bangladesh’s Rooppur Nuclear Power Plant has received the final regulatory clearance, paving the path to commence electricity generation.

Rooppur, the country’s first nuclear power plant, was given the nod to advance to Stage C by the Atomic Energy Regulatory Authority (BAERA), the Daily Star reported on Thursday.

During this stage, the reactor’s power will be increased to produce steam, boost the turbine, and generate electricity. Capacity will gradually be increased in phases.

“Now Unit 1 will proceed with the power startup to begin the generation process,” BAERA Chairman Mahamudul Hasan was quoted as saying by the news outlet. He added that the authority would determine the startup date.

Rosatom head Alexey Likhachev earlier this month told media that the plant is on track to be connected to the national grid by end of next month.

The Rooppur plant, located 160 kilometers from Dhaka on the banks of the Padma River, was built at a cost of $12.65 billion, with Russian financial and technical support.  With two VVER-1200 Russian-designed reactors, it will have a combined capacity of 2,400 megawatts. Moscow is financing $11.9 billion in state loans for the project.

Rosatom, which is providing equipment, nuclear fuel, and engineering assistance, expects to commission the second unit in 2027.

Once fully operational, the plant is expected to deliver 12% of Dhaka’s power demand.

Likhachev recently disclosed that Moscow is in talks with Dhaka to build a new phase of Rooppur’s high-capacity units.

“We are primarily talking about a possible additional phase of large units,” Likhachev said on the sidelines of the Russian Energy Week forum, adding that Dhaka was also exploring the possibility of small-capacity units and a research reactor.
