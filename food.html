Operations Plan Germany: How the Bundeswehr trains for NATO collective defense

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

How prepared is Germany for national defense? The answer lies in the Operations Plan Germany. Military exercises such as "Red Storm Charlie" are designed to put these preparations to the test in realistic scenarios.

In a residential district of Hamburg at midnight, the streets are quiet and deserted. Suddenly, the silence is shattered: Several helicopters circle the night sky, and flashing blue lights illuminate the darkness. A long convoy of vehicles moves through the streets. Soldiers stand atop armored Bundeswehr vehicles, their machine guns at the ready. For local residents, it's an unusual, perhaps even unsettling sight in an otherwise peaceful port city.

"Red Storm Charlie" is the name of the exercise that brought parts of Hamburg, northern Germany's largest city, to a standstill for three days. The Bundeswehr, police and civilian organizations were jointly testing how well-prepared they are to respond in a crisis.

The scenario centers on a deteriorating security situation on NATO's eastern flank. NATO troops, represented in the exercise by Bundeswehr soldiers, arrive at the port of Hamburg, Germany's largest seaport, before moving eastwards through the Hanseatic city.

Acts of sabotage and demonstrations, also included in the scenario, hamper their advance. With around 1,700 participants, including 500 soldiers, the exercise held in late September was the largest of its kind to date.

"Red Storm Charlie" forms part of a classified strategy known as the "Operationsplan Deutschland" (Operations Plan Germany). Through this document, the Bundeswehr is preparing for the possibility of a Russian attack on NATO territory. The Operations Plan Germany is an integral element of NATO's broader defense planning and is closely aligned with the Alliance's overall defense framework.

In the event of war or a major crisis, not only the city of Hamburg but Germany as a whole would assume a pivotal role as a logistics hub for hundreds of thousands of NATO soldiers, weapons and pieces of heavy equipment.

"This is a highly detailed and strictly classified plan that specifies exactly where NATO allies would arrive in Germany, including which ports they would use and which routes would be required for their transit across the country," security expert Aylin Matle of the German Council on Foreign Relations told DW.

The Baltic states are widely regarded as the region where NATO considers a Russian attack most likely. In such a scenario, the alliance would concentrate large troop contingents from its 32 member states there. To reach the region, soldiers from southern and western Europe would need to pass through Germany.

"In concrete terms, this means that around 800,000 soldiers and 200,000 vehicles would have to move through our country within six months. Those are the calculations," said Marcel Bohnert, a Bundeswehr officer who contributed to the first version of the Operations Plan.

Speaking with DW in a personal capacity rather than as a representative of the Bundeswehr, Bohnert explained: "We must ensure that soldiers can use our roads and ports safely. That is why we take care of all their needs: We arrange accommodation, provide food, ensure their security and guide them through our transport network."

The plan also covers transport in the opposite direction, from east to west, including the evacuation of wounded soldiers and civilians fleeing the conflict. Its development was commissioned by Bundeswehr Inspector General Carsten Breuer in the wake of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

As Germany's highest-ranking military officer, Breuer is far from alone in his concern that Russia could target additional countries. Germany is already confronting a wide range of Russian hostile activities, from cyberattacks to acts of sabotage directed against the Bundeswehr.

A new dimension of the threat emerged with the attempted attack at Leipzig/Halle Airport in early August. An explosive-laden drone was deployed there and failed to detonate only through luck. The German government holds Russia responsible.

"The threat is becoming more tangible. It is serious, acute, and increasingly visible. This is exactly what the Russian president wants. Moscow is testing our response," Defense Minister Boris Pistorius of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) told the Bundestag on September 23.

The Bundeswehr is also addressing this threat through the Operations Plan Germany, which is continuously being refined and updated. Lessons learned from exercises such as "Red Storm Charlie" are incorporated into the plan. According to Bohnert, the plan also serves as a deterrent.

"We hope to discourage Russia from further aggression by demonstrating our strength through the rapid buildup of military capabilities and the highly organized, swift deployment of forces to the borders," he said.

How well-prepared is Germany for its role as a military logistics hub? Matle identifies one major vulnerability in the country's ageing and, in some cases, deteriorating infrastructure. Germany, she argued, must address the maintenance and repair of the bridges, roads and transport routes required for troop movements "far more quickly and comprehensively."

"Some of this infrastructure dates back to the Cold War and was not designed to accommodate heavy loads or military equipment," she pointed out.

A further challenge lies in striking the right balance between confidentiality and public involvement. To guard against acts of sabotage, sensitive details such as the precise transit routes for NATO troops must not fall into Russian hands. At the same time, the plan can only succeed through close cooperation between the Bundeswehr and civilian society, including local and regional authorities, the police, and relief organizations.

"Parts of the plan must be publicly accessible because we need support from society, as well as from ministries and governments, to make it work," said Bohnert.

The debate over the confidentiality of the more than 1,000-page document recently resurfaced after the far-right Alternative for Germany(AfD) emerged as the strongest party in recent elections in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt.

Security experts expressed concern that the party, which is classified in part as right-wing extremist and is regarded as Russia-friendly, could pass information from the plan to Moscow if it were to assume the office of state premier.

The Defense Ministry has rejected these concerns, noting that no federal state is given access to the complete document. Instead, the sensitive sections of the Operations Plan are shared only in excerpts and strictly on a need-to-know basis.

According to observers, however, the growing number of hybrid threats is underscoring the need for secrecy. During the "Red Storm Charlie" exercise in Hamburg, accredited journalists were not informed of routes or locations until the very last moment because of concerns over potential disruptions and acts of sabotage.

¿Es la fibra la nueva proteína? La industria de la comida insana se apunta al ‘fibermaxxing’

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Los productores de ultraprocesados se suben a una tendencia con fundamento nutricional razonable, pero cuyo aprovechamiento comercial alimenta expectativas que no se corresponden con la realidad

¿Harto de nuevos palabros, nuevas modas, añagazas de la industria alimentaria y conceptos que hacen furor en las redes sociales? Pues atento, que vienen curvas. Ya tienes un nuevo término, estrategia comercial y punto de palanca de fantaciencia para incorporar a tu vocabulario: hola fibermaxxing, adiós hiperproteinizado. Bueno, lo más probable es que ambos términos terminen coexistiendo en una marquetiniana relación simbiótica.

El asunto, en resumen, consiste en incorporar más fibra en la dieta. Por lo menos esta vez, y a modo de excepción, el punto de partida es loable: en general, consumimos bastante menos fibra de la recomendable. Alentar hacia un mayor consumo podría aportar ciertos beneficios para la salud, empezando por el funcionamiento intestinal y extendiéndose; según el tipo y la cantidad de fibra, a otros aspectos metabólicos y fisiológicos. Se trataría de llevar a la práctica lo que los profesionales sanitarios llevamos décadas recomendando a voz en grito y parece haberse descubierto ahora, convenientemente rebautizado en inglés y convertido en #trendingtopic .

La tendencia se ha popularizado a través de vídeos en los que se muestran desayunos, comidas y tentempiés especialmente ricos en fibra, se contabilizan los gramos consumidos a lo largo del día, se señala a la microbiota como principal protagonista, se trufan los contenidos con notas científicas del tipo “ácidos grasos de cadena corta”, “butirato”, etcétera y se proponen estrategias para alcanzar determinada ingesta. Hasta aquí, nada particularmente preocupante. Al contrario, que se ponga de moda una recomendación nutricional sensata podría considerarse una buena noticia.

Los problemas –porque son varios– empiezan cuando una recomendación dirigida a mejorar el conjunto de la alimentación se convierte en una obsesión por acumular gramos de un determinado nutriente (sin que importe su procedencia). Cuando las redes sociales se llenan de reels que buscan acumular visitas diciendo sandeces; y, sobre todo, cuando la industria alimentaria descubre que puede aprovechar esta renovada fascinación por la fibra para vendernos productos que tienen bastante poco que ofrecer desde el punto de vista nutricional.

La Autoridad Europea de Seguridad Alimentaria (EFSA) considera adecuada una ingesta de 25 gramos diarios de fibra para mantener una función intestinal normal en adultos, una cantidad que buena parte de la población no alcanza. En España, según el estudio ANIBES, el consumo de fibra en todo el conjunto de adultos estudiados fue de 12,6 gramos por día, y de 15,9 gramos diarios entre quienes declararon ingestas energéticas plausibles.

Nuestra alimentación ha experimentado profundos cambios en las últimas décadas, algunos de los cuales han supuesto relegar a un segundo plano alimentos que antes tenían una presencia mucho mayor y cuyo aporte de fibra era (y es) extraordinario. Uno de los ejemplos más llamativos es el de las legumbres: en España, durante la década de los sesenta, el consumo anual rondaba los 13 kilos por habitante. Hoy apenas supera los tres; en poco más de medio siglo hemos reducido su consumo aproximadamente a la cuarta parte. No estamos hablando precisamente de un alimento nutricionalmente irrelevante. Las lentejas, garbanzos y alubias son fuentes excepcionales de fibra, aportan cantidades interesantes de proteínas vegetales, minerales y otros nutrientes y, por si fuera poco, figuran entre las opciones más económicas de nuestra despensa. Además, se prestan a infinidad de preparaciones, desde los tradicionales platos de cuchara hasta ensaladas, salteados, cremas, patés vegetales o guarniciones. Pocos alimentos ofrecen tanto por tan poco.

Otro caso singular es el de los frutos secos. Almendras, nueces, avellanas o pistachos son alimentos ricos en fibra, grasas mayoritariamente insaturadas y proteínas, por no hablar de otros micronutrientes de interés (vitaminas y minerales). Sin embargo, todavía arrastran una reputación injustamente negativa por su elevado aporte energético. Durante años, su contenido calórico ha servido para desaconsejar su consumo, especialmente a quienes se preocupaban por su peso. Una simplificación que no se sostiene a la vista de la evidencia actual: numerosos estudios encuentran que incluir frutos secos en la alimentación habitual no se traduce necesariamente en un aumento del peso corporal. Eso sí, hablamos de frutos secos naturales o tostados, sin sal añadida y sin coberturas azucaradas, no de cualquiera de las múltiples golosinas comerciales que también pueden presentarse bajo esta denominación.

Este progresivo distanciamiento de una alimentación tradicionalmente más rica en productos vegetales también tiene consecuencias bastante menos glamurosas que las que suelen protagonizar los vídeos de TikTok. Por ejemplo, sobre nuestras propias deposiciones. Ya en 1972, The Lancet publicó un estudio –hoy un clásico– que mostraba diferencias extraordinarias en el peso de las heces según el patrón alimentario de distintas poblaciones: unos 460 gramos diarios en determinados grupos africanos con dietas tradicionales ricas en fibra, frente a aproximadamente 110 gramos en poblaciones europeas.

No es solo una curiosidad científica de hace medio siglo: una revisión sistemática publicada en 2026, que reunió 113 ensayos clínicos, confirma que aumentar el consumo de fibra incrementa el peso fecal, aunque la magnitud del efecto depende, entre otras cosas, del tipo y cantidad de fibra. Así que, antes de celebrar el descubrimiento de la fibra como si fuera la última frontera de la nutrición, quizá convendría preguntarnos por qué hemos ido abandonando algunos de sus mejores proveedores. Porque la necesidad de aumentar su consumo es real. Lo que no está tan claro es que para conseguirlo necesitemos una nueva generación de productos alimenticios especialmente diseñados, enriquecidos y publicitados para tal fin.

No todas las fibras son iguales. Bajo esta denominación se agrupan sustancias con propiedades y efectos fisiológicos diferentes: algunas aumentan el volumen de las heces, otras contribuyen a mejorar determinados parámetros relacionados con el colesterol o la glucemia, y otras son fermentadas por las bacterias intestinales (dando lugar, entre otros compuestos, a los célebres ácidos grasos de cadena corta). La solubilidad, la viscosidad y la fermentabilidad de cada fibra condicionan unos efectos que, en el caso de las fibras fermentables, también pueden variar según características individuales, como la composición y actividad de la microbiota y, en determinados casos, factores genéticos. Así, dos productos con idéntica cantidad de fibra pueden tener repercusiones fisiológicas notablemente diferentes.

Tampoco es lo mismo consumir fibra como parte de los alimentos que la contienen naturalmente que incorporarla de forma aislada en productos que antes apenas la tenían. No porque la fibra añadida sea necesariamente inútil; sino porque una legumbre, una fruta o un fruto seco aportan mucho más que fibra. Proteínas, vitaminas, minerales, grasas insaturadas y otros compuestos forman parte de alimentos cuya contribución nutricional no puede reproducirse simplemente añadiendo un ingrediente a un refresco, unas galletas o unas palomitas recubiertas de saborizantes.

Este recurso es uno de los viejos conocidos del marketing alimentario: el nutricionismo –poner en alza la presencia de un nutriente sin considerar el valor nutricional del producto en su conjunto– y el consiguiente ‘efecto halo’ (o “efecto talismán”). Así, una característica favorable de un producto, convenientemente destacada, termina condicionando nuestra percepción sobre todo lo demás. Igual que en unos cereales de desayuno “con fibra”, si un refresco anuncia que contiene fibra prebiótica, el consumidor puede interpretar que está ante una bebida especialmente recomendable para su salud intestinal; aunque esa conclusión no se desprenda necesariamente de su composición. La fibra que contiene es real; la superioridad nutricional que el comprador pueda atribuirle no tiene por qué serlo.

La legislación europea también establece límites a lo que puede afirmarse sobre estos productos. El Reglamento (CE) 1924/2006 permite utilizar declaraciones como “fuente de fibra” cuando un alimento contiene al menos tres gramos por cada cien gramos o 1,5 gramos por cada cien kilocalorías, y “alto contenido de fibra” cuando alcanza seis gramos por cada cien gramos o tres gramos por cada cien kilocalorías. Pero cumplir estas condiciones no autoriza a atribuirle cualquier beneficio para la salud: las declaraciones saludables deben estar específicamente autorizadas y cumplir sus correspondientes requisitos. Añadir fibra no equivale a disponer de un cheque en blanco para prometer mejoras en la microbiota, el tránsito intestinal o el control del peso.

Hay otra cuestión importante: ¿qué otros ingredientes contiene el producto y cuál es su perfil nutricional en conjunto? La presencia de fibra no elimina los azúcares libres, no reduce por sí misma un contenido elevado de sal ni convierte en aconsejable cualquier producto de consumo ocasional. Preocuparse solo por sumar gramos de fibra tiene riesgo de repetir el reduccionismo que ha acompañado durante años al enriquecimiento con proteínas, vitaminas y minerales, utilizando el nutriente estrella para atribuir al conjunto una virtud que no posee. Si de verdad queremos mejorar nuestra alimentación, quizá convendría prestar bastante más atención a los alimentos que hemos ido desplazando y dejando de consumir.

No tienes por qué estudiar etiquetas ni familiarizarte con nombres como inulina, galactooligosacáridos o psyllium. Para consumir más fibra, en la mayor parte de los casos, basta con recuperar el protagonismo de determinados alimentos que conocemos de sobra y que no necesitan anunciar en letras mayúsculas lo que contienen. Como decíamos, lentejas, garbanzos, alubias y compañía deberían tener una presencia mucho más habitual en nuestra alimentación. La Agencia Española de Seguridad Alimentaria y Nutrición (AESAN) aconseja consumir al menos cuatro raciones semanales, con la posibilidad de llegar a una ración diaria (pueden ser cocidas en conserva).

Un puñado de almendras, nueces, avellanas o pistachos al día –al natural o tostados sin sal– también puede contribuir al aporte de fibra y otros nutrientes. La AESAN en este caso recomienda al menos tres raciones semanales, pudiendo llegar a una diaria, preferiblemente de unos 20-30 gramos de variedades naturales o tostadas sin sal, azúcares ni grasas añadidas que pueden incorporarse al desayuno, ensaladas o cremas de verduras.

También ayuda elegir habitualmente pan auténticamente integral en lugar de blanco, sustituir parte del arroz o la pasta refinados por sus versiones integrales, consumir fruta entera diariamente y aumentar la presencia de hortalizas en comidas y cenas (lo ideal es que estén presentes en cada una de las comidas principales). La avena, algunas semillas y determinadas frutas especialmente ricas en fibra pueden completar el conjunto.

Quien habitualmente consume poca fibra puede experimentar ciertas molestias digestivas, gases o distensión abdominal si aumenta bruscamente su ingesta, especialmente cuando incorpora grandes cantidades de fibra fermentable. Lo razonable es introducir los cambios progresivamente, mantener una hidratación adecuada y adaptar la alimentación a la tolerancia individual. En algunas situaciones clínicas, además, las recomendaciones sobre cantidad y tipo de fibra necesitan individualizarse; si te cuesta aterrizar estos consejos, la mejor recomendación es que te pongas en manos de un buen dietista-nutricionista.

Resumiendo: no se trata de añadir fibra a cualquier precio, sino de mejorar el patrón alimentario. Más legumbres, frutas, hortalizas, frutos secos y cereales integrales no solo incrementa el aporte de este componente; también modifica favorablemente la presencia de otros nutrientes y desplaza productos de menor interés nutricional. La cuestión es cuánto espacio estamos dispuestos a devolver a los alimentos que la aportan de forma objetivamente saludable: para eso solo hace falta llenar más a menudo el plato de alimentos que nunca necesitaron llamarse fibermaxxing para resultar recomendables.

El fibermaxxing es más que una moda pasajera de las redes sociales. Ciertas multinacionales alimentarias ya están apostando sin disimulo por el concepto:

Quizá lo más significativo sea que el fenómeno no se limita a decisiones individuales de unos cuantos fabricantes: esta es una ola que todo el mundo quiere surfear. En septiembre de 2021 la patronal británica Food and Drink Federation ya puso en marcha su campaña Action on Fibre. Entre sus compromisos figuraban desarrollar nuevos productos ricos en fibra, reformular los existentes y, literalmente, utilizar el poder del marketing para orientar las decisiones de compra de los consumidores. En julio de 2026, la propia organización presumía de haber introducido unas 500 nuevas referencias o reformulaciones con más fibra y haber aportado, siempre según sus propios cálculos, más de dos mil millones de raciones de fibra a través de esos productos.

La campaña cuenta, además, con una vertiente política nada despreciable. La FDF ha pedido al Gobierno británico que reconsidere los cambios previstos en el modelo de perfilado nutricional utilizado para identificar los productos sujetos a restricciones publicitarias y promocionales, argumentando que podrían perjudicar también a determinados productos ricos en fibra. Es decir, el mayor contenido de este nutriente no solo está sirviendo para promocionar alimentos, sino también para defender que determinados productos puedan seguir beneficiándose de estrategias comerciales que las autoridades pretenden limitar por motivos de salud pública. ¿Por qué será?

Aumentar el contenido de fibra de un alimento puede constituir una mejora nutricional real; lo que resulta bastante más discutible es que esa mejora parcial se utilice para presentar cualquier producto como una elección saludable. Porque la calidad nutricional de un alimento no depende de un único nutriente, por mucho protagonismo que le concedan las campañas comerciales.

Megan Khung was abused for over a year by her mother and her mother's boyfriend before she died as a result of a punch to the stomach.

Nouvelle Chua Ruoshi (left), seen arriving at the Supreme Court on Oct 26, 2026, and Megan Khung, who died in February 2020. (Photos: CNA/Lim Li Ting, Instagram/@simonboyyyyyyy)

SINGAPORE: A woman who helped dispose of four-year-old Megan Khung's body and was part of the conspiracy to abuse her pleaded guilty on Monday (Oct 26).

Megan had been abused by her mother, Foo Li Ping, and her mother's boyfriend, Wong Shi Xiang, for over a year before she died as a result of a punch to her stomach.

After Megan's death, Foo and Wong disposed of her body with the help of their friend, Nouvelle Chua Ruoshi.

Chua pleaded guilty on Monday to charges that included conspiracy to commit child abuse, disposal of a corpse and drug consumption.

Throughout the abuse, she had not protected Megan, taken her to see a doctor for her visible wounds or reported the case to the authorities.

As part of court proceedings, video clips of the abuse, some filmed by Chua, were played in court. The judge told those in the courtroom that the clips were distressing, asking those who wished to leave to do so.

Megan was born to Foo and Foo's then husband, Khung Wei Nan or Simonboy as he is known online, in October 2015.

Foo and Khung's marriage broke down, and Foo took Megan back to live with Foo's mother.

Foo began dating Wong in late 2018 and moved to live with him from January 2019, with Megan staying over on some weekends.

Sometime in 2019, Chua came to know Foo, and they became close friends, with Chua staying over at Foo's flat with her daughter.

Megan was abused by Foo and Wong when she stayed at the flat from February 2019.

She was caned when she urinated on furniture, leaving bruises, slapped, pushed and punched, forced to wear a soiled diaper over her head and made to eat food from a dustbin.

In a video clip taken by Chua, Megan cried as her mother forced her to wear the soiled diaper on her head, whimpering: "I don't want to wear."

A Moroccan crocodile relative’s teeth resemble those of prehistoric filter feeders, suggesting that different reptile groups independently evolved similar tools for catching tiny aquatic prey.

A crocodile relative that lived around 170 million years ago had a long, narrow snout lined with exceptionally thin, needle-like teeth. No known close relative has teeth quite like them. Their delicacy suggests a diet of very small fish, crustaceans such as fairy shrimp, and other small aquatic creatures rather than large prey.

Scientists at the University of Portsmouth and Hassan II University of Casablanca, Morocco, identified the new species, named Ctenosuchus tenuidens. It belonged to the thalattosuchians, a group of marine and freshwater reptiles that lived during the age of the dinosaurs. Its fossils came from rocks formed in ancient rivers and lakes in what is now the Atlas Mountains of southeastern Morocco.

The researchers examined the fossil teeth under powerful microscopes and studied their internal structure, finding specialized ridges and a closely spaced arrangement. For lead author Hamzah Imran, from the University of Portsmouth’s School of the Environment and Life Sciences, the teeth’s fragility offered a clue to what the animal could safely catch.

He said: “The teeth are so fine that we don’t think it was catching normal prey. If it caught a big fish, and the fish wriggled, those delicate teeth would get smashed. We think the animal was probably eating soft prey like shrimp, worms and tiny fish, so nothing that would break its teeth.

“These feeding habits make this particular species very different to other long-snouted crocodiles, so it really is quite an exciting discovery!”

Recognizing the teeth in the first place required a microscope and careful work with a scalpel. Imran recalled: “We noticed these little black circles on either side of the long bone in the middle of one of the fossils, so we put it under a microscope.

“We carefully scratched around one of the black circles with a scalpel, and as we uncovered more of it, it became longer and longer. That’s when we realized these little black circles were actually incredibly thin teeth.

“These comb-like teeth inspired the name Ctenosuchus tenuidens as ‘cteno’ means ‘comb,’ ‘suchus’ means ‘crocodile,’ and ‘tenuidens’ means ‘thin teeth.’”

Although unusual among its close relatives, Ctenosuchus had teeth resembling those of other prehistoric animals. Similar teeth occur in ancient aquatic reptiles called mesosaurs and in certain long-snouted pterosaurs that lived millions of years later. Its jaws and teeth share features with those of the Jurassic pterosaurs Gnathosaurus and Ctenochasma, which scientists believe were filter feeders, using their teeth to separate small food items from water. These similarities indicate that different groups independently evolved comparable feeding adaptations.

The Moroccan fossils broaden scientists’ understanding of the habitats and feeding roles occupied by Jurassic crocodile relatives in Gondwana, the southern supercontinent that included modern-day Africa. They also add to the picture of the varied prehistoric ecosystems that once existed in Morocco.

Imran added: “It just shows there is much more out there that needs to be explored. Morocco has an incredibly rich fossil history, but there is still so much waiting to be discovered.”

I've worked as an interior designer for over 20 years. Here are 8 decorating 'rules' worth breaking.

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In my years working in interior design, I've had many clients approach me looking for definitive "rules" for decorating their homes.

They want to know how to make their home look good and function better, assuming there is a set of guidelines to help them get there.

Certainly, there are building codes, safety requirements, and circulation considerations that must be met. However, much of what we think of as design and decorating is guided by rules of thumb, best practices, and even cultural assumptions.

The longer I practice design — over 20 years now — the more I learn that there are very few set rules to follow. Although some can be a good starting place, they often overlook the people who actually live in the space we're designing.

Here are a few design "rules" I think are worth breaking and why.

Open any shelter magazine, and you will likely see open shelving as a prominent kitchen feature.

Open shelving is beautiful, often breaking up a wall of cabinetry, making a kitchen feel lighter and more open while creating a place to display dishes, glassware, cookbooks, or other collected personal effects.

Although I, too, am drawn to open shelves, I question whether it's the right choice for every client. Those beautifully styled shelves require you to decide what stays visible, keep some degree of order, and deal with more dust and kitchen grime.

For some people, that's no problem — or even enjoyable. For others, it's one more thing to maintain.

Before my clients make this design choice, we talk through what would actually live on those shelves and whether they want the responsibility of keeping them looking the way they imagine.

After all, a trend can show you a new possibility, but it can't tell you whether that possibility fits your life.

I have many clients who make this assumption: If my home has a traditional architectural character, my new kitchen or bathroom must also have more traditional elements — think Shaker cabinetry, crown molding, and bronzy hardware.

Although I agree that architectural continuity can create cohesion, the architecture of your home is context, not a mandate.

When someone doesn't identify with their home's traditional character, I think it's important to honor the individual. I have seen many contemporary interiors set within traditional homes, and the juxtaposition can be breathtaking.

When a space feels cramped or isn't functioning well, I often see clients immediately assume they must add square footage.

Sometimes an addition is the right answer, but often the problem lies in how the existing space is being used.

For example, I had a client who was considering an addition to their kitchen. As we studied the existing space together, it became clear that the kitchen already had plenty of room, but the current layout wasn't making good use of it.

Rather than expanding the footprint of their home, we focused on reorganizing the space they already have so it functions better for them.

Before assuming more space is needed, identify where the friction is coming from: difficulty moving through a room, belongings that pile up in unwanted places, or things stored far from where they're used.

It's impossible to solve a problem you don't fully understand.

If you've ever invested in beautiful or expensive furniture, you may have worried about how you'll protect it from getting "ruined" by daily life.

But here's the rub (pun intended): When protecting furniture starts interfering with actually living in your home, we may have a problem.

Think about the white sofa featured in the aspirational home decor imagery in magazines or on social media. It's beautiful. It's pristine.

However, every Friday night, it needs to be covered with a blanket before anyone settles in to watch TV with a glass of red wine and a piece of chocolate. At some point, the needs of the furniture began to take priority over those of the person using it.

Here is the truth: Furniture is meant to be used, and a home is meant to be lived in.

This is a pretty standard kitchen-planning rule of thumb: Put waste collection close to the area where food prep and cleanup happen.

However, this is based on an assumption about how people use their kitchens. Sometimes, there are better layouts depending on your situation.

For example, many of my clients compost most food scraps. One such client keeps a small compost container on her counter, which is convenient during food prep and clean-up. Between composting and recycling, her regular landfill garbage gets very little use.

Once we uncovered how little this client uses the conventional trash can in her kitchen, we decided to relocate it elsewhere, freeing up valuable cabinet space near the sink and dishwasher.

This may not follow the design "rule," but it's a more practical arrangement for her space.

Sometimes an empty wall, shelf, or corner of a room can make a space feel unfinished, and our natural inclination is to fill it.

However, is empty space actually a problem that needs to be solved? No … at least not immediately.

I often encourage my clients to experiment with leaving something empty as part of the design process. It gives you an opportunity to live with the space and discover what — if anything — you actually need there.

You may discover a function you hadn't anticipated, eventually find something you genuinely want there, or realize that the room works better without anything at all.

If a space feels unfinished, disjointed, or just "off," we often believe it needs a substantial change — new furniture, different finishes, or even a remodel.

And with so many dramatic reveals and before-and-afters, it's no wonder we think meaningful improvements require meaningful (and expensive!) interventions.

Rather than replacing everything, sometimes small, thoughtful changes can alter how a whole space feels.

For example, I had a client whose guest bathroom was remodeled several years ago. The materials, fixtures, and finishes were beautiful, but they didn't quite come together as a whole.

Rather than replacing them, we added a single wall of peel-and-stick wallpaper, a DIY project the client could tackle over a weekend. It connected what was already there and made the room feel cohesive.

Because an interior designer has professional expertise and experience, homeowners assume their aesthetic judgment is more valid — or that the designer has the "right" design answer, and they do not.

Designers understand proportion, scale, color, materials, and composition, but clients know more than they give themselves credit for. They notice when a color feels wrong, have strong reactions to particular materials or furnishings, and can identify what they prefer even if they don't have the design vocabulary to explain why.

Although a designer can help you understand those preferences and translate them into a cohesive space, your own sense of what feels right should be part of the design process.

After all, expertise isn't about applying rules indiscriminately — it's about knowing when they serve the person in front of you and when they don't.

I have a saying in my practice: "You do you!" You are the expert in how you live, what you like, and what makes you feel at home.

New Research Reveals Cows Have a Little-Known Ability To Recognize Humans

A stranger’s face can hold a cow’s attention longer than a familiar handler’s, video tests suggest, offering clues to how cattle recognize the people around them.

When cows watched videos of people, they looked longer when the face on screen matched the voice they heard. The finding suggests that these highly social animals, which live in close contact with humans, can connect a person’s appearance with their voice.

Océane Amichaud of INRAE in Nouzilly, France, and colleagues tested these abilities in 32 Prim’ Holstein cows (Bos taurus taurus). Their study, published in the open-access journal PLOS One, examined both how the animals responded to human faces and whether they could link information from sight and hearing.

In the visual tests, the researchers played muted videos of familiar and unfamiliar male faces and measured how long the cows watched them. Unfamiliar faces held the animals’ gaze longer than a familiar handler’s face. That preference for new faces indicated that the cows could distinguish between people they knew and people they did not.

The researchers also tested cross-modal recognition, the ability to connect information received through different senses. They showed videos of familiar and unfamiliar faces while playing a recording of one of the two men speaking. Each man said the same sentence, keeping the words consistent across the recordings.

“In this study, using visual preference and cross-modal tests, we showed that cows are able to process human faces presented in 2D on videos and to associate familiar and unfamiliar faces with the corresponding voices by integrating multiple sensory modalities,” the authors said.

Heart rate measurements taken while the cows watched the videos did not reveal evidence that familiar or unfamiliar faces and voices affected their emotional responses. Differences in how long the animals looked therefore did not correspond to an emotional change detectable through this measure.

The authors caution that watching a video and hearing a recording are not equivalent to interacting with a person. Although the results suggest that cows can identify people through their faces and voices, further studies could examine how the animals interact with specific individuals. Understanding those relationships could help guide efforts to support their welfare.

Orangutans Prefer Things Associated With Laughter Even When the Rewards Are Identical

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

An unusual experiment suggests laughter can change how orangutans remember and value their experiences.

Give an orangutan a choice between two places to find a snack, and you’d expect it to pick the one that has offered the best treats. But what if both places have been equally rewarding? What could possibly tip the scales?

According to a new experiment, the answer might be laughter.

Scientists played recordings of orangutans laughing before six captive apes searched for food. Days later, when the researchers gave them a choice between equally rewarding options, the orangutans substantially favored the one they had learned to associate with laughter.

Positive emotions may influence how orangutans remember experiences and assign value to them. But how can we tell whether animals experience joy, and what does that joy actually do?

The experiment involved six orangutans, ranging in age from 8 to 51, living at zoos in Belgium and the United States.

Researchers led by Daan Laméris of Indiana University first taught the apes to forage for treats, including almonds and cashews, hidden beneath materials such as wood chips, shredded paper, or yarn.

The orangutans quickly learned which materials concealed food. In a preliminary test, four of the six also demonstrated that they could remember which materials had previously yielded larger rewards.

Over four days, the orangutans learned to associate two different materials with identical food rewards. Before searching through one material, the apes listened to five minutes of recordings of unfamiliar young orangutans laughing and squeaking during play. Before searching through the other, they heard ordinary wind-like sounds.

On the fifth day, the researchers brought back both materials and gave the animals 30 opportunities to choose between them, with equivalent reward schedules.

All six orangutans favored the material associated with laughter. Four showed a statistically significant individual preference. Across the group, the laughter-associated material attracted roughly 67% of choices.

The sounds didn’t appear to make the apes learn any faster. Rather, laughter seemed to change how much they valued what they had learned.

“It is surprising to find these effects with orangutans, because they are the least social of all the great apes,” Laméris said in a statement. “So even among relatively nonsocial animals, laughter is important.”

Erica Cartmill, the study’s senior author, compared the phenomenon to preferring your grandmother’s pancakes. They likely aren’t objectively better than most other pancakes, but because of the happy memories associated with eating them, you’re likely to subjectively favor them.

Animal behavior research has traditionally devoted considerable attention to negative emotions, particularly fear, stress, and suffering, and how they change how animals manifest. Positive emotions such as joy have received comparatively less scrutiny, partly because they are so difficult to measure.

After all, an orangutan cannot tell scientists that it’s happy.

But researchers can investigate whether experiences associated with positive emotions change how an animal behaves later.

A growing body of evidence suggests they do. In a 2025 study involving bonobos, researchers found that the apes tended to behave more optimistically after hearing recordings of laughter, although the findings were not conclusive. Previous experiments have also linked play and laughter-like vocalizations to more positive expectations in rats.

The orangutan study suggests that playful sounds can influence the value animals attach to remembered experiences. One possible explanation is emotional contagion, the phenomenon in which another individual’s emotional expression influences our own feelings.

However, the experiment included only six orangutans, and scientists didn’t directly measure their emotional states. The animals’ preferences are consistent with laughter producing a more positive mood, but they cannot prove that the orangutans actually felt joy.

Still, the implications are intriguing. The same mechanisms might help explain why humans develop lasting preferences for experiences or subjects associated with positive feelings, even when the objective rewards are no different.

For Cartmill, the larger ambition is to understand animal lives at an intimate level.

“Part of this project was to ask, ‘What are the moments that help shape the lives of the animals we share a planet with?’” she said. “It isn’t just about surviving to reproduce, but it’s actually about creating a narrative that also involves friendships and memories and building toward the things that give life meaning.”

The answer, it seems, may involve something as simple as hearing someone else having a good time.

The study was published in the journal Biology Letters.

To examine how different pollinators interact, the team divided flower visitors into five groups: honey bees, bumble bees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and an 'other' category containing butterflies, beetles, and wasps.

Over 93 hours of observation, the scientists documented encounters in which one insect interrupted another's feeding or prevented it from reaching a flower. Each interaction was classified according to its outcome. A 'win' occurred when one insect gained access to a flower at the expense of another, which experienced a 'loss' of feeding opportunities. A 'share' meant both insects were able to access the flower without conflict.

The results revealed a clear pattern. Locations with greater concentrations of honey bees generally had fewer other pollinators and experienced more competition for flowers. Honey bees also emerged victorious in a larger proportion of their encounters with other insects.

On bramble flowers, doubling the density of honey bees was associated with a 14% decrease in bumblebee abundance. On ivy, higher honey bee numbers were linked to reduced numbers of solitary bees and hoverflies.

The honey bees did not simply reach flowers before their competitors. They frequently initiated aggressive encounters, driving other insects away to secure feeding opportunities.

Other pollinators actively avoided honey bees in 28% of observed interactions on bramble and 36% of those on ivy. In at least 20% of the encounters honey bees won on either plant, they physically pushed aside or chased away competing insects.

Aggressive behavior initiated by species other than honey bees was uncommon. However, one insect stood out as an exception.

The ivy bee, a solitary species not native to Britain that was first documented in the country in 2001, was the only pollinator observed actively challenging honey bees. Unlike most other insects, ivy bees sometimes defended their feeding spots by chasing or displacing honey bees, successfully competing for access to flowers.

Climate Change Could Make Competition Worse

The findings are part of a broader research effort to understand competition among pollinators and identify ways to reduce its ecological effects.

The results also come as beekeeping continues to expand across the UK. The number of registered beekeepers increased from 19,000 in 2010 to 47,000 last year, reflecting growing numbers of managed honey bee colonies.

Researchers warn that rising temperatures could add to the pressure on wild pollinators. Their observations showed that competitive encounters became more frequent during warmer conditions.

One possible explanation involves changes in flower nectar production. When plants experience 'heat stress', they can produce less nectar, potentially forcing more insects to compete over a smaller supply of food.

Temperature also affects different pollinators in different ways. Bumblebees and hoverflies are sensitive to high temperatures and may forage more effectively in cooler weather. Honey bees, by contrast, generally become more active as temperatures rise. Previous research found that increasing the temperature by 12°C caused a tenfold increase in the number of honey bees leaving their nests.

Together, these differences suggest that continued warming could give honey bees an even greater competitive advantage over some wild pollinators.

Protecting Wild Pollinators as Honey Bee Numbers Grow

Lead author Victoria Buckle, a PhD student at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, emphasized that the research is intended to improve conservation efforts rather than discourage people from keeping honey bees.

"The aim of our study was not to discourage beekeeping, but to better understand how competition happens between wild and managed pollinator species with the aim of helping to conserve our wild pollinators, which play such an important ecological role.

"We found that most interactions when competing for food were instigated by honey bees and they had a greater number of 'wins', resulting in a loss of forage for other pollinators. Bumblebees, solitary bees and hoverflies would avoid, be displaced by and on some occasions be chased off by honey bees.

"An interesting exception was the ivy bee, which would win contests more often against honey bees. It is the first time, to our knowledge, such behaviors have been observed and shows ivy bees are able to 'hold their own' in such encounters.

"However, honey bees outcompeted all other wild pollinators and based on our findings, future predicted warming in the UK is only likely to further exacerbate interference competition from dominant honey bees. Therefore, it's important to find ways to ensure honey bees and wild pollinators can continue to coexist, particularly in locations with a high density of hives, such as urban areas."

Scientists discover ghostly, eyeless creature hidden in Chinese cave

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

A newly identified freshwater cave snail in China is offering scientists a rare glimpse into the little-known aquatic ecosystems hidden beneath East Asia. Published in the journal Subterranean Biology, the discovery represents an important step forward in understanding the region's underground wildlife. Until now, no freshwater cave snails had been documented in China, and the country's subterranean freshwater mollusks had received relatively little scientific attention.

The newly discovered species, Erhaia dijiang, has evolved remarkable features that allow it to live in complete darkness. As a true cave-dwelling animal, it has entirely lost its eyes, with the structures normally associated with vision having fully degenerated. The tiny snail also has a cone-shaped, nearly transparent shell, a delicate body without pigmentation, and long, slender tentacles.

Its unusual appearance inspired a name drawn from ancient Chinese mythology. Researchers chose dijiang in honor of "Di Jiang," a mountain patron god portrayed as a faceless beast in the ancient text Classic of Mountains and Seas (山海经). The connection is particularly fitting because the snail is extremely sensitive to light. During laboratory observations, individuals consistently retreated into dark spaces beneath rocks whenever they were exposed to illumination.

The location of the discovery is just as remarkable as the animal itself. Scientists found Erhaia dijiang at an elevation of 2,062 meters, making it the highest-altitude cave snail yet recorded within the family Erhaiidae. The finding demonstrates that freshwater cave snails can inhabit limestone cave systems at elevations above 2,000 meters.

Life in the Dark Waters of a Mountain Cave

Within its underground habitat, Erhaia dijiang lives among submerged gravel and broken rock in water less than one meter deep. The cave's water temperature stays close to 20°C, providing a relatively stable environment for the tiny mollusks.

Even when heavy seasonal rains increase the flow of water and occasionally wash a few snails out through the cave opening, they have not been observed in nearby streams. Researchers found that the animals remain confined to the cave environment, staying within two meters of its entrance.

Laboratory observations also provided clues about what these elusive creatures eat. The snails were seen feeding on diatoms, microscopic algae that grow on rock surfaces, and they also consumed fish food supplied by the researchers.

Only Three Living Snails Found, Raising Conservation Concerns

Although the discovery sheds new light on China's largely unexplored underground aquatic biodiversity, researchers are concerned about the species' future. They describe its conservation outlook as "far from optimistic."

During the first field investigation in August 2025, the team encountered an exceptionally small number of snails. Despite searching the shallow cave waters, researchers located only three living adults and one empty shell. They found no juveniles, suggesting that the known population is extremely sparse.

The scientists are now urging more comprehensive investigations of cave ecosystems throughout China to uncover additional species and better understand these fragile underground habitats. They also emphasize the need to develop conservation protections quickly for these vulnerable mollusks, which are native to highly restricted environments and could face serious threats before their populations are fully understood.

Why You Should Always Read the Label on Your Cleaning Spray

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Until I started reviewing kitchen gear five years ago, I rarely read product manuals or user guides. I’m sure that’s due in large part to my ADHD, as my brain tends to move more quickly than step-by-step instructions allow. Plus, I like to figure things out on my own, thank you very much!

But my rogue, DIY, quick-start method hasn’t always worked out in my favor. I inadvertently killed my first Dyson stick vacuum (that I saved for months to buy) because I failed to educate myself on proper filter maintenance. And I’ve owned my car for a decade and still have no clue what a couple of the buttons actually do.

While user error can make for a funny punchline, it can also get expensive. For example, it might seem silly to read the manual for a nonstick frying pan or enameled cast-iron Dutch oven, but that’s where you’d learn that putting it in the dishwasher or using metal utensils can destroy or degrade the cooking surface.

In the case of cleaning products, the stakes can be even higher—especially in this era of, er, less restrictive food safety regulations.

Before you get to the instructions on the back of a cleaning spray bottle, you need to pay close attention to what’s on the front, because not all kitchen cleaning sprays (no, not even all all-purpose cleaning sprays) are created equally.

Though they might have some very minimal, naturally occurring disinfecting properties from ingredients like vinegar or essential oils, basic cleaning sprays like Mrs. Meyer’s and Aunt Fannie’s have one job—and that is to clean the surfaces in your kitchen. And by “clean,” I mean they contain surfactants, which help to dislodge grease and gunk so you can more easily wipe away a mess.

Mrs. Meyer’s Clean Day All-Purpose Cleaner Spray

Aunt Fannie’s All-Purpose Cleaning Vinegar Spray

But simply removing discernible debris won’t leave you with a germ-free countertop, which is why you also need a disinfectant to kill bacteria and viruses (not to be confused with a sanitizer, which only kills bacteria). And if you want to ensure your counters are so clean you could eat off of them, I recommend using both. If you spray a disinfecting solution on top of a spill, it’s not disinfecting the counter underneath the spill—it’s sanitizing the surface of whatever is stuck to your counter.

Here’s the real kicker, and the reason it’s important to read the instructions on the back of your disinfecting (or sanitizing) spray: In order for the product to kill the number of germs the label claims, it needs to sit on the surface in question for anywhere from about two to 10 minutes. This period is called the “dwell time” or “contact time,” and the product needs to remain visibly wet for however long it takes to be truly effective.

For example, Lysol's All-Purpose Cleaner (which is truly all-purpose when used properly), is a sanitizer at 10 seconds, but it only "kills 99.9% of viruses and bacteria" as the front of the label claims, if it’s left to sit for 10 minutes. The little asterisks after that claim lead you to the back label, which details the dwell time along with the common bacteria and viruses it kills.

All-Purpose Cleaner Spray, Lemon Breeze Scent, 32 oz.

“Use as directed” shows up four times in the online product description for Clorox’s Multi-Surface Disinfecting Mist, which also claims to kill 99.9% of viruses and bacteria. It even has a specific instruction for kitchens (to rinse off of any food-prep surfaces after a 10-minute dwell time).

The moral of the story? Use your cleaning products as directed, which unfortunately does require reading the fine print on the label. While you’re at it, you might want to consider replacing your microfiber cleaning cloths after a 2010 study found that microfiber becomes less effective at removing contaminants from surfaces the more it gets used.

But the one thing almost everyone reports is that you eat far less food on these things.

These are all good, but you’ve heard them before.

The one piece of advice you might not have heard, that sounds a bit unconventional, but that becomes useful especially as you start approaching your ideal weight: eat more.

Try to eat a bit more than you feel like.

Many issues we see with these drugs are from undernourishment. You’re running a deficit and, depending on the drug in question, burning more energy. You need to eat a little more than you think.

High-Antioxidant Foods

📅 Aug 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

We're all supposed to eat a “healthy diet,” but what does that really mean? Off the bat, you probably think getting enough about protein and fatty acids (notably the vital omega-3s), plus important vitamins and minerals. But there’s also the less obvious: things like prebiotics and, the topic of today’s post, antioxidants.

Antioxidants, as I’ve discussed previously, defend the body from free radical damage. Free radicals are unstable molecules that knock around, pillaging electrons from other molecules and causing DNA damage and oxidative stress. When free radicals run amok, the damage they cause contributes to premature aging and chronic diseases. Antioxidants’ job is to limit free radical formation and neutralize them when their numbers become too great.

Plant and animal foods deliver important antioxidant vitamins, minerals, flavonoids, carotenoids, and other substances that the body can’t produce itself. To cover your antioxidant bases, since there’s no way to avoid free radicals, here are some antioxidant-rich foods to include in your diet.

Certain antioxidants are fairly easy to quantify. You can use any food tracking app to look up the amounts of antioxidant vitamins A, C, and E you consume in a day. Others, like antioxidant enzymes, are considerably more complicated. Scientists have devised various methods of measuring the total antioxidant capacity of food. You have your crocin bleaching assay (CBA), trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC), total oxyradical scavenging capacity assay (TOSC), ferric reducing antioxidant potential (FRAP), DPPH assay, and many more.1

Don’t worry, there won’t be a quiz later.

ORAC, or oxygen radical absorbance capacity, was developed by scientists at the National Institute on Aging in 1992. Despite its popularity, the USDA renounced ORAC in 2012, on the grounds that ORAC doesn't necessarily predict how much antioxidant activity happens in the body and to discourage supplement manufacturers from making strong claims about the power of their antioxidant formulations.

Nevertheless, researchers continue to use ORAC, and it remains the best way for the average person to look up which foods have relatively higher or lower antioxidant content.

The question of whether these foods actually supercharge your body's ability to fight free radicals is still up for debate. A lot of factors probably go into it, including how much of these compounds you absorb and the freshness of the food you consume.

As I see it, the best reason to consider antioxidant values is that you’ll probably end up revisiting some otherwise nutritious foods you might not be eating regularly.

The foods that tend to score highly on antioxidant metrics are also ones that are widely considered healthy by other standards—colorful vegetables and fruits, organ meat, and seafood.

Plant foods tend to be higher in antioxidants than animal products, though the latter are still important for checking certain antioxidant boxes. “Eating the rainbow” will net you a good spectrum of vitamins, minerals, and other antioxidants. How to Build Habits that Stick

📅 Mar 12, 2025  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

We all start with the best intentions—whether it’s exercising regularly, eating healthier, or prioritizing self-care. But let’s be real… sticking to new habits can be hard. Life gets busy, motivation fades, and before you know it, you’re back to old patterns.

So how do you create habits that actually last? The key isn’t willpower—it’s strategy. By setting yourself up for success, you can build healthy routines that feel effortless and become part of your lifestyle.

Here’s how to make your habits stick for good!

1. Start Small & Be Specific

One of the biggest mistakes people make is going too big, too fast. If your goal is to work out every day, don’t start with an hour-long workout. Instead, commit to at least 10 minutes a day—something so easy you can’t say no. Once that’s locked in, build from there.

Example: Instead of saying, “I want to eat healthier,” say, “I will add a serving of veggies to my lunch every day.” Making your goals specific make it easier to follow through!

2. Habit Stacking

One of the easiest ways to build a new habit is to pair it with something you already do. This is called “habit stacking” and helps your new habit feel more natural.

Example:

  • Want to start journaling? Do it right after brushing your teeth in the morning.
  • Want to drink more water? Have a glass before your morning coffee.

The more effortless it feels, the more likely you are to stick with it!

3. Make It Easy

If a habit feels complicated or overwhelming, you won’t stick with it. Remove obstacles so it becomes the easiest choice.

Example:

  • Want to work out in the morning? Lay out your workout clothes the night before.
  • Want to eat healthier? Meal prep snacks so you always have good options on hand.

Set yourself up for success by making the habit as easy as possible.

4. Track Your Progress

There’s something powerful about seeing your progress—it keeps you motivated! Track in my MOVE APP or in the #GOALS PLANNER.

Even if you miss a day, don’t let it derail you. The goal isn’t perfection—it’s consistency!

5. Focus on Identity, Not Just Behavior

Instead of just thinking about what you want to do, shift your mindset to who you want to become. When you identify with your habit, it becomes a part of you.

Example: Instead of saying, “I’m trying to work out,” say, “I am someone who moves my body every day.” When you believe in your new identity, your actions will follow.

6. Reward Yourself (But Not with What You’re Avoiding)

Positive reinforcement is key! Celebrate your wins—big or small—to keep yourself motivated.

But here’s the trick: Don’t reward yourself in a way that contradicts your habit.

✅ Good reward: Bought a new yoga mat after sticking to a month of workouts.
❌ Not-so-great reward: Cheat days filled with unhealthy food and no workouts as an “reward."

Keep your rewards aligned with your goals!

7. Give Yourself Grace & Keep Going

Nobody is perfect. You will miss days, and that’s okay. What matters is that you get back on track quickly instead of quitting altogether.

Make It a Lifestyle!

Building habits that stick isn’t about temporary motivation—it’s about creating a lifestyle that supports your goals. By starting small, making habits easy, and tracking progress, you can create lasting change and feel amazing while doing it!

How to Make Better Pie Crust, According to a Pastry Chef

📅 Oct 20, 2020  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

There is a lot of subtlety that goes into making a good pie crust, the types of intuitive moves or small tips you pick up in a professional kitchen (or by baking with an experienced relative). But a few principles make all the difference: keeping the butter cold, avoiding overmixing, letting the dough rest, and baking the finished pie long enough.

Below, and in my book A Good Bake, I cover the little things that matter, from making and rolling out pie dough to crimping the crust and knowing when a pie is done. These are the techniques I use to make delicious, professional-looking pies, no matter what recipe I’m following.

A Good Bake by Melissa Weller with Carolyn Carreno

In a professional bakery, cooks prepare pie dough up to several days in advance. Set yourself up the same way at home. Making the dough ahead gives it time to rest and chill, so it’s ready to roll when you need it. You can also freeze pie dough up to one month in advance; wrap it well and defrost it overnight in the fridge.

The essential ingredients in pie dough are flour, water, a pinch of salt, maybe a very small amount of sugar, and some fat. Many traditional American pie dough recipes call for lard, rendered pork fat, which makes for an exceptionally flaky crust.

I love lard, but quality lard is not always easy to find, so I use good old-fashioned unsalted butter for my pie and tart crusts. With good technique, you can achieve plenty of flakiness and get that great buttery flavor.

Some big pieces, some smaller pieces, just right.

Many pie recipes call for cutting the butter into ¼" cubes. I prefer largish ½" chunks. After cutting the butter into pieces, I arrange them onto a plate and freeze them for 10 minutes so the butter gets as cold as can be without turning rock solid.

When mixed into the flour, some larger chunks remain intact. When you roll out the dough, those chunks form striations of butter. As the crust bakes, the water in the butter turns to steam, creating microscopic flaky layers—and a light, tender crust.

Looking for maximum flake? Check out our guide to laminating pie dough →

Lots of recipes call for adding ice water to the dough. I find that cold water from my refrigerator (like from a water filter or door dispenser) works fine. That said, if you want to use super-chilled water, it certainly won’t hurt.

Add water only after you mix the flour and butter. When the water joins the party, gluten starts to form. Too much gluten development toughens pie dough, making the finished crust heavy and dense rather than light and flaky. Mixing the flour with cold butter first coats the flour’s proteins, impeding the interaction between the water and the flour and reducing gluten development.

It’s okay if there are still some floury bits!

After bringing the dough together, I shape it into a round disk or brick, depending on the shape I’ll roll it into. Then I wrap it in plastic wrap, run a rolling pin over it to compact and flatten it, and put it in the refrigerator to rest.

A good rest lets the flour fully absorb any moisture so that the dough comes together completely. Resting the dough also relaxes the gluten, making it easier to roll out and less likely to shrink in the oven.

If your kitchen runs warm, work quickly and give the dough extra time in the refrigerator whenever it starts to feel soft or tacky. You can also chill your mixing bowl, bench scraper, and rolling pin before you start.

Take the dough out of the refrigerator, put it on the counter, and let it sit until it feels malleable but still firm (10 to 15 minutes) before rolling. The goal is for the dough to be just pliable enough to roll but not so soft that it starts to stick. Conversely, rolling our cold dough straight from the fridge can cause it to crack.

Once you start rolling, move quickly. Even the firmest dough block will soften quickly under the friction of the rolling pin and the heat of your hands. The whole process shouldn’t take more than a minute or two.

It may be unconventional, but I always butter a pie plate before arranging the dough. The butter helps hold the dough in place as I shape it and provides insurance for removing it from the pan after baking. I smear enough butter on the bottom and sloped sides of the plate to leave a visible layer. I usually use cold butter, but slightly softened also works.

There are two ways to “line” a pie plate—that is, to make sure the dough fits snugly inside. Either fold the dough in half, lift it from the surface you rolled it out on, and place it in the pan. Or roll the dough onto your rolling pin and carry it over on the pin. I fold the dough when lining a round pie plate, and I roll it onto a rolling pin when using a jelly roll pan, which requires a larger piece. Use whatever method you find easiest.

In either case, carry the dough until it hovers over the pie plate, tart pan, jelly roll pan, or other vessel, centering it as much as you can. Then gently lay it onto the dish and unfold or unroll it. Adjust the dough so that it’s centered on the pan.

Ease the pie dough into the bottom corners of the pan without stretching it.

Gently lift one edge of the dough with one hand and use the other to press it into the bottom of the pan, working your way around the perimeter. Use a delicate touch, and resist pulling the dough, which will cause it to spring back when baked and create thin spots in the crust.

Next, gently run your finger along the rim to press the dough onto it, just enough to ensure it adheres. This allows you to see how much dough is overhanging. Make sure it is even all the way around and sitting nicely on top of the rim.

Use kitchen shears to trim the overhanging dough so there is an even 1" hanging over the perimeter of the pie dish. (Discard the trimmings or smush them together into a disk and refrigerate or freeze the dough to make a pie another day.)

If you are making a double-crust or lattice-topped pie, trim the top sheet of dough or lattice so it is flush with the outside edge of the pan.

Keep it even: Place your thumb in the indent from your index finger just made.

In pie making, “crimping” means forming the decorative, wavy edge around the perimeter of a pie. To crimp, tightly roll the overhanging dough toward the center of the pie to form a thick lip that rests on the ridge of the plate. While many recipes call for folding the excess dough under, I prefer folding it up and over. When you’re finished, the dough shouldn't hang over the rim.

The process is the same whether you are making a single- or double-crust pie. For a double crust, roll the bottom crust over the top crust, sealing the filling inside.

As you form this ridge, use your fingers to shape the edge of the rolled crust so that it is rounded and even. Once the entire edge is rolled, place the index finger and thumb of one hand on the inside edge of the ridge and use the index finger of your other hand to push the outside edge toward the center of the pie, forming a V or U shape. Continue around the perimeter until you have crimped the entire crust.

There is a lot of chilling when making pie. First, you chill the butter. Then you chill the finished dough before rolling it out. You’ll also want to chill the pie dish after lining it, and again after crimping and filling the dough, before baking.

If you’ve ever wondered why pies in professional bakeries look so much prettier than your homemade ones, it’s because they chill them—or even freeze them—before baking. Chilling the uncooked pie helps it hold its shape better during baking.

In my kitchens, we often freeze a fully assembled fruit pie (overnight is best), then bake it directly from frozen. To bake a frozen pie, put it in the oven straight from the freezer and add 10 to 20 minutes to the total baking time, using the recipe’s visual indicators for doneness in the recipe.

Always put a baking sheet under pies before baking. This is especially important with fruit pies, to prevent the bubbling fruit juices from spilling over the edge of the pan onto your oven floor.

Also called pre- or par-baking, blind baking refers to baking the crust before adding the filling. For many custard pies, which cook faster than fruit pies, you’ll partially blind-bake the crust to keep it from getting soggy or even staying raw. For pies and tarts whose fillings are not baked at all, you’ll blind-bake the crust until it’s fully golden brown

To blind-bake a crust, line the shaped, crimped, unbaked pie shell with parchment paper (crinkle the paper first to fit the pie shell better), then fill the parchment-lined dough with pie weights to prevent the crust from puffing while it bakes. BA’s favorite pie weights are inexpensive steel ball bearings, but many cooks use dried beans or rice; label any food items you use as “pie weights” when the pie is done, since they will not be good for cooking after baking.

For the full technique, see BA’s guide to blind-baking pie crust.

Depending on how cold the pie is when you put it in the oven—and depending on your oven’s hot spots and other quirks—baking time can vary, so I always rely on the recipe’s visual cues for doneness.

The crust should be a rich shade of golden brown. For a fruit pie, the filling must be bubbling through the lattice in the center of the pie or the vent cut into its top. If the filling isn’t bubbling in the center (not just around the sides), the fruit hasn’t cooked down enough. Remove it too early, and you risk a filling that weeps when you cut a slice.

Custard pies must bake until they are set. A set custard will wobble slightly when you jiggle the pan, but it shouldn’t slosh around like liquid. Avoid baking a custard pie so long that it’s fully set, which can cause surface cracks as the pie cools.

Cooling time is essential to pie making. Custard pies need to set (and sometimes chill for several hours), and fruit pies need ample time to gel and firm. For beautiful slices that hold their shape, the wait is worthwhile.

Overmixing or overhandling the dough encourages gluten development, which can make the baked crust tough rather than tender. Mix only until the dough begins to come together; it should still look a little shaggy, with visible pieces of butter.

Pie dough can shrink if it hasn’t rested long enough or if you stretch it while fitting it into the pan. Let the dough rest in the refrigerator before rolling, gently ease it into the pie plate without pulling, and chill the shaped crust again before baking.

Slumped crusts can happen if you remove the pie weights too early or you don’t fill the crust enough. Add weights to fill the crust to the rim of the pie dish, and don’t remove them until the crust is set (it should look dry and the parchment will no longer stick).

The butter may have gotten too warm or worked too thoroughly into the flour. For distinct flaky layers, keep the butter cold and leave some larger pieces intact. If the dough becomes soft or tacky while you're working, return it to the refrigerator until firm.

Why does my pie dough crack when I roll it out?

Dough that’s too cold can crack when you roll it, while dough that hasn’t hydrated enough may crumble. Let chilled dough sit until it’s pliable but still firm before rolling. If it remains dry and won't hold together, the problem may be insufficient hydration.
