The future of friendship is here — and things are looking a bit artificial.

Based on the critically acclaimed 2021 novel by Nobel Prize-winner Kazuo Ishiguro, “Klara and the Sun” — out in theaters Friday — stars Jenna Ortega as the titular Klara, an optimistic, solar-powered Artificial Friend who yearns to be someone’s companion. She finds that fellowship in Josie (Mia Tharia), a chronically ill teenager. The two become inseparable, despite great disapproval from Josie’s mother Chrissie (Amy Adams).

But audiences should not expect to see the 24-year-old — best known for her macabre role in Netflix’s “Wednesday” — with a deadpan expression or in a morose mood.

“People are not used to seeing me be so expressive and especially over the years I’ve become a little bit more introverted,” she said. “It was very futuristic and just visually different than anything that I’d ever done before. And it’s so rare that you get the opportunity to be so expressive and to be so big on screen.”

Directed by Taika Waititi (“Thor: Ragnarok,” “Jojo Rabbit”), the film is set in a dystopian society where the use of artificial intelligence is meant to address loneliness — a topic that feels all too pertinent.

One 2025 study by the Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research found that roughly a quarter of individuals under 30 say they use AI for companionship. A Pew Research Center survey of U.S. teens that same year found that 16% use chatbots for casual conversations while 12% rely on it for emotional support or advice.

Despite the subject matter, Waititi and Ortega said the film is not a commentary on artificial intelligence.

“It was like three years ago when we shot this,” Waititi said. “That whole [AI] thing has taken off since then.”

“In the process of putting this project together, ChatGPT wasn’t really a part of discussion,” said Ortega, who added that she prefers audiences view the film in the same light as the 1984 sci-fi action flick “The Terminator.”

Still, that didn’t stop the pair from giving their opinions on the encroaching role that the technology is taking on in Hollywood. A Los Angeles Times survey over the summer of major studio job postings found that more than one in 10 was likely connected to AI, whether to build AI tools or defend intellectual property against unauthorized AI use.

“I don’t think [AI] has a place, or it cannot ever replace humans. I got into this whole filmmaking thing or into the arts because I wanted to work with people. I don’t wanna be sitting around in my home just pushing buttons,” said Waititi, who is also set to direct “Barn 8,” an adaptation of the satirical novel by Deb Olin Unferth. “The reality is it’s gonna be used, and it’s gonna be adopted probably more and more, and I don’t know what the best version of this is. It can’t replace people.”

While some companies have kept their AI plans under the radar, some notable stars have defended its use including “Star Wars” creator George Lucas, who this summer called it the “future”; “Goodfellas” director Martin Scorsese has openly embraced generative AI.

“It’s the same way as looking at our phones makes everyone feel sick,” said Ortega, who is also cast in the upcoming December comedy “The Gallerist,” alongside Natalie Portman. “Artificial intelligence isn’t creative. It mimics. Nothing about it is real or genuine.”

Much like her character Klara, Ortega chooses to have faith in humans and their spontaneous wonder.

“I think there’ll be this wonderful sort of bubbling underground movement that brings back the importance of things coming from the source and individuals themselves,” Ortega said.

Wenn man seinen baufällig gewordenen Wohnsitz wieder bewohnbar machen muss: Kann man dann die Kosten von der Steuer absetzen?

Wien. Bei vielen Menschen stehen sie nach wie vor ganz oben auf der Wunschliste – die eigenen vier Wände. Erst kürzlich hat das wieder eine Umfrage bestätigt: Laut der Wohnstudie 2026 der UniCredit Bank Austria wollen rund drei Viertel der Österreicherinnen und Österreicher in einem Eigenheim leben, auch wenn es schwer finanzierbar ist (die „Presse“ berichtete).

Dass man, sobald der Kredit abbezahlt ist, weniger fürs Wohnen ausgeben muss, ist dabei ein großer Anreiz, auch im Hinblick auf die Pensionsvorsorge. Aber was, wenn gerade dann hohe Sanierungskosten anfallen? Vor allem bei Altbauobjekten kann das leicht passieren und die erhoffte finanzielle Entlastung zunichte machen. Nicht ganz unbeachtlich ist dabei auch die Frage, ob man die Kosten wenigstens von der Steuer absetzen kann – Stichwort „außergewöhnliche Belastung“.

Bisher galt das freilich als weitgehend aussichtslos, abgesehen von Extremfällen wie einer Wiederherstellung des Eigenheims nach Umweltkatastrophen oder Feuer. Denn nur Aufwendungen, die eben wirklich außergewöhnlich und unvermeidbar sind, können unter diesem Titel abgesetzt werden. Und dazu zählt Sanierungs- und Verbesserungsaufwand normalerweise nicht. Eine aktuelle, richtungweisende Entscheidung des Verwaltungsgerichtshofs relativiert das jedoch: Die Absetzbarkeit bleibt zwar die Ausnahme und hat enge Grenzen, aber sie beschränkt sich eben doch nicht ausschließlich auf Katastrophenfälle.

Den Anlass lieferte ein Bauernhaus, das vom Echten Hausschwamm befallen war – einem holzzerstörenden Pilz, der große Schäden an Gebäuden anrichten kann. Die Sanierungskosten machten rund 266.000 Euro aus. Das Finanzamt lehnte die Anerkennung der Kosten als außergewöhnliche Belastung ab: Denn die Sanierung sei aufgrund von Baumängeln nötig geworden und habe der Vermögenserhaltung gedient – und Aufwendungen dafür gelten an sich weder als „außergewöhnlich“ noch als zwangsläufig entstanden.

Das Bundesfinanzgericht (BFG) kam jedoch zu einem anderen Ergebnis: Das Haus sei der einzige Wohnsitz der Eigentümerfamilie und wäre ohne die Sanierung unbewohnbar geworden – in bautechnischer wie auch in gesundheitlicher Hinsicht. Die Sanierung sei daher alternativlos gewesen, die Kosten seien absetzbar.

Das letzte Wort hatte dann der VwGH, und er schlug quasi einen Mittelweg zwischen den beiden konträren Rechtsstandpunkten ein. Grundsätzlich bestätigt das Höchstgericht die Ansicht des BFG, dass Sanierungskosten unter solchen Gegebenheiten absetzbar sein können. In welcher Höhe, ist jedoch noch offen.

Und auf welche Kriterien kommt es dabei an? Zunächst einmal muss es sich um Ausgaben handeln, die außergewöhnlich sind, zwangsläufig entstehen und die wirtschaftliche Leistungsfähigkeit wesentlich beeinträchtigen, heißt es in der höchstgerichtlichen Entscheidung (Ro 2023/15/0027). „Zwangsläufigkeit“ bedeutet eine rechtliche, sittliche oder tatsächliche Verpflichtung – und dazu zählt es auch, wenn dem Betroffenen die weitere Lebensführung ohne Wiederbeschaffung des zerstörten Wirtschaftsgutes unzumutbar ist.

„Außergewöhnlich“ sind Aufwendungen, die höher sind als bei der Mehrzahl der Steuerpflichtigen mit gleichen Einkommens- und Vermögensverhältnissen. Bei Mietkosten, die einem Wohnungseigentümer entstanden waren, dessen Wohnung wegen Einsturzgefahr unbenützbar wurde, hatte der VwGH das schon in einem früheren Fall anerkannt, denn wer im Eigentum wohnt, zahlt normalerweise keine Miete. Und dasselbe gilt laut dem aktuellen Judikat für die Rettung des vom Hausschwamm befallenen Wohnsitzes.

Aber: Absetzbar sind solche Kosten nur insoweit, als sie in die Wiederherstellung des ursprünglichen Zustands des Gebäudes fließen und ihnen keine Wertsteigerung gegenübersteht. Im konkreten Fall ist daher wieder das BFG am Zug. Es muss nun feststellen, welcher Mehrwert sich daraus ergibt, dass etwa Heizung, Fußbodenbelag, Fliesen, Türen und Fenster erneuert wurden und somit länger nutzbar sind. Dafür muss ein entsprechender Betrag abgezogen werden (Vorteilsausgleich). Erst daraus ergibt sich die Höhe der tatsächlichen „außergewöhnlichen Belastung“.

Zum selben Ergebnis kam der VwGH übrigens auch in einem weiteren, ähnlich gelagerten Fall: Da ging es um die Behebung von Schäden, die Marder im Dachgeschoß eines Zweifamilienhauses angerichtet hatten. Auch diese Ausgaben gelten als außergewöhnliche Belastung. Der durch die Erneuerung des Daches entstandene Vorteil muss aber angerechnet werden.

Rechtssatz des VwGH (Ro 2026/15/0025): Die Wiederherstellung der Bewohnbarkeit eines Hauses bzw. einer Wohnung kann zu einer außergewöhnlichen Belastung führen. Erfasst werden können dabei allerdings nur jene Aufwendungen, die unerlässlich sind, um die Bewohnbarkeit in ihrem ursprünglichen Zustand wiederherzustellen.

Darüber hinausgehende Sanierungsmaßnahmen und Verbesserungsmaßnahmen begründen keine außergewöhnliche Belastung, weil die Sanierung und Verbesserung sanierungsbedürftiger Gebäudeteile an sich nicht außergewöhnlich ist.

I’m not even going to try to pretend I don’t have a bit of a girl-crush on Shoshana Berger. She’s a former WIRED editor turned editorial leader for companies like Notion and Ideo. She’s super smart and chic. And she’s the cofounder, with Nina Willdorf, of the Aged Intelligence Society, a buzzy events startup that hosts invite-only dinner parties where women “solve midlife problems using frontier tools.”

Berger and Willdorf having a refreshingly practical take on AI. “This is the most formative technology of our generation, and it’s here,” Willdorf says. “If you choose to sit it out, you are doing yourself a disservice.”

They also practice what they preach, especially as they juggle kids entering college, their own aging parents, and their increasingly in-demand business. “The midlife cognitive and emotional labor is so heavy and so intense that we need an assistant,” Berger says. “Actually, we need the National Guard, but AI will help in the interim.”

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

Zoë Schiffer: How did the Aged Intelligence Society start?

Shoshana Berger: Nina and I are both recovering magazine editors. We ended up at a friend's birthday party, and we were both obsessing over Claude, our new French lover. All the other women had glazed eyes while we were talking; they just weren’t part of the conversation. At some point I said, “Nina, I think this might be a business, because all these women need to be doing what we're doing.”

Nina Willdorf: We were super nervous before the first event …

Berger: We needed to take beta blockers and ashwagandha because we were like, wait, we're inviting all of these baller women, these really smart, incredibly accomplished women, and we’re not AI experts. But we have been doing what we usually did as reporters, which is test the tools in a way many women did not seem to be doing.

There's a million reasons for women to bow out of the conversation, and we were like, “no, no, no, you cannot bow out of this conversation if you are going to be relevant in the work world. You need to be fluent.”

Willdorf: We are not like pom-pom advocates for everything about this, but we feel committed to this idea that being involved is really important.

How do you set up expectations around the event for attendees?

Berger: There are rules of engagement for every party. One is we have an ideological parking lot. So if you want to talk about how AI is going to ruin everything, this is not the best space to do that. There are lots of ways in which we are also terrified about AI and find it can be quite menacing, especially for creatives like us, but that's not what we're here to do. We're here to gain fluency, test things out, and reduce our administrative load.

Willdorf: And eat some really good cheese and drink some wine.

Berger: We have everyone bring a laptop and a midlife problem. We call it a midlife quandary. We spend 15 minutes doing what we call library time. We all bring our laptops out, and you work on your problem. And Nina and I kind of walk around the room sherpa-ing people through the experience: “You might want to try this tool, try this LLM instead. Your prompt is feeling a little weak. You're not getting the answer that you like. Let's work on that and iterate.”

Willdorf: Sometimes people are like, “You know what? I don't really want AI to write for me.” And we're like, “Oh no, no, we don't either. Let us show you a couple other things you can think about.”

You’ve had a wait list for these parties since you started. How do you decide who gets to attend?

Willdorf: We have really found that the optimal number is between like eight and 12. When it gets bigger than that, it's a little bit chaotic. Our best events have been ones where there is a magical mix, and you have somebody that works in nonprofit next to somebody who works in design next to somebody who is home with the kids next to a CEO next to someone who works in philanthropy.

Most people, in my experience, cannot immediately identify a software-shaped problem in their life. How do you help people see an issue that AI could actually help with?

Berger: So much of what we're trying to do is show the pragmatic ways in which it's helping with midlife overload. We're often labeled the sandwich generation. We hate that term because it just sounds like we're the tasteless tomato in between two slabs of bread. But the real part of that is that we are dealing with parents who increasingly need help and kids who maybe are launching, maybe not.

One woman came to an event with a mother who was dealing with an Alzheimer's diagnosis, and she wanted to know whether her mother was qualified for long-term care because she had an insurance plan. Well, the insurance carrier sent her a 300-page packet. Who is going to read through that? This is how insurance companies keep you from using your insurance policy. So of course, she fed it to AI. And the AI came back saying, yes, your mother is absolutely qualified for care, and would you like me to write a letter to the insurance company to argue your case?

When I first started out as a consultant, I, in a typical female way, completely lowballed my consulting fee. I fed my proposal to Claude and said, “What's wrong with this? Make this bulletproof.” And it came back and said, “You're not charging enough for this scope of work.” And I was like, “Well, what should I charge?” And it said, “Start by doubling your rate.” I took a deep breath, and I did it. The client did not blink.

I feel that a lot of people don’t want to get near AI because they see slop and get the ick. But you’re talking about something different—using AI for economically valuable tasks.

Berger: The economic value piece is really important, because women have long suffered from being stuck at home as domestic laborers, because we weren't seen as economically valuable in the marketplace, right? So the idea behind Aged Intelligence Society is that if anyone needs to optimize their time, it's women.

In some ways, AI is the new washing machine or the new laundry or whatever it is. It's giving women many, many hours of the day back by offering them automation.

The reason we narrowed the aperture to people 40-plus is that we feel like the product-market fit for women who are over 40 is so spot on. One, because we have very discerning taste after many decades of honing it. We know how to ask really sharp questions. We have very strong bullshit meters, so we're not going to take just the first answer. We're going to keep probing and pushing.

And so we feel like in some ways this tool was built for people who have a certain level of intelligence and accrued experience. It's not really built for young people. It's not built for people who have not already gone through the failure and the friction of life and learned for themselves what makes for a good question.
