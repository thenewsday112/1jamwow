We Linked 150 of Ikea’s $10 Speakers to Make a Wall of Sound

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Ikea launched its super-cheap $10 Bluetooth Kallsup speakers in April, aside from the rock-bottom price and bright, white/green/pink colors, the claim that really caught WIRED’s ear was that the Swedish furniture brand said it was possible to chain 100 of these 2.75-inch sonic cubes together, should you so wish.

Now, because it’s highly unlikely any one household will ever own more than a handful of mini Bluetooth speakers, this is one of those consumer-tech statements that rarely gets tested. So we asked the good folks at Älmhult in Ikea headquarters if they’d let us do just this. To our surprise, Ikea not only agreed, but it also upped the ante, saying it might be possible to link up to 150 without breaking the connection, so that’s exactly how many it sent us. “No one has ever tried this, though,” an Ikea spokesperson said. “We haven’t.”

That is how we ended up building a literal wall of sound at our London office, stacking 150 Kallsups carefully on top of one another to test how many would chain-play from a smartphone. Sadly, one casualty during shipping from Sweden—a single Kallsup—despite lighting up correctly and looking ready to chain, made an ominous rattling sound as if its innards weren’t entirely secure. Sure enough, it refused to play ball (or anything else), so we ended up with 149 speakers.

The chain process itself is simple. Once you have paired a single Kallsup with your music device—in our case, an iPhone 16 Pro—and it’s up and running, simply power on another cube while playing and hold down the Play button until a chime tells you it’s in “multi-speaker” mode. It should then immediately start playing whatever the Bluetooth-connected Kallsup is playing.

Thanks to this near-effortless chaining process, linking up all these Kallsups took much less time than we'd expected, about 30 minutes. We stopped at 100 to test how loud the “system” sounded at that stage before resuming the build and moving on to the maximum amount. As the music continued while we chained the speakers, it would be obvious when the collective refused to add more cubes. But this didn’t happen, and the plucky Kallsups joined forces without grumbling right up to the full 149.

It’s tempting to preface the next few paragraphs with the phrase “for a $10 speaker,” because, despite Ikea’s vast buying power, experienced research and development team, and global presence, the ability to produce a tiny speaker that doesn’t sound absolutely horrible for less than a pint of half-decent craft beer is pretty incredible.

Each Kallsup has a single 1.8-inch, full-range driver powered by a 3-watt amplifier, with Bluetooth 5.3 support. No separate tweeter or woofer, just the one tiny driver.

On its own, the Kallsup speaker sounds fine. Yes, it lacks anything resembling the deep rumbling bass people talk about in audio reviews, but did we mention it’s a $10 speaker? It is a bit tinny, and you won’t want to push the volume very hard, but as a little desktop speaker, one by the side of the bed, or to throw in a suitcase, the Kallsup does the job. It doesn’t match the performance of one of our best Bluetooth speaker recommendations, but keep the cost in mind, and it’s hard to be disappointed.

Even at low background volumes, the Kallsups worked surprisingly well together, especially playing acoustic tracks. As we paired more speakers, the sound gradually grew nicely in scale. Nothing was overpowering or dominating, just the cumulative effect of all those tiny drivers moving small amounts of air.

With 149 individual 1.8-inch drivers, the combined surface area is roughly equivalent to one enormous 22-inch driver. Each Kallsup also has its own 3-watt amplifier, giving our wall a combined rated output of 447 watts. That doesn’t mean it behaves like a giant subwoofer or a conventional 447-watt speaker, but it explains why things got so loud.

But before we turned these up, if you will, to 149, we listened at 100, and when we pushed the volume, the collective Kallsups were seriously loud. But for the record, loud like a fire alarm, not a hi-fi system. With the wall complete, we worried the collective vibrations might send the whole thing crashing down. Disappointingly for the socials, it stayed resolute. Instead, we played Trans Lunar by Twin Dimensions (WIRED director of consumer tech and culture Mike Calore’s excellent band) and slowly increased the volume to maximum.

We almost wish we hadn’t. The noise was brain-numbingly loud as we blasted, somewhat appropriately, Richard Strauss’ Also Sprach Zarathustra. We couldn’t hear each other speak, and we could feel our internal organs vibrating. Any sense of fidelity disappeared, but—all power to the Kallsups—it worked. We’d successfully linked 149 Bluetooth speakers, though sadly we’re well short of the world record of 1,500 speakers set by Majority Audio in March 2026.

At more modest, realistic volumes, everything sounded considerably more composed, even if the music lacked levels of depth and detail. The overall lack of clarity could also be due to minor timing differences between the individual drivers.

At $1,490, a wall of Kallsups is quite an investment, and not one we’re recommending, but it is extremely impressive that we managed to sync and play music wirelessly through so many small, cheap speakers.

We also tested the wall alongside WIRED’s choice for best overall party speaker, the $500 LG Xboom Stage 501. Cranked up, both reached similarly ear-splitting, frankly uncomfortable volumes. But with two 5.25-inch woofers, two 2.5-inch full-range drivers, and clever digital signal processing, the LG delivered understandably fuller, deeper bass and a considerably more musical experience, as expected.

What’s remarkable about this experiment is that so little went wrong. Only one of the 150 speakers shipped didn’t work, clearly broken in transit. We managed to get all 149 sound cubes chained easily and quickly. And when we weren’t blasting the wall of sound at max volume, instead keeping levels moderate, the speakers sounded surprisingly competent as a collective.

However, there were hints of the Kallsup chaining capacity reaching its limit, as Ikea suspected. As we got beyond 100 units linked together, the incidence of failing to chain on the first try increased. Still, no speaker refused more than twice as we got toward the end of the build.

We also would have preferred indentations on the tops of the speakers, which would have given us a bit more confidence that the whole stack wasn’t going to collapse in a heap as volume ramped up (which it didn’t). Such a simple design feature would help whether you’re dealing with just a couple placed on top of each other, let alone 149—but this is splitting hairs.

Lastly, although this setup was impressively loud, no one in their right mind would do this in their own home. Ikea has been a good sport letting us try this out, but the ultimate, unsurprising lesson is: If you want high-quality audio, spend your money on a better speaker. As for the Kallsup? Simple to use, easy to chain, with approachable sound and clean design. You really can’t ask much more for $10.

More dads taking paid family leave for a new baby, sometimes as much as moms are

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Karlo Campana plays Magnatiles with his daughters Gianna and Giselle, while his 5-month-old son Gianluca watches. Campana is one of the growing number of California men taking advantage of the state's paid bonding leave program. Dania Maxwell for NPR hide caption

Karlo Campana is usually outnumbered these days. He has three young kids under age 4.

On a recent Saturday, he played a mini-game of basketball with his two older kids in their Southern California home, shooting a ball into a hoop on the door, while his baby boy watched from his bouncy chair.

This story was produced in partnership with KFF Health News.

Campana took paid time off in May after the birth of his son — just as he did for his daughters.

It's sometimes called "bonding" or "family" leave, to distinguish it from the paid leave ("maternity" or "disability" leave) that mothers can take to physically recover from childbirth.

"You need that adjustment period," he said. Campana, who works at an ad agency, said having the time off not to think about work — or worry about losing income during that time — was crucial for him and his family during a sleep-deprived period.

"My wife isn't doing it on her own," he said. "She doesn't feel like she's alone on this journey. She feels like she has support." He jokes that by kid No. 3, he thought he had nailed diaper changes without too much mess — but he was wrong.

"The benefits are I get to spend time with each of them when they were in the infant stage, the baby stage, and I get to hold them and, you know, change the poopy diapers," he said. " It's like the funny moments of, like, being a parent, like, 'This is it right here, you're in the thick of it.'"

Karlo Campana plays with his daughter Giselle and his infant son Gianluca at home on October 12 in Santa Ana, Calif. With three children under 4, paid paternity leave gave him something invaluable: time to bond with his kids and support his wife in the thick of early parenthood. Dania Maxwell for NPR hide caption

Campana is among a growing number of fathers who take leave to bond with a new child in the states that offer paid family leave programs — and are part of a larger cultural shift that has seen dads handle more caregiving duties.

In California — as in several other states — men make up about half of bonding claim applications. Last year, men even surpassed women — as in Colorado and Washington — for the first time in the state's history. This trend comes at a time when the federal government has said it wants Americans to have more babies, and families report affordability is an obstacle in raising them.

There's no federal law guaranteeing paid parental leave, but California is one of 14 states, plus Washington, D.C., that have passed legislation to enact paid family leave programs — and in many of those places, more dads are opting in.

"We're in a very different place in terms of our understanding of gender roles, of paternity leave, of dads' roles than we were 20-plus years ago," said Molly Weston Williamson, policy director at Paid Leave for All, an organization that advocates for paid family leave policies.

California's program offers, within a year, up to eight weeks of paid bonding leave for parents after a birth, adoption, or foster care placement. That's on top of six to eight weeks of paid disability leave that moms get to recover after birth.

Karlo Campana at dinner with his wife Lily and their three children. His experience reflects a broader shift across California, where fathers accounted for 51% of paid bonding leave claims in 2025, surpassing mothers for the first time. Dania Maxwell for NPR hide caption

Campana said he initially wasn't sure if he should take paternity leave because of lingering gender stereotypes.

" I felt guilty, for sure," Campana said. " I think dads maybe feel they need to be the provider. They need to be the ones that are only helping their family by bringing money or working."

Campana said that when he was growing up, his father worked three jobs. "It was hard for him to be present, and I think that's the one thing now — it's like, 'No, be present. Be there for your kids.'"

Research has shown that paternity leave confers benefits beyond allowing a father extra time to bond with a new child: It has positive effects on the whole family, including better health outcomes for both parents.

Paid parental leave has also been linked to lower odds of postpartum depression and a decrease in infant mortality rates.

In California, men accounted for 51% of bonding claims filed in 2025. It's a massive shift from when the state program started in 2004, and men made up about 18% of claim applications.

States fund their paid family and medical leave programs with different combinations of employer and employee contributions; California is among six states where family leave is entirely funded by employee payroll deductions.

Karlo Campana poses for a portrait with his wife Lily and their three kids (from left to right) Gianna, Gianluca, and Giselle. Dania Maxwell for NPR hide caption

In addition to changing gender norms, Williamson said other factors are likely contributing to the increase in claims by men in the state: greater awareness about the program, and recent changes to the benefit.

In 2025, the state boosted the amount of income a worker can recoup while on family leave. Now, in California and in other states, the leave benefit can be as high as 90% of their regular salary.

"That makes it a realistic option for a lot more families, and I think we're seeing dads and moms responding to that change," Williamson said. Still, she said, there's a lot the new data about dads isn't telling us.

"We're getting one particular vantage point into this data, which is useful and valuable, but it's only telling us sort of part of the story, and we'd need other kinds of information to better understand the full picture," Williamson said.

"Is it that more dads are working and therefore are potentially eligible for these benefits? Is it that women are deciding not to take leave?"

While private employers can offer their own paid leave as a benefit, this data also reflects only people who took leave through California's paid family leave program.

Karlo Campana washes Giselle's hands after dinner on October 12 at home in Santa Ana, Calif. "My wife isn't doing it on her own," he said of his decision to take paid leave from his job after the birth of their third child. "She doesn't feel like she's alone on this journey. She feels like she has support." Dania Maxwell for NPR hide caption

A spokesperson for the California Employment Development Department, which administers the state's family leave program, said the department couldn't provide definitive reasons for the increase in male bonding claims.

One thing is clear: The growth in these states comes as more parents are feeling strained economically.

President Trump has said, "We want more babies," and has promoted policy initiatives like expanding access to in vitro fertilization and offering investment accounts for newborns.

"People really are very worried about the affordability of kids," said Jeremy Pope, a professor of political science at Brigham Young University, and a principal investigator of the American Family Survey.

Karlo Campana plays with his new son Gianluca as his wife Lily prepares dinner in Santa Ana, Calif. "You need that adjustment period," he said. Dania Maxwell for NPR hide caption

According to the latest survey in 2025, 7 in 10 Americans said raising children had become unaffordable, according to the survey. It was the biggest single-year jump since the survey started more than a decade ago.

"I don't think they want government involved in the decision to have kids," Pope said. "But they worry a lot about how much it costs and they would like to see ways of making those costs smaller or getting help with those costs."

The Pew Research Center found in a recent survey of full-time working parents who are not self-employed, 84% said paid parental, family, or medical leave would be "extremely or very helpful" to them, but only half said it was available to them.

Parece un acto simple, pero pedir ayuda no siempre lo es. Todos necesitamos ayuda alguna vez: quizás nos sintamos abrumados por un reto laboral y nos vendría bien la perspectiva de un compañero. O necesitamos flexibilidad de horario porque estamos pasando por un mal momento personal. O sencillamente nos iría genial hablar con alguien.

“El problema es sentir que nunca es suficiente”: el coste emocional de ser muy perfeccionista

Pese a todo, muchas personas no piden ayuda a menos que estén en una situación desesperada, porque no quieren molestar a nadie o porque temen que les digan que no. Otras piensan que pedir ayuda o algún favor, por pequeño que sea, nos hace parecer incompetentes e incluso vulnerables. Muchas veces, el problema es que no sabemos cómo pedirla, así que no lo hacemos.

“Pedir ayuda implica reconocer que necesitamos algo de otra persona y esto nos coloca en una posición de cierta vulnerabilidad”, reconoce Franc Rubio Rodríguez, psicólogo. Aunque sea un pequeño favor, “pueden aparecer pensamientos como ‘no quiero molestar’, ‘debería poder hacerlo solo’ o ‘van a pensar que no soy capaz’”, admite Rubio.

Detrás de la dificultad de pedir ayuda suele haber “vergüenza o miedo a mostrarnos necesitados porque hemos asociado mucho la autonomía con ‘poder con todo’, cuando en realidad una persona autónoma también puede reconocer sus límites y apoyarse en los demás cuando lo necesita”, reconoce Rubio.

Solemos pensar que la gente rechazará nuestras peticiones o nos menospreciará, así que no pedimos ayuda tan a menudo como deberíamos. Pero la negativa a hacerlo “depende de cada persona, y muchas veces no nos asustan tanto el ‘no’ como lo que interpretamos que ese ‘no’ dice sobre nosotros. Podemos vivirlo como ‘no le importo’, ‘soy una carga’ o ‘no debería haber pedido nada’”, afirma Rubio.

Sin embargo, lejos de esta creencia y tal y como demuestra esta investigación publicada en Psychological Science, por lo general solemos subestimar la disposición de los demás a ayudar, cuando en realidad se ha demostrado que cuando las personas realizan actos de bondad, aumenta el bienestar.

“Existe una exigencia interna de tener que poder con todo, lo que nos lleva muchas veces a ser más duros con nosotros mismos de lo que seríamos con otra persona”, afirma Rubio para referirse a otro de los motivos por los que nos cuesta pedir ayuda. Porque, cuando es un amigo el que necesita ayuda, “probablemente no pensaríamos que es débil, pero si somos nosotros quienes la necesitamos, sí podemos juzgarnos de esa manera”, reconoce el experto.

De ahí que sea importante no solo preguntarnos “‘¿me da miedo que me diga que no?’ sino también ‘¿qué significaría para mí que me dijera que no?’”.

La ayuda que necesitamos en un momento determinado puede variar desde algo relativamente sencillo, como cubrir un turno de trabajo, que nos lleven a algún sitio o pedir consejo, hasta algo más importante, como ayuda para el cuidado de un hijo, un pequeño préstamo económico o apoyo para un problema médico. Sea cual sea el reto, contar con la ayuda de otros puede ahorrarnos tiempo y esfuerzo, además de brindarnos el apoyo emocional que tanto necesitamos.

Para Rubio, el objetivo no es tanto el hecho de que pedir ayuda nos haga sentir cierta incomodidad, si bien “al principio puede aparecer vergüenza, miedo o sensación de estar molestando: podemos aprender a hacerlo incluso sintiendo eso”. Para empezar, recomienda recurrir a “personas de confianza con peticiones pequeñas y concretas: ‘¿Puedes acompañarme?’, ‘¿Tienes diez minutos para escucharme’?, ‘¿Me puedes echar una mano con esto?’. Es más fácil empezar así que esperar a encontrarnos en una situación límite”, reconoce Rubio.

Acercarse a la persona correcta en el momento adecuado nos ayudará a que el favor sea concedido y que nadie se resienta por nuestra petición. Por tanto, antes de contactar con alguien es fundamental tomarnos un tiempo para considerar si esa persona realmente nos puede ayudar.

Otra de las claves que apunta Rubio es detenernos antes y preguntarnos: ‘¿Qué necesito realmente?’ y ‘¿Qué me estoy diciendo por necesitarlo?’. “Muchas veces descubrimos que el problema no está en la petición sino en cómo nos juzgamos por tener una necesidad”, explica.

Las relaciones se basan en dar y recibir, por lo que no debemos pensar en aceptar ayuda como una deuda incómoda. Porque, como reconoce Rubio, “con el tiempo vamos acumulando experiencias nuevas: pedimos algo, la otra persona responde con normalidad y comprobamos que necesitar apoyo no nos hace menos capaces”.

Aprender a recibir ayuda a veces cuesta. Pero, una vez la pedimos y nos la dan, ¿cómo podemos gestionar la vulnerabilidad que podemos sentir al dejarnos cuidar? Para Rubio, recibir ayuda puede resultar incómodo: algunas personas sienten que tienen que devolver el favor, le quitan importancia o demuestran que en realidad no necesitaban tanto esa ayuda. “En estos momentos puede ser útil hacer algo aparentemente sencillo: permitirse recibirla, no intentar compensarla inmediatamente, no justificarse y poder decir simplemente: ‘gracias, me ha ayudado mucho’”, afirma el psicólogo.

La capacidad de pedir y aceptar ayuda es una valiosa habilidad porque cargar con una gran responsabilidad sin los recursos o el apoyo adecuados puede provocar agotamiento. De hecho, las investigaciones en este campo han demostrado que la falta de apoyo social puede estar directamente relacionada con una mala salud mental y física.

“Dejarnos cuidar supone aceptar durante un momento que no tenemos que sostenerlo todo nosotros solos. Y eso puede generar mucha vulnerabilidad, especialmente en personas acostumbradas a ser quienes ayudan o quienes siempre se mantienen fuertes”, matiza Rubio.

Sin embargo, el experto apunta que puede ser una experiencia muy importante. “Comprobar que podemos mostrar una necesidad, que alguien puede responder a ella y que eso no nos convierte en débiles ni nos hace perder autonomía es importante, como lo es entender que sentirnos capaces de cuidar de nosotros mismos y permitir que otros nos cuiden no son cosas incompatibles”, concluye Rubio.

Anthropic was beaten to the punch by a salad dressing company

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What do you do when you think your company’s technology may fundamentally alter the fabric of the economy—or rend it apart entirely?

Anthropic, which is widely rumored to be approaching a record-setting IPO, apparently has an answer: Cut the public in on a piece of whatever comes next.

Last week, the company announced that it would dedicate 5% of its shares to a company-owned public benefit trust.

If Anthropic’s products are as lucrative as its sky-high valuation suggests, those shares could be worth billions on day one—and could grow to be worth hundreds of billions over time.

By my calculations, if Google had done the same thing before its 2004 IPO, the shares it set aside for the good of humanity would be worth $184 billion today.

So Anthropic’s plan is a bold one. Depending on where you sit, you’ll likely see it either as a genuine effort to give back to the world, or an attempt to shake regulators off the company’s back—or stop pitchfork-wielding mobs from storming its lovely new office building in SoMA if things get really bad.

And though the scale is unprecedented, it’s not a new idea. In fact, Anthropic was beaten to the punch by a movie star turned salad dressing tycoon. 

Yet Anthropic’s approach has far more concerning implications.

Exploiting Yourself

By age 57, actor Paul Newman was doing pretty well for himself. He had starred in almost 50 movies—including classics like Cool Hand Luke and Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid—and was on the path to an eventual Oscar.

Somehow, he’d also managed to find the time to win second place in the grueling 24 Hours of Le Mans auto race, among other wild and wonderful accomplishments.

When someone suggested he slap his iconic blue-eyed visage on bottles of salad dressing to boost sales, Newman apparently thought, Are you crazy?

He said the idea felt like “exploitation.” But then Newman realized that Hollywood had always been exploiting him for his good looks and boyish, all-American charm. Why not do it for a good cause?

And why not do it as fully as possible? In Newman’s words, “Why not, really, go to the fullest length, and the silliest length, in exploiting yourself, and turn the proceeds back to the community?”

So that’s what he did. At nearly 60 years old, he launched Newman’s Own—a salad dressing company that would donate 100% of its proceeds to charity. The company eventually expanded, putting his name and likeness on tons of other products in the same act of “exploitation.”

Today, as any Whole Foods shopper can attest, Newman’s Own still exists (and makes some pretty respectable salsa). 

More importantly, the idea worked. The company has reportedly generated over $600 million in charitable donations since Newman founded it in 1982—a massive sum for a company the actor started “on a bit of a lark.”

Altruism, Full Circle

At the time, Newman seems to have been mostly messing around. It’s unlikely that he knew his company would prove so successful in its charitable mission.

But a far more concrete version of the philosophy Newman intuited now drives the actions of companies like Anthropic.

Newman called it “shameless exploitation in pursuit of the common good.” Today, we call it effective altruism. As The New York Times explored earlier this year, it’s a mindset that’s absolutely pervasive in the world of frontier AI.

The idea is essentially to amass as much wealth and power as possible, with the goal of giving it all away, or otherwise turning your influence to the good of humanity.

It sounds nice on paper. But the philosophy, as practiced nowadays, can have a dark side, as when it allegedly drove “crypto king” Sam Bankman-Fried to defraud his way to riches, the better to give them to causes of his choosing.

It could easily have a dark side in the AI world, too—with potentially far graver consequences.

Even if Anthropic gave away all its shares à la Newman, the drive to make those shares as valuable as possible for humanity could motivate the company to scrimp on safety or to callously build things that, by its own admission, risk destroying the world.

Anthropic’s desire to share some of its massive wealth is probably a good one. But as the humans stuck holding the bag if AI goes horribly wrong, we shouldn’t let Anthropic’s gesture blind us to the much bigger questions of AI safety, creative rights, and societal impacts that still surround its work.

It’s a good thing for the powerful and influential to consider their impact on the world. But Anthropic should take a page from Newman’s book and remember that altruism isn’t just about money, nor do the positive ends always justify the means.

There’s not much point in setting aside cash to help humans, for example, if your technology has inadvertently stamped them out.

Mais au-delà des moyens et de la proximité, c’est le contact humain qui semble faire défaut. Ainsi, si 53 % des Français jugent facile de réaliser leurs démarches sur Internet, 73 % estiment difficile d’obtenir une réponse adaptée à leurs besoins et 75 % un retour rapide. Plus frappant encore, 81 % peinent à obtenir un rendez-vous avec un agent et 87 % à trouver un interlocuteur humain. Le numérique facilite certaines formalités, mais, d’évidence, laisse souvent les usagers sans accompagnement face aux situations complexes. On touche là aux limites de la volonté d’avoir un État 100 % numérique.

Ces frustrations pèseront-elles dans les urnes ? En tout cas, 87 % des répondants assurent qu’ils tiendront compte des propositions des candidats sur les services publics lors de la présidentielle de 2027, dont 67 % « beaucoup ».

Autre résultat notable, dans un contexte de déficits publics et de recherche d’économies, 72 % des Français privilégient l’amélioration de la qualité des services, même si elle suppose de leur consacrer davantage de moyens. Seuls 24 % préfèrent réduire la dépense, quitte à accepter une moindre qualité. Cela ne vaut toutefois pas adhésion à une hausse des impôts – la question n’a pas été posée – mais c’est un net démenti aux candidats comme David Lisnard qui veulent s’inspirer des méthodes du président argentin Javier Milei et sa tronçonneuse contre les services publics…

Les Français sont également 62 % à souhaiter confier davantage de responsabilités aux collectivités locales pour organiser les services au plus près des besoins, contre 36 % qui préfèrent maintenir ces compétences au niveau de l’État afin de garantir les mêmes prestations partout.

Enfin, les priorités assignées au prochain président sont sans ambiguïté : la santé arrive largement en tête (83 %), devant l’Éducation nationale (67 %) et la justice (54 %), bien avant la police et la gendarmerie (31 %). Exactement les trois services que les Français jugent aujourd’hui parmi les plus défaillants.

Au final, les Français ne réclament pas moins de services publics, mais des services publics qui fonctionnent mieux. Reste à savoir comment les candidats concilieront cette attente avec les contraintes budgétaires.

He still owns the British brand founded in 1934 by his father, who turned to selling and manufacturing leather goods in the UK after fleeing Nazi Germany via Paris and a stint in the film industry.

Ettinger began making wallets and sourcing leather items, and today the company supplies a range of luxury goods to wealthy customers, including King Charles and Queen Camilla whose royal warrants they hold.

The company’s bestseller is still a wallet, but they also sell leather pill containers, cigar cases, picture frames and various accoutrements for dog owners, from treat cases to dog-waste bag holders, all of which can be personalised with initials.

The brand sits in a niche between the big label luxury and high street with a British hand-crafted leather tote bag for just under £800 compared with more than £2,000 charged by a big swinger such as Louis Vuitton.

An array of machines resembling pastry-cutters are used to punch out leather shapes that are then glued by hand with a brush to build up each item with trims and linings.

The tiny seams around the leather edges are thinned down with a shaving machine, known as a skiver, and turned inward before being stitched with a sewing machine. Each piece is then carefully checked and trimmed using a scalpel and sharp scissors before being polished and wrapped in tissue paper.

Gerry Ettinger, the founder, began by using an array of manufacturers before buying his original factory at the heart of London’s leather-working trade in Clerkenwell near the Smithfield meat market in the 1950s.

Robert Ettinger says: “When I was a kid, there were dozens and dozens of small factories, making leather goods, and they all specialised. One made suitcases, and one made handbags, and one made wallets.”

As the area gentrified in the 1990s, the Ettingers decided to move and bought out one of their suppliers, James Homer in Walsall, established in 1890. It was then mostly making corporate promotional leather goods. Staff were trained up and new machinery brought in to make the high standard of goods expected by Ettinger’s new international clientele led by the Japanese.

Walsall has been a centre of leather-working from the middle ages. It was focused on saddle-making with more than 100 firms operating in the town by the end of the 19th century.

As the use of horses in everyday life declined, the factories began expanding into making other leather goods, ranging from bicycle saddles, car upholstery and furnishings to bags, gloves and purses.

The industry thrived during the first world war when there was high demand for military saddle wear but began to suffer in the 1950s as it faced competition from abroad and from human-made alternatives to leather.

Still, by 1970 there were about 50 leather goods manufacturers in Walsall, and the town produced around half of the British-made fancy leather goods, according to the Wolverhampton Heritage and History Society.

Tracy, expertly glueing and building Ettinger’s elaborate cigar cases before they are sewn, has been at the company for 15 years and working in the leather industry since she left school 40 years ago.

“When we were that age it was all leather work. You could walk out of one job and into another on the same day,” she says.

That history is recognised in street names and the local Saddlers Centre shopping mall and there is a leather skills training centre which offers courses in bridle making.

Launer, whose customers included the late Queen Elizabeth II, J&E Sedgwick, the bridle maker, and small leather goods firm E Hulme, founded in the 1920s, continue to make leather goods near Ettinger’s factory. However, all employ only small numbers of people and the local industry is a shadow of its former self.

Even Walsall’s leather museum, housed in a former factory that dates back to the 1880s, is now closed amid disputed plans to move it to a modern city centre site.

That reflects a rapid decline in the leather goods manufacturing industry across the UK with just 7,000 people employed in it today compared with 37,000 30 years ago.

Ettinger itself had to cut its staff numbers by about seven after the Covid pandemic hit trade.

Robert Ettinger says he never really considered shifting production overseas, despite most of his customers coming from outside the UK.

“We might have made more profit, but then we would also have much more competition because when you’ve got 50 companies bringing in a purse from the far east, then it’s all about price.”

He says the gambit is now paying off as a younger generation increasingly appreciates British craft, with online sales up 30% on last year and UK sales up by a fifth, with shoppers aged from their early 20s.

“A lot of younger people are now thinking that they want something that will last, that won’t go out of fashion in three months’ time. Yes, it costs more, but it lasts for 15 or 20 years, and can be repaired. I’ve just repaired my wallet which is 17 years old.

“Our products are affordable luxury. A purse or wallet for £200 or £300, it’s the price of some trainers and they’re not made in the UK. So it’s not out of reach of most people.”

Still, expanding production in the UK might be tricky. Billingham says it is hard to find and train new staff. Even those who attend Walsall’s Leather Skills Centre are more likely to go into making car upholstery or saddles.

“We are all getting old now, that’s a problem, we are from 40s to 60s. If you find someone working for somebody else you take them,” says Billingham.

“To take five to 10 people on would be great but the reality is they are not there.”

Robert Ettinger says the UK should look at how young people are trained into the trade in mainland Europe, where he was sent by his father to serve an apprenticeship.

“They spend a few years really learning a trade, everything from making leather goods, to being a chimney sweep, or a butcher, or baker. When you pass all the apprenticeship exams, you then go on to do more and become a master, a Meister. We don’t have that here. So, it’s harder for us to take on younger people.”

He is optimistic things could change. “People might go into jobs which they don’t really enjoy or get bored of doing, and then they join us, and, you know, it’s like a family in our factory.”
