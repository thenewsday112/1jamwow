Arctic Wildlife Research Suffers as Geopolitical Competition Grows

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A polar bear on sea ice in the Arctic Ocean. Sebnem Coskun / Anadolu via Getty Images

As national security and the hunt for rare minerals come to dominate Arctic agendas, governments are slashing support for wildlife research. Scientists say the loss of monitoring is unprecedented and comes as climate change dramatically reshapes life in the Far North.

Decades ago, one of the world’s largest subpopulations of nesting eider ducks spent its summers on tiny Mitivik Island, near the mouth of Hudson Bay. But as concern grew, in the 1990s, over their steep decline globally, the Canadian government set up a rudimentary research station near the island in the hopes of forestalling a collapse.

The modest investment paid enormous dividends over the next 30 years as Inuit partners from nearby Southampton Island, who hunt common and king eiders for food and feathers, worked with scientists from all over the world to solve the mystery of where the birds spent their winters and the causes of, and remedy for, their initial decline. In the field for months at a time, researchers also gained critical insights into how climate change, disease, diminishing sea ice, and polar bears were affecting bird populations.

As remarkably successful as the pioneering research station was, though, Canada’s Environment and Climate Change department quietly shut it down in 2024. By then, the number of nesting eiders had crashed again, from a high of 8,000 pairs to just a few hundred.

Today, federal research continues in only three of the 14 migratory bird sanctuaries in the Arctic. Hundreds of other wildlife research endeavors in the circumpolar world, including in Greenland and the U.S., have also been shuttered in recent years.

Like many other agencies and programs operated by Environment and Climate Change Canada, the Canadian Wildlife Service has suffered from the high cost of conducting research in the Arctic, the increasing likelihood of polar bear encounters as sea ice recedes, and a series of crushing funding cuts. In the most recent blow, the Canadian government in 2025 committed to reducing the Environment and Climate Change workforce by 10 percent.

In the past, funding to monitor wildlife in the Arctic often had to be justified by its relevance to oil and gas development and how roads, pipelines, and oil wells would affect animals such as migrating caribou, according to Larry Hinzman, an expert in Arctic change at the University of Alaska, Fairbanks. But as the search for Arctic oil and gas began to ramp down in the late 2000s, due to low natural gas prices and breakthroughs in fracking for oil and gas that spurred new production in the south, climate change became a priority.

The current circumpolar decline in funding, Hinzman said, is not just about rising costs. It stems from governments shifting priorities once again, this time from climate change to national security issues and the search for rare minerals. According to the Harvard Kennedy School’s Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs, geopolitical tensions are also undermining the work of the Arctic Council, an international forum comprising the eight Arctic countries that plays a key role in monitoring environmental changes in the region.

Wildlife technician Uli Steiner tags a king eider netted on Mitivik Island before research efforts were halted in 2024. Grant Gilchrist

While support for research in the Arctic wanes, Canada is investing $405 million to advance the construction of a new highway to the Arctic coast to facilitate resource development and connect remote communities, part of a $40 billion plan to assert sovereignty over its Arctic territory.

Research in Alaska has been particularly hard hit by the suspension of hundreds of climate-based collaborations with Russia following its invasion of Ukraine, and by the closure last year of the Arctic Research Consortium of the United States after the National Science Foundation eliminated its operating grants. Between 2021 and 2024, the number of U.S. research publications on climate change in Greenland fell by 32 percent. And in July the Greenland Institute of Natural Resources, the territory’s leading environmental research center, temporarily halted any new collaboration with U.S. partners when President Donald Trump threatened to take over the island.

“There have always been ebbs and flows in the support for environmental monitoring in the Arctic,” said Hinzman. “But what we are seeing now is unprecedented. It’s heartbreaking because we need to know more than ever how to respond to a warming Arctic that is affecting almost every facet of life.”

The decline in Arctic research comes at a time when at least 96 Arctic and sub-Arctic mammal and bird species may be responding to warmer winters, earlier spring snowmelt, and profound landscape changes by migrating earlier in the year and traveling farther afield, sometimes in new directions.

But dozens of studies have shown that these journeys don’t always lead to more food. And they often expose birds to additional parasites and pathogens and put them at greater risk of predation. The new migrations also happen at a time when critical stopover sites — like the Salton Sea, in California — are being badly degraded by drought and development.

Polar bears and eiders are among the many animals that appear to be struggling with rapidly warming northern marine environments, from the Baltic to the Canadian Arctic Ocean to the eastern seaboard, stretching from Newfoundland to Maine. But without researchers in the field, it’s difficult to predict how or whether these species may adapt.

The situation was far different when eiders began their mysterious worldwide decline in the 1990s. Then, researchers working in Mitivik, in the East Bay Migratory Bird Sanctuary, which would later be named the Qaqsauqtuuq Migratory Bird Sanctuary, implanted satellite transmitters into the abdomens of eiders and learned where they were overwintering. The findings led to tighter regulations on eider hunting in Canada and Greenland that dramatically boosted eider numbers.

That is, until they crashed again due to disease and polar bear predation. In 2005, Grant Gilchrist, the founder of the research station, and his colleagues identified an outbreak of avian cholera — which is rare in the Arctic and likely linked to global warming — that would kill as many as 3,000 nesting females in 2006 and about half the population over the eight seasons it spread through the nesting colony.

U.S. soldiers conduct cold-weather training near Fairbanks, Alaska, in February, after President Trump threatened to invade Greenland. Victor J. Blue / Bloomberg via Getty Images

Gilchrist was also on hand to witness how receding sea ice, brought on by climate change, forced polar bears onto land just when the eiders were sitting on their eggs. “The bears were like grazing cows leisurely moving through a pasture, stopping to feed [on eggs] whenever they were hungry,” he said. And herring gulls, which can’t force an eider off its nest, learned to eat the eggs that the bears had missed while the eiders were away.

Canadian Wildlife Service scientist Jennifer Provencher and her colleagues discovered that even though the eiders nesting in Mitivik hosted larger-than-expected communities of gastrointestinal worms and flukes, which benefit from warming temperatures, the parasites reduced the ducks’ exposure to contaminants such as mercury and lead. The parasites appear to absorb and clear contaminants from eider tissues. This is significant because mercury and lead, which are leaching from melting permafrost in many parts of the Arctic, can result in reproductive failure in ducks, and they can be harmful to humans who rely on ducks for food. The Inuit have higher levels of mercury than the general Canadian population.

Other researchers found that eiders, like some other animals exposed to deadly disease, eventually developed an immunity to avian cholera.

“Had we not been there, we would never have been able to document any of this,” said Gilchrist, who says he has little doubt that most of what Mitivik researchers discovered is happening in other parts of the Arctic.

In the past, Indigenous hunters have reported dramatic changes in the Canadian Arctic, such as sharp declines in ivory gull numbers; orcas moving into the region and killing narwhal and bowhead whales; and grizzly bears migrating north, mating with polar bears, and producing hybrids. But it is almost impossible to say what is going on in the vast areas that are too far afield for Inuit hunters without putting scientists in the field. In Arctic Canada, there are approximately 150,000 people living in an area that is about half the size of the Lower 48 states. Only a fraction of these people lives above the Arctic Circle.

Environment and Climate Change Canada did not respond to questions about funding cuts and the cessation of research in Mitivik. In a statement, the department said it is “committed to the long-term conservation of biological biodiversity and abundance,” noting that experts are working on new research programs with territorial governments and Indigenous organizations, and with new technologies to help track and understand birds and other animals, including polar bears.

Still, Andrew Derocher, a University of Alberta scientist who has conducted research over the past 40 years on polar bears in Norway, Canada, and Alaska, notes that support for polar bear research in Canada is at an all-time low. Determining how polar bears are faring in Canada — which is home to 13 of the world’s 19 polar bear populations — is becoming increasingly difficult, he says, if not impossible, because of a lack of government funding.

Eiders found dead after an avian cholera outbreak on Mitivik Island in 2006. Grant Gilchrist

“We’ve pretty much stopped any research beyond the long-term monitoring in Western Hudson Bay and a project in James Bay” funded by a private foundation, he says. “There is almost no other research on polar bears in Canada now.”

Ornithologist Mark Mallory spent 12 years conducting research at migratory bird nesting sites across the Canadian Arctic while working for the Canadian Wildlife Service, but he left that job for a research chair at Acadia University in part because of the high cost of conducting research in the polar regions and the endless struggle to secure consistent funding. Mallory is still very much interested in what is happening in the Arctic, but his years in the field and as co-editor-in-chief of the journal Arctic Science have given him a broader perspective on the challenges scientists face when trying to figure out how a warming climate is affecting wildlife.

“No credible scientist would deny that not enough is being invested in long-term monitoring,” he says. “But the bigger problem is not enough is being done to act on what we know is happening… Do we really think 10 or 20 more years of data showing ranges shrinking north, or clutch sizes reducing, or skinnier species is going to change anything in the minds of politicians or the public?”

U.S. push for Greenland’s minerals faces harsh Arctic realities. Read more.

Gilchrist appreciates Mallory’s frustrations with governments that refuse to act on climate change, but he — like many Arctic researchers — still believes that long-term monitoring with Inuit partners is essential. “Somebody should still be out in nature monitoring wildlife and the natural environment, and quantifying changes that are occurring or not occurring,” he said.

He doesn’t know whether the eiders of Mitivik Island will eventually return or whether they have found safer areas to nest. Because of the high costs of operating at Qaqsauqtuuq and the increased odds of running into polar bears, Gilchrist is now focusing his research at a site on Baffin Island, where it is cheaper to work and where there are fewer bears.

Frustrated by Losses in the Courts, Missouri Republicans Want to Change How Top Judges Are Selected

Some 86 years ago, Missouri voters enshrined a system for selecting top judges that would remove political influence from the courts. And almost immediately, it came under attack.

Under what has come to be known as the Missouri Plan, lawyers help choose a slate of three candidates from which the governor must make an appointment for the state Supreme Court, appeals courts and, in some populous areas, the circuit courts.

In the first years after adoption, it faced pushback from party bosses who had historically used judicial seats for political patronage. They said it gave too much power to the lawyers. Voters, though, decisively defeated a repeal measure in 1942.

More recently, the conservative activist Leonard Leo, who viewed Missouri as a crucial battleground, tried in 2007 to persuade Republican Gov. Matt Blunt to reject a slate of Supreme Court candidates — part of a broader effort to weaken a judicial selection system that had become a model for some two dozen states around the country.

Blunt resisted and picked one of the three judges from the approved slate.

Then, in 2012, GOP lawmakers put a proposed overhaul before voters that, if approved, would have given the governor more control. Voters, by a 3-to-1 margin, turned back the plan.

Now the attacks are back, driven by a new grievance.

At President Donald Trump’s behest, Republican lawmakers redrew the state’s congressional map in a mid-decade gerrymander. The goal: to give the GOP a shot at winning seven of Missouri’s congressional seats, instead of the six it now holds.

But voters gathered enough signatures to force a referendum on the map and put it on hold. While Secretary of State Denny Hoskins tried to keep the referendum off the ballot, the Missouri Supreme Court overruled him. It ordered the old map restored until voters decide on the new one.

Hoskins, citing a federal court ruling that contradicted the state’s highest court, directed officials to use the new map anyway, prompting Chief Justice Brent Powell to demand that Hoskins appear in court to show why he shouldn’t be held in contempt.

Fourteen Republican lawmakers responded by threatening to impeach “responsible members of this Court” for “willful neglect of duty, incompetency and misconduct” if the justices held Hoskins in contempt; the court did so anyway. Hoskins backed down and instructed local election officials to distribute ballots using the old map; the justices decided not to have him arrested.

The drama played out as Republicans in Missouri and elected officials nationwide have sought to weaken institutions that limit their power. In North Carolina, the Republican-dominated General Assembly stripped authority from Democratic governors. In Wisconsin, Republicans threatened to impeach a newly elected Supreme Court justice to protect gerrymandered maps. And in New York, Democrats bypassed an independent commission to pass a gerrymander that favored them, but the state’s highest court struck it down.

Rather than electing top judges, as Illinois does, or giving the governor primary responsibility for choosing them, like California, Missouri selects its appellate and Supreme Court judges through a commission made up of three lawyers, one elected by lawyers in each appellate district; three non-lawyers appointed by the governor, one from each district; and a state Supreme Court judge selected by the court. After they are appointed, judges face a retention vote at the first general election after completing one year in office, then every 12 years, with voters deciding whether they keep their positions.

In response to the controversy, a number of Missouri Republicans have attacked the system. U.S. Rep. Jason Smith called for the state to switch to electing its Supreme Court judges. Republican Party executive director Miles Ross declared on X, “The Missouri Plan is done.” Dan Seitz, chief of staff to Sen. Brad Hudson, called for replacing it with a system in which the governor could nominate judges directly, subject to Senate confirmation.

State Rep. Cathy Jo Loy, a Carthage Republican who has introduced legislation in each of the past two sessions to replace the Missouri Plan with gubernatorial appointment and Senate confirmation, said she intends to file a new proposal Dec. 1, the first day lawmakers can file bills for next year’s session. Loy said her opposition to the system predates the recent clashes with the Supreme Court. But she acknowledged that those defeats could give the proposal new momentum.

“There will probably be more appetite because there are several senators that feel the same way,” she said.

Republicans, who hold every statewide elected office, supermajorities in both chambers of the legislature and six of the state’s eight congressional seats, seem to have suddenly found the limits of their power. At the ballot box, voters have overwhelmingly rejected major pieces of the party’s agenda, including an effort to weaken their power to bypass the legislature by amending the constitution or putting laws passed by lawmakers to a statewide vote.

Time and again, when elected officials have sought to limit voters’ use of the ballot initiative, the courts have protected voters’ use of direct democracy. Courts repeatedly rewrote ballot summaries prepared by Republican state officials that they found unfair or misleading, including language describing the initiative that ultimately established abortion rights in 2024.

By the end of that year, after the Missouri Supreme Court rejected a last-minute effort by Republican lawmakers and anti-abortion activists to keep the abortion measure off the ballot, Senate President Pro Tem Cindy O’Laughlin was calling to repeal the Missouri Plan. She proposed replacing the system for selecting Supreme Court and appellate judges with a nonpartisan election. But her bill stalled in committee.

Then came Hoskins. The former state representative and senator from west-central Missouri took office as secretary of state in January 2025 and brought a more combative approach to overseeing elections and ballot measures. Hoskins made a bright-red sport coat his sartorial trademark, often posing with a wood baseball bat and speaking about politics as a series of battles and himself as a conservative warrior.

Hoskins didn’t respond to questions sent to his office. He has previously said that he disagreed with any suggestion that he and Republican leaders were defying the will of voters. He hasn’t publicly called for changing how Missouri selects Supreme Court judges, but his office’s official X social media account reposted Smith’s message calling for the end of the Missouri Plan.

Gov. Mike Kehoe also complained, saying in a post on X that “unelected judges” have shown a “lack of respect for the legislative process.” On a radio show the next day, Kehoe acknowledged that changing how judges are selected hadn’t even been on his radar before the ruling.

“But I think it’s definitely something a lot of people are going to start asking about,” he said. “And I’m not sure the judges across our state, whether Supreme Court or other judges, really wanted that on people’s radar screen.”

Kehoe’s office said it would not respond to questions from ProPublica.

Although some Republicans have derided the state Supreme Court as being a bastion of liberal activists, five of the Supreme Court’s seven judges were appointed by Republican governors. Among them is Powell, whom the then-chair of the state GOP hailed as a “good conservative” before Republican Gov. Eric Greitens selected him in 2017.

Powell, who also serves as chair of the judicial selection commission, declined to comment for this story. In remarks on Sept. 17 to the joint annual meeting of the Missouri Bar and the Judicial Conference of Missouri, he warned of “threats from those who seek to detract from the ideals of our republic and to undermine faith in our sacred institutions — including our legal profession and our courts.”

“It is incumbent on all of us as lawyers and judges to rise up and defend our constitutions and the rule of law,” he added.

Whether there’s enough support to repeal the Missouri Plan remains to be seen. Peverill Squire, a professor emeritus of political science at the University of Missouri, said some of the bills that would eliminate the Missouri Plan might go to committee hearings, “but my guess is there probably won’t be enough appetite among enough Republicans to really want to put it on the ballot.”

Any change would ultimately have to be approved by the voters, something Loy acknowledged could be difficult after Republicans’ recent losses at the ballot box.

But the renewed calls have also mobilized the system’s longtime defenders. Ed Dowd, a former U.S. attorney who for two decades has helped lead the Citizens for Missouri Courts, a campaign that advocates for preserving the Missouri Plan, said the group has historically become most active when the system is under threat.

Dowd’s St. Louis-based firm, Dowd Bennett, recently contributed $100,000 to the campaign.

“It’s something that we take very, very seriously and don’t want to take any chances on losing something that we have that works so well,” he said.

Spooky Lava Lamps – Halloween Science

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Are you ready to brew up some bubbly science this Halloween? Our spooky lava lamps are a fun alternative to a traditional pumpkin and a fantastic way for kids to learn about density and chemical reactions! Using just water, oil, food colouring, and an Alka-Seltzer (or effervescent vitamin tablet), you can create a science activity kids will remember for a long time!

I also have a pumpkin lava lamp that’s perfect for a Halloween evening if you place a torch behind it!

Fill the bottle or jar a quarter full with water.

Top up to the (near) top with the vegetable oil.

The oil and water should separate into two layers, water at the bottom and oil sitting on top. Add about 6-8 drops of food colouring once they have separated.

Pop in half an Alka-Seltzer tablet and watch the bubbles form.

Add more Alka-Seltzer bit by bit to keep the bubbles rising and falling.

Another idea is to use small plastic cups made to look like pumpkins or ghosts for a mini version.

Water and oil don’t mix because water is made of polar molecules. A water molecule has a positive electrical charge on one end and a negative charge on the other. Opposite charges attract, so water molecules stick together because the positive end of one water molecule is attracted to the negative end of another.

Oil molecules are different; they are non-polar, meaning their charge is more evenly spaced out. This means oil is not attracted to water. Oil is hydrophobic (water-fearing) and tries to stay as far away from water as it can.

Oil sits on top of the water because it is less dense than water.

When you add Alka-Seltzer (made of citric acid and sodium bicarbonate), it reacts with water to form carbon dioxide gas and sodium citrate. As the carbon dioxide bubbles rise, they carry bits of coloured water with them. When the gas escapes at the surface, the coloured water drops sink back down, creating a lava lamp effect.

Doris Dörrie und "Frau Winkler": Sterbehilfe per Roboter?

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Während die Welt über die Gefahren Künstlicher Intelligenz diskutiert, lotet die Filmemacherin Doris Dörrie aus, wie humanoide Roboter unseren Umgang mit Alter, Pflege und selbstbestimmtem Sterben verändern könnten.

Hamburg in naher Zukunft: Statt Paketboten bringen Roboter die Pakete, im Café fährt ein sprechender Servierwagen zwischen den Tischen umher, auf den Straßen begegnet man humanoiden Robotern, die Hunde ausführen; oder auch umgekehrt: Menschen, die einen Roboterhund spazieren führen. Ansonsten wirkt diese Welt erstaunlich normal.

In dieser Szenerie beginnt Doris Dörries neuer Film "Frau Winkler verlässt das Haus". Im Mittelpunkt steht eine ältere Dame, die nach einem Sturz nicht mehr alleine zurecht kommt. Doch ihre einzige Tochter kann die Pflege nicht dauerhaft übernehmen. Die Lösung scheint schnell gefunden: ein Pflegeroboter mit eingebautem EOL-Programm. EOL steht für "End of Life Mode", sprich: Der Roboter begleitet nicht nur durch den Alltag, sondern leistet unter bestimmten Voraussetzungen auch Sterbehilfe, nämlich wenn der Mensch dies selbst wünscht und medizinische Kriterien erfüllt sind.

Doris Dörrie greift in ihrem Film mit großer Leichtigkeit gleiche mehrere gesellschaftliche Schwergewichte auf: Es geht unter anderem um die Herausforderungen, vor denen alternde Gesellschaften wie beispielsweise Deutschland, Japan oder auch China stehen, es geht um das Thema selbstbestimmtes Sterben und eine Mutter-Tochter-Beziehung, die die Schuldfrage zwischen den Generationen stellt. Es sind Themen von universeller Relevanz, auch wenn die Handlung in Deutschland verankert ist.

"Ich schreibe das, was mich selbst bewegt und interessiert", sagt Doris Dörrie im Interview mit der DW. Dörrie zählt zu den bekanntesten deutschen Filmregisseurinnen der Gegenwart. Der Durchbruch gelang ihr 1985 mit der Komödie "Männer"; später machte sie unter anderem mit Filmen wie "Kirschblüten – Hanami" auch international auf sich aufmerksam.

Sterbehilfe gehört zu den komplexesten ethischen und gesellschaftlichen Fragen unserer Zeit. Entsprechend unterschiedlich fallen die Regelungen weltweit aus: Während einige Länder aktive Sterbehilfe unter bestimmten Voraussetzungen erlauben, ist in anderen jede Form der Beihilfe zum Sterben verboten und strafbar.

Doris Dörrie stellt mit ihrem Film die Frage: Was passiert, wenn eine KI beginnt auszurechnen, wie kostenintensiv die Pflege eines alten Menschen ist und ob sich diese Belastung für eine Gesellschaft noch "lohnt"? Der Grundgedanke an sich ist nicht neu, sagt Dörrie, und verweist auf die japanische Legende von Narayama, die auch im Film Erwähnung findet: Der Überlieferung zufolge war es die Aufgabe der ältesten Kinder, ihre betagten Eltern auf einen Berg zu bringen, um sie dort sterben zu lassen, weil die Nahrung nicht für alle ausreichte. "Und in der Diskussion befinden wir uns ja auch schon zum Teil: Können wir uns das überhaupt noch leisten, dass die Alten immer älter werden?"

In Dörries Film darf der alte Mensch zwar ab einem bestimmten Gesundheitszustand selbst entscheiden, die Entscheidung aber dann auch nicht mehr zurücknehmen. Doch was genau heißt hier selbst entscheiden? "Das ist schon vertrackt", sagt Dörrie, denn die Frage sei ja, inwieweit man zu dieser Entscheidung auch gedrängt werde, "weil es dann nicht nur ökonomisch sinnvoller ist, sondern weil man auch für die Nächsten, also hier die Tochter, keine Belastung mehr ist?"

Der Film wirft diese Fragen teilweise indirekt und mit viel schwarzem Humor auf, etwa wenn die Pflegeberaterin von Frau Winkler das EOL-Feature als attraktive und vor allem unkomplizierte Zusatzoption anpreist, so dass man selbst gar nicht weiter tun müsse.

Doris Dörrie geht es nicht darum, die KI zu verteufeln. Ganz im Gegenteil. Im Film schafft es der Pflegeroboter, dem Frau Winkler den Namen "Pawlowitsch" gibt, dass die alte Dame wieder ein Stück Lebenslust gewinnt und tatsächlich das Haus verlässt - daher auch der Titel des Films.

"Wenn man durch Deutschland fährt, sieht man überall diese kleinen Einfamilienhäuschen, und in jedem zweiten Häuschen, wenn man sich die Statistik anschaut, lebt ein alter Mensch allein." Wenn KI hier helfen könne, warum nicht, sagt die Filmemacherin. Sie wolle genau diese Ambivalenz der Dinge untersuchen. "Die Dinge in Schwarz-Weiß einzuteilen, wäre sowieso nicht interessant und auch nicht richtig, glaube ich."

Sie selbst und das Filmteam hätten zudem beim Dreh erstaunt festgestellt, wie schnell man als Mensch eine Beziehung zu einem Roboter aufbauen könne. Sie erklärt sich das mit dem menschlichen Gehirn. "Wir halten alles, was sich bewegt und spricht, für ein Lebewesen. Wir können nicht anders." Und genau da müsse man hinschauen, so Dörrie, wenn man mit einer KI kommuniziere. "Wie sehr sind wir uns jedes Mal bewusst, dass wir mit einer Maschine reden und nicht mit einem Lebewesen? Das sind alles Dinge, die müssen wir jetzt erst lernen."

Schaut man sich die Entwicklung von KI an, zeigt sich, dass die Grenzen zwischen Mensch und Maschine immer mehr zu verschwimmen scheinen. Und genau deshalb ist es wichtig, darüber zu reden, so Doris Dörrie. Problematisch werde es ihrer Ansicht nach dort, "wo wir uns nicht mehr distanzieren und wo wir keine eigenen Gedanken mehr fassen". Als Menschen müssen wir uns die Frage stellen, so Dörrie, wer wir sind und was uns wirklich ausmacht. "Ich glaube, dass es einen großen Unterschied zwischen dem Roboter und uns gibt, und das ist das Spielen." Der Roboter könne ein Spiel vielleicht initiieren, "aber das Spielen an sich ist doch etwas zutiefst Menschliches, weil es erst mal sinn- und zweckfrei ist".

"Wir haben auch über Atomwaffen irgendwann international geredet. Wir haben über alle möglichen Dinge in der Vergangenheit nicht nur geredet, sondern es auch geschafft, Lösungen zu finden." Und das müsse nun dringend angegangen werden. Ich nenne es Pesto Marrakesch, weil es Datteln enthält, Kreuzkümmel, Koriandersamen und außerdem Walnüsse, frische rote Peperoni (sorgt für eine schöne Schärfe), getrocknete Tomaten… Sie werden es noch kennenlernen. Dazu noch etwas Feta und…

Schon der Geruch des Gerichtes veränderte Capreses Laune. Beim Essen war er dann wie verwandelt. Schwärmte von Marokko, der Sonne, dem Essen. Dann fing er sogar an zu singen. Das fand ich übertrieben – aber was sollte es, ich wollte ihn ja auch aufheitern. Ihnen viel Freude mit diesem Gericht.

Zutaten für ein kleines Glas (circa 70 ml)

15 Walnusshälften 4 entsteinte Datteln (Sie wissen schon – die aus der Tüte) 2 getrocknete Tomaten (nicht eingelegt) 1 TL gemahlene Koriandersamen 2 TL Kreuzkümmel 15 g frische, rote Peperoni circa 40 bis 60 ml Olivenöl Geschmorter Chicorée

2 Datteln 1 Packung Feta 150 g und den Rest der Gewürzmischung Zubereitung

Die getrockneten Tomaten und die Datteln in kleine Stücke schneiden und mit den Walnüssen mahlen. Das mache ich mit meinem Pürierstab und einem Aufsatz.

Den Kreuzkümmel und die Koriandersamen mahlen. Das mache ich mit einem Mörser.

Die Walnuss-Dattel-Tomaten-Mischung zum Pürieren in ein hohes Gefäß geben. 2 TL der Gewürzmischung (den Rest benötigen wir noch) aus Koriandersamen und Kreuzkümmel dazugeben und die Peperoni – und zusätzlich (zuerst einmal) 40 ml Olivenöl. Über das Olivenöl bestimmen Sie die Konsistenz. Also gerne während des Pürierens Schritt für Schritt mehr Olivenöl dazugeben. Nun alles Pürieren.

Die Masse quillt ja etwas. Sie können als – wenn zu fest – noch etwas Olivenöl dazugeben.

Die Chicoréestauden halbieren und den Strunk unten keilförmig entfernen. (siehe Foto)

Die Hälften auf beiden Seiten circa 5 Minuten in der zerlassenen Butter schmoren, bis sie goldbraun sind.

Während die Chicoréestauden schmoren bereiten wir den Feta vor.

Den Ofen auf 220 Grad (Umluft mit Grill oben) vorheizen.

150 g Feta in zwei Hälften teilen, im unteren Drittel in die Auflaufform legen (darüber legen wir später den Chicorée – deshalb), mit der Gewürzmischung bestreuen und 1 EL Olivenöl darübergießen. Die Auflaufform kommt dann auf den Grillrost – mittlere Schiene. Die Fetascheiben sollten so kleine braune Flecken bekommen. Finish

Die Auflaufform aus dem Ofen nehmen. Die Chricoréehäften in die Auflaufform legen. Alles mit etwas Butter übergießen. Mit Dattelstücken dekorieren. Und einen Klecks Pesto Marrakesch auf die Chicoréestauden geben. Eventuell mit Peperonischeiben – oder streifen dekorieren. Viele Grüße aus dem singenden Kochlabor:-)

Life alongside the impacts of Alberta’s fracking boom

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Drew Anderson has reported on climate and the environment, with a particular focus on oil and gas and electricity in Alberta, for The Narwhal since 2021. He is based in Calgary.

It is easy to get lost in the numbers when talking about fracking in Alberta: to be temporarily dazzled by the growth of production, the insatiable thirst for water and the profits that follow.

The practice has seen a dramatic increase over the past 13 years, with production rising sevenfold between 2013 and 2024 to the equivalent of more than 640 million barrels of oil per year. Water consumption also soared 335 per cent, from 6.5 billion litres to almost 30 billion litres of freshwater per year.

Those sorts of figures are almost impossible to comprehend. You could count how many swimming pools that water might fill (164 million), or try to imagine a stack of barrels teetering high into the atmosphere, but what does that really mean?

In parts of rural Alberta, you don’t need those kinds of mental gymnastics to understand the scale of the industry and its impact. The evidence is all around you. Creeks run dry. Hoses and pumps seem to line every ditch. Then there are the trucks and drilling and noise.

This is particularly true in the rural areas of west-central Alberta, a long stretch of the province sandwiched between the foothills to the west and the frenetic Calgary-Edmonton corridor to the east.

It’s here where ranches, farms and communities sit side by side with a fracking boom that is outcompeting the locals for water.

Looking back on the past ten years, the summer of 2026 was an anomaly for the area of Alberta an hour drive southwest of Edmonton. Parched fields were inundated with rain early in the season, and rivers and creeks flowed with renewed vigour. Some areas received more rainfall than ever before.

But that moisture wasn’t enough to quell concerns from some residents in the Blindman and Medicine River watersheds, where fracking activity has been increasing — and water concerns are front and centre.

Fracking involves injecting water deep into the ground to break up rock where gas is trapped in tight crevasses, often thousands of metres under the surface. That water, vast quantities of it, is almost entirely lost to the water cycle as it remains trapped in the reservoirs that previously held gas. The water that does come to the surface is contaminated.

The Narwhal spent a day travelling through the Rimbey, Alta., area with Daniel Iseli-Otto and Assar Grinde, two members of the Blindman River Watershed Society, in July this year, visiting impacted landowners, following pumping hoses to enormous reservoirs and stopping for a photoshoot among Grinde’s cattle.

There’s an image in many a head of what kind of people raise concerns over oil and gas. Former Alberta Premier Jason Kenney often dismissed those with environmental concerns as “foreign-funded radicals,” but those stereotypes don’t hold.

Butch and Nicole Smith, who live in the area, blame the construction of a massive reservoir across from their isolated property for the decline of their bison herd. They’re moving.

Grinde, whose family has owned its ranch for generations, talks about watching the creek on that land dry up and how the oil and gas companies were still pumping water for fracking while he was left with mud.

Iseli-Otto curses as we turn onto another gravel road, heavily pitted from the trucks moving back and forth from a fracking reservoir site. “This is industrial pulverization of county roads,” he says, as he slows down in his Ram 3500 pickup truck.

Dana Kreil, the chair of the nearby Medicine River Watershed Society, and a councillor for Lacombe County, jumped in Iseli-Otto’s truck in the afternoon. She says the road in front of her home was “trashed” by one company active in the area.

Travelling through rural Alberta, it feels like the frustrations that come alongside the pay cheques and the royalties of the broader oil and gas industry are increasing in step with a years-long trend of companies failing to pay their share — whether they frack wells or not.

Even with record profits, investments and jobs don’t stack up the way they used to. Too often, oil and gas companies fail to pay their bills, and that has consequences. Unpaid municipal taxes hurt local governments — and eat into budgets for things like road maintenance — and unpaid leases force landowners to seek government compensation on behalf of delinquent companies. At the same time, there are more and more orphan and inactive wells.

All of those local impacts intertwine with the changing climate and the associated droughts, fires and floods.

Grinde says no one really thinks of the water the companies pull from the rivers and underground aquifers until there’s not enough to go around, and then it becomes a priority. And although the summer of 2026 was altogether too wet for many farmers, the drought years are predicted to return as part of a longer-term pattern of increased volatility.

The cause of those swings in climate are once again related to big numbers that are hard to grasp.

$168 billion in revenue. $22 billion in royalties the government relies on. 310 billion litres of water consumed by Alberta’s oil and gas industry. Then there’s the five billion tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions from global oil and gas production, seven billion tonnes from transportation and the unfathomable heating of oceans.

The big picture is abstract; hard to fathom. But here at Blindman Creek, the impacts are tangible.

“It’s really obvious that it’s an issue, but it’s really hard to make it concrete for people,” Iseli-Otto says. “This is an issue for people in Calgary, this is an issue for people in Edmonton, but they don’t know. The amount of water that’s getting sucked out is mind blowing.”

Inspired by Paul Ford and Aboard Foundary's new web app Tiny Little Office, which provides a suite of web app content editors (a Word Processor, Outliner, Presentation Builder, Spreadsheet, Calculator, Drawing Tool, Diagram Maker, Animator, Calendar, and Trip Planner).

Tiny Little Office's signature trick is to store all of the document data in the #fragment section of the URL, so every document created using it becomes a bookmarkable and shareable link.

It also has an llms.txt file telling agents how to construct URLs that will render as documents. Claude used that file to generate this link to a deck that explains how the URL format works:

Tiny Little Office inspired me to have Claude Code (Opus 5.5 Extra) build a similar tool that works with LaTeX. Here's the sequence of prompts I used:

I want a tool where you can link to it with a fragment hash #code-here and it treats that code as LaTeX and renders it really effectively. It should also support a prefix which means the latex is compressed with a custom compression dictionary optimized for latex. Use the best available latex render library from a CDN (consider WebAssembly too). If loaded without a fragment hash show an editor you can paste latex into and then toggle to a "rendered" view. That rendered view should be bookmarkable. The #fragment view should let you switch to edit source too.
