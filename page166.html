Mouammar Kadhafi : il voulait être le « roi des rois »… Où son rêve s'est-il arrêté ?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mouammar Kadhafi, devenu Premier ministre de la Libye le 16 janvier 1970, n'aurait jamais imaginé que son régime durerait 42 ans et prendrait fin de la manière dont il s'est terminé.

Au cours de son mandat, il a brutalement réprimé tous ses opposants. C'est peut-être pour cette raison qu'aucune alternative au pouvoir ne s'est imposée tant qu'il était au pouvoir.

Puis la vague de réveil politique qui a déferlé sur le monde arabe, et qui a débuté en Tunisie en 2011, a balayé le régime du colonel Mouammar Kadhafi.

Mouammar Kadhafi, tué par les rebelles le 20 octobre 2011, était le fils d'un agriculteur bédouin. Mais comment est-il devenu l'un des dirigeants les plus influents du monde arabe ?

Selon l'Encyclopédie Britannica, Mouammar Kadhafi est né en 1942 et son père était un agriculteur bédouin. Après avoir terminé sa scolarité, il s'est rendu à Benghazi pour faire des études universitaires.

Il a été exclu de l'université de Sabha en 1961 en raison de ses activités et opinions politiques. C'est à cette époque qu'il a obtenu un diplôme de droit à l'université de Libye. Il a également eu l'occasion de s'engager dans l'armée libyenne, qui offrait à l'époque une bonne formation et était considérée comme un moyen efficace d'améliorer sa situation économique.

C'est sans doute pour cette raison qu'il s'est engagé dans l'armée avant d'avoir terminé ses études supérieures.

C'est cette décision qui l'a ensuite conduit au sommet du pouvoir. Dans sa jeunesse, Kadhafi était un partisan du président égyptien Gamal Abdel Nasser et de sa politique.

Il a également pris part aux manifestations de 1956 contre l'invasion menée par la Grande-Bretagne, la France et Israël à la suite de la nationalisation du canal de Suez par l'Égypte.

Après avoir terminé sa formation militaire, il a été envoyé en Grande-Bretagne en 1965 pour y suivre une formation complémentaire.

Kadhafi a gravi les échelons de l'armée libyenne à un rythme effréné tout en commençant à ourdir un complot visant à renverser la monarchie.

Alors qu'il était encore en formation militaire, il eut l'idée de renverser la monarchie libyenne. Finalement, après son retour du Royaume-Uni en 1969, il mena un coup d'État militaire depuis Benghazi et se proclama chef de la Libye.

Le 1er septembre 1969, un coup d'État militaire mené par le colonel Kadhafi renversa le roi de Libye. Kadhafi devint chef de l'armée, président du Conseil révolutionnaire et chef de l'État, tout en conservant le grade de colonel.

Une fois au pouvoir, il démantela les bases militaires américaines et britanniques en Libye et, en 1970, expulsa la plupart des Italiens et des Juifs du pays.

En 1973, il nationalisa l'industrie pétrolière libyenne. Il interdit également l'alcool et les jeux d'argent.

Au cours de cette période, il déclara aux dirigeants des compagnies pétrolières internationales :

« Ceux qui ont vécu cinq mille ans sans pétrole peuvent bien vivre encore quelques années pour obtenir ce qui leur revient de droit. »

Cette prise de position porta ses fruits, et la Libye devint le premier pays en développement à s'assurer une part importante des recettes issues de sa production pétrolière. D'autres pays arabes lui emboîtèrent le pas, une initiative qui jeta les bases du grand succès économique des pays producteurs de pétrole dans les années 1970.

À cette époque, la Libye affichait une production pétrolière comparable à celle de certains pays du Golfe, mais sa population était faible, avec seulement environ trois millions d'habitants. Le pétrole a donc très rapidement enrichi la Libye.

Kadhafi était un farouche opposant aux négociations avec Israël et s'opposait à l'accord de paix entre l'Égypte et Israël. Son gouvernement a également soutenu des tentatives de coup d'État dans certains pays voisins.

Au début des années 1970, Kadhafi a présenté son idéologie politique dans le « Livre vert », qui prônait le socialisme islamique. À travers cet ouvrage, il a exprimé son intention de nationaliser les secteurs clés de l'économie.

Selon lui, la solution aux problèmes de la société n'était ni la démocratie ni le multipartisme. Il affirmait que les grands partis conduisaient à la dictature et que les pays devaient être gouvernés par des comités populaires chargés de tous les aspects de la vie.

En 1979, Kadhafi a officiellement renoncé à la fonction de chef de l'État, mais dans les faits, il a continué à détenir tous les pouvoirs essentiels.

Sur la scène internationale, Kadhafi et son régime étaient connus pour leurs agissements imprévisibles.

Il a financé divers groupes à travers le monde, notamment les Black Panthers américains et la Nation of Islam. Il a également soutenu l'Armée républicaine irlandaise (IRA) en Irlande du Nord.

Des agents libyens ont continué à prendre pour cible les opposants à Kadhafi en exil, et son gouvernement a été impliqué dans plusieurs complots d'assassinat.

En 1986, une bombe a explosé dans une discothèque de Berlin fréquentée par des soldats américains. Cet attentat a été attribué à des agents libyens.

Le président américain Ronald Reagan a ordonné des frappes aériennes contre Tripoli et Benghazi. Bien que de nombreuses personnes aient trouvé la mort, dont la fille de Kadhafi, ce dernier a lui-même survécu.

En 1988, un avion de la Pan Am s'est écrasé au-dessus de Lockerbie, en Écosse, faisant 270 victimes. La Libye a été tenue pour responsable de cet accident.

Dans un premier temps, Kadhafi avait refusé de livrer à l'Écosse les deux suspects de l'attentat de Lockerbie. Après des années de négociations et de sanctions internationales, il les a livrés en 1999. L'un d'eux a été condamné à la prison à perpétuité, tandis que l'autre a été libéré par la suite.

En août 2003, la Libye a reconnu sa responsabilité dans l'attentat dans une lettre adressée aux Nations unies et a versé 2,7 milliards de dollars d'indemnités aux victimes.

En septembre 2003, le Conseil de sécurité des Nations unies a levé les sanctions contre la Libye.

La Libye a également abandonné son programme d'armes de destruction massive et versé des indemnités aux victimes d'autres catastrophes aériennes ainsi qu'à celles de l'attentat de Berlin.

L'accord de Lockerbie, associé à l'abandon d'un programme secret d'armes nucléaires et chimiques, a ouvert la voie à la normalisation des relations entre la Libye et les pays occidentaux.

Après la levée des sanctions, plusieurs dirigeants mondiaux, dont le Premier ministre britannique Tony Blair, ont rendu visite à Kadhafi dans sa célèbre tente.

Cette tente occupait une place importante dans sa vie et l'accompagnait lors de ses nombreux voyages internationaux.

Au cours de cette période, la Libye a signé de nombreux accords commerciaux avec des compagnies pétrolières et des fabricants d'armes occidentaux.

Kadhafi était également connu pour son mode de vie excentrique et pour le groupe de gardes du corps féminines qui l'entouraient souvent.

En 2011, les manifestations qui ont renversé Zine El Abidine Ben Ali en Tunisie et Hosni Moubarak en Égypte ont incité les Libyens à se révolter contre Kadhafi.

Son gouvernement a réagi par la force, recourant notamment à des tirs, à des avions de combat et à des hélicoptères contre les manifestants.

Les organisations de défense des droits de l'homme et la communauté internationale ont condamné ces agissements.

Le 22 février 2011, Kadhafi a refusé de démissionner dans un discours télévisé, qualifiant les manifestants de « traîtres ».

Il a continué à affirmer que ses opposants étaient soutenus par Al-Qaïda.

Au fil du temps, les rebelles ont pris le contrôle de vastes zones de la Libye et ont assiégé Tripoli.

Le 26 février, le Conseil de sécurité des Nations unies a imposé de nouvelles sanctions contre le régime de Kadhafi et a gelé les avoirs de sa famille.

L'OTAN a ensuite lancé des frappes aériennes, infligeant de lourdes pertes à l'armée libyenne.

En avril 2011, son plus jeune fils, Saif al-Arab, ainsi que trois de ses petits-fils, ont été tués lors d'une frappe aérienne de l'OTAN.

Le 27 juin, des mandats d'arrêt ont été émis à l'encontre de Kadhafi, de son fils et de son chef des services de renseignement. Les rebelles ont ensuite fait leur entrée à Tripoli en août et, le 23 août, ont pris le contrôle du complexe de Kadhafi dans le quartier de Bab al-Azizia. Kadhafi lui-même n'a toutefois pas été retrouvé et, dans une série de messages audio, il a exhorté le peuple libyen à se rebeller contre ses opposants.

Pendant ce temps, la ville de Syrte était assiégée et, selon certaines informations, le colonel Kadhafi, accompagné de certains de ses partisans, aurait tenté de briser l'encerclement imposé par les combattants du Conseil de transition.

L'attaque de l'OTAN a détruit 15 véhicules militaires. Le colonel Kadhafi et certains de ses hommes ont survécu à l'attaque et se sont cachés dans deux grandes canalisations d'eau, mais les combattants les ont rapidement encerclés.

Several years ago, Android introduced the ability to “bubble” a text conversation, or move that app to a floating bubble on your home screen. It’s a convenient way to keep a conversation easily accessible.

As part of the Android 17 rollout, users got the option to use app bubbles. When you bubble an app, it sits on top of everything else on your screen, making it easy to access when you need it. You can place up to five apps in your bubble.

A bubble only saves a tap or two each time you use an app, but it’s a fantastic multi-tasking tool if you have a handful of apps you use often. You could do something similar to this before, but only for Google Messages and Facebook Messenger chats.

The ability to bubble an app on Android is starting as a Pixel exclusive, but the feature will come to other manufacturers over time. There is an existing, similar feature for Samsung called Smart Pop Up View, and instructions for that are below.

To get started, choose the app you want from either your app screen or your home screen.

Press and hold on the app until a pop-up menu appears.

The menu will show options like App info, Add app to home screen, or Pause app. Select the Bubble option.

Your selected app will shrink to an icon that you can move around your home screen. You don’t have the freedom to place it anywhere, but it can go anywhere along the left or right side of your screen. The app will stay on top of everything else on your screen, and you can bubble multiple apps at once, but they will always move together.

Samsung has a similar feature called Smart Pop-Up View that lets you respond to notifications on selected apps without leaving the app you’re in. You might need to enable your “Multi window for all apps” setting first.

Once you’re in advanced settings, find and choose Floating Notifications. Then select Smart Pop-Up view.

After a three-month micro-policy blitz from Downing Street, the question now being asked is: did Reeder pass said file on to the government’s battalion of special advisers? In the past week Andy Burnham has vowed to clamp down on social pariahs blaring out TikTok videos on public transport, outlaw “rip-off” private car park fines and – on Thursday – fill in potholes in 7,000 miles of England’s roads.

Before that, as part of the focus on “everyday fixes”, the prime minister announced a plan to remove VAT from electricity bills, a £2 cap on single bus fares across England and take action on “auto-renew” subscriptions. There’s also promises to crack down on pricey branded school uniforms, ban specious discounts, limit vape and betting shops and stop airlines charging families to sit together on planes. At this rate all minor annoyances in the UK will be eradicated by Christmas.

The idea is simple, says one policy adviser. “People expect the government to be able to do something about things which are unfair – big or small,” they said. “It sends a message that the government is on people’s side, its ready to act, and it can genuinely make a difference.”

Government insiders say the policies are not the product of standing around in a circle, throwing a stress ball and shouting out ideas. “There isn’t someone with a whiteboard brainstorming new bicycles for special constables,” says one, furthering the Thick of It comparison. Instead, the everyday fixes were a central policy strategy from Burnham’s first day in office. “We did this sort of thing under Keir too,” said another source. “But under Andy it’s a whole different thing; he’s put rocket boosters under it.”

In the first days of his premiership, Burnham gave his close ally Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state, the job of establishing taskforces for three core areas – cost of living, public control and regeneration. One government source said: “There is a very intentional drumbeat of those because they do make a difference, but also it’s constantly showing people that we understand where those frustrations and low-level pressures are.”

Ideas are “kicked around” between the cost of living taskforce in the Cabinet Office and the policy unit in No 10, with all departments encouraged to feed in. “There’s a sort of filtering up process,” the source said. “It’s quite a sprawling sort of cross-government approach.”

Another said that while some departments were better placed to find “not hugely spendy” fixes, every minister was encouraged to contribute. “I think a lot of people had sort of resigned any hope that the government can actually get things done – and this is showing them that, yes, actually, we can,” another source said.

The small policy announcements have been, one delighted adviser said, a roaring success. Another source said: “Some people are very snotty about it, but in a world where it’s really hard for a government to cut through, this is working. You put some ideas through the grid and you often don’t know if they’re going to fly. But when you see one go gangbusters, it’s kind of like Christmas morning.”

One single fact policy idea dominated the news cycle for an entire day. Another generated tens of thousands of posts on X. Burnham’s faintly hokey video on antisocial phone use garnered 2m views. “It helps that he’s just really good at it,” the source said.

The small-fix policies have not been appreciated by everyone. Reform UK’s Robert Jenrick said the UK was “going down the pan and our prime minister is faffing around tweaking parking tickets” – despite trying to implement the policy himself when he was a Conservative minister. The former Olympian Sharron Davies said: “As Paris burns our prime minister has stopped people playing loud music on buses & trains,” leading some to question whether she expected Burnham to invade the French capital.

Kemi Badenoch, meanwhile, has been dismissive, saying in a speech this week that cracking down on loud music on public transport was a “job for ticket inspectors, not the prime minister”. Reprising the theme at prime minister’s questions on Wednesday, the leader of the opposition implicitly accused Burnham of fiddling while Rome burns. “We are spending more on interest payments than we do on schools, the army and the police combined,” she said. “Meanwhile, the prime minister is worrying about loud music on trains. This is not serious.”

Across the dispatch box, Burnham hid his delight at her response behind a disappointed look. “I’m not going to apologise for doing the small things that help people have better lives,” he said sombrely, while his advisers likely high-fived in private.

Regardless of the reaction of Burnham’s opponents, the focus on minor grievances is a canny strategy that appears to be working, said the pollster Luke Tryl, the director of More in Common. In recent polling, when people were asked whether Burnham understood the pressure of high energy bills on ordinary people, 45% said he did. When the same question was asked about Starmer, that figure was 23%. “I think the Tories and Reform have got it dead wrong on this,” Tryl said. “People bring this sort of stuff up in focus groups all the time. It cuts across left- and rightwing voters. People care about immigration, about the NHS – but they also care about everyday frictions.”

But behind the scenes there is an acknowledgment that a focus on ticking off small things from the PM’s to-do list will be significantly more challenging in moments of crisis. There has to be a “twin-track” approach, said one insider. “It’s combining a big picture, long term, dismantling 40 years of neoliberalism and addressing questions about ownership and control, but at the same time tackling more immediate daily bugbears,” they said. “What you’ve seen so far is these smaller level interventions, but the bigger structural, longer term piece will be in the 10-year plan.”

While the everyday fixes may be popular, the sugar-high they produce could be short-lived, said Ed Shackle, the head of qualitative research at the policy consultants Public First Global. “The government should be under no illusion that as soon as any real substantive event rears its head, any small popularity boost will be instantly blown away,” he said. “Quick fixes are popular, but they are a poor substitute for fixing the big issues the public care about.”

Rabbits Were Actually Once a Luxury Reserved for Kings. Then the Black Death Changed Everything

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Home → Features → History and Humanities → History

Once the preserve of the aristocracy, rabbits became commonplace by the end of the Middle Ages as the world was turned upside down after the arrival of plague.

The European rabbit is a common sight in the British countryside today. Yet it was not always so. They are not native to the British Isles and are one of the most devastating invasive species.

The early history of rabbits in the British Isles tells the captivating story of social change in medieval England. From a rare luxury gifted by kings and the Norman aristocracy, rabbits became more commonplace after the living standards of the poor improved in the years after the Black Death (1346 to 1353).

The satirical depictions of rabbits found in medieval books reveal a gallows humour for social inversion that would not feel out of place today.

Once thought to have been introduced by the Normans, archaeologists have since uncovered a rabbit bone from Roman Britain. However, documentary evidence of rabbits in the British Isles has only been found from the Norman period onwards.

One of the earliest references survives from the Scilly Isles in the form of a tithe, which was a tenth of produce given to the church as an offering. In 1176, the landowner Richard de Wyka admitted that he had withheld paying tithes on his rabbits because he had believed that “tithes were not payable on things of this kind”. His confusion suggests that rabbits were a novelty.

Rabbits continued to be sufficiently rare to warrant the king’s care and attention in the 1200s. In 1235, Henry III gave a gift of ten live rabbits to Geoffrey Despenser, the English landowner and Lord of Martley in Worcestershire, out of his park at Guildford.

A few years later, the king sent men to catch 30 or 40 rabbits in his Guildford park. They were told only to collect them if the warren was well stocked, indicating that the king didn’t want the colony to be depleted.

At the time, rabbits were kept for their meat and fur. Henry III sent out orders for the supply of venison, boars, fish, swans, peacocks, hens, eggs, and hares for various feasts in the early 13th century. It had not been until his Christmas feast of 1240 that he added rabbits to his requisition list.

Rabbits were an expensive delicacy, costing four or five times as much as chickens. Their owners guarded their warrens against poaching and predators to the point that watchtowers and stone lodges were built to protect them.

Yet rabbits did not remain the preserve of the aristocracy. The Black Death (1346 to 1353), which killed between a third and two-thirds of the European population, led to improving living standards for the survivors. Land became more freely available. Labourers could also charge more for their work. And many adopted the lifestyles of those higher up the social ladder.

Legislation was introduced in 1363 to restrict what people could wear according to their social status. Its introduction was explained because of “the excess of dress of people beyond their estate, to the very great destruction and impoverishment of the land.”

Rabbits reflected this social change. They became more commonplace during the 14th and 15th centuries as their meat was eaten by wealthier peasants and their furs worn by aspiring craftsmen.

The fur of the common grey rabbit was used for warmth, but some warrens specialised in rarer silver-grey or black rabbits. Rabbits were still fit for a king and Henry VII had night attire tailored with black rabbit fur.

As rabbit numbers increased through breeding, so too did the number of complaints about the destruction caused by them. Manorial accounts included repeated protests about the loss of crops and damage to land caused by the lord’s rabbits.

At the same time, hunting rabbits and other game was seen as potentially subversive. In 1390, following the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381, parliament introduced legislation against poaching and hunting by labourers and artisans. It was feared that, under the guise of hunting, such people would “make plots and conspiracies of rebellion” against the king.

The king consented to the legislation, extending the ban to the use of “hounds and ferrets, hays, nets, hair-pipes, cords and all other devices to take or destroy beasts of the forest, hares or rabbits, or other sport of gentlefolk”.

Rabbits and hares also reveal the satirical humour of medieval society. The Killer Rabbit of Caerbannog, the fictional and deceptively deadly rabbit from Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975), is but a modern adaptation of this ironic imagery associated with medieval rabbits.

Rabbits adorn the margins of medieval manuscripts. Playful portrayals of rabbits jousting on the backs of snails point to a gentle self-mockery that pervades medieval culture – in this case of chivalric ideals. More in line with the killer rabbit myth, some went even further, depicting rabbits as violently attacking people and seeking retribution.

The Smithfield Decretals, a manuscript illuminated in London in the 1340s, contains one of the most elaborate of such examples. In it, rabbits are portrayed as turning the tables on their hunters, capturing them, holding them trial, and then executing their human and greyhound hunters.

Such examples fit well within the medieval culture of inverting society – the world turned upside down – for comedic relief. Similar examples include the practice of electing a boy as the bishop for the day in Durham or electing a “lord of misrule” during May Day games.

By 1555 the Swiss naturalist Conrad Gesner could write that “there are few countries wherein coneys (rabbits) do not breed, but the most plenty of all is in England”.

Fewer people are flying this year, but with airfare this high and climbing, airline executives say that's just fine.

At the New York Hilton in Midtown Manhattan on Friday night, the aviation industry held its annual Wings Club gala, honoring Ed Bastian, CEO of profit-leader Delta Air Lines.

North American airline CEOs and other executives at the black-tie bash told CNBC that demand trends aren't worrying them, but persistently high, profit-crimping fuel prices are.

The number of people screened at U.S. checkpoints is on track for its first annual decline since 2020. Major U.S. carriers have said capturing higher fares matters more than fully filling planes or flying record numbers of travelers.

"We're actively prioritizing yield versus load factor and really happy as we see it. It fits right in with the premium investments that we're making," American Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper said on the company's earnings call Thursday.

American's load factor, a gauge of how full planes are flying, fell to 83.4% in the third quarter from 86% in the same quarter of 2025.

Plane ticket prices have skyrocketed as airlines grapple with a spike in jet fuel prices, their biggest expense after labor, and pass more of those costs on to customers.

Fares in September were up more than 22% from a year earlier, according to the latest inflation read. Fare tracker Hopper said it expects this holiday season to come with the most expensive ticket prices in years.

The average Southwest Airlines fare in the third quarter, for example, was $231.69, up close to $50 from a year earlier. That doesn't include Southwest's new fees, like its extra charges for checked bags or a seat closer to the front of the plane.

The airline's revenue passenger count, however, fell 3.2% from a year ago to about 33.5 million people.

Still, both fares and the passenger counts exceeded Southwest's expectations.

"Passenger fares came in about $3 above what our forecast was and the more important thing is that passenger volumes also exceeded our expectations," Southwest CEO Bob Jordan said on an earnings call Thursday.

Since April — when airlines began warning in earnest about profits taking a hit from the spike in jet fuel — through Thursday, about 9.3 million fewer people were screened at airport checkpoints than in the same period of 2025, according to the Transportation Security Administration. That's down less than 2% from last year, but it follows years of growth.

With jet fuel prices more than double year-ago levels — as seen in U.S. Gulf Coast spot prices at $4.63 a gallon, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration — airlines have focused on recovering as much of the fuel spike as possible.

Despite some drops, airline executives said during their earnings calls that overall travel demand is robust, especially for profitable premium cabins like business class.

And not every carrier is seeing a decline. United Airlines flew 2.4% more passengers in the third quarter than a year ago and said its unit revenue rose 16%, the biggest increase since the pandemic.

"I've been pleasantly surprised at the industry's ability this year to raise yields and pass through much of the increase in fuel prices," CEO Scott Kirby said on the company's earnings call Wednesday. "So it's possible that airlines will continue to focus on yields at the expense of load factor and that alone could get us to double-digit margins next year."

Most airlines expect fuel costs to keep biting, but executives say they're confident in their pricing power.

United, for example, said it expects to pay an average of $4.80 per gallon for fuel in the fourth quarter, up from $3.90 per gallon in the third.

American, United, Delta and Southwest all cut their 2026 profit forecasts this month when they reported earnings. American is the only one of the four that expects to post a loss this year.

American and others have also reduced growth plans or warned the industry could dial back its capacity expansion goals, which could keep fares elevated. Reduced capacity can make bookable seats scarcer, driving up prices even more if demand holds.

The carriers said demand has been particularly strong for premium seats, which command hundreds, if not thousands of dollars, more than coach tickets.

"Today, we find ourselves yet at another inflection point for our industry and the world. ... Geopolitics affecting our business, driving the price of jet fuel to historic highs," Bastian said in his award-acceptance speech, adding that artificial intelligence makes it hard to predict months or weeks ahead.

"It causes many people to question yet again the durability of our industry. But I'm not worried because I know the people in this room, and I know your resilience and how you've demonstrated that time and time again," he said.

Smaller airlines set to report results in the coming weeks, starting with JetBlue Airways next week, followed by Frontier Airlines and Allegiant Air, have traditionally focused on more price-sensitive customers, which could show if those travelers are pulling back at all.

One factor that could also affect air travel is the demise of Spirit Airlines, which went out of business in early May.

"Some of its customers have likely stopped flying," Bank of America said in a research note Thursday.

Many of the problems that plagued the airline industry for years are starting to abate.

In some cases, aircraft deliveries are still years behind schedule. But Boeing, whose CEO, Kelly Ortberg, attended on Friday night, and its main rival, Airbus, have ramped up production. Boeing will update investors on its production outlook when it reports quarterly results Tuesday.

Jack Price joined the marines in fall 2021 knowing he would need a car to get around base and that he couldn’t afford a new one. A joyless, month-long search through dealer classifieds and Facebook Marketplace listings ended when his mother spotted a 2010 Volkswagen Jetta posted on her office listserv by a co-worker moving abroad. With just 60,000 miles (96,560km) on the meter, the car (a manual!) was mechanically sound, and the air conditioning, sunroof and Bluetooth radio worked too. At $6,000, it was a steal. Price paid in cash.

Five years on, the Jetta, which he called the Pewter Pebble, is still carting him from A to B in unassuming style. “I’ve completely driven it into the ground,” says the 30-year-old, who lives in Los Angeles. “All over bases, cross-country a couple times, not really lovingly. I get so much crap from people, but not people my age.”

For generations, buying a first new car has been a rite of passage in the US, the clearest sign of a young person responsibly striking out on their own. But younger millennials and gen Z are increasingly finding that path blocked. The average new vehicle costs more than $50,000, an alarming figure that reflects Americans’ insatiable appetite for progressively larger SUVs and pickups. Meanwhile, adults under 35 are disappearing from showroom floors, accounting for less than 10% of new vehicle registrations in 2024 – a new low.

According to the credit reporting agency Experian, the average monthly car payment in the US is $765 for a new car and $542 for a used one, with the loan terms now stretching to six, even seven, years. A record 21% of new-car buyers pay $1,000 or more a month, according to the autos research group Edmunds. And that’s before factoring in maintenance, fluctuating gas prices and the eye-watering insurance premiums often charged to drivers under 35.

For many young Americans already under the strain of student loans, high housing costs and an unforgiving labor market, buying a new car or even a newer used one is out of the question. Enter the Y2K car – the retro ride that has become a trendy romantic choice for some younger Americans. “There’s definitely a golden era of cars from the late 90s and early 2000s that people just really want all of a sudden,” says Charlie Spears, a service adviser at an independently owned repair shop in Atlanta who conducts prepurchase inspections of used cars on demand. “A lot of people just want something that’s not going to fail them.”

Y2K cars don’t encompass everything produced in that era. They are the ones that simply won’t die. Think wedge-shaped Honda Accords and slab-sided Toyota Camrys: dependable, hand-me-down transport that can last 150,000 miles or more with routine maintenance. (Price’s Jetta, which was built at the end of a model production run that began in 2005, just makes the cut.) They’re economical to run and relatively inexpensive to insure, owing to their drastically lower worth. Best of all, the purchase price is low enough to pay in cash. Even when buyers need a loan – at steep interest rates as modern cars have in effect become mortgage payments on wheels – they can pay it off in a fraction of the time it takes to finance a newer car.

The supply of these bargains is dwindling: used vehicles priced under $15,000, the sweet spot for Y2K cars, have shrunk from 21% of inventory a year ago to 15%, according to industry analysis from Cox Automotive – numbers that speak to the demand for affordable cars. “Part of the draw is the nostalgia factor – people wanting cars they grew up with,” says Consumer Reports’ autos expert Keith Barry. “An old car with the right music on is kind of the closest thing to a time machine.”

On social media, the Y2K car has emerged as an ironic status symbol – a marker of maturity, grit and lack of pretense. Defects – busted aircon, glitchy windows, bits and bobs that have failed, fallen off or been held on with gaffer tape – are simply treated as part of a Y2K car’s personality. “I don’t know what kind of disease that is in the brain,” says one TikTok creator, who has to pry open the fuel door of her 2003 Mitsubishi Outlander with a screwdriver. “But I don’t get frustrated. I’m like, ‘Oh, this is fun.’”

In a Y2K car that takes a lickin’ and keeps on tickin’, creature comforts are beside the point. For many, part of the appeal is the absence of dashboard infotainment displays and other screens that have replaced basic switches and knobs in newer cars, often in the name of cost-cutting. Automakers’ zeal to monetize in-car technology, sometimes charging extra to activate physically installed features such as heated seats and remote start, only reinforces young buyers’ doubts about the value of a new car.

At the same time, do-it-yourself repairs have become harder as cars grow more digitally complex and auto manufacturers cut off access to owners and even independent mechanics. Oftentimes, they require investing in specialty digital readers or computer software to diagnose problems, and bespoke tools for wrenching. Most car owners would sooner punt, take the car to the dealership and pay whatever the maintenance and repair costs – which, of course, is exactly the vertical integration that shareholder-bound manufacturers are after.

Hence the romance with Y2K cars: you can fix ’em yourself, their old-school controls fall easily to hand, they’re tried and tested, seemingly built to last. They just have an aura of invincibility – never mind that their age makes them fundamentally more dangerous and susceptible to frequent repairs. “I’ve used my Jetta as if it were a truck,” Price says. “Transported trees in it, put my surfboard through the sunroof. I’ve done every single thing possible to thrash it beyond its abilities.”

Price’s passengers, however, don’t have much appreciation for Pewter Pebble’s quirks. Often while carpooling to US military installations, senior officers compelled to hold up the sagging headliner would ask him when he was trading in his beater for a new truck or SUV. It was a similar story when Price left the military last year and started chauffeuring Alex Vindman, the Iraq war veteran turned US Senate hopeful, on the campaign trail during Florida’s special election. “I drove him around a couple times in it, and he was like, ‘What is going on?’” Price says. Generally speaking, he says, “the fact that we expect folks who are getting paid, like, a grand a month to have a new car is nuts.”

Twenty-five miles north-west of downtown Atlanta, a half hour’s drive in optimistic traffic, sits Town Center at Cobb, a typical suburban mall in recession. But on the first Sunday of every month, the mall parking comes alive for Caffeine and Octane, a sprawling car show that’s open to the public, free to attend and also has long lines for coffee and donut trucks. And even though the usual turnout of 2,000-plus cars was winnowed down to a few hundred on the gray, showery morning of October’s show, it was still an impressive array of vintage American muscle, Japanese domestic market (or JDM) unicorns and multimillion-dollar exotics. The Y2K cars stood out, too.

Sitting right at the entrance of the show was Anthony DeNaples’s 2000 Buick Regal GS, a sleeper sedan that has become a cult classic. He bought it four months ago for $18,000 on Facebook Marketplace, a price he considered a bargain for such a rare commuter. “Person didn’t really know what they had,” says the 37-year-old custom car builder, who uses the car to ferry his two young daughters. His oldest is constantly goading him to do burnouts, which she calls “making stripes”. Modern cars, he reasons, have become so laden with safety systems – for keeping the car in lane, or slamming on the brakes when sensors detect an obstacle – that they’re less fun to drive hard. “A lot of newer cars, if you want to drive it how it’s meant to be driven, you’ve gotta shut off all these things, and a lot of times it doesn’t completely shut off.”

A few rows down from the Buick were Mike and Kate Landaverde in his-and-hers Honda Civics from the 90s: a barebones CRX coupe called Dirty Gertrude and a slightly less spartan wagon called Tatiana. The couple bought them cheap, in keeping with the Y2K ethos. Mike, 34, treats having to bump-start the CRX and his sweat-soaked commutes in summer traffic as part of the thrill ride. “I’m smiling the whole time,” says Mike, who manages a local Wendy’s. They keep a 2021 Toyota Corolla hatchback in the garage as a backstop. That car’s got AC.

When young Wendy’s customers pull up to the drive-through in newer barges he assumes carry huge monthly payments, Mike can’t help feeling a bit smug. The couple’s Hondas, however, haven’t necessarily come cheap. Kate, 39, reckons the money she has put into modifications on Tatiana alone could buy a new car. But the occasional outlays – on an engine swap, most recently – can be done at a self-directed pace. “You can do the mods at your own wallet bracket,” says Kate, a software specialist who does her own wrenching. “You can go eBay parts. But all my friends call me bougie because I’m not gonna do anything cheap.”

Meanwhile, making a case for best in show was Alex Jimenez in a 2001 Volkswagen Eurovan tricked out to look like the Scooby-Doo Mystery Machine. “My family is growing,” said Jimenez, a 32-year-old facilities manager, as his 14-year-old daughter Izel sat shotgun scrolling through her phone while a Scooby-Doo cartoon played on the aftermarket infotainment display. Beyond the extra space, he says, “we love it because I can literally fix everything at home. I don’t need special equipment or tools or a computer to diagnose what’s wrong with it. And it puts smiles on people’s faces everywhere we drive. I mean, everybody’s taking pictures.”

Y2K cars have their charms, sure. Barry, of Consumer Reports calls it “survivorship bias” – seeing the most durable makes and models still on the road contributes to assumptions that all from the era must be rock solid. But there’s a reason the automotive industry moved on. “If you go back to cars in the 1990s, our road-test data and reliability surveys were showing problems that were unheard of on modern vehicles: defective paint, parts falling off; the steering wheel would be on crooked,” Barry says. Rust is another scourge of Y2K cars, though it’s less of a concern in the Sun belt. Newer models are far better protected against corrosion. “The technology that allows a car’s body and frame to survive longer is just so much more advanced,” says Barry, adding that a modern car from a manufacturer that prioritizes reliability can reasonably be expected to last 200,000 miles or more with proper maintenance. To his point: the average car on American roads is now 13 years old.

Still, some young Y2K-car owners welcome the opportunity to get their hands dirty. Che Bidal, a 19-year-old Pit operator who also belongs to the furry community, was idling inside his 2004 Mazda6 as the rain poured on Caffeine and Octane. He takes immense pride in having bought the broken sedan for $900 and getting it running again with another $1,500 in parts and help from AI chatbots and YouTube tutorials. Having Frankensteined a Y2K car back to life, the prospect of taking on a new car payment – in this economy – only to be discouraged by the manufacturer from making his own repairs seems ridiculous. “Car companies are banks now,” says Bidal, who was further emboldened by his positive experience under the hood to start a wrenching group for furries. “Coming from fandom into the [auto enthusiast] community is a whole different thing – pretty amazing.”

Nevertheless, there’s a critical difference between a car that’s gratifying to restore and one that can protect you when something goes terribly wrong. Modern cars are subject to increasingly demanding safety standards, and the differences in structural reinforcements and accident-avoidance features between models just three years apart can be dramatic. Consumer Reports has long recommended that drivers prioritize safety as much as their budget allows – advice that its largely Boomer readership can more easily take onboard, naturally.

Barry went through an old-car phase too. “When I was driving around in my 1988 Renault Medallion, I’d see someone come across the center line as they’re looking on their phone and think, ‘I would not stand a chance in an overlap collision,’” he says. “You might think that the older car you’re driving is a tank. Well, the car might be fine. It’s the things inside the car, the new safety features, that help you survive.” Features like blind-spot monitoring, driver attention monitoring and electric stability control, which intervenes to maintain traction in a skid.

The US auto industry has made some effort to address young consumers’ affordability concerns. In 2021, Ford introduced the Maverick, a four-door compact pickup with an available hybrid powertrain and a starting price below $30,000. A startup called Slate, which offers an infinitely customizable electric truck with a starting price of about $25,000, hopes to begin fulfilling orders at the end of this year.

In China, buyers can get new cars with features that would seem extravagant at the same price point in the US – the payoff from decades of Chinese investment in innovation and American offshoring of manufacturing. An affordable, widely available Chinese car with modern features would give young US car buyers something truly tantalizing to consider. But Chinese automakers are in effect shut out of the US market. Even if they weren’t, Barry cautions: “We’re still not getting one for $8,000. Think closer to $20,000. You’re still going to have a car payment. And even if the interior is simple, it’s a different kind of simplicity than someone driving a 99 Camry is looking for.”

Ultimately, young consumers just want cars that deliver on the industry’s fabled promise of independence – and right now, Y2K cars are meeting the brief. These days, Price’s Pewter Pebble struggles with a leaky sunroof, and the headliner is still drooping. “It’s a Volkswagen so it’s coming back to bite me in the butt,” he says. “I’ve probably had to put three grand into it this year.” But with more than 150,000 miles on the clock, it’s taken him further than even he could have imagined.

The absence of a car note helped him become a homeowner in his 20s. It softened the blow of Alex Vindman’s upset loss to Democratic socialist Angie Nixon in Florida’s August Senate primary. Rather than scramble for another job, Price took a European vacation to decompress and plot his next move. As it happens, he spoke to the Guardian from the Spanish Pyrenees. “If I had a car payment and I lost my job,” he says, “I wouldn’t have the flexibility to hike a mountain.”
