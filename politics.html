Handelsangestellte sollen sich „umorientieren“

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Der Chef des Instituts für Höhere Studien (IHS), Holger Bonin, hat bei den Lohnverhandlungen für „differenzierte Abschlüsse“ plädiert. Die Lage in der österreichischen Wirtschaft sei „sehr, sehr unterschiedlich“, sagte der Wirtschaftsforscher am Sonntag in der ORF-„Pressestunde“. Für den Handel zeichnete er etwa eine düstere Prognose, Handelsangestellte müssten bereit sein, sich „umzuorientieren“. Bonin sprach sich auch für eine Modifizierung der Benya-Formel aus, Kritik kam vom Österreichischen Gewerkschaftsbund (ÖGB).

Die vom früheren Gewerkschafter Anton Benya erstellte Formel bildet seit den 1970er Jahren die Grundlage für die jährlichen Lohnverhandlungen. Laut dieser Formel werden die jährliche Inflation – also der durchschnittliche Verbraucherpreisindex – und ein Teil des gesamtwirtschaftlichen Produktivitätszuwachses gefordert.

Im Handel, bei dem vergangene Woche die Lohnverhandlungen begonnen haben, sei man nach wie vor mit der steigenden Konkurrenz durch den Onlinehandel konfrontiert, „das Kaufverhalten der Bürgerinnen und Bürger hat sich massiv verändert“, sagte Bonin. Im Handel habe man eine „komplett andere Situation“ als etwa in einem erfolgreichen Segment der Industrie, „wo exportiert wird“ und wo man davon profitiert, „dass der Welthandel wieder steigt“.

Seinen Wunsch nach „differenzierten Abschlüssen“ würde die „Benya-Formel“ aber eigentlich ohnehin besagen: „Wir orientieren uns an der Inflation und schauen uns dann an, wie sich die Produktivitätsentwicklung in den einzelnen Branchen eigentlich entwickeln sollte.“

Man müsse vermehrt auf die Produktivitätskomponente schauen, sagte der IHS-Chef. Denn man habe diese Produktivitätskomponente „oft am Durchschnitt der Wirtschaft festgemacht“ und „nicht so sehr geschaut: Wie geht es denn bei einzelnen Branchen?“

Zu den Forderungen der Arbeitnehmerseite bei den Verhandlungen zum Handelskollektivvertrag sagte Bonin, der Wunsch nach 3,8 Prozent Lohnplus sei „am oberen Rand dessen, was eben möglicherweise erfüllbar ist für Teile des Handels“. Auch innerhalb des Handels gebe es „sehr unterschiedliche Unternehmen“.

Die Produktivitätsentwicklung im Handel sei auf längere Frist aber negativ. „Und das muss man eben in irgendeiner Form bei den Handelsverhandlungen berücksichtigen.“ Die Löhne würden sich nicht mehr so dynamisch wie in anderen Bereichen der Wirtschaft entwickeln.

Man müsse damit rechnen, dass die Löhne im Handel über einen längeren Zeitraum nicht mehr nach oben gehen. Wenn man etwa in die Innenstädte schaue, dann merke man, „dass es immer weniger Geschäfte gibt“ und diese etwa durch Restaurants ersetzt werden. Diese Veränderungen werde man nicht mehr zurückdrehen können, allerdings würden etwa im Bereich der Freizeitindustrie neue Jobs entstehen.

Menschen, die im Handel beschäftigt sind, müssten diese Signale sehen und bereit sein, sich „umzuorientieren“, so Bonin. Gefragt – mit Blick auf die Demonstrationen in Frankreich –, ob man nicht auch den sozialen Frieden etwas einpreisen müsse, sagte Bonin, der soziale Frieden werde nicht dadurch gewahrt, dass man auf Dauer Bereiche der Wirtschaft fördert, die nicht langfristig tragfähig sind. Man müsse den Strukturwandel sehen, da könne man nicht dagegen agieren.

Mit Blick auf die Wirtschaft zeichnet sich laut IHS ein leichtes Wachstum ab, die Teuerung bleibt aber weiterhin hoch. Von Preiseingriffen hält der IHS-Chef wenig. Vielmehr müsse geprüft werden, ob eine Kopplung einzelner Bereiche an die Teuerung tatsächlich zielführend sei, etwa bei den Mieten.

Bonin stellt infrage, ob Mieten automatisch und „eins zu eins“ an die Inflation angepasst werden müssten, „nur weil zum Beispiel das Glas Rotwein im Restaurant besonders teuer wird“. Um Wohnen in Österreich günstiger zu machen, müsse vor allem mehr gebaut werden, sagte er.

Zudem plädiert Bonin – langfristig – für eine Anhebung des Pensionsantrittsalters. Dabei spiele die steigende Lebenserwartung eine Rolle. Gleichzeitig müsse man Antworten darauf finden, dass Menschen mit gesundheitlichen Einschränkungen nicht so lange arbeiten können.

Angesprochen auf die Sozialhilfereform der Bundesregierung hält Bonin die angestrebten jährlichen Einsparungen von 200 Millionen Euro für realistisch. An der Reform selbst übte er aber Kritik: Zwar sei es notwendig, die Leistungen bundesweit einheitlicher zu gestalten, allerdings müsse man die verschärften Anforderungen hinterfragen.

So sei etwa noch offen, ob ausreichend Kurse des Arbeitsmarktservice (AMS) angeboten werden könnten und ob diese geeignet seien, die Betroffenen nachhaltig in den Arbeitsmarkt zu integrieren. Das AMS müsse in den kommenden Jahren sparen und gleichzeitig zusätzliche Kurse für die betroffenen Langzeitarbeitslosen und Menschen mit Migrationshintergrund anbieten.

Entscheidend sei, ob die Maßnahmen den jeweiligen Bedürfnissen gerecht würden. „Es hilft uns am Ende nichts, wenn die Menschen dann alle Foodora-Fahrer sind“, sagte Bonin. Mit gezielter Qualifizierung könnten sie möglicherweise auch in anderen Bereichen zu dringend gesuchten Fachkräften werden.

Holger Bonin, Direktor des Instituts für Höhere Studien (IHS), äußerte sich in der „Pressestunde“ am Sonntag unter anderem über die Sozialhilfereform, wobei er die Details der Regelung kritisierte.

Der ÖGB wies die Aussagen Bonins am Sonntag zurück: „Die Kritik an Kollektivverträgen und Benya-Formel sowie Forderungen nach einem höheren Pensionsantrittsalter gehen aus Sicht des ÖGB an der Lebensrealität der Beschäftigten vorbei“, sagte ÖGB-Bundesgeschäftsführerin Helene Schuberth.

Bonins Kritik an „angeblich zu hohen Lohnabschlüssen“ greife zu kurz. „In Österreich werden Kollektivverträge seit jeher nach Branchen verhandelt. Die jeweilige wirtschaftliche Lage wird dabei berücksichtigt“, so Schuberth. Gleichzeitig mussten die Lohnabschlüsse der vergangenen Jahre hohe Kaufkraftverluste infolge der hohen Inflation ausgleichen, betonte sie.

FPÖ-Sozialsprecherin Dagmar Belakowitsch bezeichnete die Aussagen Bonins als „Signal für eine Politik der sozialen Kälte“. Die Vorschläge zu Lohnverhandlungen und Pensionen seien ein „unsozialer Frontalangriff auf die arbeitende Bevölkerung und die ältere Generation“ und würden die wahren Probleme im Land „völlig ignorieren“.

Rekordbeteiligung zeichnet sich ab

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Bei der vorgezogenen Parlamentswahl in Serbien hat sich am Sonntag eine Rekordbeteiligung abgezeichnet. Selbst bei Wahlschluss um 20.00 Uhr standen noch zahlreiche Menschen vor den Wahllokalen. Umfragen sehen ein Kopf-an-Kopf-Rennen zwischen der aus einer Protestbewegung hervorgegangenen Studentenliste „Students Win“ und dem von Ex-Präsident Aleksandar Vucic angeführten Wahlbündnis „Vereintes Serbien“. Überschattet wurde die Wahl von Manipulationsvorwürfen, im Zentrum Belgrads gab es viel Polizeipräsenz.

Die Beteiligung erreichte schon vor Wahlschluss ein Rekordniveau. Bis 18.00 Uhr gaben 64,9 Prozent der Wahlberechtigten ihre Stimme ab, so viel wie noch nie seit dem Zerfall Jugoslawiens im Jahr 1992. Die Wahlbeteiligung dürfte über 70 Prozent steigen, bis 19.00 Uhr lag sie laut der unabhängigen Wahlbeobachtungsagentur CRTA bei 67,6 Prozent.

Die Auszählung dürfte sich verzögern, gab es doch um 20.00 Uhr Berichte über lange Warteschlangen. Allen, die sich bei Wahlschluss vor dem Wahllokal eingefunden hatten, muss nach serbischem Recht noch die Stimmabgabe ermöglicht werden. Rund 6,45 Millionen Wahlberechtigte sind offiziellen Angaben zufolge dazu aufgerufen, die 250 Abgeordneten der Volksversammlung zu wählen.

Besonders groß war der Andrang in der Diaspora, auch in Wien. Vor dem serbischen Konsulat bildete sich eine Warteschlange von Menschen, die ihre Stimme abgeben wollten – mehr dazu in wien.ORF.at. Auch aus vielen Orten in Serbien und anderen europäischen Metropolen wurde von langen Schlangen vor Wahllokalen berichtet.

In sozialen Netzwerken geteilte Videos aus Städten wie London, Paris, Zürich und Stockholm zeigten ähnliche Bilder. Die Diaspora gilt traditionell als Hochburg der proeuropäischen Opposition. In Zürich berichtete eine Wählerin einem Journalisten des „Tages-Anzeiger“, dass sie siebeneinhalb Stunden auf die Stimmabgabe gewartet habe.

Das Ergebnis entscheidet darüber, ob Vucic seine teilweise autoritär ausgeübte Macht bewahren kann oder nicht. In wechselnden Funktionen bestimmt der 56-Jährige bereits seit 2012 die Politik des Landes, Kritiker werfen ihm Korruption und Verbindungen zur organisierten Kriminalität vor. Er war Ende September von seinem Amt als Staatspräsident zurückgetreten, um im Falle eines Wahlerfolgs seiner Partei Regierungschef zu werden. Sein zweites Mandat als Präsident hätte im Mai nächsten Jahres geendet, ein drittes Mal hätte Vucic nicht kandidieren können.

Die Bewegung, die Vucic nun herausfordert, ging aus den Protesten nach dem Einsturz des Bahnhofsvordachs in der nordserbischen Stadt Novi Sad im November 2024 hervor. Dabei waren 16 Menschen ums Leben gekommen. Kritiker führten das Unglück auf Korruption und Schlamperei der Vucic-Regierung zurück.

Am Samstagabend bedankte sich die studentische Protestbewegung beim serbischen Volk für seine Unterstützung und rief die Wählerinnen und Wähler zur Stimmabgabe auf. „Serbien erwacht! Ihr wart und bleibt unsere Stärke. Es ist an der Zeit, diese Stärke auf die Freiheit zu richten“, hieß es in einem im Onlinedienst X veröffentlichten Schreiben.

Vucic meldete sich bereits kurz nach Wahlschluss zu Wort. Er versicherte, dass er sich „immer verantwortungsvoll“ verhalten werde. Zudem versicherte er, dass er eine Niederlage anerkennen würde. Zugleich kündigte er an, dass gegen 23.00 Uhr der Wahlsieger verkündet werde. Einem Bericht des Fernsehsenders N1 zufolge musste auch Vucic zwei Stunden lang in einer Warteschlange stehen, um seine Stimme abgeben zu können.

Planmäßig blieben die Wahllokale bis 20.00 Uhr (Ortszeit) geöffnet. Erstmalig galt das auch für die im Ausland lebenden Stimmberechtigten, die mancherorts per Beschluss des Außenministeriums dadurch mitten in der Nacht zur Stimmabgabe gebeten werden. Mit aussagekräftigen Teilergebnissen wird am späten Abend gerechnet. Die Wahlen finden in einer angespannten Atmosphäre statt, begleitet von Vorwürfen der Wählereinschüchterung und des Drucks auf Regierungsgegner.

Zum ersten Mal in Serbien haben sich kurz vor dem Wahltermin an die 300 ehemalige Polizeispitzenfunktionäre, aber auch viele noch im Dienste stehende Ordnungshüter an die Berufskollegen mit dem gemeinsamen Appell gewandt, sich in ihrem Vorgehen am Wahltag und den Tagen danach an die Verfassung, Gesetze und Berufsethik zu halten.

„Die Bürger Serbiens müssen in der Lage sein, ihr demokratisches Wahlrecht in einem friedlichen und sicheren Umfeld wahrzunehmen“, betonte der EU-Botschafter in Serbien, Andreas von Beckerath, in einem Posting auf X. Er rief alle Beteiligten zur Ruhe auf.

Für Unruhe sorgte am späten Nachmittag, dass ein großes Aufgebot von Polizisten in Kampfausrüstung vor dem Sitz der staatlichen Wahlbehörde in Belgrad Stellung bezog. Zudem rotteten sich in einem Park im Stadtzentrum zahlreiche bewaffnete Männer in schwarzer Kleidung zusammen, offenbar Anhänger des Regierungslagers. Es wurde befürchtet, dass sie Auseinandersetzungen mit Oppositionsanhängern provozieren könnten, um den Sicherheitskräften einen Grund zum Eingreifen zu liefern.

Regierungskritiker berichteten von zahlreichen Unregelmäßigkeiten, etwa versuchtem Stimmenkauf oder der illegalen Anfertigung von Wahlprotokollen durch Beisitzer der Regierungspartei. Für Unruhe sorgte auch, dass den wenigen verbliebenen regierungskritischen Medien die Akkreditierung für die Wahlparty der Vucic-Partei SNS verweigert wurde.

„Wir sind einen Schritt davon entfernt, dass wir Geschichte schreiben“, sagte eine Sprecherin der oppositionellen Studentenliste nach Wahlschluss um 20.00 Uhr. Zuvor wurde zudem versichert, dass man sich entschlossen gegen die Manipulationsversuche stelle. Sie rief ihre Anhängerinnen und Anhänger auf, Ruhe zu bewahren. Vertreter der Oppositionsbewegung seien in jedem Wahllokal präsent, „und wird haben jede Stimme unter Kontrolle“, betonte sie. Die EU zeigte sich „zutiefst besorgt über Berichte von gewaltsamen Vorfällen in einer Reihe von Wahllokalen“.

Für die EU-Wahlbeobachtungsmission unter der Leitung von Andreas Schieder (SPÖ) wurden die Sicherheitsmaßnahmen erhöht. „Aufgrund der jüngsten Ereignisse mussten wir die Vorkehrungen für Herrn Schieder verstärken“, bestätigte Sprecherin Cristina Castagnoli aus dem EU-Parlament heute der APA. „Wir haben in Belgrad eine Polizeieskorte für die gesamte Delegation“, sagte die Sprecherin.

Weitere Angaben wollte Castagnoli nicht machen. Aus Sicherheitsgründen könnten keine weiteren Details genannt werden, hieß es. Neben Schieder ist auch der NEOS-EU-Abgeordnete Helmut Brandstätter Teil der siebenköpfigen Delegation des Europäischen Parlaments unter Schirmherrschaft der Organisation für Sicherheit und Zusammenarbeit in Europa (OSZE).

Nobel Prize in literature: How the winner is secretly chosen

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Can Xue is considered one of this year's favorites; Margaret Atwood and Salman Rushdie are other strong contenders. But what goes on behind the scenes in the selection of the Nobel Prize in literature?

On Thursday, October 8, at around 1:00 p.m. CEST, all eyes in the literary world will be set on Stockholm.

That's when the new permanent secretary of the Swedish Academy, Ingrid Carlberg, will hold a press conference to announce the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature, marking the end of a lengthy selection process that officially began a year earlier.

The Swedish Academy is the body responsible for awarding the prize. Its 18 members consist of writers, professors and intellectuals.

In September of the previous year, the Academy's Nobel Committee sends forms to hundreds of individuals and organizations eligible to submit nominations for the Nobel Prize in literature.

According to the Nobel Prize website, these include professors of literature and linguistics at universities and colleges, previous Nobel laureates in literature and the heads of writers' associations that represent the literary community in their respective countries. Anyone from one of these groups who has not received a form may also submit a nomination.

"The list can reach 200 names per year of nominated authors," explains Gisele Sapiro, research director at France's National Centre for Scientific Research. She studies how the Nobel Prize, as a global institution, influences the international circulation of literature, power dynamics in the literary world and translation markets.

"The committee can also add names itself, such as authors who were nominated in previous years," she says.

In the spring, the Nobel Committee then selects five names through a multistage process and presents them to the Academy. Over the summer, Academy members read the nominees' works and discuss them. The Academy always announces the decision in early October.

As for the criteria the Academy uses to make its decision, it prefers to keep them to itself.

The public doesn't learn which nominees missed out until 50 years later — that's how long the Academy waits before releasing the list.

Sometimes decades pass between an author's first nomination and actually receiving the prize. Danish writer Johannes V. Jensen, who won the Nobel Prize in literature in 1944, waited 40 years. Doris Lessing received the award in 2007 after 35 years, while Thomas Mann, who won in 1929, had to wait 27 years.

In his will, Alfred Nobel, the prize's founder, stipulated that it should be awarded to the person who had produced "the most outstanding work in an idealistic direction." That leaves plenty of room for interpretation.

According to Gisele Sapiro, the Academy aims to honor ambitious, influential literature that has, for example, given rise to a new literary movement. "At the same time, the work has to be universal, being able to transcend its own culture," she says.

Translations can be one indication of this. "I noted in my research, however, that all the laureates since 1990 had been translated into English, French and German — except for Abdulrazak Gurnah [the Tanzanian-British 2021 Nobel laureate], who had not been translated into German before winning — meaning that I think these three languages are the most important," Sapiro explains.

Sapiro says it is also a major advantage if nominees have already won other major literary awards, such as the International Man Booker Prize, the Cervantes Prize or the Büchner Prize.

Politics can also influence the Swedish Academy's decisions.

On several occasions, the prize has gone to writers who openly opposed the regimes in their home countries, such as Belarusian writer Svetlana Alexievich in 2015. She is an outspoken critic of authoritarian President Alexander Lukashenko.

Genre, on the other hand, does not matter. In the past, laureates have been honored not only for novels but also for poetry and plays. In 2016, singer-songwriter Bob Dylan received the prize for his song lyrics.

So far, only 33 of the 122 laureates have come from outside Europe. There is also a clear gender imbalance: only in the past eight years have men and women alternated as winners, significantly increasing women's share of the total number of laureates to 14.9%.

Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

Prague honors Roma resistance fighters against Hitler

The exhibit explores the role of Czech and Slovak Romani resistance fighters during the Nazi German occupation and the dismantling of Czechoslovakia from 1938 to 1945.

Until now, Czech and Slovak Roma appeared in the history of World War II almost exclusively as victims of the Holocaust against Roma and Sinti. In the so-called Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, Nazi Germany murdered approximately 90% of the Czech Roma during World War II.

However, what is less well known is that a large number of Czech and Slovak Roma fought against the German occupiers as partisans in resistance groups and as soldiers in the Czechoslovak Underground Army, or Defense of the Nation, ON.

The exhibition at the Museum of the Roma National Movement therefore marks a turning point in how the Roma are viewed during World War II. At least that is the hope of museum director Emil Scuka, a lawyer, former prosecutor and one of the best-known Czech Roma activists.

"We were not only victims; we were also heroes," Scuka said in his speech to several hundred attendees at the exhibition opening, most of whom were Roma from Czechia and Slovakia. "Many people, not even among the Roma themselves, are aware that we Roma fought against Nazism," he noted. In his view, "organizing and opening the exhibition was something my generation owed to these Roma heroes and today we are repaying that debt."

Scuka called on those present: "Bring your children and grandchildren here so they can see for themselves and realize that the Roma have heroes too!"

He also pointed out that this topic had been completely marginalized in Czech and Slovak historical writing for decades and that the Roma had therefore decided on their own to document the fates of their war heroes.

The exhibition presents the detailed life stories of 100 of the most significant Roma partisans and Roma soldiers from the Czech underground army and resistance groups who fought against the Nazis.

"We found over 1,400 Romani fighters in the archives. From among them, we selected the 100 most interesting stories for this exhibition," said exhibition curator Radoslav Scuka at the opening.

Among these figures is Anton Facuna, a sergeant in the American intelligence agency Office of Strategic Services (OSS), who was flown to Slovakia in October 1944 on a secret mission. Slovakia had been a puppet state of Hitler from 1939 to 1945.

Another is Josef Serinek, known by the nickname "Black Partisan from Vysocina," who managed to escape from the Nazi concentration camp for Roma in Lety and subsequently founded and commanded the large Capajev-Cerny partisan unit in the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands.

In 2022, Czech President Milos Zeman posthumously awarded him the Medal for Heroism. Toward the end of his life, Serinek shared his memories with historian Jan Tesar, who later wrote the three-volume book "Ceska cikanska rapsodie", which translates to Czech Gypsy Rhapsody.

Except for Tesar's book, the history of Roma resistance against Nazism has not been researched by Czech and Slovak historians to this day. "I am not aware of any scholarly historical work that deals with Roma resistance against Nazism," Prokop Tomek, a historian at the Military History Institute in Prague, told DW.

"The number of 1,400 Roma who took part in the fight against Nazism as members of the resistance or in the foreign army does not strike me as an exaggeration and corresponds to the proportion of the Roma population in Czechoslovakia," Tomek said, adding that the "exhibition on the Roma resistance against Nazism during the war is undoubtedly groundbreaking."

To this day, many Roma in Czechia and Slovakia do not publicly acknowledge their ethnic identity. This is partly due to experiences with the Holocaust during the Nazi era that have been passed down through generations. Czech historians and politicians continue to debate the extent to which the Czech authorities of the Protectorate were complicit in the extermination of the Czech Roma.

However, one thing is certain: the police registration of all Roma, which had been enshrined in law in Czechoslovakia since 1927, facilitated the extermination of the Roma by the German Nazis.

According to estimates by the Czech government, approximately 250,000 Roma live in the country today. In the 2021 census, however, only 21,000 people identified as Roma.

Of all ethnic groups in Czechia, which has a population of 10 million, the Roma are among the three largest ethnic minorities in terms of numbers. However, they are the most socially disadvantaged, have very little political representation, and are therefore scarcely present in Czech politics.

Aleksandar Vucic, the Serbian leader brought down by students

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Serbia’s longtime leader concedes defeat to a student-led protest movement in parliamentary elections.

Aleksandar Vucic, who has shaped Serbia’s political life for more than a decade, has lost snap parliamentary elections to a student-led movement that led protests against him for almost two years.

After a heated race, Vucic conceded defeat to the Student List in Sunday’s elections, which saw voter turnout reach its highest level in more than 30 years.

“It is clear that we have lost these elections,” the leader of the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) told supporters after a sample of votes showed his electoral list was expected to win 105 seats in Serbia’s 250-member parliament, compared with 135 to 140 for the Student List.

The student movement emerged after 16 people were killed when a train station canopy collapsed in the city of Novi Sad on November 1, 2024.

The youth-led protests grew into a broader wave of social unrest and evolved into the Student List political platform, which promised to tackle corruption, depoliticise public institutions and restore citizens’ rights.

During the election campaign, the movement was led by Ilija Srdanovic, a professor at the Faculty of Medicine in Novi Sad.

Vucic referred to the demonstrators, who included university professors, doctors and workers, as “blockaders” and accused them of seeking to destabilise Serbia.

A former ultranationalist, Vucic cultivated an image of a pragmatic leader who maintained ties with the European Union, Russia and China as well as, increasingly, with Israel in recent years.

Born in Belgrade in 1970, the nearly 2-metre-tall (6.5ft-tall) Vucic graduated from the University of Belgrade’s faculty of law. He entered politics in 1993, joining the ultranationalist Serbian Radical Party led by Vojislav Seselj.

His political record during the 1990s was marked by nationalist rhetoric. One of his most remembered statements came in Serbia’s parliament in July 1995, nine days after the Srebrenica genocide: “Kill one Serb, and we will kill 100 Muslims.” Vucic made the comments during a debate on possible NATO strikes against positions held by the Army of Republika Srpska in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Vucic’s political mentor at the time, Seselj, was sentenced in 2018 to 10 years in prison by the Appeals Chamber of the International Residual Mechanism for Criminal Tribunals for persecution, deportation and forcible transfer of non-Serbs during the wars in the Balkans in the 1990s.

Aleksandar Seselj, the son of Vojislav Seselj, was on Vucic’s electoral list in Sunday’s elections.

After a split within the Serbian Radical Party, Vucic and Tomislav Nikolic founded the SNS in 2008. The new party sought to move closer to mainstream European politics.

Safeta Bisevac, a Serbian journalist who has long followed Vucic’s political career, believes he was initially less popular than Nikolic, who appeared closer to common citizens.

“Vucic lagged far behind in popularity at the time and was essentially the organiser of the newly formed party,” Bisevac told Al Jazeera.

In 2012, the SNS came to power promising to fight corruption, organised crime and abuses committed under previous governments.

One of the most prominent cases was the December 2012 arrest of businessman Miroslav Miskovic and his son Marko on suspicion of financial irregularities and tax evasion. After years of legal proceedings, both were acquitted and the state paid them compensation.

Bisevac said the results of the fight against corruption remained modest while the authorities expanded their control over institutions, the media and political life.

Vucic served as first deputy prime minister from 2012 to 2014 before ascending to the post of prime minister in 2014. Three years later, he became president of Serbia and won re-election in 2022. His term was due to run until 2027.

Under pressure from the student-led protests, he resigned in September and called early elections. Although the Serbian presidency is constitutionally limited, Vucic has retained a dominant influence over his party and state policy.

“He made many promises and fulfilled few of them, but in the meantime, he established almost complete control over the state apparatus,” Bisevac said.

The Serbian economy has expanded significantly since Vucic took Serbia’s political centre stage in 2012. Unemployment dropped from 26 percent to 8 to 9 percent while nominal gross domestic product nearly tripled, reaching about 90 billion euros ($101bn), according to official data cited by the Reuters news agency.

Annual foreign direct investment inflows rose from about 800 million euros ($896m) in 2012 to a record 5.2 billion euros ($5.8bn) in 2024.

A network of modern motorways has also been built.

However, rising wages have not necessarily translated into a proportional improvement in living standards. The rising cost of living and public debt remain important elements of the debate over the government’s economic record.

Political scientist Dusko Radosavljevic said “the students simply gave a voice to the justified anger of citizens.”

Speaking to Al Jazeera about Vucic’s policies, Radosavljevic said the past decade was characterised by “systematic pressure and the destruction of social institutions”.

“State intelligence services have spent less time on their actual duties and more time monitoring political opponents while critical thinking has been pushed out of the public media,” Radosavljevic said.

Birthkeeper who allegedly ‘groomed’ pregnant women to fear hospital could face criminal charges

Victorian coroner hears Emily Lal told Stacey Warnecke that her ‘condition and mindset’ needed to be ‘solid’ to avoid a hospital birth

A birthkeeper who allegedly “groomed” pregnant women to fear hospital could be referred to prosecutors to face potential criminal charges, a Victorian coroner has heard.

Stacey Warnecke died in September 2025 at Frankston hospital in Melbourne after giving birth at home and suffering a postpartum haemorrhage with birthkeeper Emily Lal by her side, having paid her $6,000 to support her throughout pregnancy.

Birthkeepers have no clinical training and operate outside the medical system.

The inquest into Warnecke’s death began in June, but closing submissions to coroner Therese McCarthy were delayed after a forensic analysis of Warnecke’s phone.

Hearings resumed on Monday, when Rachel Ellyard, in her role as counsel assisting the coroner, said that the completed analysis had revealed numerous messages, including in a group chat on the platform WhatsApp coordinated by Lal for women who were choosing freebirth. A phone call between Warnecke, a 30-year-old nutritionist and wellness influencer, and Lal was played to the court.

Ellyard said the phone evidence directly contradicted evidence from Lal, given in June, that she was hired by Warnecke primarily to play the role of a supportive friend, and that the money paid to her was primarily for her time, but not for medical advice.

“Ms Lal saw her role as to groom her clients into being strong enough to resist the temptation to seek medical help during labor, to seek pain relief during labor, to go to hospital during labor,” Ellyard said.

“It’s clear that Ms Lal thought that in most cases, going to hospital would represent a failure of someone who hadn’t done the work, who hadn’t understood enough what the intensity of labor was going to be.”

During the call, Warnecke told Lal that she envisioned having a “wise woman” next to her during her first childbirth, to guide her when asking: “Am I dying, or am I alright?”

Warnecke told Lal she was afraid of the hospital.

Lal responded: “You should be scared of the hospital.”

“It’s a terrifying place. For me personally, I think the biggest, the key … to actually staying home and having your baby … is that your mindset and your condition needs to be so solid.”

Lal also told Warnecke that midwives who attend home births sometimes inadvertently sabotage them, because if the birth goes on too long, they tell the woman to go to hospital. This is sometimes just because the midwife is exhausted, Lal said.

She told Warnecke: “I manage my own energy as much as I can in that way, so that I don’t subconsciously sabotage a person.”

Lal previously told the court that she was not clinically trained, and that it was not her responsibility to make Warnecke’s birth safer, or to call an ambulance unless specifically asked. Lal said she never gave Warnecke medical advice, and only shared her own experiences of freebirthing her own children.

Ellyard said on Monday that given the evidence from the call and many messages and documents obtained from Warnecke’s phone, “the role that Stacey thought Ms Lal would play during the freebirth … was to help her by keeping her safe”.

“Stacey was placing considerable reliance on Ms Lal, in whom she had placed considerable trust.

“Ms Lal was an expert. She was providing advice. She was providing referrals. She was selling supplements. She was offering commentary.

“This is all quite inconsistent with the idea of just being a friend who pulls your hair back and wipes your forehead. It’s something much more structured, much more deliberate.”

Ellyard said Warnecke’s husband, Nathan, had also been “reassured or indoctrinated into a sense that his role was just to just sit back and support, and that whatever Stacey experienced was alright”.

Lal was invited to return to court to give further evidence after the phone analysis, but had declined to, the court heard.

Lal only gave evidence previously on the condition that the coroner grant her a certificate of protection so that her evidence could not be used against her in any future proceedings.

Ellyard put to the coroner on Monday that it would be open to her to find that Lal had committed perjury “in relation to the falsity of the evidence” and to refer her to the Office of Public Prosecutions.

The OPP prepares and conducts criminal matters on behalf of the director.

“Having regard to the materials that have now become available … it would be open to your honour to form the view that Ms Lal wasn’t truthful in the evidence that she gave to you, and that a referral could be made in relation to the offence of perjury,” Ellyard said.

She said there was also evidence Lal had been “holding herself out as someone capable of giving such expert advice and capable of meeting the needs of pregnant women in a range of circumstances” despite having no authority.

The court previously heard that Lal had asked Warnecke three times whether she wanted an ambulance called after she gave birth to the placenta, lost up to 1.5 litres of blood, then began struggling to breathe.

Lal previously told the court that it was not her role to call an ambulance against her client’s wishes, even if the woman was unconscious and dying.

Warnecke said yes to an ambulance the third time, but by the time paramedics arrived she was seriously unwell, having suffered a massive postpartum haemorrhage.

Warnecke died in hospital several hours later from related complications, with the hospital exhausting its blood supply trying to save her.

Ellyard said on Monday: “Stacey would have survived if she was in hospital.”

Midwives and doctors know that postpartum haemorrhage is the most common complication of childbirth and are so well trained to identify it and treat it that “women do not die in hospital” from it in well-resourced countries, Ellyard said.

Warnecke also “would have survived if she was giving birth at home with a registered midwife,” Ellyard said, drawing from evidence given by medical professionals earlier in the inquest.

“She suffered a known complication of childbirth, which was eminently treatable if identified and responded to immediately.”

The coroner will deliver her findings at a later date.

Dies trägt zu einer hohen Bruttomarge der amerikanischen Raffinerien bei, was sich in boomenden Gewinnen und Aktienkursen ausdrückt.

Dennoch ist davon ist nichts zu sehen. Die Erdölindustrie ist ein wichtiger politischer Unterstützer, mit dem sich Trump nicht anlegen will. Lieber geht er stattdessen auf die Ukraine los, auch wenn das der Sache gar nicht dient.

Warum sind die Dieselpreise überhaupt so hoch? Die weltweite Produktion von Diesel und Gasöl (im Wesentlichen Heizöl) ging laut Daten der Internationalen Energieagentur (IEA) im dritten Quartal gegenüber dem Vorjahr um 1,7 Millionen auf 27,2 Millionen Fass pro Tag zurück. RIO DE JANEIRO – Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, Brazil’s president granted an unprecedented fourth term in office, once likened the events of his life to a miracle.

The titan of the political left made history by becoming the first person to win a presidential run-off after losing the first round in Brazil.

His shock win follows a long and storied political career, beginning in the bowels of a metalworkers’ trade union and even including stints behind bars.

He charted a rocky road to his fourth election victory.

Questions about his ability to do the job at almost 81 encouraged Lula to do squats and jog along the campaign trail to prove that he was fitter than ever.

Initially startled by his second-place finish to Flavio Bolsonaro earlier in October, Lula dusted himself off and launched into an energetic run-off campaign.

He upped his social media game at the eleventh hour, posting selfie videos and buying a cellphone for the first time.

The shift highlighted a certain disconnect with his country’s extremely online, deeply polarised population, 14 years after he was first elected.

His victory preserves Latin America’s largest country as a leftist stronghold amid a regional lurch to the right championed by US President Donald Trump.

Lula, a defining figure in Brazilian politics over four decades, rose from poverty to the presidency, fell into disgrace and was jailed, before once again rising from the political ashes when he returned to power almost four years ago.

His first-round campaign focused heavily on his life story and legacy, which critics said was a misreading of what weary Brazilians wanted to hear.

The president grew up in deep poverty, the seventh of eight children in a family of illiterate farmers in the arid north-eastern state of Pernambuco.

He worked as a shoeshine boy and peanut vendor before becoming a metalworker and trade unionist. In the 1960s, he lost a finger in a workplace accident.

After losing three presidential bids, Lula finally entered the presidential palace in Brasilia in 2003 and was re-elected in 2006.

He left office in 2010 as a blue-collar hero who presided over a commodity-based economic boom that helped lift 30 million people out of poverty.

Massive corruption scandals haunted Lula and his party, fuelling a wave of disgust that propelled Jair Bolsonaro into the presidency in 2018.

Lula was jailed before the election until the charges were annulled on procedural grounds and the lead judge on the case was found to be biased.

However, Lula was not exonerated and the scandal contributed to enduring polarisation in Brazil.

Bolsonaro’s presidency brought more division, and by the 2022 election, Lula pulled off a dramatic comeback by a razor-thin margin.

In his third term, Lula is credited with restoring social programmes eroded by his predecessor, curbing the destruction of the Amazon rainforest, and putting Brazil’s democracy back on a more secure footing.

But the father of five still triggers fierce rejection among voters frustrated by crime and corruption.

He has also faced criticism over Brazil’s mounting public debt, which, with high interest rates, costs the government around US$18 billion (S$23 billion) a month in interest alone.

Lula came under fire for an election-season spending push, including an increase to the Bolsa Familia social welfare benefits which, launched 20 years ago, remains his most significant legacy.

“He has that love for the poor. That’s all it is,” fisherman Antonio Carlos Lima Ferreira told AFP at his home in the interior of Maranhao state, recalling times when he had to send his son to bed hungry.

“Today, at my table, I can choose what I want to eat.”

Lula said recently he did not want to be remembered as “just the Bolsa Familia president”.

Lula, who has not cultivated a potential successor, campaigned on a pledge to stop the far right from returning to “destroy” the country.

His improbable victory on Oct 25 awed his supporters, many of whom feared an erosion of rights under a theoretical Flavio Bolsonaro presidency.

Lula “will always remain a symbol of the defence of democracy”, civil servant Gustavo Monteiro, 58, told AFP at a leftist rally.

“He represents the fight against political repression.”

The leftist veteran has already spent more time in office than any other democratically elected leader in Latin America’s biggest country.

“There is no one in this country with more experience as president than me,” Lula told foreign media, including AFP, in an interview. AFP

Xi Jinping’s corruption crackdown in the spotlight as China prepares for meeting of top leaders

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

The fifth plenum comes against a backdrop of years of purges that have had some analysts wondering if the issue is corruption or a lack of loyalty

When Xi Jinping came to power in 2012, he pledged to stamp out corruption all through the ranks of the Communist party. More than a decade later, the pace of investigations has not slowed. Last year, China’s disciplinary authorities opened investigations into more than 1 million people, including more than 100 officials at or above ministerial or provincial level.

As Chinese Communist party (CCP) officials kick off an important meeting this week, discipline – and party loyalty – is high on the agenda. For those who try to read the tea leaves of elite Chinese politics, concern about the apparent lack of discipline is dominating CCP discussions.

The “fifth plenum” began on Monday and is set to focus on “major issues on advancing the party’s full and rigorous self-governance”, according to a Chinese state media. In party-speak, “governance” is a byword for discipline, loyalty and anti-corruption.

As Xi continues to crack down on corruption, the fact that investigations nearly always result in a conviction begs the question over whether targets are truly guilty of corruption, or are they just political rivals to the people in charge.

Kerry Brown, director of the Lau China Institute at King’s College London, explained that in Xi’s worldview, which is obsessed with discipline and loyalty, there may not be much difference between the two. “If you’re corrupt, you’re not politically loyal. If you’re politically loyal, you’re not corrupt,” Brown said.

He added that in the CCP system, “corruption is not just material things”, but also “spiritual corruption”, which includes political disloyalty.

Some analysts argue that the reason that the anti-graft drive is so enduring is because corruption is a feature rather than a bug of an authoritarian, top-down political system with a sprawling bureaucracy that governs 1.4 billion people.

Neil Thomas, a senior fellow at the Asia Society thinktank, said: “Xi has made the party less corrupt and strengthened his control, but he knows that tougher punishment cannot fully resolve the problem of officials putting their own interests ahead of the party’s.

“Xi’s answer is to keep tightening discipline, making anti-corruption a permanent method of governing with no obvious point at which he can declare the job done,” Thomas said.

The job may be never ending, but the plenum is an opportunity to refocus the party’s energy on discipline. The fifth plenum is the fifth of seven gatherings that normally happen in every five-year cycle between CCP congresses. The plenums bring together more than 300 members of the CCP’s Central Committee, the most senior officials in the party hierarchy.

The meeting is particularly significant because it is likely to be the last substantive meeting before next year’s 21st Party Congress, where a new Central Committee will be chosen, and where Xi is expected to be anointed for a fourth term as the head of the CCP.

Eliminating anyone who poses a risk to his authority or to the party’s credibility is a key priority for Xi. And potential threats are everywhere. Nearly 15 years after Xi took office, according to CCP proclamations and a host of recent investigations, the problem is still not stamped out. “It is a deeply ingrained part of [Xi’s] worldview,” said Brown. “Everyone else is corrupt”.

In the past, Xi has warned that the party could “lose its ruling status and inevitably be eliminated by history” if it could not get its house in order. “This is by no means an exaggeration,” he said.

The current focus on party governance comes amid a host of high-profile investigations against senior party and military leaders.

In January, Zhang Youxia, China’s top general and a man second only to Xi in the military hierarchy, was placed under investigation; he was expelled from party last month, having already been removed from the military’s top ruling body, the Central Military Commission (CMC)

A host of other purges means that the CMC, which normally has seven members, has been reduced to just two: Xi and Zhang Shengmin, the CMC’s head of discipline.

Other recent scalps include Ma Xingrui, the former party secretary for Xinjiang, and Huang Qifan, the former mayor of Chongqing.

Huang had long been known as the ultimate survivor in Chinese politics because of his connections to the scandals in the early 2010s in which Xi’s political rival, Bo Xilai, the former Party boss for Chongqing, was embroiled in the death of the British businessman Neil Heywood. Bo was ultimately convicted of bribery and embezzlement, for which he is serving a life sentence, but Huang survived. No longer.

Some China analysts have asked whether the recent purges, which have reached the highest echelons of the Party and military ranks, indicate instability in the regime. But Xi likely sees it differently. Corruption investigations demonstrate who is in charge. Plus, Brown added, they are “the most easy and natural tool by which to enforce unity and discipline”.

SAO PAULO – Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva won an unprecedented fourth term on Oct 25, the country’s electoral court said, in a shock result that defied polls, precedent and global anti-incumbent sentiment to put the brakes on a wave of right-wing election victories across Latin America.

Lula, an icon of the global left and the most dominant force in Brazilian politics in this century, defeated Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the eldest son of former president Jair Bolsonaro.

The senator had finished first in the election’s first round but lost in the runoff by over four million votes, said he would ask for the result to be annulled due to a Supreme Court ruling on Oct 25 that extended voting for two hours.

Bolsonaro’s refusal to concede defeat has echoes of his father’s reaction after losing to Lula in 2022. Former president Jair Bolsonaro never accepted his loss and was later given a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup to remain in power.

The scale of Flavio Bolsonaro’s loss to Lula – by more than twice his father’s losing margin – and the fact his campaign also asked for an extension due to rains in the south, make any annulment unlikely, analysts said.

Mario Braga, a Latin America analyst at the risk consultancy RANE, said there could be right-wing protests in coming days, but “it’s very improbable that it will lead to the reversal of the result”.

For Lula, his victory marks another comeback in a career full of them. The 80-year-old was jailed from 2018 to 2019 on corruption charges that were later overturned, paving the way for him to beat Jair Bolsonaro in the 2022 election.

“He is a statesman, a giant,” leftist federal lawmaker Erika Hilton, who was with Lula in Sao Paulo during the vote count, told Reuters.

At times, Lula’s re-election looked like a long shot, as voters soured on him and his Workers’ Party after years of corruption scandals and rising prices.

Sweeping societal changes also left the former metalworker and union leader looking out of touch with an increasingly app-based economy and its growing ranks of informal workers.

A Tokyo-listed exchange-traded fund tracking Brazil’s Bovespa index fell nearly 15% in Asia trade on news of Lula’s victory.

Pollsters said better-than-expected turnout had powered Lula to victory. Leonardo Barreto, the co-founder of consultancy Think Policy, said voters had chosen Lula’s familiar embrace over Bolsonaro’s novelty.

“People today are choosing between change, with some apprehension, and a known path, which guarantees certain rights,” he said.

During the campaign, Lula pointed to a track record of falling poverty and inequality, while unemployment, illiteracy and deforestation hit record lows during his third term.

He warned that Bolsonaro would cut welfare spending, backslide on social advances and hand over Brazil’s sovereignty to US President Donald Trump, whose State Department led a campaign to get the Bolsonaros back to power.

Lula’s win deprives Trump of a major addition to what has been a growing bloc of friendly right-wing Latin American governments that have embraced Washington’s agenda on security and migration.

Celso Amorim, a former Lula foreign minister, told Reuters the leftist “would be very important for establishing a point of balance in Latin America. Lula is that balance”.

Lula becomes the first candidate since Brazil’s return to democracy in the 1980s to be elected after finishing second in the first round.

Lula, who was president between 2003 and 2011, campaigned on a potent mix of pragmatism and populism that has shaped his career in national politics for half a century, rolling out a slew of reforms aimed at improving the lives of workers and poor Brazilians.

These gained steam with late-campaign pledges that ranged from free nationwide weekend public ​transport, which has to be formally submitted to Congress, to a ‌15% increase in his trademark welfare programme that is already being implemented.

Lula also championed a constitutional amendment that became Brazil’s biggest labour reform in decades, which passed with wide support in Congress.

Still, Lula’s fourth term could be his most difficult yet, after Brazil elected its most conservative Congress since returning to democracy in the 1980s.

Combined, Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party (PL) and allied parties could control over 280 seats in the Lower House and up to 50 seats in the Senate, enough for comfortable opposition majorities in both chambers.

That may deepen recent trends of presidents ceding power to Congress on the budget, major legislation and key nominations.

Lula faces an economy that his government forecasts will grow a meager 2.3% in 2027, adding to fiscal concerns tied to high interest rates and public debt that has risen past 82% of GDP, the highest since the pandemic.

The President’s critics blame spending, though borrowing costs have driven most of the increase in debt.

Goldman Sachs said it expects Lula to make modest progress at most to contain spending in a fourth term, as he stays committed to a state-led growth model.

Surging household debt also concerns economists. Trying to address the issue, Lula rolled out a provisional ban on online betting and unveiled a new debt relief program during the campaign.

Donnelly said both federal criminal law and state election law bar noncitizen voters from casting ballots, but "the problem is that California isn't interested in enforcing that provision."

Nach der Parlamentswahl in Serbien hat der langjährige Präsident Aleksandar Vucic die Niederlage seiner seit vielen Jahren herrschenden Fortschrittspartei eingeräumt. "Auf der Grundlage einer geschichteten Stichprobe ist klar, dass wir diese Wahlen verloren haben", sagte Vucic am Sonntagabend vor seinen Anhängern in der Parteizentrale in Belgrad. Bei der Wahl hatte die aus einer studentischen Protestbewegung hervorgegangene Studentska Lista Vucics Serbische Fortschrittspartei (SNS) herausgefordert.

Die Wahlbeteiligung hatte bereits vor Schließung der Wahllokale einen Rekordwert erreicht. Bis 18 Uhr hatten nach Angaben der Wahlkommission 64,89 Prozent der Wahlberechtigten ihre Stimme abgegeben. Serbien: Präsident Aleksandar Vučić gesteht Niederlage bei Parlamentswahl ein

Andere Parteien hätten den Einzug in ‌die Volksvertretung verpasst.

Auf den Straßen der serbischen Hauptstadt Belgrad brach Jubel aus. Autokorsos rasten hupend durch die Stadt. Vor einem Kaufhaus im Zentrum von Belgrad feierten hauptsächlich junge Menschen ausgelassen.

Kurz nach der Ansprache von Vučić veröffentlichte die Wahlforschungsgruppe CRTA das Ergebnis ihrer Projektion. Demnach habe die Studentenliste 51,5 Prozent der Stimmen auf sich vereint, sagte Programmdirektor Rasa Nedeljkov auf einer Pressekonferenz. Die SNS kam der Projektion zufolge auf 40,4 Prozent. Keine weitere Partei habe den Einzug ins Parlament geschafft. Für den Einzug gilt in Serbien eine Drei-Prozent-Hürde beziehungsweise eine Ein-Prozent-Hürde für die Parteien der ethnischen Minderheiten.

Bis 18.00 Uhr hatten nach Angaben der Wahlkommission 64,89 Prozent der Wahlberechtigten ihre Stimme abgegeben. In den Monaten vor der Wahl hatte eine von Studenten getragene Protestbewegung massiv gegen die Regierung mobilisiert.

Auf der Studentenliste stehen nahezu keine Studierenden. Aufgestellt hat sie eine Bewegung, die über keine Führungsgremien verfügt und ihre Entscheidungen in basisdemokratischen Versammlungen trifft. Die Liste besteht vor allem aus Lehrkräften der Universitäten, Ärzten und Künstlern. Angeführt wird sie von dem Kardiologen Ilija Srdanovic aus Novi Sad. Das Elf-Punkte-Programm verspricht die Wiederherstellung von Rechtsstaat und Demokratie sowie die Verfolgung von Korruption und Kriminalität.

Die Wahl am Sonntag fand in einer angespannten Atmosphäre statt. CRTA berichtete von zahlreichen Indizien für einen Stimmenkauf und eine Wählereinschüchterung im ganzen Land. In 48 Prozent der Wahllokale seien Stimmzettel fotografiert worden. Auch über gewalttätige Angriffe auf Wahlbeobachter und Journalisten berichteten die Medien.

Rund 6,4 Millionen Bürgerinnen und Bürger waren offiziellen Angaben zufolge dazu aufgerufen, die 250 Abgeordneten der Volksversammlung (Skupstina) zu wählen. Umfragen sagten ein Kopf-an-Kopf-Rennen zwischen der regierenden SNS von Vučić und der neuen Studentenbewegung voraus.

Der Wahl waren fast zweijährige Proteste gegen mutmaßliche Korruption und zunehmenden Autoritarismus vorausgegangen. Auslöser der Demonstrationen war der Einsturz eines Bahnhofsvordachs in ‌der Stadt Novi Sad im Jahr 2024, bei dem 16 Menschen ums Leben kamen. ‌Die Studentenbewegung hatte seitdem massiv ​an Rückhalt ‌in der Bevölkerung gewonnen und ‌sich ​zur größten Bedrohung für Vučić entwickelt.
