‘The Right to Be Named’: Russians in Amsterdam Remember Victims of Political Repression

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

AMSTERDAM — On a dark October evening in Amsterdam’s Frederiksplein square, around 50 people quietly wait their turn to read out the names of strangers into a microphone.

“Nikolai Khristoforovich Bashlykov. 26 years old. Timber receiver, serviceman. Executed on Dec. 2, 1937.”

“Sabina Savelyevna Bogusevich. 42 years old. Polish. Cook at a sugar factory in the Krasnodar region. Executed on Dec. 14, 1937.”

One by one, participants take a card from a volunteer, read the names into a microphone and step aside. Acquaintances greet each other in hushed voices. Others move quietly around the gathering, photographing the crowd and the flickering candles.

Organizers repeatedly remind them to only photograph people who had given their consent. Many people are afraid of being seen at a public commemoration, even abroad, due to the ongoing threat of repression in Russia, they say.

The gathering was part of Returning the Names, an annual initiative commemorating victims of political repression held every year on Oct. 29.

Established nearly two decades ago to remember people killed during Stalin’s Great Terror, it has since become an occasion to reflect on the relationship between Soviet violence and political repression in present-day Russia.

For years, its main gathering took place at the Solovetsky Stone on Moscow’s Lubyanka Square, just steps from the headquarters used by the Soviet security services and now occupied by the Federal Security Service (FSB).

The event was organized by Memorial, the human rights organization founded to document Soviet political repression. With Memorial now designated an “extremist” organization in Russia, Returning the Names is no longer formally affiliated with it.

Despite this, activists continue to gather to read the victims’ names every Oct. 29. Events took place in more than 100 cities worldwide this year, from Russia’s Irkutsk and Kirov to Melbourne, San Jose and Miami.

In Amsterdam, the commemoration has been organized by writer and historical memory researcher Nikolai Epple since 2022.

“After the Russian experience, it’s surprising,” he told The Moscow Times. “This year, the Dutch police called me just to ask how they could help. It’s very strange and sweet.”

But he lamented the challenges of persuading people to turn out to the event.

“We’re holding it for the fifth time in the Netherlands. Around 50 people always turn up, which feels strange when the diaspora here is so large, around 11,000 people,” Epple said. “I think Russians are still afraid of taking part in any public event. The willingness to do so was systematically stamped out of people over the years.”

Some local Russian-language Telegram channels declined to share announcements for the event, including those aimed at Russians who left after the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, he said.

“They told us: ‘We’re about culture, not politics’,” he said.

He said he believes this reluctance reflects Russians’ tendency to avoid politics as much as a fear of personal consequences.

For the non-Russian participants who regularly attend Returning the Names, organizers make announcements in English and prepare the name cards in several languages so that people who do not speak Russian can participate.

“Maria Georgiyevna Babek. 41 years old. Slovak. Head of the council of Kazan’s Catholic community. Executed on July 7, 1938,” says a man with a Dutch accent, stumbling slightly over the place names and dates. Those who had already taken their turn stood nearby and listened.

“People here have a strong culture of participating in events like this. Showing solidarity matters to them,” Epple said. “And not just over things happening in their own country. When Navalny was killed, a lot of Dutch people came to the rally that day.”

The gathering took place beside the memorial to Walraven van Hall, a leading figure in the Dutch resistance during World War II. Nearby, candles and photographs formed a memorial to the late Kremlin critic Alexei Navalny beneath a large tree.

“Our history and Dutch history are linked,” he said.

Toward the end of the gathering, Navalny’s name was read aloud.

“Alexei Anatolyevich Navalny. Killed on Feb. 16, 2024, in a penal colony in the settlement of Kharp.”

Returning the Names was traditionally understood in Russia as a memorial event rather than a political protest, Epple said.

“But the fact that it was held right on Lubyanka, of course, gave it immense political significance — an event in memory of victims right in front of the building of the organization responsible for that terror,” he said.

Since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, that meaning has become more pronounced.

Last year, the event took place in 150 cities worldwide. The initiative’s decentralized structure means that organizers in each city can choose what they would like to emphasize. Some read only the names of Soviet victims, while others include current political prisoners.

“For us, the crucial thing is to make that direct connection [between past and present] explicit,” Epple said. “Because Soviet terror was never properly examined, reckoned with and condemned, it continues to block any movement toward democracy. Today’s war and today’s terror are a direct consequence of those historical lessons going unlearned.”

Among those waiting to read out the names was Polina, a 37-year-old psychologist who left Russia in 2022 and has lived in Amsterdam for several years.

“Justice is my most important value,” she said, asking to withhold her last name for safety reasons. “Hundreds of thousands of people were executed in Soviet times, and more than 1.5 million died in the camps.”

She said that behind every name was a life, a family, a job and plans that were cut short.

“When I read a name, I give that person back something that was taken from them: the right to be named, rather than remain a number in a case file,” Polina said. “It’s my way of showing that I do not accept this injustice and that we will not forget.”

She agreed with Epple’s belief that remembering the past is inseparable from Russia’s present.

“Unfortunately, those lessons haven’t been learned. Today, Russia once again has thousands of political prisoners, and the sentences keep getting longer,” she said. “We will do everything we can to ensure that the lives swallowed up by the regime are not forgotten.”

Personalrat fordert dreimal jährlich Training: ÖBB-Sicherheit im Tunnel-Test

Stillstand im Tunnel, Leben im Gleichgewicht

Ein Blick in den grauen Morgennebel des Wiener Hauptbahnhofes: Das Personal der ÖBB steht am Bahnsteig, die Hände in den Taschen, während ein Güterzug langsam rollt. Die Stimmung ist angespannt. Seit Monaten fordern die Beschäftigten mehr Notfalltrainings – ein Wunsch, der nun auch in den Schlagzeilen landet, nachdem ein Vorfall im Tunnel die Diskussion entfacht hat. Die These: Das aktuelle Trainingsangebot reicht nicht aus, um die wachsenden Risiken auf Österreichs Schienen zu bewältigen. Die Folgen betreffen jeden Fahrgast, der pünktlich nach Hause möchte, und die Sicherheit im gesamten Netz.

Der Standard berichtete unlängst, dass viele ÖBB-Beschäftigte auf die versprochenen Trainings warten. Ein konkretes Beispiel ist der Notfall im letzten Jahr, bei dem die Reaktionszeit um wertvolle Minuten verzögert wurde, weil Personal fehlte. Eisenbahnsicherheitsbeauftragter Andreas Krainer erklärt: „Wenn der Notfall eintritt, zählt jede Sekunde. Ohne regelmäßige Übung verpufft das theoretische Wissen.“ Die ÖBB selbst gaben an, dass das aktuelle Programm oft zu starr sei und nicht auf die spezifischen Gegebenheiten verschiedener Streckenabschnitte eingehe. Besonders in den älteren Tunneln im Wiener Raum, wo die Sichtverhältnisse schlecht sind und die akustische Orientierung durch den Tunnelcharakter erschwert wird, fehle das Üben in Realbedingungen. Die Konsequenz: Im Ernstfall greifen die Sicherungssysteme, aber die menschliche Hilfe komme zu spät, was insbesondere bei Personen mit eingeschränkter Mobilität kritisch werden kann.

Der Wunsch nach mehr Training stößt bei den Arbeitern auf offene Ohren, aber auch auf Frustration. Eine junge Lokführerin, die am Westbahnhof ihre Schicht beginnt, berichtet: „Wir sollen die Sicherheit ernst nehmen, aber wir bekommen kaum Zeit für das eigentliche Fahren. Zusätzliche Trainings werden oft als zusätzlicher Arbeitsaufwand gesehen, nicht als Schutz.“ Der Personalrat hat jetzt eine Liste konkreter Forderungen vorgelegt: mindestens dreimal jährlich ein ganzer Tag intensivem Training in simulierten Notfallsituationen. Das ist nicht nur eine Frage der Ergonomie, sondern der absoluten Lebensrettung. Wenn ein Tunnel evakuiert werden muss, insbesondere bei extremer Kälte oder dichter Rauchentwicklung, entscheide sich darüber, ob die Passagiere in Sicherheit gebracht werden können oder ob der Stillstand Tage andauere und die Wirtschaftsschäden in die Höhe treibe.

Natürlich geht die Forderung nach mehr Training auch mit Kosten einher, die am Ende beim Fahrgast ankommen könnten. ÖBB-Sprecherin Martina Berger betont jedoch: „Sicherheit hat ihren Preis, aber einen Stillstand durch einen vermeidbaren Unfall kostet uns und den Steuerzahler langfristig viel mehr.“ Die Organisation rechnet vor, dass jedes ausgebildete Team das Risiko schwerer Unfälle um signifikante Prozentpunkte senke. Eine kürzliche Analyse des Verkehrsministeriums untermauert diesen Zusammenhang: Gut ausgebildetes Personal reduziere die Unfallbearbeitungszeit um bis zu ein Drittel. Ob die Politik die zusätzlichen Mittel bereitstelle, bleibe abzuwarten. Fakt sei: Ohne das Training bleibe das Risiko hoch, und die Passagiere zahlen letztlich den Preis in Form von Verspätungen, aber vor allem in Form eines verringerten Vertrauens in das Verkehrssystem.

Ein vernünftiges Gegenargument lautet, dass die ÖBB bereits jetzt ein hohes Sicherheitsniveau vorweisen könnten und das Training nur ein Kostenfaktor sei. Tatsächlich zeigen die Unfallstatistiken der letzten fünf Jahre einen leichten Rückgang von Schadensereignissen. Doch genau hier setze die Kritik an: Die Zahlen trügen nur deshalb, weil die Technologie besser geworden sei – moderne Bremsensysteme und digitale Leit- und Sicherungstechnik (ETCS) leisteten Pionierarbeit – nicht weil das Personal besser ausgebildet sei. Ohne das menschliche Element – das schnelle Denken und Handeln in der Krise – sei jede technische Verbesserung nur eine halbe Lösung. Die ÖBB müssten beweisen, dass sie das Geld nicht nur in Schienen und Signale stecken, sondern auch in die Menschen, die diese Schienen am Laufen halten und die in Notsituationen die letzte Verteidigungslinie darstellten.

Für den Pendler, der jeden Morgen die U-Bahn oder den Regionalzug nimmt, mag das zunächst abstrakt wirken. Doch die Realität ist einfach: Ein besser trainiertes Personal bedeute schnellere Hilfe bei Panik, bessere Evakuierungsstrategien und vor allem ein Gefühl von Sicherheit, das man nicht kaufen könne. Die ÖBB stünden nun unter Druck, ihre Zusagen einzulösen, bevor der nächste Wintereinbruch die Strecken erneut lahmlege. Die Forderung ist klar: Wer auf Schienen Sicherheit wolle, müsse auch in das Training der Fahrer und Helfer investieren – jetzt und nicht erst nach dem nächsten Alarm.

Warum fordern die ÖBB-Beschäftigten mehr Notfalltrainings?

Die Belegschaft kritisiert, dass das aktuelle Trainingsangebot nicht ausreiche, um auf die spezifischen Gegebenheiten verschiedener Streckenabschnitte, insbesondere älterer Tunnel im Raum Wien, einzugehen. Verzögerte Reaktionszeiten bei Notfällen seien die Folge.

Welche konkreten Forderungen stellt der Personalrat?

Der Personalrat fordert mindestens drei Mal pro Jahr einen ganzen Tag intensives Training in simulierten Notfallsituationen, um die Lebensrettung im Ernstfall zu gewährleisten.

Welche Rolle spielt die Technik bei der Sicherheit?

Technologische Verbesserungen wie moderne Bremsensysteme und die digitale Leit- und Sicherungstechnik (ETCS) hätten in den letzten Jahren für einen Rückgang der Unfallzahlen gesorgt. Das Personal und das Training seien jedoch die entscheidende Ergänzung, da Technik allein in der Krise nicht ausreiche.

Wie hoch ist das aktuelle Sicherheitsniveau der ÖBB?

Die Unfallstatistiken der letzten fünf Jahre zeigen einen leichten Rückgang von Schadensereignissen. Die ÖBB führen dies jedoch primär auf technologische Fortschritte zurück, nicht auf verbessertes Personaltraining.

Welche Auswirkungen haben Verzögerungen bei Notfallmaßnahmen für Fahrgäste?

Im Ernstfall, etwa bei einer Tunnelung, könne der Stillstand Tage andauern. Für Pendler bedeute dies nicht nur Verspätungen, sondern auch ein verringertes Sicherheitsgefühl und mögliche wirtschaftliche Nachteile.

Två tåg har kolliderat i Spanien – många döda

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Minst 68 personer har omkommit efter att två tåg kolliderat på höghastighetsbanan mellan Madrid och Sevilla. Det ena uppges ha kört på det andra bakifrån med en kraftig kollision som följd, och flera tågvagnar spårade ur.

Det är den andra allvarliga olyckan med höghastighetståg i Spanien på mindre än ett år.

Olyckan inträffade strax innan klockan 9 utanför staden Urda i provinsen Toledo, på höghastighetsbanan där tågen kan köra i uppemot 300 kilometer i timmen.

Minst tre tågvagnar spårade ur i kollisionen och vittnen beskriver en kaotisk olycksplats. En polis säger till ABC att de knappt visste var man skulle inleda hjälpinsatsen.

– Det fanns kroppar bland de förvrängda vrakdelarna av tågvagnar. Jag har aldrig varit med om något liknande, människor skrek och grät. Det är verkligen en tragedi av enorm storlek.

Antalet döda har nu stigit till 68, enligt den regionala regeringschefen Emiliano García-Page, och ytterligare ett 80-tal personer uppges skadade. Svenska UD meddelar att de i nuläget inte har några uppgifter om skadade eller omkomna svenskar.

Orsaken till olyckan involverar både tekniska och mänskliga faktorer, säger Spaniens transportminister Óscar Puente. Efter att ha sett en full rekonstruktion och lyssnat igenom radiokommunikationen lämnar han denna preliminära redogörelse:

Runt 8.30: Ett fel inträffar i kontrollsystemets kommunikation, vilket gör att 60 kilometer av höghastighetsbanan hamnar i radioskugga. Detta utlöser automatiskt ett stopp för sex tåg på den aktuella sträckan.

Runt 8.36: Kontrollcentret ger manuellt instruktioner om att bortse från varningen och låta trafiken fortsätta.

– Det är ett uppenbart felaktigt beslut, säger Óscar Puente vid en presskonferens.

Runt 9.00: Under de efterföljande minuterna färdas det ena tåget cirka 25 kilometer innan det till slut kraschar in i det stillastående tåget framför.

Enligt officiella siffror var det totalt närmare 550 passagerare ombord. Jesús Jerónimo, en passagerare i en av det påkörda tågets främre vagnar, kallar händelsen ”brutal”.

– Jag jobbade på min dator när jag kände en väldigt stark acceleration och flög in i sätet framför mig. Människor kastades av tåget, vi trodde att vi hade spårat ur, säger han till El País.

Tågsäkerhet är sedan tidigare en omdebatterad fråga i Spanien, efter att två höghastighetståg spårade ur i januari i år med ett 40-tal döda som följd. På grund av upplevda säkerhetsbrister har fackförbund utlyst ett antal strejker, varav en var planerad att inledas på torsdagen.

• Onsdag morgon den 28 oktober kolliderade två tåg på höghastighetsbanan i regionen Toledo.

• Minst 69 personer har bekräftats döda. Nästan 180 personer skadades, enligt rtve.

• Det ena tåget stannade innan olyckan, och kördes in i bakifrån av det andra. Båda tågen spårade delvis ur.

• Orsaken är inte klar. Enligt Spaniens transportminister Óscar Puente är tekniska och mänskliga faktorer involverade. SVT har tagit del av läckta ljudfiler från samtalet mellan trafikledningen och lokföraren på det höghastighetståg som orsakade olyckan.

• Tågsäkerhet har sedan tidigare debatterats i Spanien. I januari spårade två höghastighetståg ur och över 40 dog.

• Spaniens största lokförarfack, Semaf, hade sedan tidigare utlyst strejk på grund av upplevda säkerhetsbrister. Den inleddes på torsdagen, enligt rtve.

• Efter krocken har hundratals demonstrerat i Spanien.

Efter den svåra tågolyckan kräver oppositionen transportministerns avgång och att premiärminister Pedro Sánchez ska ställas till svars. Regeringssidan menar att det inte är läge att göra politik av frågan, säger SVT:s korrespondent Tomas Thorén. Samtidigt är det val om en månad i Spanien. Förut handlade debatten mycket om bostadsfrågan – men nu dominerar olyckan där 69 personer hittills bekräftats döda.

– Nu har hela politiska debatten helt skiftat till att handla om säkerhet och infrastruktur och det kan istället gynna oppositionen. Det är mycket som tyder på att det här kommer att stå i fokus för valdebatten den närmsta månaden, säger Tomas Thorén.

Spaniens konservativa oppositionsparti Partido Popular (PP) kräver att premiärminister Pedro Sánchez förklarar sig i parlamentet efter landets två tågolyckor med totalt 120 bekräftade dödsfall på mindre än nio månader. Det rapporterar El Mundo.

Partiet anser inte att det räcker att transportminister Óscar Puente, som själv begärt att få komma till parlamentet, redogör för händelserna.

Tusentals spanjorer har slutit upp för att hjälpa de skadade efter tågolyckan. Under onsdagen lämnade omkring 4 100 personer blod i Madridregionen och ytterligare 2 000 i Castilla-La Mancha, enligt nyhetsbyrån Europa Press.

Engagemanget var så stort att myndigheterna i Madrid uppmanade vissa blodgivare att vänta till torsdagen, för att säkerställa att blodreserverna räcker även de kommande dagarna.

SVT:s reporter Tomas Thorén har talat med en av torsdagens blodgivare, se klipp.

Redan innan onsdagens tågolycka hade det spanska lokförarfacket Semaf utlyst strejk till torsdagen. Facket kräver förbättrad järnvägssäkerhet och protesterar mot brister som man anser inte har åtgärdats.

För resenärer innebär både strejken och olyckan inställda avgångar och störningar i trafiken.

– Nu känns alla transportmedel säkrare än tåget. Men det är det enda sättet om man vill kunna resa ofta här. Så vi har inget annat val, säger en resenär till SVT Nyheter.

Se mer av de drabbade resenärerna i klippet ovan.

Flera av de kritiskt skadade har förts till Universitetssjukhuset i Toledo. Där jobbar sjuksköterskan Mario Martinez Torija som beskriver situationen på sjukhuset som ”svår”.

– Det blir en högre belastning på sjukhuset än vanligt, säger Martinez Torija.

Patienterna som har kommit till sjukhuset har haft olika typer av skador, en del neurologiska och andra av typen som kräver kirurgi.

– Men vi har haft bra samordning med andra sjukhus som kunnat ta emot patienter, säger han.

Vid Toledos bårhus pågår identifiering av dödsoffer efter tågolyckan i Spanien.

Men myndigheterna uppmanar dem att i stället lämna dna-prov via blod på andra platser för att identifiera offren – eftersom det är svårt att göra det med igenkänning, säger SVT:s Europakorrespondent Tomas Thorén.

Fabrizio Favara – VD:n för Iryo som är ett av tågbolagen vars tåg krockade – varnade för mindre än en månad sedan för fel i signalsystemet LZB. Det rapporterar El Mundo.

I går morse inträffade ett fel i just det systemets kommunikation som gjorde att båda tågen stannade. Ett av tågen körde senare in i det andra bakifrån.

Transportministern Óscar Puente har tidigare sagt att det är vanligt att datasystem slutar fungera och att det inte är systemen som ger ”felaktiga” order, skriver El Mundo.

Men Favara varnade för mindre än en månad sedan om systemet i senaten, inför utskottet som utredde två andra tågolyckor och situationen för spansk järnväg.

CIAF, kommissionen som utreder järnvägsolyckor i Spanien, varnade redan 2024 i en rapport för risken för en olycka om man förlitar sig på den ”mänskliga faktorn”, skriver rtve.

Efter en incident vid tågstationen i staden Cuenca skrev de att det finns en ”potentiell risk” för kollision när bara en visuell kontroll av trafikledaren ska bekräfta att spåret är fritt från andra tåg innan ett tåg kan köra vidare.

”Denna säkerhetsåtgärd är sårbar för fel, eftersom den är helt beroende av den mänskliga faktorn. Det tydliggör behovet behovet av att införa tekniska riskreducerande åtgärder”, står i dokumentet som rtve tagit del av.

Ett 70-tal personer har hittills bekräftats döda i gårdagens tågolycka i spanska Toledo, söder om Madrid. Landets civilförsvar har fått in över 60 anmälningar från anhöriga om saknade personer.

Kremlin Signals Desire to Maintain Serbia Ties After Vučić Ousted by Student Movement

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

The Kremlin said Monday that it wants to maintain and develop ties with Serbia despite the defeat of former President Aleksandar Vučić’s ruling coalition in this weekend’s parliamentary elections, while noting that building those relations will require effort from both sides.

“We deeply value the contribution that Mr. Vučić has made to the development of our bilateral relations,” Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters at a daily briefing.

“We know that he will remain on the political stage, and we hope that even in opposition, he will continue to contribute to advancing our ties,” Peskov added.

Vučić, who dominated Serbian politics for more than a decade as prime minister and then later as president, conceded defeat on Sunday night following a historic snap election. The vote pitted his ruling coalition against a student-led movement that had spent months protesting against his government.

“Based on a stratified sample, it is clear that we lost these elections,” Vučić told supporters at his party headquarters in Belgrade.

The defeat followed Vučić’s resignation as president in September, a risky political maneuver intended to allow him to reclaim the prime minister post. It also came after nearly two years of anti-government demonstrations triggered by the November 2024 roof collapse at the Novi Sad railway station, an accident that killed 16 people and sparked widespread outrage over state corruption.

During his tenure, Vučić balanced Serbia’s candidacy for EU membership with a refusal to join Western sanctions against Russia over its 2022 invasion of Ukraine. He had remained one of the few European leaders to maintain direct contact with the Kremlin and make official visits to Moscow.

Peskov said Monday that Moscow hopes the incoming government in Belgrade will work “constructively” to preserve bilateral relations since, in its view, doing so would bring “tangible benefits to Serbia itself.”

“For our part, we intend to continue doing everything possible to foster our relations with Belgrade. But as you know, it takes two to tango,” he said.

Jovo Bakić, a representative of the Student List, told the Kremlin-backed broadcaster RT Balkan that the incoming government would not impose sanctions on Moscow or abandon Russian gas supplies despite pressure from the European Union.

He argued that sanctions imposed by a country of Serbia’s size would change little but would send a negative signal symbolically.

“We respect Russia as a great power and as a historically important partner, and in many cases as a protector,” Bakić said. “There will be no changes on this issue.”

Amid acrimony at Pickering City Hall, voters mull calming the waters

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Down the lakeshore from Toronto, acrimony on council clouds local elections

The Globe is reporting from cities and towns in Ontario, Manitoba and British Columbia to hear from residents about the issues affecting their communities and their votes in the municipal elections. Explore the entire series here.

Oakwood Drive is a long residential street that cars, e-bikes and scooters treat like a raceway, annoying residents and making it a treacherous place to walk or stand by the roadside.

It’s the kind of thing that Abdullah Mir, a candidate for the Ward 1 city council seat in Monday’s municipal election, describes as an ordinary, “unsexy” problem that city governments deal with all the time.

But in Pickering the basics of local government have lately taken a back seat. Over the last four years city council has been a raucous arena where divisive hot-button issues have sucked the air out of civic governance. Misconduct has become an issue of growing concern for local government in a number of cities and towns and people are looking for solutions.

The mood at Pickering City Hall has been fraught recently: A 2024 city bulletin described ‘unruly, disruptive, and intimidating behaviours from visitors.’

Abdullah Mir is running to represent Ward 1 in this year’s municipal election.

In Pickering, the seven-member council has been wrapped up in the controversy surrounding outgoing Ward 1 city councillor, Lisa Robinson, first elected in 2022. Ms. Robinson is running for mayor this time in an attempt to unseat incumbent Kevin Ashe. She was a Conservative candidate in the 2021 federal election, but was dumped by the party after inflammatory and Islamophobic tweets surfaced, which at the time she denied writing.

Ms. Robinson, who did not respond to messages from The Globe and Mail, was the subject of nine reports by the city’s integrity commissioner during the last council term and was docked 90-days pay several times for actions that included maligning staff.

She has defended herself in the past by saying she is neither racist nor homophobic and that she is fighting an establishment at City Hall that’s protecting its own interests. She says government should be open, councillors shouldn’t be expected to fall in line and residents shouldn’t be dismissed as interruptions.

“They call me disruptive. I am disruptive because City Hall needs disrupting,” Ms. Robinson says on her campaign website. “Every sanction, every statement, every attempt to isolate me has made one thing clear: I cannot be controlled by the current establishment.”

She attracted a loyal following to council meetings who cheered her on as she opposed things like Pride flags and gender neutral washrooms, railed against what she called attempts to silence her and even accused colleagues of corruption. The atmosphere deteriorated to the point that council stopped holding meetings in person, due to “unruly, disruptive, and intimidating behaviours from visitors,” according to a 2024 city bulletin.

How a level of government normally preoccupied with potholes and parks became a vicious battleground is bewildering to many residents, Mr. Mir included.

“The last four years have been a massive distraction because of all the nonsense,” Mr. Mir says. “I’m looking to put all that behind us.”

Pickering is a bedroom community of more than 100,000 people at Toronto’s eastern edge.

With a waterfront to the south, forests and farmlands to the north and a short commute to the country’s largest economic hub, the province’s growth plan calls for it to double in size in the next 25 years.

Mr. Mir, 33, lives in the community with his parents, who came to Canada from Pakistan. He saved up two years of holidays at his federal civil service job, managing a team at the Department of Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship, to run for office.

He says he’s running because he wants to see the area grow in a productive and responsible way.

“A lot of the things that people want here are similar across political lines,” Mr. Mir says. “A good city is one where we have good parks and good roads and good libraries and good snow clearing.”

He’s delighted to talk about his time leading an organization that opposes sprawl in Pickering, and how it kept parts of the Greenbelt out of the hands of developers, or his time on the library board, where he pushed for a local bookmobile. He does not want to talk much about Ms. Robinson.

Road safety is a common complaint in the ward where Mr. Mir is running.

On the campaign trail, Mr. Mir encounters voters who want reassurance that, if elected, he would be a different kind of councillor.

Nancy Ronchin, a volunteer canvasser for Mr. Mir, comes down Oakwood Drive for a debrief with the candidate. She tells him that residents are complaining about drivers acting as though this is Highway 407 and many have mentioned Ms. Robinson.

As one resident puts it, he wants a local representative who can disagree without descending to hostility.

Ms. Ronchin says she has never been involved in politics and didn’t know Mr. Mir until recently, but she made up her mind that she had to do something.

She was particularly upset about a fight over a gender-neutral washroom at a local recreation centre, which she says is useful but that Ms. Robinson opposes.

“I can’t just complain about it from my couch,” Ms. Ronchin says.

The city hall in Whitby, east of Pickering, has also had challenges with uncivil behaviour, hence the police at this April council meeting.Sammy Kogan/The Globe and Mail

Conflict that extends to incivility has affected other local governments and put pressure on provinces to step in.

In neighbouring Whitby, Ont., which borders Pickering to the east, a councillor was docked pay earlier this year for maligning city staff and eroding public trust. In Sarnia, Ont., a local councillor has been investigated by the integrity commissioner and banned from attending council meetings in person. And in Kamloops, there have been lawsuits and investigations spawning from a dispute between the mayor and city council.

Partly in response to these concerns, Ontario passed a Municipal Accountability Act in May that gives councils the ability to remove from office those who’ve committed serious transgressions. B.C. introduced a bill in April to standardize codes of conduct that wasn’t passed before the provincial election was called.

Back in Pickering, Mara Nagy, councillor for Ward 2, says the last term on council has been difficult. She describes herself as one of many who opposed Ms. Robinson’s ideas and rhetoric. She felt frustrated by chaotic meetings that sometimes went on for five or six hours, she says.

“There were a couple meetings where we actually had to pause to leave the chambers because it was ready to turn into a riot.”

“If you tell the whole story, it sounds unbelievable that something like this could happen in what is, for all intents and purposes, a suburb.”

Mr. Mir says that, if elected, he'd advocate for the reopening of this bridge to Toronto, closed since 2024 because of safety concerns.

As he ends his day of campaigning, Mr. Mir ushers a reporter away from the roadside for safety. He says Pickering can be the best of both worlds, a place close to nature and the waterfront, that is near the city but offers something different. It can grow into something great over the next generation, he says. But someone needs to mind the parks and potholes and scrutinize the planning.

Looking at the stretch of road as another vehicle speeds past, he’s hit with a burst of enthusiasm for traffic controls. Maybe there should be a speed bump, or a chicane – he’d consult with residents, of course, but something can be done.

“This is what we need to be fixing. Not arguing about who’s using what bathroom,” Mr. Mir says.
