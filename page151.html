Países Bajos no está hecho para los tanques

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

El Tribunal de Cuentas advierte de la necesidad de fuertes inversiones para adecuar carreteras, puentes y ferrocarriles para el paso de armamento

Países Bajos, la quinta economía de la UE, suele presentarse como la puerta de entrada al continente debido a su ubicación junto a Francia, Bélgica y Alemania, y el mar del Norte. También, por la pujanza del puerto de Róterdam y el aeropuerto de

Ámsterdam-Schiphol, y por el servicio de trenes que conecta con Bruselas, Londres o Fráncfort en pocas horas. En caso de que hubiera una guerra en suelo europeo, sin embargo, ese emplazamiento privilegiado pasaría a convertirse en estratégico, y las rutas de tránsito militar (corredores, en lenguaje técnico) no cumplen con los acuerdos de la OTAN para grandes transportes. Se trata de una red de carreteras, puentes y ferrocarriles que necesitan una inversión de al menos 9.500 millones de euros para mejoras, según el Tribunal de Cuentas, que ha publicado este mes un informe a la vista del aumento de la inestabilidad geopolítica en Europa.

Sin un hipotético panorama bélico sobre la mesa, el Gobierno neerlandés afronta ya un déficit total de 80.000 millones de euros para proyectos de equipamientos a escala nacional, tal y como indica la carta remitida en marzo pasado al Congreso por el ministerio de Infraestructuras y Gestión del Agua. “No se puede hacer todo, ni se puede hacer todo al mismo tiempo”, dijo entonces su titular, Vincent Karremans, que hizo un llamamiento al Parlamento para “tomar juntos decisiones difíciles”.

De producirse un conflicto, gran parte de los recursos militares de la OTAN entrarían en Europa a través de los puertos neerlandeses, pero las carreteras, vías férreas y puentes que deberían usarse para ello no pueden resistir ese tipo de tráfico. Se trataría de tanques, vehículos más pequeños o sistemas de armamento, y entre los requisitos militares señalados por el alto mando de la OTAN “se incluyen, por ejemplo, las alturas de los viaductos o los puentes ferroviarios que soporten vagones con cargas pesadas”, según reza el informe del Tribunal de Cuentas. El cálculo de sus expertos cifra la inversión necesaria “en 4.500 millones de euros en mejoras de las que también se beneficiarían los ciudadanos”. Unos 5.000 millones de euros adicionales se destinarían a “cumplir con los requisitos militares”.

En 2023, la UE designó una serie de corredores en Europa destinados al transporte rápido de material militar. El Tribunal de Cuentas advierte de que algunos vehículos de grandes dimensiones no podrían circular por la parte neerlandesa de esas rutas especiales. Bien porque las carreteras son hoy demasiado estrechas, o porque los puentes no aguantarían el peso. En el caso del ferrocarril, el informe señala que como esta clase de material precisa de un transporte más ancho, “los trenes que circulen en sentido contrario no podrían hacerlo, o tendrían que desviarse”, con el consiguiente disturbio y retrasos para la ciudadanía.

En tiempo de paz también hay transportes militares regulares, y el Servicio Nacional de Trafico colabora con el Ministerio de Defensa para adaptarse al estado diario del tráfico. En caso de conflicto, tener que cambiar de ruta puede suponer un problema si una pasarela es demasiado baja, un puente necesita reparaciones o unas traviesas están hundidas en algún tramo de la vía del tren. En su respuesta por carta al Tribunal de Cuentas, el ministro Karremans abunda en el hecho de que “gran parte de la infraestructura nacional está obsoleta y con necesidad de mantenimiento”, pero “no se dispone de recursos financieros suficientes para ponerla a punto al ritmo necesario”. De ahí que se esté llevando a cabo una “reordenación de las prioridades”, para el mantenimiento de las infraestructuras “que garanticen el transporte militar a gran escala a través del país”, y para mantener a su vez “el funcionamiento de la sociedad con la mayor normalidad posible”. Si bien el trazado exacto de los corredores militares es secreto por razones de seguridad, Defensa colgó en 2021 en su web una nota donde decía que “se habilitarán tres distribuidos geográficamente por Países Bajos”, que incluyen vías por carretera, ferrocarril y vías navegables, adaptadas al material más grande y pesado.

Oppo's ambitious plans for the Find X10 Pro and its future high-end phones are America's loss but Europe's gain.

If the only two names that pop into your head when you think about the best phones on the market are Apple and Samsung, frankly, you’re thinking too small.

Chinese phone-makers aren’t just making phones that rival those from Apple and Samsung; in some respects, they’re surpassing the iPhones and Galaxys — in batteries and fast charging, and especially when it comes to cameras.

On Wednesday, Oppo launched its latest photography-focused high-end phones, the Find X10 and Find X10 Pro, on the sun-bleached Spanish island of Mallorca. A finca covered in bougainvillea and surrounded by gnarled olive groves and roving wild goats might at first glance seem like an offbeat choice for a Chinese tech giant to stage a global launch event, but the decision is far from random. Spain is Oppo’s biggest European market, and it’s only getting bigger.

It’s a story we’re seeing repeat itself around the world. According to researchers Counterpoint and Omdia, Oppo was the fourth-largest phone-maker globally by shipments in the first half of this year. Already well established in China, the company is now seeing growth across the globe, and in Europe in particular.

It’s an impressive feat given that growing a smartphone business is harder than it’s ever been. It’s no secret that RAMageddon, the ongoing memory shortage, is pushing up phone prices and causing a massive drop in shipments.

Phone-makers might be putting on a brave face as they release new devices to the world, but behind the scenes they’re scrambling to draw up new strategies that will hopefully see them through in both the short and long term.

Growing sales of flagship and premium phones will be key to their survival, said Runar Bjorhovde, principal analyst at market research firm Omdia, during a briefing at the Oppo event. Compared with the budget and midrange segments, the high-end segment is relatively resilient.

You might think that means more exciting (if expensive) phones everywhere – witness the launch of Apple’s folding iPhone Duo, which starts at $1,999 – particularly in wealthy countries such as the US. But that’s not exactly the case.

When it comes to premium phone markets, none is larger or more profitable than the US. And yet Oppo and other Chinese smartphone-makers are steering well clear.

There are several reasons for this. Firstly, they don’t have Apple’s and Samsung’s long-standing carrier relationships that would facilitate their phones being presented as solid options to buyers. Together with the geopolitical roadblocks affecting Chinese tech companies, this makes the US simply too tricky and precarious right now to make that investment worthwhile.

“The barriers to enter make it almost impossible to go in and actually scale there unless you have lots of money to invest,” said Bjorhovde. Instead, the money they do have to invest is being poured into Europe. (Oppo said it will increase its investment in its European business by 30% over the next three years.)

This is bad news for Americans, who simply don’t and won’t be able to benefit from the full array of choices on offer to people in Europe and beyond, including the Find X10 Pro. In CNET’s review of the device, Editor at Large Andrew Lanxon described it as “a powerhouse phone that’s well worthy of its flagship title,” praising its cameras as the best on the market alongside the iPhone 18 Pro and Xiaomi’s Leitzphone.

Of these three, only one is on sale in the US, but Europeans can take their pick.

“It definitely feels like US consumers are missing out by not having the latest and greatest smartphones from the leading Chinese manufacturers,” said Ben Wood, chief analyst at FDM CCS. It’s especially noticeable when it comes to some of the cutting-edge foldable phones from companies like Honor, Oppo and Xiaomi, he added.

The Apple-Samsung duopoly that dominates the American market also exists in Europe, albeit to a lesser extent. But Elvis Zhou, Oppo’s European CEO, wants to see it broken completely.

“People deserve more exciting options,” he said in a briefing with journalists. When it comes to picking a premium phone, he thinks people should have a choice between three or four brands, rather than just two.

This reflects the more diverse array of successful phone brands in China, he added. In his company’s home country, there are only a few percentage points difference between Huawei, Apple, Oppo, Vivo, Xiaomi and Honor.

Xiaomi, Honor and Vivo are all now vying with Oppo for a slice of the European pie. One sign of intensifying competition is the shrinking gap between products being launched in China and Europe, which has historically spanned months. Zhou says that’s changing — the Find X10 series launched in China only a month ago.

When it comes to choosing a premium phone, Omdia’s research shows that buyers place more importance on camera quality than do people buying budget and midrange devices, but also “softer” values, including preferred brand, perceived status and aesthetics. Oppo has nailed its camera system, but the same isn’t necessarily true of the latter values.

Zhou isn’t worried, though. Oppo is squarely targeting Gen Z with the Find X10 series (Omdia research shows that people under 35 are most likely to buy premium phones), and he thinks they’re savvy enough to look beyond ads to first-person recommendations and chatbots that will provide specs-based advice to discern which product will offer the best value.

“We want to focus on the young generation because … they know how to make the decision very logically – they will not make the decision only following the brand name,” he said. “Young people really care about the product itself, and they can get all the information they want from AI and from social media. It’s a good opportunity for me.”

The company has already found success using this strategy for the Find X9 series, which saw its share of the premium European market quadruple between 2025 and 2026, according to Omdia.

But while Oppo might have made headway in Europe, it might be difficult to sustain this effort over time, said Wood — especially given that all of its Chinese rivals are adopting a very similar strategy at the very same moment.

Competition might be fierce, but Oppo is ready to play the long game. “We’re very patient,” Zhou said with a wry smile. “We’re not rushing here in Europe.”

Un verano con temperaturas calurosas de récord, incendios de sexta generación —de rápida propagación y difíciles de predecir—, meses de sequía y, en los últimos días, lluvias torrenciales. El impacto del cambio climático en España ya es una realidad con la que deberemos convivir, como confirma la comunidad científica. Los periodos de sequía aumentan su duración e intensidad mientras la disponibilidad de agua dulce disminuirá en las próximas décadas. Al mismo tiempo, la demanda de recursos hídricos y energéticos va en aumento, por lo que el país afronta un reto sobre su cohesión territorial, su actividad económica y la calidad de vida de la ciudadanía.

La alteración de las pautas meteorológicas afecta con especial severidad la cuenca mediterránea. La falta de precipitaciones regulares mella las reservas de los embalses y exige un giro estructural en la gestión de los bienes comunes. Las administraciones y el tejido industrial buscan respuestas eficaces para adaptar los servicios urbanos a este escenario extremo y, para conseguirlo, es preciso diseñar infraestructuras críticas con criterios de eficiencia, flexibilidad y sostenibilidad que mantengan la actividad sin degradar los ecosistemas. Ante este escenario, el concepto de seguridad ambiental, el conjunto de protocolos y acciones para garantizar el acceso continuo y fiable a servicios esenciales como el agua y la energía en momentos de incertidumbre climática y geopolítica, se convierte en esencial.

En el ámbito de la gestión hídrica, la regeneración del agua se erige como un eje estratégico para garantizar la seguridad hídrica futura. España dispone de plantas avanzadas donde el agua residual recibe un tratamiento integral antes de volver al ciclo productivo y ambiental. La compañía Veolia, que suma más de 18.000 profesionales en el país, regenera más del 15% del agua que procesa en sus instalaciones. Su modelo de trabajo impulsa la conversión de las antiguas depuradoras en ecofactorías, infraestructuras autosuficientes que limpian el recurso para nuevos usos, valorizan los residuos e integran fuentes renovables como la energía solar para operar con la máxima eficiencia.

La ecofactoría de Gavà-Viladecans, situada en el entorno metropolitano de Barcelona, ejemplifica esta visión circular. La planta produce agua regenerada para el riego de cultivos locales y preserva el equilibrio ecológico del Parque Agrario junto a las reservas naturales del delta del Llobregat. Asimismo, la ecofactoría de Palencia combina la cogeneración con paneles fotovoltaicos. Esta infraestructura asegura la calidad del vertido ambiental a los cauces fluviales y alcanza la autosuficiencia energética mediante recursos limpios. Ambos centros demuestran que la reutilización del agua fortalece la resiliencia de los municipios ante la escasez hídrica sostenida.

La protección del entorno ante el aumento de las temperaturas también abarca el cuidado de las masas arboladas. La acumulación de biomasa sin gestión en los montes incrementa la propagación de los grandes incendios en la época estival. La retirada ordenada de residuos forestales, agrícolas y podas urbanas ofrece una doble solución ambiental: por un lado, la limpieza del campo reduce el combustible vegetal que alimenta los fuegos. Por el otro, este material se transforma en energía renovable local. Veolia gestiona en España más de 250.000 toneladas anuales de biomasa forestal con certificación de sostenibilidad SURE. Este proceso integral cuida los bosques, disminuye el riesgo de incendio y suministra una fuente térmica limpia que sustituye a los combustibles fósiles.

Junto al medio rural, los núcleos urbanos concentran un volumen elevado de consumo energético y emisiones de dióxido de carbono y otras sustancias. La descarbonización de las ciudades requiere redes de climatización eficientes, que aprovechen la energía residual de la industria local. El proyecto Ecoenergies, desarrollado por Veolia junto al Ayuntamiento de Barcelona, suministra calefacción, refrigeración y agua caliente a edificios públicos e industriales de la capital catalana y de L’Hospitalet de Llobregat. A su vez, el uso de la geotermia aprovecha el calor del subsuelo para ofrecer climatización constante sin depender de la meteorología exterior. En el año 2025, el despliegue de esta tecnología evitó la emisión de más de 800 toneladas de dióxido de carbono en España, cifra equivalente a la absorción anual de 42.000 árboles.

Hacer frente al cambio climático exige medidas concretas. El desarrollo de infraestructuras resilientes, la regeneración del agua y el uso de energías limpias de proximidad constituyen las bases de la sostenibilidad en el siglo XXI. Garantizar el suministro diario de los servicios fundamentales en tiempos de sequía precisa esquemas circulares que protejan el territorio. Requiere fórmulas que integren la innovación técnica y la gestión local para construir comunidades preparadas para soportar los desafíos climáticos del futuro.

Can Bibi pull off his greatest political trick yet?

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The prime minister has defined and dominated his country’s politics for three decades and is putting his rivals on the defensive before Tuesday’s general election

They chant his name, they cheer and clap – and yet he is not the most dominant person in the room. Looming over the crowd that has gathered to hear Gadi Eisenkot, the former general who is the lead opposition challenger in Tuesday’s Israeli election, is a man who is not there. It is the man Eisenkot seeks to replace as prime minister, the man who first served in that role three decades ago and who has held it, on and off, for 19 of the past 30 years. It is Benjamin Netanyahu.

Naturally, the warm-up speakers at this evening rally in the modest city of Modi’in, halfway between Tel Aviv and Jerusalem, stress the virtues of Eisenkot, a son of working-class Moroccan Jews who rose through the ranks to become chief of staff of the Israel Defense Forces and entered politics only four years ago. But every sentence might as well end with two words that go unspoken: unlike Bibi.

Even the name of Eisenkot’s party, Yashar, meaning straight or direct, carries the implied contrast with Netanyahu, who is regarded by friends and enemies alike as a maestro of the darkest political arts. When Eisenkot calls for “a country without lies”, when he calls for “responsibility and leading by example”, when he suggests US-style term limits for the role of prime minister, “because power corrupts”, everyone knows what – and who – he means.

None of this comes with rhetorical fire or theatrical panache. But the lack of charisma serves only to emphasise that Eisenkot is every inch the un-Bibi. “I think that’s an advantage,” Noa Sadeh, 56, told me after Wednesday night’s rally. “I’m fed up with people who know how to talk – like Bibi. We need someone who will do the doing part.”

Spotting a journalist with a notebook, David Nadler, 74, a retired colonel who served under Eisenkot, insisted I write down the word “integrity”, meaning “he says something, he means it, he will do it”. Another sentence ending with those silent words: unlike Bibi.

It’s hard to overstate Netanyahu’s dominance not only over this campaign but over Israeli politics and political culture. The two blocs in this election are not left and right but Bibi and anti-Bibi. The latter camp includes at least two figures – the former PM Naftali Bennett and the Moldova-born Avigdor Lieberman – whose natural home is on the nationalist right and who both once worked closely with Netanyahu, only to fall out with him spectacularly.

Almost every political figure is defined, or defines themselves, in relation to Netanyahu. Eisenkot offers himself as the antidote, but listen to the pitch made by the far right and it too is all about the prime minister.

Netanyahu is like a shopping trolley, says Yishai Fleisher, of Itamar Ben-Gvir’s Otzma Yehudit (Jewish Power) party, to a group of about 30 orthodox voters in a study hall in Bet Shemesh on Thursday evening. “He has a tendency to go left, so you have to pull him to the right.” Fleischer makes the case for yet more Jewish settlements in the West Bank, to say nothing of settling Gaza; for a “ministry of emigration” that will encourage the “voluntary” departure of Palestinians; for the death penalty; for ever harsher treatment of Palestinian prisoners; for putting more guns and more ammunition into the hands of Jewish Israeli civilians – but all in the context of strengthening the resolve of a new Netanyahu-led coalition. On the right, Ben-Gvir is a moon that is rising, but Netanyahu is still the sun.

Partly, that’s a function of sheer longevity. But it’s also down to Netanyahu’s undimmed ability to set the political agenda. Even in this election, he has demarcated the terrain on which battle is fought. His latest poster campaign offers voters a rhyming choice of Yamin, meaning the right, or Falastin, meaning Palestine. On the right side is, of course, his own face and the Israeli flag; on the other, the leaders of the opposition parties, including Bennett and Lieberman who are adamantly opposed to a Palestinian state, all of them surrounding two heads of parties that represent Palestinian citizens of Israel, who make up about a fifth of the country’s population. The message is not subtle: vote for me, or you’ll get the Arabs.

Indeed, the closing weeks of the campaign have centred on the question of whether the anti-Netanyahu bloc of Jewish Israeli parties – currently forecast to do better than the Netanyahu bloc but to fall short of an overall majority in the Knesset – will do some kind of parliamentary deal with the mainly Arab parties, most of which have, historically, both refused to join Israeli governments and not been invited. Backed by that chunk of the media that is loudly and vociferously pro-Netanyahu, most notably the Fox News wannabe Channel 14, Netanyahu has succeeded in putting his opponents on the defensive.

A rare exception to that pattern came just this week when a Netanyahu uber-loyalist, Channel 14’s Yinon Magal, characterised Eisenkot as a Moroccan “monkey”. The racial slur was so crude that had it been used by the left to describe a politician of the right, it would have had Netanyahu denouncing Ashkenazi elitism from the rooftops (even though Netanyahu is, of course, a privileged scion of the Ashkenazi elite).

Tellingly, while the warm-up speakers in Modi’in mentioned the “monkey” remark, Eisenkot himself did not. To his admirers, that was the mark of a decent man reluctant to follow his opponents into the gutter. To the doubters, it was a sign of a politician still playing by Marquess of Queensbury rules – one who doesn’t quite appreciate what, or rather who, he is up against.

For an outside observer, the most puzzling question about this Israeli election is: how is this even close? How can the polls be showing the two blocs, pro- and anti-Netanyahu, neck and neck? Abroad, many will be baffled that Netanyahu can still be in contention given the devastation his government brought down upon Gaza, flattening much of the territory and killing more than 70,000 Palestinians, even if, as Israel says, one-third of them were Hamas combatants.

But even inside Israel, where the impact of the war on the people of Gaza is all too rarely discussed, it is no less of a riddle that Netanyahu is a serious candidate in these elections, let alone one who could emerge on top.

To start with the most elemental fact, the biggest disaster in the country’s history happened on his watch. The massacres inflicted on civilian Israel on 7 October 2023, at the Nova music festival and at a string of southern kibbutzim, are still fresh in the memory. Stickers depicting the faces of those killed on that day adorn bus stops and railway platforms from one end of the country to the other. As Oded Levi, who voted for Netanyahu in 2022 but was in the Modi’in crowd for Eisenkot, put it: “Whoever was prime minister [then] is responsible. He has to go. For this reason alone, we need a change.”

But it hardly ends there. Netanyahu promised a war that would destroy and eradicate Hamas. That didn’t happen. Instead, Israel destroyed its own international reputation, leaving the country isolated and fast becoming a pariah state. Netanyahu promised to destroy Iran’s nuclear threat and that too didn’t happen.

Add to that the “judicial coup” in the months before 7 October, where Netanyahu tried to weaken the restraint on his power represented by the supreme court, thereby splitting Israel in two, and the Qatargate scandal, in which his closest aides stand accused of taking money from Doha in return for boosting Qatar’s image. Along with his ongoing corruption trial, it all adds up to the mystery of why Netanyahu was not counted out long ago.

Any explanation begins with the loyalty of his base, built over three decades. Their support for him is unshakeable, unmoved even by reports that Netanyahu received credible warnings of a Hamas attack on 7 October and failed to act. They are kept on side by the PM’s enduring skill in crafting a narrative, in the case of 7 October, that everyone else is to blame except him, that he was kept in the dark by army and intelligence chiefs acting out of incompetence or something more sinister, a baseless claim advanced by his “poison machine” of media and online outriders. And he remains a consummate political operator, so gifted in the business of divide and rule that many can foresee him playing the coalition arithmetic on Wednesday morning far better than any of his rivals.

Still, that prospect has prompted a fierce reaction, one whose intensity can be felt thousands of miles from Israel. In the departure lounges of airports around the globe, you can find expat Israelis, many of them liberal and secular, heading home to vote on Tuesday. I saw it for myself as I travelled in a plane packed with those who felt it their duty to fly back and cast a vote to remove Netanyahu. That’s a group not picked up by pollsters and, who knows, in a contest that could turn on the fate of just one of the Knesset’s 120 seats, it may make a difference.

Some of that same determination was visible at the state ceremony in Jerusalem on Thursday marking the anniversary of the assassination in 1995 of the former prime minister Yitzhak Rabin, remembered still as a would-be peacemaker gunned down before a lasting accord with the Palestinians could be struck. It was not well attended – Netanyahu stayed away and sent a low-ranking minister – and it was tempting to view it as further evidence of how far to the right Israel has shifted in these past three decades, in this age of Bibi. Here were the luminaries of the old Israeli left, gathered in a burial ground.

And yet, Yair Golan, the leader of the Democrats, successor party of Rabin’s, was dismissive of such talk. A former general who served with Eisenkot, and a hero of 7 October who drove south to rescue people in his own car that fateful day, Golan told me: “I’m optimistic. I never went into battle thinking ‘all right, I’m going to be killed, I’m going to be wounded’. No, I’m going to win, and we are going to win.”

Golan even believes he and his fellow opposition leaders can agree on the beginnings of “some sort of a regional, strategic process in order to bring security for the long run”. He didn’t use the words “peace process”, but it’s clear what he meant.

The word “peace” became tainted for many Israelis, associated with moves such as the 2005 withdrawal from Gaza which Netanyahu and his allies have succeeded in casting as an early step on the road to 7 October. And yet it has not vanished. A video went viral earlier in the week showing scores of young Israelis at a Negev music festival dancing and singing to Shir LaShalom, the song for peace, the anthem of those 1990s peace efforts and the last song Rabin sang before he was shot leaving a peace rally 31 years ago.

Yes, the polls are deadlocked and, yes, Netanyahu is a political magician, but the sight and sound of those young people, like the queues for planes home, have given at least some Israelis the hope that change might be coming – that the age of Bibi might, at last, be drawing to a close.

But the demonstrations could weigh on tourism and therefore tax-free purchases, which account for around 20% of LVMH's revenue in France, Thozet said.

"A further six months of political vacuum is a very long time for markets, the economy and entrepreneurs," he added.

Consuming political news can hurt your job performance

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

With the midterm elections around the corner, political anxiety is in season—and productivity could pay the price.

A new study from researchers at Texas A&M University shows that consuming political news and social media outside of work affects employees’ emotional states and workplace behavior, even if they aren’t discussing politics at the water cooler.

Researchers tracked more than 300 workers over nine weeks during the 2024 presidential primary season, asking them to report how often they consumed political information, whether they viewed it as positive or negative, and how engaged, anxious, hopeful, and helpful they felt at work.

During weeks when workers consumed political information that they perceived as negative, they tended to report higher anxiety and lower engagement. During weeks when they consumed more political news that they saw as positive, they reported stronger engagement and greater willingness to help coworkers.

The takeaway is that the broader political environment affects the workplace even when politics aren’t directly discussed at the office. 

“Political information is designed to capture attention and provoke emotional reactions,” the study’s lead author, Ian Hughes, an assistant professor of psychological and brain sciences, said in a statement. “Those reactions don’t necessarily disappear once people close an app or turn off the TV.”

Organizations should be aware that employees are often dealing with additional stress during election season, he added.

“Political information has emotional consequences,” he said. “Those emotions can affect other parts of life, and that includes work.”

The study recommends that organizations provide mental health resources and support for stress management. Managers can reduce political stress by providing time off for voting or encouraging volunteering, which can increase employees’ sense of agency and control.

Workers should also engage in leisure and recovery activities, pursue “resource-building” practices such as mindfulness, gratitude, and sleep, and take breaks from media consumption.

Why leaders shouldn’t have to apologize for private remarks

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you’re in a public-facing role, you are likely to get caught up in some kind of “hot mic” event in which an offhand remark said in private gets aired publicly. (Indeed, this piece was inspired by the recent experience of a former colleague.) These events are embarrassing, and they almost always end with a standard—and unsatisfying—apology in which the speaker regrets the remarks and says that they did not mean them.

There are cases in which a leader really ought to apologize, or even step down, because of private-turned-public comments they make. Racist and sexist remarks undermine a leader’s credibility and can signal that they’re not fit for their role. Leaders who reveal in their private conversations that they do not have the institution’s best interests at heart also have a significant apology ahead of them.

However, in most cases, I believe a leader shouldn’t have to apologize for remarks made in private. Because, it turns out, leaders are human too and deserve privacy, just like the rest of us.

Leadership is stressful

Leaders of large institutions lead stressful lives. They are bombarded by requests and complaints and must deal with many people who want things. They have to face them all with aplomb. Most leaders in these roles are adept at putting on their game face and handling these moments gracefully. Periodically, they may lapse and have a more heated public exchange with someone than they intended. And in private moments, they may complain. When those complaints get recorded, it creates an opportunity for embarrassment.

The fact is that most leaders love their jobs and are dedicated to the institutions they run. The complaints they make in private moments are more expressions of overall frustration than they are complaints about particular individuals. And even when a leader does have a gripe about a particular person, they typically respect that person’s contributions as well.

There is a belief that leaders will maintain a superhuman ability to keep themselves in check at all times. Before everyone routinely carried internet-enabled recording devices with them, people had some expectation that they could have private moments in which they could let their guard down, particularly when talking with people with whom they have a longstanding relationship.

Which is why those apologies bug me. They feed into the narrative that those private statements are a better view into the attitudes of a leader than the leader’s public actions. They also imply that the leader did something wrong by having those private conversations. Both of those assumptions are a problem.

Normalize everyone’s right to private conversation

I think it is time that we start to normalize the importance of private conversations. If a leader says something that betrays a deeply inappropriate prejudice or attitude, that is worthy of an apology. Otherwise, a leader’s accomplishments are what really matters, and if they have a track record of contributing positively to their organization, that should speak more loudly than leaked remarks.

When played in public, those conversations are taken out of the social and emotional context in which they were spoken, and they are read later as if they reflect a more deliberate opinion on the part of the speaker than was likely intended in the moment. Indeed, these private conversations serve two important functions that would dampen leaders’ effectiveness if they had to monitor their speech at all times.

First, moments of petty whining (what the British call whingeing, a word I wish would catch on in the U.S.) serve a social function: to solidify the connections among people who are in similar roles. Often, these petty complaints exaggerate leaders’ true feelings for the sake of affiliating with their conversation partners. Having good relationships with these peers is important, and losing tools to cement these bonds would be a shame.

Second, leaders need to be able to discuss lots of things with each other. There is evidence that people may find some outcomes so distasteful that they would find it unbecoming if leaders even debated the issue and considered it seriously. For example, a university leader might need to consider a variety of options to cut costs. Faculty members might believe it unacceptable to close down a department or program. But leaders should not be penalized for considering all options, even ones that some people may object to on moral grounds. It is impossible to make good decisions in an organization without questioning deeply held assumptions.

If private conversations are routinely leaked, leaders may have concerns about discussing potentially controversial policy decisions, and this may ultimately hamper decision-making at the organization.

For this reason, a leader can express regret that a conversation intended to be private was leaked, but they should stop short of apologizing for having private conversations.

Receta de Lacón con Pan y Variantes Gallegas Tradicionales

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Si hay algo que define la esencia de la cocina del noroeste peninsular es su capacidad para convertir ingredientes humildes en auténticos manjares. El lacón con pan es el ejemplo perfecto de esta filosofía, naciendo como una solución ingeniosa para no desperdiciar las sobras del tradicional cocido de grelos. Es un plato que huele a hogar, a cocina de aldea y que demuestra que, con un poco de pimentón y aceite de oliva, se puede crear una cena contundente y llena de sabor.

Hablar de lacón es referirse a la pata delantera del cerdo que ha pasado por un proceso de curación al aire y salazón. Aunque antiguamente era un lujo reservado para las clases más pudientes, desde mediados del siglo XX se ha democratizado, convirtiéndose en un alimento fundamental por su aporte nutricional en zinc, fósforo y calcio. Ya sea servido en lonchas aliñadas o salteado con pan, este producto es la estrella indiscutible de cualquier mesa que quiera transportar sus comensales directamente a Galicia.

Para lograr este plato, lo ideal es partir de lacón ya cocido. Si tienes la pieza cruda, debes hervirla a fuego suave, evitando que borbotee demasiado, durante aproximadamente 90 minutos. Un truco fundamental es dejar que la carne se temple en su propio caldo para evitar que se reseque y mantenga toda su jugosidad.

Para cuatro personas, necesitarás unos 400 gramos de lacón cocido cortado en dados de 3 cm, 300 gramos de pan de masa madre o centeno del día anterior cortado en trozos, cinco dientes de ajo laminados, una cucharadita de pimentón (dulce o una mezcla con picante) y aceite de oliva virgen extra. Si quieres darle un toque especial, puedes añadir unas gotas de vinagre de Jerez y perejil fresco al final.

No cualquier pan sirve para esta receta. Para evitar que el resultado sea una papilla sin estructura, es imprescindible usar panes de miga densa y fermentación lenta. El pan de centeno gallego o el de masa madre ecológica son las mejores opciones porque aguantan mejor la fritura y equilibran la grasa del cerdo con su ligera acidez.

Debemos evitar el pan de molde, el brioche o el pan recién salido del horno, ya que su humedad excesiva hace que se apelmacen. Si solo tienes pan fresco, un truco es tostarlo en el horno a 150°C durante unos 8 minutos antes de llevarlo a la sartén.

El lacón es tan versátil que permite salir de la receta tradicional para crear tapas y montaditos espectaculares. Una opción muy popular son las tostas de lacón con queso de tetilla. En este caso, se utiliza una rebanada de hogaza tostada, se coloca el lacón cortado a cuchillo y láminas de queso de tetilla, dando un golpe de horno para que el queso se funda. Acompañar esto con pimientos de Padrón fritos crea un contraste de saboresy texturas inmejorable.

Otra propuesta innovadora es la tosta con base de crema de patata y cebolla. Se prepara un puré muy fino triturando patatas cocidas con leche, nata y un sofrito de cebolla. Sobre esta base cremosa se disponen lascas de lacón y se finaliza con un aceite infusionado con pimentón de la Vera, logrando un bocado sofisticado ideal para eventos o picoteos.

Para quienes buscan algo más complejo, la empanada de lacón y setas es una joya. El proceso comienza con el desalado del lacón durante 12 horas antes de cocerlo. El relleno se elabora con un sofrito de cebolla, pimiento y tomate, al que se añaden setas salteadas con ajo y los trozos de lacón. La masa, hecha con harina, levadura y el propio aceite del sofrito, envuelve este relleno jugoso para hornearse a 180°C hasta quedar dorada.

Para quienes prefieren el lacón simple, la clave es el corte. Se recomienda cortar en finas lonchas siguiendo la fibra de la carne. Si utilizas olla rápida para la cocción, el tiempo se reduce a 20 minutos desde que sube la válvula, pero siempre es vital dejar que la pieza repose en el caldo hasta el día siguiente.

Para evitar errores comunes, recuerda siempre colar el caldo de cocción para eliminar impurezas y no excederte con la sal, ya que el lacón es un producto curado. Si quieres conservar el producto ya loncheado, el envasado al vacío es la mejor opción para mantener la jugosidad en la nevera durante un par de meses.

Desde la sencillez de un salteado de pan y ajo hasta la complejidad de una empanada artesana, el lacón representa la cultura del aprovechamiento y el respeto por el producto en Galicia. La combinación de una carne curada de calidad, un pan de fermentación natural y el toque ahumado del pimentón crea una experiencia gastronómica que une la tradición centenaria con la cocina actual, asegurando que nada se desperdicie y todo se disfrute.
