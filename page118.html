Antarctica’s “Doomsday Glacier” Could Raise Sea Levels Twice As Much as Scientists Feared

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Even without further warming, Thwaites Glacier could contribute twice as much to sea level rise by 2100 as previously estimated, according to a revised ice sheet model.

Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica has been retreating and thinning rapidly, while satellites show substantial acceleration in its ice flow. Traditional ice sheet models have depicted slower movement, leaving scientists struggling to reconcile their simulations with the observations. The glacier lies in the Amundsen Sea Embayment, a sector responsible for roughly one-third of the Antarctic Ice Sheet’s mass loss.

A new ice sheet model developed by glaciologists at the University of California, Irvine and NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory projects that melting at Thwaites could contribute up to 30 millimeters, or about 1.2 inches, to sea level rise by 2100. That is twice previous estimates, even without additional climate warming. The study was published in Nature Communications.

“Glaciologists have long based their assumptions of ice sheet dynamics on the ‘grounding line,’ a discreet, narrow and mostly stable demarcation where ice leaves the land and begins floating on water,” said lead author Mattia Poinelli, who was a UC Irvine postdoctoral scholar in Earth system science when the study was conducted. “We have come to acknowledge a different arrangement beneath Earth’s major ocean-terminating glaciers. Satellite observations demonstrate the existence of a ‘grounding zone,’ sometimes kilometers wide, where warm ocean water is reaching deep underneath the ice sheets and causing vigorous melting.”

Synthetic aperture radar readings from the Finnish ICEYE satellite constellation allowed the researchers to detect kilometer-scale fluctuations in this zone along the main trunk of Thwaites. The data indicate that seawater penetrates several kilometers beneath grounded ice and lifts it during tidal cycles. This flushes the underside of the ice with pressurized, warm seawater, which currents altered by climate change push toward the Antarctic coastline.

That contact with the ocean affects how firmly the glacier remains anchored to ridges on the seafloor. According to Poinelli, a NASA JPL research affiliate who is now a project scientist at UCLA, the study showed that grounding zone processes accelerate the glacier’s detachment from these stabilizing features, allowing faster ice flow and deterioration.

“The Thwaites main trunk is being temporarily stabilized by the Mouginot Ridge,” he said. “Once that ridge is unpinned, the glacier will continually retreat across a predominantly retrograde seafloor toward its next topographic high, the South Ridge.”

A retrograde seafloor slopes downward farther inland, so retreat would carry the glacier into deeper water before it reaches the next rise in the terrain.

The researchers calibrated their model against more than 20 years of observations. By including a grounding zone instead of a grounding line, they reconstructed changes in the glacier from the 1990s to the present that were four times as pronounced as those represented in traditional models.

Simulations incorporating a grounding zone 4 to 6 kilometers wide, or about 2.5 to 3.7 miles, predict a 60 percent probability that the glacier will lose its anchoring contact with Mouginot Ridge by the end of the century, Poinelli said. “When you factor in key physical processes and calibrate against historical observations, you gain a reliable pathway for narrowing uncertainty in projections,” he said.

“We have documented and quantified the impacts of climate change on our planet’s sensitive ice sheets for decades, but a mounting realization gained in recent years of what is happening on the undersides of glaciers like Thwaites is leading to an even greater sense of alarm,” said co-author Eric Rignot, UC Irvine professor of Earth system science. “Most projections up-to-present have struggled to match the rapidity of the mass loss because of missing physics; our new model with more complete physics is able to match the record and then produces projections that are twice larger than before. These results predict greater risk of and exposure for coastal flooding than anticipated in the coming decades.”

Reference: “Sea-level contribution from Thwaites Glacier doubles in an observation-constrained ice-sheet model” by Mattia Poinelli, Eric Rignot, Hélène Seroussi, Joshua Cuzzone, Tyler Pelle and Eric Larour, 12 October 2026, Nature Communications. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-77976-1

ICE said a man jumped on the hood of their vehicle. Video — and police — say otherwise.

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When footage of a man being hit by federal immigration officers’ SUV in Northern California became public, the Department of Homeland Security responded by saying the man had jumped on the vehicle’s hood.

Local police raised doubts about federal officials’ account as they announced a criminal investigation into Tuesday morning’s incident involving the pedestrian, 37-year-old Antonio Moreida, citing video footage that contradicted DHS’ statement.

Footage from a nearby surveillance camera, a bystander’s cellphone recording and Moreida’s GoPro, which had already been made public at the time of the agency’s statement, told a different story of what happened on a residential street in Woodland, an agricultural city just outside of Sacramento. The incident is the latest in which DHS’ account has been contradicted by officials and video evidence amid the Trump administration’s immigration crackdown.

In a statement Wednesday morning, DHS said Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers didn’t hit a pedestrian.

“An agitator jumped onto the hood of an ICE vehicle and the officer driving applied the brakes,” a DHS spokesperson said in a statement in response to CNN’s request for information. “The agitator slid off the hood. ICE immediately contacted EMS and the Woodland Police Department. Flopping for the camera is dangerous, especially when it interferes with a federal law enforcement operation,” apparently accusing the man of faking his injuries.

After the incident on Tuesday, Woodland Police Chief Ryan Kinnan said in a statement he found footage of the incident troubling. Investigators were working to gather evidence and statements and to review available audio and video related to the incident, including video taken from multiple perspectives, police said in a Facebook post. Woodland Police didn’t take part in the ICE operation the agents were involved in that morning, police said.

The following day, Woodland Police called DHS’ account “inconsistent.”

“Certain claims made in the DHS statement are inconsistent with the video evidence obtained and reviewed by Woodland Police investigators,” Kinnan said in an update Wednesday. The update said investigators had established, based on video evidence, the pedestrian had been hit by the vehicle driven by ICE agents and that he had not jumped onto the vehicle.

The local police department is conducting a criminal investigation into the events leading up to the collision and the actions of those involved, Kinnan said Wednesday.

By Thursday, in response to follow-up questions about the video footage, federal officials no longer said the person jumped on the hood.

“This individual chose to directly impede a law enforcement action and stand in the middle of an active road,” an ICE spokesperson said in a statement to CNN. “Our officers deliberately attempted to avoid making contact with this anti-ice protestor and drive around him, while also braking. We strongly urge individuals to not stand in the middle of active roadways while a law enforcement vehicle is approaching.”

Footage reviewed by CNN shows Moreida, a Woodland resident, in the middle of the street at around 9:40 a.m. Tuesday. Moreida said he had responded to a call that morning by NorCal Resist, a group which monitors immigration operations, about ICE activity in Woodland, he told CNN on Tuesday, hours after the incident.

He said he was trying to record agents’ unmarked vehicles. He had been recording one vehicle, standing on the car’s driver’s side, when it turned left and drove off, which he said pushed him into the middle of the street.

Video from Moreida’s GoPro shows him following a black Dodge Durango with tinted windows as it drives down the street.

Then, he said he heard a loud engine before seeing the agents’ black Chevy Tahoe drive toward him, which is shown in his GoPro footage, though the video has no audio.

Down the street, the bystander’s cellphone video shows the Durango pass the Tahoe, which has tinted windows, on a residential street. In the video’s background, people can be heard blowing whistles, presumably to alert people nearby of ICE’s presence in the neighborhood.

The ICE agent driving the Tahoe moves toward Moreida and between the cars with its brake lights on, footage shows. In the bystander’s video, Moreida isn’t yet visible.

The SUV’s wheels begin to turn to the left only a few feet from Moreida, a wider angle of surveillance footage from a home shows.

“The individual was standing in the street long enough that a reasonable person should be able to see them and recognize that they were there,” said Ryan Schwank, a former ICE attorney and training instructor who reviewed the videos provided by CNN.

Schwank also took issue with ICE’s characterization that Moreida was standing in the middle of an active road and impeding law enforcement’s actions.

“When we’re looking at Antonio’s actions, he’s not impeding. He’s not standing blocking the vehicle from moving,” he said. “He’s standing and filming, and that’s well within his bounds to do. He’s well within his rights to do what he was doing.”

Moreida’s GoPro appears to show when he’s struck by the front-right side of the vehicle before he drops his camera.

“Before I knew it, it just drove straight into me,” he said. “I took the hood of the car straight to the chest. It initially took the wind out of me and I couldn’t breathe.”

The surveillance footage shows Moreida take a step back when he appears to make contact with the car. He is then seen falling toward the ground, off the front-right side of the car, the footage shows.

In the bystander’s footage from behind, a screeching noise is heard before the Tahoe jolts forward. The SUV veers left before straightening out, the video shows.

The bystander’s angle shows the Tahoe brake lights turn off. Moreida is then visible in the bystander’s video, falling to the ground and rolling, first on his back with his legs swinging above him. He then appears seated upright in the nearby surveillance footage, this time facing the sidewalk.

The SUV’s brake lights turn back on briefly before it drives farther down the street, the surveillance footage shows.

The driver, an ICE agent, stayed near the scene and called local dispatch, Woodland Police said. The agent cooperated with police during the initial investigation, the local agency said.

Moreida said an off-duty EMT provided him aid at the scene before Woodland Police officers arrived. Federal agents didn’t approach him after he was hit by the SUV, he said.

Moreida said he was eventually transported to the hospital. He said he had X-rays taken of his swollen knees, where he said he had the most damage. In addition, he said he has bruises and cuts from the road.

ICE has reduced the amount of training for officers, including in vehicle operations and how to deal with confrontations, said Schwank, who is now an agency whistleblower after resigning in February.

On Saturday, ICE said it is instituting additional training for officers.

“ICE training will now be extending to 72 days. The change, effective June 2, 2026, will apply to all new academy classes beginning July 1, 2026. All graduates of the previous academy curriculum will attend follow-on training at the Advanced Field Officer Training Program,” an ICE spokesperson said in an emailed statement to CNN. “As we have said all along, ICE training does not end when recruits graduate from the academy. ICE officers go through a rigorous on-the-job training and mentorship. This additional training is tracked online and monitored closely.”

Soon after it became public, local officials expressed dismay over the incident, which occurred in a rural Northern California community that’s about half Latino. More than a fifth of the population is foreign-born, according to US Census estimates.

“The lack of truth in the Department of Homeland Security’s statement is evidenced by video taken at the scene,” Woodland Mayor Tom Stallard said in a statement issued Wednesday. “All of us can be assured that the Police Department’s investigation into this disturbing incident will continue to be speedy, fair, and impartial.”

US Rep. Mike Thompson, a Democrat whose district includes Woodland, said ICE’s statements about the facts surrounding the incident were disappointing but not surprising.

“ICE has a history of trying to hide the facts and mislead the public,” he told CNN. “And this is another example of that happening.”

On the day of the operation, Thompson called on Acting ICE Director David Venturella to investigate the collision, including by preserving evidence and cooperating with local authorities. As of Friday, the congressman had yet to receive a response to his letter, his office told CNN.

CNN’s Taylor Galgano contributed to this report.

Oil Spills Wreak Havoc on the Bottom of the Ocean, Even a Decade Later

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This story originally appeared on Inside Climate News and is part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

Across hundreds of miles of the northern Gulf of Mexico, a deepwater ecosystem collapsed after the massive 2011 Deepwater Horizon oil and gas spill, and recovery is uncertain, new research concludes.

The study, published Monday in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, found that animal populations—including octopus, krill, squid, and shrimp—declined by 60 to 85 percent in the three and a half years after the spill, with few signs of recovery since. Krill and lanternfish, both foundational species in the Gulf’s food web, were among the hardest hit. The findings also suggest that the die-off is causing genetic bottlenecks that could further hinder ecosystem recovery.

The researchers identified it as the “fastest and most severe faunal decline reported for Earth’s largest ecosystem type” ever documented. The widespread die-off of marine organisms may also reduce the ocean’s ability to transport carbon dioxide from the atmosphere to the deep sea. After ruling out other factors, such as water temperature and oxygen levels, the researchers identified the Deepwater Horizon blowout as “the most parsimonious explanation” for the ecosystem’s collapse.

Comprehensive natural resource damage assessments and restoration efforts involving federal, state, and tribal trustees are ongoing. In 2016, BP agreed to a $20.8 billion settlement, including up to $8.8 billion for environmental restoration.

Remote ocean depths and habitats miles below the surface may seem buffered from the impacts of human activity, the authors wrote, but their findings suggest an industrial disaster pushed part of it into a diminished state with “limited resilience to facilitate recovery.” They said their findings also show that proposed industrial deep-sea mining could damage the ecosystem before baseline conditions are assessed.

A transoceanic drilling rig, operating for BP about 50 miles southeast of the Mississippi Delta, exploded on April 20, 2010. The explosion and fire killed 11 people and triggered the largest accidental marine oil spill in history.

Oil and gas spewed uncontrollably from the broken well nearly a mile deep for 87 days, ejecting 4.9 million barrels of oil at a rate of about 400 tanker trucks per day, enough to fill up 1.2 million cars. The spill also released about 7.7 billion cubic feet of fossil gas into the deep sea at a rate of 88 million cubic feet per day, enough to supply about 820,000 US homes. State and federal agencies, including the Environmental Protection Agency, also authorized BP to apply 1.84 million gallons of chemical dispersants to try to reduce the impacts.

Some of the oil and gas dissipated and was transported out of the deep Gulf of Mexico region. Some coated barrier islands and devastated coastal wetlands and shellfish habitat, and some settled onto the ocean floor, where currents can stir it back into the water. But enough hydrocarbon and chemical-dispersant remnants stayed suspended long enough to do almost immeasurable harm.

A series of studies since the spill have shown a wide range of toxic effects on marine organisms, from impaired development and reproduction to increased mortality and lasting health problems in dolphins and whales. The new paper suggests that lingering pollutants may interfere with some species’ ability to find mates in the dark by disrupting their highly developed sense of smell.

The impacts continue to ripple through the ecosystem, said lead author Tracey Sutton, a professor of marine and environmental sciences at Nova Southeastern University in Florida. A 2024 study found an 80 percent decline in deep-diving toothed whales, he said.

“We used to have a big resident population of sperm whales right off the Mississippi River, but we haven’t seen a sperm whale in 10 years,” he said. Large predators need big, occasional dense patches of prey, and those have disappeared, he added.

Scientists have developed accurate ways to count fish. For this study, they started with a baseline from nine months of sampling in 2011 and compared it with nine subsequent surveys between 2015 and 2023. The researchers sampled deep-pelagic habitat along a large grid around the spill site from about 650 feet below the surface to about a mile down. The deep-pelagic zone accounts for about 90 percent of the Gulf’s total volume.

Across a grid of stations, scientists on slippery decks lowered and hoisted nets that open and close at different depths, with experts from different species groups to identify the catch. They sampled around noon and midnight to account for species that migrate vertically each day. Ship-mounted scientific echosounders provided an independent check, measuring sound reflected by marine life in the water column.

The research is “comprehensive and carefully composed,” marine biologist Bruce Robison, a senior scientist at the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute, said via email. “There is no other study that I know of with such an incredible number of net samples,” added Robison, who did not participate in the new study.

The research focuses on species that are not commercially fished and shows for the first time that human impacts can deplete marine life even in very deep ocean water, he said.

“It is important for the public to understand just how disastrous this oil spill was,” Robison said. “The Gulf has not recovered, and it’s been over 15 years.” Robison said other activities, such as deep-sea mining, could also have severe negative consequences for deepwater pelagic ecosystems by discharging wastewater containing metals or lubricants.

Three lines of evidence—net hauls, acoustic surveys, and genetic sampling—corroborate each other, which is “unusually strong evidence to support his conclusions,” said Jeffrey Drazen, a marine ecologist at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, who did not participate in the new study.

The study examines all possible causes of the decline and shows only one aligns with the scientific evidence, he said.

“Through logical elimination,” he said, “and without actually pointing a finger, they leave no doubt that the oil spill is the cause of the decline.”

Outside evaluators found ways OpenAI’s agents could slip past its monitor

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

METR and Redwood Research spent three weeks testing the OpenAI monitoring system that watches its coding agents. OpenAI says it has patched the flaws, but the evaluators have not checked.

OpenAI stickers at the company’s DevDay 2026 event.

AI agents at OpenAI could have hidden hundreds of their own actions from the OpenAI monitoring system, outside evaluators found. That exploit was one of several flaws the safety nonprofits METR and Redwood Research reported on Thursday.

Each group sent one researcher inside OpenAI from 21 July to 11 August. They could see internal code, documentation and the monitor’s prompts, and they interviewed about a dozen staff, METR said. They worked on OpenAI devices that “never left the premises” and had an escort on site.

OpenAI told the evaluators it has patched every flaw that affected its production safeguards. METR and Redwood said they have not evaluated that claim. TNW has not independently verified the findings.

The system uses an AI model to review what OpenAI’s internal agents do and to flag signs of misalignment for human review. Misalignment is when a model acts against what its makers intend. On 21 October, TNW reported that a GPT-6 Astra agent at OpenAI removed a safety monitor, then denied doing it.

The evaluators estimated that 75% to 94% of agentic traffic from OpenAI’s strongest models was eligible for monitoring. For internal Codex agents, the figure was 99.2%. When the review began, the monitor did not cover training runs that use reinforcement learning.

One gap came from compaction, when an agent condenses its memory to make room for more work. The monitor skipped those events. METR estimated that this left about 10% of eligible Codex traffic unwatched from May to July. OpenAI staff had spotted the problem before the review and fixed it during it.

The monitor only ran when an agent handed control back to its user. So during that period, an agent could have put off its checks indefinitely by simply continuing to act. The evaluators could not check whether any agent did.

The researchers also found ways to defeat the monitor outright. Codex agents could in principle get past their sandbox and its Auto-Review check, and then take any action their user could take.

OpenAI redacted the details of the exploit that hid hundreds of actions. It also hid the steps of the exploit itself, METR said.

Agents could likely craft inputs that make the monitor fail, so it would not flag them for review. Two settings a client controls could switch off monitoring for new agents, although the monitor might catch the act of using them. A simple exploit could also have let agents use two “read-only” tools to take any action in their sandbox. The evaluators did not test that one in practice.

Future monitoring should “withstand deliberate, strategic, and sustained attacks from highly capable internal agents at scale,” the evaluators wrote.

The pilot tested how deep outside access could go. It was not built to tell the public much. OpenAI received the full 61-page report. METR and Redwood agreed to publish only a short summary, and OpenAI could redact it. They expect to share more after future exercises.

“We’re using what we learned to strengthen our safeguards and shape future collaborations with independent evaluators,” an OpenAI spokesperson told CNBC.

Chief executive Sam Altman pledged in September to give outside evaluators “employee-like access”, CNBC reported. President Donald Trump’s voluntary AI accord also urges companies to work with an independent evaluator. Some AI companies fear that evaluators inside their walls could take secrets to rivals, Reuters reported.

The review began on 21 July, the day OpenAI confirmed that its AI had breached Hugging Face. The Register noted that overlap. OpenAI has since described wider monitoring coverage in a post on that incident.

A day after the report, OpenAI said it would cut live internet access from all frontier model training. It cited the current state of monitoring systems among its reasons. Regulators are also watching: the US Federal Trade Commission subpoenaed OpenAI and Anthropic over AI safety incidents this month.

Low-dose aspirin linked to 70% lower dementia risk, but there's a catch

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A new analysis from Monash University suggests that low-dose aspirin may be linked to a dramatically lower risk of dementia in older adults with certain genetic characteristics. Researchers found that people with a specific genetic profile who took aspirin had a 70 percent lower relative risk of developing dementia, raising the possibility that genetic testing could eventually help identify people who might benefit from preventive treatments.

The findings, published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association, were based on genetic data from more than 13,500 participants in the landmark ASPREE Trial (Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly). Scientists examined whether differences in participants' genes could help explain why aspirin might affect dementia risk differently from one person to another.

Aspirin Linked to 70% Lower Dementia Risk in Certain People

The most striking results involved genes associated with platelet count. Platelets are small components of the blood that help form clots, and researchers wanted to determine whether genetic variations related to platelet levels could influence aspirin's potential effects on dementia.

After assigning participants genetic scores associated with platelet count, the team compared dementia rates among those who received aspirin and those given a placebo.

Among people in the highest 20 percent of platelet count genetic scores, just 1 percent of aspirin users developed dementia, compared with 3.3 percent of participants taking a placebo. That difference translates to approximately a 70 percent lower relative risk of dementia among those receiving aspirin.

However, the apparent benefit was accompanied by a significant safety concern. In the same genetic group, 4.4 percent of aspirin users experienced major bleeding, compared with 2.1 percent of those receiving a placebo.

A Genetic Clue Could Change Dementia Prevention

Lead author Dr. Peter Fransquet, a Research Fellow at Monash's School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, said the results suggest that a person's genetic makeup could eventually play an important role in determining which dementia prevention strategies are most appropriate.

"Previous trials found aspirin didn't prevent dementia when everyone was considered together," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Our findings suggest there may be more to the story.

"In people with this particular genetic profile, we saw substantially fewer cases of dementia among those taking aspirin.

"It raises the possibility that genetics could one day help us identify who may benefit from a preventive treatment, rather than taking a one-size-fits-all approach."

The possibility of more targeted prevention is especially important as dementia becomes increasingly common. Dementia Australia estimates that approximately 446,500 people in Australia are living with the condition in 2026. That number is expected to exceed one million by 2065.

Blood Platelets Reveal an Unexpected Dementia Connection

Previous research has connected dementia with platelet activation and aggregation, the processes through which platelets become active and stick together. However, scientists have had a less complete understanding of how a person's overall platelet count might relate to dementia.

The new findings point toward platelet biology as a potentially important area of investigation. Notably, the genetic association identified by researchers did not involve the well-established genetic risk factors typically linked to Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia.

"What's particularly interesting is that the signal wasn't linked to the established genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease and dementia," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Instead, it's pointing us towards platelet biology and gives us a new avenue to investigate."

Why More Research Is Needed Before Taking Aspirin

Although the findings suggest that aspirin could have different effects depending on a person's genes, researchers emphasize that the results must be confirmed before they can guide medical recommendations.

The next step is to investigate whether the association appears in other populations. Scientists would then need to conduct a clinical trial specifically involving people with the genetic profile identified in the analysis.

"We now need to confirm the finding in other studies and ultimately test it in a trial designed specifically for people with this genetic profile.

"People shouldn't start taking aspirin to prevent dementia on the basis of this study without consulting with their doctor, particularly given the increased risk of serious bleeding.

"If it holds up, the fact that aspirin is already cheap and widely available could make personalized dementia prevention a real possibility."

What you might not realize, though, are the demands that Android Auto and CarPlay put on your phone. Your handset is essentially doing all the regular phone stuff and then powering a large tablet-like display at the same time. That typically includes constantly updating a map in real time.

With that in mind, your Android phone or iPhone can quickly start to lose battery life and heat up when it's connected to your vehicle, which you've perhaps already noticed. To keep your handset cool and your battery in good shape, here's what you need to do.

Wireless Android Auto and CarPlay are convenient, just like wireless charging is convenient, but it puts more stress on your phone. If you've got the option of a wired connection, that's preferable—but that introduces a new potential heat problem, which is that your phone will always be charging up.

To counter that, you can set charge limits (Battery and charging from Android Settings, Battery > Charging from iOS Settings), or simply make sure your phone is kept in a cool, dark spot of the car.

This wired option has the added bonus of letting you switch to airplane mode on your phone, which will go a long way to limiting heat. Again there's a trade-off though: No one will be able to reach you, and you'll need to have your maps, music, podcasts, and whatever else you need downloaded to your device in advance.

If you do stick with wireless Android Auto or CarPlay, try to avoid charging your phone at the same time, especially on the wireless pad built into the car’s dash (which generates more heat). You don't want to end up with a dead phone half way through the journey, so you may have to compromise at certain times.

As mentioned above, where your phone is in the car makes a real difference to how hot it's going to be. If you have your phone's interface up on your car dashboard, then you don't necessarily need to have it fixed to the dashboard, too, possibly under direct sunlight. The glove compartment will do just fine.

If you have a mount for your phone, think about attaching it in front of one of your car's air vents. Blasts of air conditioned air will significantly reduce how hot your handset gets, working like a DIY cooling system.

You might also want to think about taking the case off of your phone for the length of the trip, if your phone is fitted with one. Your handset is less likely to be dropped or knocked while you're driving anyway, and a case can trap the heat in.

The rules aren't too different to keeping your phone cool generally, though with the added wrinkle of needing to keep it connected to your car somehow. Make sure the cabin in general is at a comfortable temperature, though you're probably already doing that for your own sake. The bigger thing to remember is to avoid leaving your phone behind in a vehicle that's going to heat up in the sun if you're stopping off somewhere.

If your phone is hooked up to Android Auto or CarPlay, then it's probably got some work to do—whether it's getting you to a destination or streaming music. However, you can still reduce heat by keeping phone tasks down to a minimum. If your calls and (voice-activated) messages can wait, leave them until the end of the journey.

We mentioned offline syncing above, but it doesn't have to go along with airplane mode. If you sync your tunes, podcasts, and maps to your phone ahead of time, your handset has less work to do while you’re driving since it doesn’t need to pull data from the cloud while also constantly searching for a wireless signal as you travel. Less stress on the modem means less heat overall.

Both Apple Maps and Google Maps let you download maps ahead of a trip. In Apple Maps, tap your profile picture, then Offline Maps, then Add Offline Map and find your location. With Google Maps, tap your profile picture, then choose Offline maps > Select your own map to choose the coverage.

There may be times when your phone has to be charging, wirelessly connected, making calls, in direct sunlight, and streaming music over a cellular connection, but try to keep these times to a minimum. Check regularly on how hot your phone is getting while connected to Android Auto or CarPlay, so you can take action if needed.
