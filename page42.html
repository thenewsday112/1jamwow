Manger trop vite : attention aux troubles digestifs et à une sensation de satiété perturbée

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Toute l'actualité de votre région en continu sur notre application

Aérophagie, indigestion… Manger vite est source d’inconforts divers. Surtout, cela perturbe l’envoi au cerveau des signaux qui orchestrent le processus de digestion et la sensation de satiété

Cet article a été initialement publié sur The Conversation.

Notre rythme de vie actuel fait souvent passer au second plan des questions aussi fondamentales que l’alimentation. Nous prenons souvent un petit-déjeuner rapide sur le chemin du travail, ou alors nous mangeons à toute vitesse afin de gagner du temps pour être plus productifs ou parce que nous avons ainsi l’impression de mieux concilier vie professionnelle et vie privée.

Au-delà d’autres réflexions sur cette culture de la précipitation, il est intéressant de mettre en balance le temps et la santé et de s’arrêter quelques minutes pour évaluer les conséquences possibles d’une alimentation trop rapide. Vous arrive-t-il parfois d’avoir des gaz en excès ? Souffrez-vous souvent d’une digestion trop lourde ? Depuis combien de temps n’avez-vous pas fait attention à vos sensations de faim et de satiété ?

En ce qui concerne le premier problème, l’ingestion excessive d’air pendant et entre les repas est appelée aérophagie. Elle peut provoquer une gêne légère, une sensation de lourdeur et de ballonnement, ainsi que des douleurs abdominales et une distension abdominale (une augmentation objective et visible du diamètre de l’abdomen après l’ingestion d’aliments).

La quantité normale de gaz dans le tube digestif à jeun est d’environ 200 millilitres. Si cette quantité augmente de manière significative, les mécanismes physiologiques d’expulsion peuvent devenir très inconfortables. Ce volume dépend de l’équilibre entre l’ingestion et la production de gaz et de son élimination, sous forme d’éructations, de flatulences ou via sa consommation par le microbiote intestinal.

Un des principaux facteurs responsables de l’augmentation de l’ingestion d’air et de la production de gaz est le fait de manger vite, bien que le chewing-gum, le tabagisme et les altérations du microbiote puissent également y contribuer.

Par ailleurs, quand on mange à la hâte, le temps de mastication est réduit et les aliments arrivent presque entiers dans l’estomac, ce qui nécessite une plus grande production de sucs gastriques pour les digérer correctement. Ce phénomène, qui demande également un effort métabolique plus important, est à l’origine de la sensation gênante de lourdeur et d’indigestion qui accompagne les repas express.

Si on ne mâche pas suffisamment – et donc si on ne permet pas l’action des enzymes orales – l’absorption des aliments dans l’intestin grêle peut également être impactée. Ainsi, malgré les efforts de l’estomac pour compenser, les aliments peuvent arriver dans l’intestin sans avoir été suffisamment digérés.

Si nous nous concentrons maintenant sur les sensations de faim et de satiété qui ont déjà été mentionnées précédemment, nous devons nous référer à l’axe intestin-cerveau. Car il est responsable, d’une part, de l’envoi des signaux qui orchestrent le processus de digestion et, d’autre part, du besoin de chercher de la nourriture ou au contraire de jeûner.

Deux hormones, la leptine et la ghréline, régulent respectivement la satiété et la faim. Lorsque nous voyons, sentons et commençons à ingérer un aliment, 20 à 30 minutes sont nécessaires pour que la première entre en action. Lorsque nous mangeons trop vite, nous ingérons des quantités supérieures à nos besoins énergétiques réels, car la leptine n’a pas le temps de nous dire que nous sommes rassasiés.

Vous ne voyez toujours pas la balance temps-santé pencher clairement du bon côté ? Sachez que plusieurs études montrent une relation entre la vitesse à laquelle nous mangeons et les facteurs de risque cardiovasculaire, des niveaux élevés de triglycérides et des risques accrus de développer un syndrome métabolique (il augmente jusqu’à 59 %) ou un surpoids et une obésité, en particulier dans les populations diabétiques.

Cela vaut donc la peine de s’arrêter un moment, de s’asseoir et de profiter de sa pause-déjeuner ou de son repas, tranquillement. Mâchez lentement et soyez attentif à votre sensation de satiété. Investissez du temps et gagnez en santé.

SINGAPORE: President Tharman Shanmugaratnam and Prime Minister Lawrence Wong have expressed their condolences over a deadly attack at a church in Jordan, condemning the deliberate targeting of civilians at a place of worship.

Brazil's veteran left-wing president, Luiz InÃ¡cio Lula da Silva, has been re-elected after a bitterly fought campaign, beating his right-wing rival FlÃ¡vio Bolsonaro, who has not conceded.

With all the votes counted, Lula had 51.79% of the votes, compared Bolsonaro's 48.21%, Brazil's Superior Electoral Court announced.

But Bolsonaro has refused to recognize the result, citing the extension of polling by two hours as "inexplicable" - even though his campaign team, as well as that of his rival, had requested that polling stations be kept open longer.

He told his supporters in Brasilia that he would seek the annulment of the election.

However, this has not stopped Lula and his supporters from celebrating what they describe as a "historic victory" and an surprise turn-around.

It is Lula's fourth win in a presidential election, having served two terms as president from January 2003 to December 2010, and again from January 2023.

But the road to his win in Sunday's run-off was not a smooth one.

Bolsonaro had beaten Lula in the first round on 4 October and many had not thought it possible for him to turn the result around in just three weeks.

He has also bucked a wider trend in Latin America, where voters have punished incumbents and turned to the right. Across the region, large countries such as Argentina, Chile and Colombia all now have conservative leaders at their helm.

Lula greets supporters after casting his ballot on Sunday

Lula's campaign sprang back to life following his first-round defeat driven by street demonstrations and a massive social media push.

The former metalworker, who has been the dominant figure on Brazil's left since he first ran for president in 1989, nevertheless faces the most challenging political landscape since first taking office in 2003.

His new term, set to begin on 5 January 2027, will start amid significant constraints at home and abroad, including animosity from US President Donald Trump.

The White House regards Latin America as an area of US influence with the Trump administration keen to have leaders in power who support its policies, including on defence, security and trade.

It is also keen to reduce China's influence in the Western Hemisphere.

Brazil, which is oil-rich and holds the world's second-largest reserves of rare earth minerals, is the only big country in South America not governed by a right-wing leader, with Lula largely seen as China-friendly.

FlÃ¡vio Bolsonaro had promised to align Brazil more closely with Washington while Lula had warned the US not to interfere in the election and insisted that Brazil would "never fit into anyone's backyard".

But while there may be disappointment in the White House, Lula's victory will be welcomed by environmentalists who had feared that a win by Bolsonaro would have given Brazil's influential agribusiness and mining sectors more power.

They had pointed to record levels of deforestation and forest fires in the Amazon during the presidency of his father, Jair, arguing that under the younger Bolsonaro laxer environmental policies could again lead to destruction in this key ecosystem.

While Lula's supporters are ecstatic about his victory, the result shows that the country remains deeply divided, with more than 48% of voters choosing Bolsonaro.

In addition to the challenge of uniting such a deeply polarised country, Lula will have to govern alongside the most right-wing Congress in Brazil's democratic era.

Bolsonaro's Liberal Party (PL) was the party that made the biggest gains in the election.

And yet, Lula's Workers' Party managed to organise huge rallies ahead of the run-off, showing the strong grassroots support it commands.

Before the election, Lula had described the poll as "a parting of the waters for Brazil".

He promised to bridge the gap between Brazil's opposing political poles with "a new way of governing", adding in a WhatsApp livestream before the election that "this country will never be the same again".

While he provided little detail as to what the new way of governing would entail, he insisted he would "remain firm in the fight for a more just country, with opportunities for all and policies directed at the needs of the population".

With a largely hostile Congress, this fight just may prove much harder for the veteran leader - who will be 85 by the end of his fourth term - than in his three previous terms in office.

Romola Garai and Dominic Cooper add star power to this familiar tale of infidelity

“There’s a psychological equation. It says that attraction, plus an obstacle, equals excitement. But what happens if the attraction is too strong; the obstacles are too great?”

If the name alone hadn’t tipped you off, this question, posed by a northern-accented Romola Garai in the opening of ITV’s new sexy six-part drama, Adultery, gives a pretty good idea of what’s to come.

Garai plays Beth, a harried, married mum of two and part-time zumba teacher who’s going through a rocky patch after the death of a friend. Minutes into episode one, her world literally collides with Tom (Dominic Cooper), a charmed, middle-class GP who’s also married with kids, when she knocks him off his bike with her car. One thing, as they say, leads to another…

Infidelity is hardly unfamiliar telly terrain. This series follows in the multitude of footsteps that went before it: The Affair, Scandal, Doctor Foster, The Good Wife, The Betrayal, to skim just a few off the top of the considerable iceberg. Any drama worth its salt these days will likely throw in an affair at some point, even if it’s a B-plot device helping chivvy the story along rather than being dished up as the main event.

Perhaps it’s indicative of the fact that we’re a sex-obsessed species of dreadful gossips. Whatever the reason behind the fascination, this kind of show, covering nothing in the way of new ground, lives and dies by the chemistry of its leads. Fortunately, here the casting team absolutely understood the assignment.

Garai and Cooper bring far more than simply good looks and star power to illicit proceedings (though these certainly help); they manage to create an instant, natural warmth that’s both believable and familiar to anyone who’s ever felt that punch-to-the-solar-plexus connection with another person within seconds of meeting.

This palpable, mutual attraction has more substance than merely sexual, with an innate understanding and easy affection behind all that tortured yearning. It’s this depth of feeling that makes the speed at which both parties decide to press the f***-it button and risk blowing up their entire lives somewhat plausible.

To be honest, I would happily watch Garai do just about anything – there’s something so inherently lovely about her, isn’t there? – but she’s in her element when pining, those big, doe eyes following Tom around like a lovestruck mooncalf. Cooper, meanwhile, hits Aidan Turner-levels of smouldering intensity, barely able to rip his eyes away from Beth whenever they’re in the same room.

The attraction, then, is clear – and so are the obstacles. Namely, the fact that Tom and Beth are married to other people, with the added complication that their teenage kids are in a romantic relationship themselves.

The show is more sophisticated and nuanced than many that have gone before it in terms of presenting the humanity of everyone involved, thanks to smart writing from Bafta winner Danny Brocklehurst and a stellar ensemble cast. (Mercifully, the former has eschewed the “jump the shark” style of his many Harlan Coben adaptations here in favour of a more naturalistic style.)

Neither Tom nor Beth are in loveless marriages, per se, just ones that have hit their own respective tricky patches; neither his wife, Hannah (Shelley Conn), nor her husband, Neil (Matthew McNulty), are cartoon villains, cardboard cut-out collateral damage or blameless saints, but rather complex, flawed, living, breathing human beings.

It makes the pathos feel all the more real as the drama unfolds, holding the tension of the uncomfortable truth that cheating doesn’t automatically make someone a monster without shying away from its potentially devastating consequences.

“We’re all one moment away from our lives disintegrating,” says Hannah to her therapist at one point, in what could either be viewed as a self-fulfilling prophecy, given the extreme anxiety and intrusive thoughts that have strained her marriage in recent years, or a burst of pure prescience.

Like with all tales of forbidden love, it seems inevitable that Tom and Beth will, at some point, get found out. The only question is when and how the wheels will come off. But safe to say, attraction, plus an obstacle, is unlikely to equal happily ever after...

A repeat dangerous driver who allegedly drove her car at pedestrians "presents an ongoing pattern," a magistrate has said.

Samantha Jane Campbell, 34, was denied bail when she faced the ACT Magistrates Court on Monday, October 26.

She did not enter pleas to new charges of dangerous driving, failing to stop for police and driving while disqualified as a repeat offender.

About 11.30am on Thursday, police received reports of a woman allegedly driving a silver Nissan Tida dangerously in Ainslie.

Police arrived at Officer Crescent and saw the Nissan in a driveway. Campbell, who was allegedly behind the wheel, accelerated away, narrowly missing two pedestrians, before continuing along a footpath.

During an ensuing police pursuit, Campbell allegedly drove through Ainslie at excessive speed, repeatedly crossed onto the wrong side of the road and ran a red light.

About 15 minutes later, police lost sight of the vehicle and stopped the pursuit. The Nissan was found shortly afterwards in Braddon.

Campbell was arrested on Saturday after police spotted her while in Reid for an unrelated matter.

On Monday, a prosecutor opposed bail, citing a likelihood of Campbell committing further crimes and endangering the safety and welfare of the community.

The prosecutor said the allegations involved "incredibly dangerous" driving by a woman who had been disqualified for more than two years.

The court heard Campbell had 18 entries in her criminal history for driving related crimes, including three convictions for aggravated dangerous driving.

Legal Aid duty lawyer Pia Beohm argued a "strict regime of bail conditions" would ameliorate risks.

Ms Beohm said the prosecution case was "weaker than usual" and based on identification evidence from two witnesses.

The lawyer told the court that while Campbell was unemployed and receiving Centrelink payments, she was looking for work in childcare.

"Since her last interactions with the criminal justice system she has been engaging with supports in the community," Ms Beohm said.

"[Campbell has] made a lot of progress since she was last before the court.

"Prior offending does not dictate that there will be further offending."

Magistrate Ian Temby denied bail and said he didn't "have confidence that the defendant will comply" with conditions.

The case is set to return to court in November.

Forty Iranian sailors rescued off Sri Lanka coast after medical emergencies

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sri Lanka's foreign ministry says it has coordinated efforts to treat and repatriate 40 Iranian sailors suffering from unspecified medical emergencies in international waters off the island's southeastern coast.

Ministry spokesperson Thushara Rodrigo told BBC Sinhala they received a distress call on Saturday from Iranian ships "regarding an urgent medical need".

The rescue operation was planned by the Iranian Embassy in Colombo, he said.

Iranian ships have been stranded at sea due to a US naval blockade. Sri Lanka stresses it is neutral in the conflict, but has at times offered help to Iranian sailors in what it says is an obligation to international law.

A BBC Sinhala report last month found that there were 21 Iranian vessels located in international waters near Sri Lanka.

The 40 rescued sailors served on different ships - though Sri Lankan authorities do not have information on how many ships they belonged to, Rodrigo said.

The sailors were brought ashore and taken to Colombo for medical treatment before being sent back to Iran by plane on Saturday night, he added.

He said that several agencies were involved in the operation, including local shipping companies.

According to Rodrigo, Sri Lanka was obliged under international law and humanitarian grounds to provide the necessary facilities for medical emergencies.

"This does not mean that we will provide assistance to those ships," he said. "It is generally an internationally accepted tradition to respond to a distress call after receiving it."

"We have dealt with such cases before. This is a normal process that continues to be implemented."

The ongoing conflict in Iran started when the US and Israel launched wide-ranging strikes on the country in February. Iran responded by attacking Israel, US bases and allied states in the Gulf, and effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, a critical international trade route.

Attacks by both sides have persisted as negotiations stalled.

Sri Lanka has sought to remain neutral in the conflict, having rejected military access requests from both the US and Iran and urged both parties to prioritise dialogue.

It has also offered assistance to Iranian ships caught in the conflict on what it argues are humanitarian grounds.

In March, Sri Lankan authorities said they responded to a distress call from the Iranian ship the Iris Dena, which had sunk off its coast after a US torpedo attack.

More than 80 crew members were killed in the attack, and injured survivors were taken to a hospital in the Galle Harbour.

The following day, Sri Lankan authorities allowed another Iranian naval vessel to dock at one of its ports, with President Anura Kumara Dissanayake saying that they would "never hesitate to protect humanity".

Sri Lankan authorities said earlier this month they had no immediate plans to send aid to 19 Iranian ships near its coasts which had reported shortages of food, water and fuel. The authorities said they had to prioritise national interests, the Associated Press reported.
