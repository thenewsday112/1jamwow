Smoke and Mirrors: The Right's Latest Attacks Against Trans People

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The MAGA movement is redoubling its attacks on trans healthcare in an attempt to win votes this November.

In the final weeks of the 2024 election, the refrain “Kamala is for They/Them, Trump is for You” blared from TVs in the battleground states during NFL and college-football games. Seeking to capitalize on voters’ frustrations after several years of inflation, the campaign ad warned that a Harris presidency would mean government-funded transition surgeries for children and migrants amid soaring food and housing costs for Americans.

This wasn’t the first time that the MAGA movement concocted a blend of populist resentment and anti-trans sentiment. That summer, Trump posted a video on social media in which he pledged to “investigate Big Pharma and the big hospital networks to determine whether they have deliberately covered up horrific long-term side effects of sex transitions in order to get rich at the expense of vulnerable patients.”

Since retaking office, the president has made good on that promise. The Department of Justice has issued subpoenas to doctors and clinics providing gender-identity care to children, while the Trump administration has moved to implement an executive order directing the Department of Health and Human Services and the DOJ to investigate and restrict such care. More recently, the federal health bureaucracy issued a report, “Wolves in White Coats: How Doctors and Hospitals Pushed and Profited From the Fraud of ‘Gender Medicine,’” alleging potentially fraudulent insurance-coding practices in gender-affirming care for minors. If the Trump regime and its allies cannot credibly run on a record of economic success, at the very least they might direct voters’ attention to someone else who, they claim, has been picking taxpayers’ pockets.

For a document that is ostensibly about healthcare, the provenance of its authors—mainly conservative women’s groups and Christian policy shops—is striking. Its most decorated author, May Mailman, is a veteran of the first Trump administration’s immigration team who returned to the White House to orchestrate the 2025 executive order “Defending Women From Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government.” Its few medical experts are Quentin Van Meter, the former president of the fringe American College of Pediatricians, which broke away from the prestigious American Academy of Pediatrics over the latter’s support of adoption rights for gay and lesbian parents, and Eithan Haim, who previously collaborated with the conservative activist Christopher Rufo to expose Texas Children’s Hospital’s defiance of the state’s criminalization of trans healthcare as potential child abuse. (Haim had been previously indicted for accessing that data under false pretenses, but the case was dropped one week into Trump’s second term).

The report itself presents what one could call a trans-exceptionalist view of the US healthcare system’s flaws. That is, it portrays the real deficiencies of a highly privatized approach to care as a problem of “ideological capture” and radical transgender activism rather than one of marketized medicine. It attempts to shock readers with sensationalized language about the “unique and lifelong revenue streams” that “sex-rejecting procedures” afford providers. Those relatively low-paid and prestige-hungry pediatricians, the report’s authors claim, stumbled upon a “strategic area of growth” in gender-affirming care, one that greedy “elite” hospital administrators were more than happy to encourage.

There is truth but far more fiction in this account of trans medicine. While medical transition does indeed require the persistent use of hormonal medications, these are often available as cheap generics. Yet the report explicitly alleges that pediatric gender-medicine programs generated substantial revenue and created groups of “captive patients” requiring long-term care. There is limited evidence, in the report or elsewhere, that leaders of hospitals consider gender-identity clinics, with their tiny patient populations, to be an especially lucrative “growth area.”

At its worst, “Wolves in White Coats” takes real problems in healthcare billing and attributes them to providers of trans care. The authors make a scandal of how some providers used creative diagnostic codes to convince insurers to pay for medically necessary treatment. For instance, the report alleges that a physician might diagnose a patient with an “unspecified endocrine disorder” rather than gender dysphoria, or file claims for adolescents with a diagnosis of “precocious puberty.” In the eyes of the Trump administration, however, these acts of clever compassion are nothing more than fraud, a violation of the False Claims Act that should be met with punishment.

The report also cites recent high-profile instances of actual systematic fraud in the Medicare Advantage system that resulted in $90 million settlements with the DOJ. These cases have nothing to do with trans medicine, but they have everything to do with the Trump administration’s support for this privatized form of Medicare—one that creates an incentive for this exact kind of con. By paying “risk-adjusted” higher rates to insurers based on the health of enrollees, Medicare Advantage basically invites its partners in the insurance industry to manipulate patient records. Unlike these insurers, however, trans-healthcare providers are not taking liberties with diagnostic codes to make anyone rich; they’re simply trying to get their patients much-needed care in a system that often withholds everything from hormones to cancer drugs.

Although Republican elites have benefited politically from the myth that a marauding gender-identity industry has been preying on unsuspecting children and their parents, they did not invent this conspiracy theory. One of the first influential articulations of the idea came in 2018, from the little-known blogger Jennifer Bilek, writing in The Federalist. Bilek described a cabal of gay, trans, and Jewish billionaires, including George Soros and Jennifer Pritzker, along with major pharmaceutical companies and Silicon Valley types, who had constructed a medical-industrial complex rivaling the military’s own sprawling network of government-subsidized firms. Over the next few years, this idea gained traction among gender-critical feminists and Christian fundamentalists before a slightly tamer version was adopted by think tanks like the Heritage Foundation and the Manhattan Institute.

In the summer of 2022, as inflation hit a 40-year high and the Democrats’ ambitious Build Back Better economic plan lay dead in the Senate, an influential conservative group put the gender-identity-industry trope to work. That August, the American Principles Project unveiled its “Top 25 Threats to the American Family” report, led by Senators Marco Rubio and Josh Hawley and featuring contributions from JD Vance, Heritage president Kevin Roberts, and others who became leaders in the second Trump administration. Although the American Principles Project had failed in its earlier attempts to mobilize anti-trans messaging for Republican politicians—its strategists were largely responsible for the first slew of gender-affirming care state bans that at first failed to do much better than the then-losing issue of trans bathroom bills—this new populist branding showed promise.

Straying from the GOP’s typical pro-business rhetoric, the American Principles Project took on Google, Amazon, and BlackRock as well as “special-interest groups” like the Chamber of Commerce and Big Pharma. Special ire was reserved for AbbVie, the manufacturer of puberty-suppressing medications, which was compared to the corrupt Tammany Hall political machine. “Woke” Silicon Valley moguls were also blamed for their role in broadcasting social-media content that allegedly spread gender dysphoria like a social contagion, eventually sending those confused children to specialists who were more than happy to sell them surgical salves.

This message ultimately resonated with many Americans, drawing as it did from a growing suspicion of Big Pharma and Big Tech in the post-pandemic era. Vaccine skeptics like Steve Bannon hosted Bilek to complain about the “transhumanist” machinations of trans-healthcare providers, while Make America Healthy Again leader Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and podcasters like Joe Rogan demonized gender clinicians and public-health officials alike. Just in time for Trump’s adoption of the conspiracy theory, the American Principles Project published its “Gender Industrial Complex” report in 2024, documenting a purported $4.4 billion market in hormones and surgeries—which might sound scandalous without the context that US healthcare spending runs in the trillions. No matter though—27 states and the federal government have now restricted best practices in medicine, spurred on by horror stories about a rapacious transition industry that cares for little more than insurance reimbursements and out-of-pocket surgical expenses.

This election season, Republicans are hoping that the anti-trans sentiment that propelled Trump back into office still has momentum. In Texas, the president’s handpicked candidate Ken Paxton has made such attacks central to his Senate race against Democratic nominee James Talarico. Resorting to schoolyard-bullying tactics, Paxton has referred to his opponent as “low-T Talarico” and “Six-Gender Jimmy”—barbs that conflate Talarico’s support for trans rights with a supposed masculinity deficit. Trump advisor Stephen Miller has gone as far as transvestigating Talarico with a backhanded celebration of the “first transgender senate candidate.”

Even more brazenly, the American Principles Project has been running a pro-Paxton ad that loops Talarico into the gender-identity-industry conspiracy. Borrowing from President Lyndon B. Johnson’s famous 1964 “Daisy” campaign ad, which cautioned against Republican nominee Barry Goldwater’s trigger-happy attitude toward the country’s nuclear arsenal, the video depicts an innocent young white girl who—according to an AI-generated LBJ voice-over—is at risk of a “trans industry [that] preys on confused children.” Although the ad begins with the familiar flowery field where the girl counts each petal that she delicately pulls from a daisy, the scene quickly shifts to a hospital gurney, over which a masked and scrubbed-in Talarico hovers. The child, now donning an anesthetic face mask, continues to count down as the disembodied LBJ tells us of the surgeries that will soon be forced upon her. And yet another Paxton-aligned PAC is currently airing an ad that warns “Talarico is for They/Them, Not You.”

Beyond Texas, these fears have also moved into intra-GOP battles over the party’s future. This was most visible in Kentucky, where the isolationist US Representative Thomas Massie fought against the Trump-endorsed Ed Gallrein in what became the most expensive House primary race in history. In one ad, a pro-Massie PAC directed attention to the fact that his rival, whom he called “woke Eddie,” was funded by the billionaire hedge-fund manager Paul Singer, a Republican known for his iconoclastic support of LGBTQ causes as well as his donations to the Israel lobby AIPAC. Combining those elements, the ad foregrounds Singer’s visage as a rainbow Star of David rises behind him while the voice-over denounces Singer as a “far-left trans-activist billionaire” member of the “LGBTQ mafia.” In conflating Singer’s Jewish heritage with his political support for Israel and trans rights, the ad recaptures the earliest fringe critics of the gender identity industry who were never shy about centering Soros in their narrative.

Elsewhere, Groyper-aligned candidates and their media champions like Tucker Carlson are harnessing due outrage against Israel, Silicon Valley, and the affordability crisis to push these and other scapegoating narratives into the political mainstream. Perhaps no one is more aware of this message’s electoral potential than Vice President J.D. Vance who co-authored the original American Principles Project report against the gender identity industry and later broadcast the conspiracy on Joe Rogan’s podcast during the final weeks of the 2024 election. Today, Vance is getting heat from within the party for his “America First” style approach to Israel and his willingness to blaspheme the GOP’s holy trinity of Reagan, Thatcher, and the Wall Street Journal, if that is what it takes to ascend to the presidency two years from now.

And yet those conservatives who are most eager to denounce Big Pharma and specific features of our privatized healthcare system are also the least likely to do anything about them. Instead, they’ve cut Medicaid and refused to extend the Affordable Care Act’s tax credits. And lest liberals remain off the hook, their failure to reckon with inequality and their complicity with the forces that keep both the healthcare industry and the country’s megadonor-driven politics intact are also to blame. After all, “Kamala is for They/Them, Trump is for You” worked because the Democratic Party’s botched economic agenda fueled the idea that trans rights are incompatible with the public good. Until something is done about the ugly truths of private power, there will be plenty of those who are drawn to these conspiratorial untruths.

Joanna Wuest is an assistant professor of women's, gender, and sexuality studies at Stony Brook University.

Iran: Wenn "Ehre" zum Todesurteil für Frauen wird

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Der brutale Mord an einer jungen Mutter entfacht im Iran erneut die Debatte über den sogenannten Ehrenmord, gesellschaftliche Rechtfertigung von Gewalt gegen Frauen und fehlenden Schutz für Betroffene.

In den frühen Morgenstunden des Montags am 5. Oktober 2026 starb Maryam Yarmohammadi. Die 28-jährige Mutter von zwei Kindern wurde auf der Insel Qeschm im Südiran von ihrem Bruder und ihrem Ehemann mit Messerstichen angegriffen. Ihre Geschlechtsorgane wurden verstümmelt. Kurz darauf starb sie im Krankenhaus. Die geständigen Täter wurden verhaftet.

Laut Medienberichten versammelten sich rund 50 Familienangehörige und Verwandte vor dem Krankenhaus und protestierten gegen die Festnahme von Maryams Bruder und ihrem Ehemann. Sie sollen der Ansicht gewesen sein, dass der Tod die "geringste Strafe" für Maryam Yarmohammadi gewesen sei.

Was die junge Frau getan hatte und warum die Angehörigen sie für schuldig hielten, ist nicht bekannt. Der Fall und die Art und Weise, wie die 28-jährige zweifache Mutter getötet wurde, weisen jedoch auf ein bekanntes Muster der sogenannten Ehrenmorde hin.

In solchen Fällen steht häufig die Vorstellung im Mittelpunkt, ein Mann müsse eine vermeintliche "Schande" beseitigen, die eine Schwester, Tochter oder Ehefrau über die Familie gebracht habe. Andernfalls könne er von seinem sozialen Umfeld abgelehnt, gedemütigt oder ausgegrenzt werden.

Was als "Anstand" gilt, kann von der Familie oder den örtlichen gesellschaftlichen und kulturellen Normen bestimmt werden. In manchen traditionell geprägten Regionen können bereits lautes Lachen oder ein freundliches Gespräch mit einem fremden Mann als Verstoß gegen diese Normen gelten.

Frauen, denen ein solcher Verstoß vorgeworfen wird, bekommen häufig keine Möglichkeit, sich gegen die Anschuldigungen zu verteidigen.

Seit Jahren versuchen Frauenrechtsaktivistinnen im Iran, landesweite Netzwerke aufzubauen, die betroffenen Frauen Beratung und Schutz anbieten. Dazu gehört die "Kampagne zur Beendigung von Ehrenmorden".

Rezvan Moghaddam, Sprecherin der Kampagne, sagt im Gespräch mit der Deutschen Welle, es scheine in der Region keine Aktivistin oder Gruppe gegeben zu haben, die Maryam Yarmohammadi hätte unterstützen können.

Mithilfe zivilgesellschaftlicher Netzwerke ist es der Kampagne in einigen Fällen gelungen, bedrohte Frauen an sichere Orte zu bringen und ihnen Zuflucht zu vermitteln.

Gleichzeitig betont sie, dass dies nicht als Vorwurf gegen Frauenrechtsaktivistinnen in diesen Regionen verstanden werden dürfe: "Ich selbst komme aus Chusestan im Südiran und bin mir der dort bestehenden Einschränkungen bewusst."

Die auf der Scharia beruhenden Gesetze im Iran behandeln Frauen in vielen Bereichen nicht als gleichberechtigte Bürgerinnen. Ihre Handlungsmöglichkeiten und Entscheidungen über ihr eigenes Leben können von der Zustimmung ihrer männlichen Verwandten abhängen, etwa wenn es um Bildung, Beruf oder Reisen geht.

Auch nach dem Tod einer Frau können männliche Angehörige im Rahmen bestimmter rechtlicher Regelungen Einfluss auf den weiteren Verlauf eines Strafverfahrens nehmen.

Im iranischen Strafrecht gilt bei bestimmten Straftaten, darunter Mord, das Prinzip der Vergeltung. Bei einem Tötungsdelikt können die Angehörigen des Opfers zwischen der Forderung nach Vergeltung – die bei Mord die Todesstrafe bedeuten kann – und der Begnadigung des Täters entscheiden.

Solche Konstellationen verdeutlichen die Schutzlücken, die im iranischen Justizsystem im Zusammenhang mit Gewalt gegen Frauen eine wichtige Rolle spielen.

Laut einer Studie der iranischen Polizeiakademie aus dem Jahr 2020 machten sogenannte Ehrenmorde – die Ermordung einer Frau durch männliche Angehörige wie ihren Vater, Bruder oder Onkel wegen eines vermeintlichen Verstoßes gegen gesellschaftliche Anstandsnormen – in einigen besonders traditionell geprägten Provinzen im Westen und Süden des Landes bis zu 45 Prozent aller dort verübten Morde aus.

Der UN-Sonderberichterstatter für Menschenrechte im Iran gab im März 2025 bekannt, dass im Jahr 2024 im Iran mindestens 179 Frauen von Männern aus ihrem familiären Umfeld ermordet wurden. Diese Fälle wurden als Femizide registriert.

Im Fall der Ermordung von Maryam Yarmohammadi hat vor allem das Ausmaß der Brutalität die Gesellschaft erschüttert. Der Fall löste in den sozialen Netzwerken eine Welle der Bestürzung und Trauer aus. Viele Frauen reagierten auf diesen von Hass im Namen von "Ehre" und "Schande" geprägten Femizid.

Demgegenüber standen zahlreiche Kommentare, die sich in Victim Blaming, also der Schuldzuweisung an das Opfer, erschöpften. Andere sprachen von der "Bestrafung einer untreuen Frau" oder versuchten, "die Wut der Männer zu verstehen".

Die Psychoanalytikerin Saba Alaleh spricht von einer Struktur, die Männern ihrer Ansicht nach seit Jahren vermittelt, dass sie Frauen kontrollieren und deren Verhalten und Entscheidungen überwachen müssten. Gleichzeitig werde männliche Identität an den "Gehorsam der Frau" geknüpft.

"Gewalt wird in einer Gesellschaft dann normalisiert, wenn Überzeugungen, Zustimmung und Schweigen innerhalb einer solchen Struktur zusammenwirken", erklärt Alaleh im Gespräch mit der Deutschen Welle.

How many jobs is your state losing to AI? This dashboard tracks the cuts – and the cost

CAIS' Wage Loss Dashboard puts AI-related job cuts into dollars and cents.

Fears of how AI will impact the economy and job availability are running rampant after a few years of steady layoffs at major companies. Much of that fear can be attributed to labs and CEOs juicing their own relevance, but some job cuts have been explicitly tied to AI’s rapid acceleration.

The Center for AI Safety (CAIS) maintains a tracker that keeps tabs on how these changes are impacting family wages.

Also: Why replacing staff with AI backfires

The Wage Loss Dashboard includes a map of where layoffs have occurred since January 2026 (it was just updated this month to include September data) and a drop-down that shows data for the entire country or an individual state. As of now, the dashboard finds just over 117,000 job cuts attributed to AI nationally this year — $8.5 billion in estimated annual wage losses.

The Wage Loss Dashboard multiplies the number of AI-attributed job cuts by the average annual salary to estimate annual wage loss. According to the dashboard, wage estimates “begin to show what those cuts can mean for households and local economies.”

The dashboard also displays the estimated average wage loss per person based on that layoff data, presented relative to everyday expenses, like a tank of gas. For the country as a whole, it’s $25 per person per year. Not much, but the numbers shift by state: In California, for example, it’s $56. That’s thanks to a much higher concentration of job cuts (just over 27,000 layoffs attributed to AI). Alaska, on the other hand, claims only 138 job losses ($14 per person).

Those aren’t the exact numbers of people laid off for AI in California and Alaska this year, however. As CAIS explains, “The dashboard estimates AI-attributed cuts for each state, converts them to estimated annual wage loss, then divides by state population to estimate the average loss per person. State estimates are not observed AI layoffs. We apply each month’s national share of cuts citing AI to that state’s announced job cuts.”

But back to that $25. As a metric of impact on everyone in a particular state — not just the people directly laid off — it’s an interesting point of view. At scale, the idea that everyone is $25 poorer as a result of these still-developing trends puts AI’s mass community effect in perspective.

The dashboard uses a few sources of data: monthly reporting from Challenger, Gray, and Christmas, which compiles layoff announcements from across the US by state as well as national job cuts credited to AI, and data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics on every state’s annual mean wage. CAIS also refers to population estimates from the US Census Bureau.

Also: 3 ways AI can help you ace your next job interview

In 2025, the Challenger, Gray, and Christmas reports identified over 54,000 job cuts that companies publicly linked to AI. That translates to one in 20 layoffs from last year. But tracking job losses alone is a more abstract metric than what those layoffs mean in monetary terms to the people experiencing them.

“There has been significant news coverage of job loss from AI, but it can be hard to quantify how these cuts affect the average person’s finances,” CAIS wrote of the project. “This dashboard aims to help illuminate the monetary costs of AI on American families and communities.”

As CAIS points out, the impact of layoffs shows up in ripples across grocery stores, small businesses, rent, and other local costs.

“One person’s layoff means less income for every business they support. Multiply that effect across thousands of workers in multiple industries, and you start to see the real economic weight of AI displacement,” the researchers behind the project wrote. “In addition, AI-attributed job cuts aren’t spread evenly. States with large tech and media sectors are experiencing greater impact.”

A few caveats: CAIS noted that this dashboard doesn’t include “jobs created by AI, productivity gains, re-employment, or secondary economic effects.” That’s partly because experts don’t have a consensus on whether or how much AI will stimulate the economy or outpace job displacement. AI-attributed layoffs are themselves murky: The data here are job cuts companies themselves said were tied to AI. Without a single definition for what that means or complete transparency from companies, it’s not always 100% provable that a layoff was the direct result of obsolescence or replacement spurred by AI.

Also: Companies embracing AI the most are hiring more people

“We don’t require an executive to say ‘we replaced a team with AI.’ This is a moderate standard, deliberately: it captures the broad pattern of AI-justified workforce reductions while excluding clearly unrelated cuts,” the Alliance for Secure AI wrote of its methodology.

Other trackers like Jobloss.ai, which is run by the Alliance for Secure AI, also catalog job loss data by company, date, and how explicitly companies cited AI around those layoffs. Because of how amorphous these data can be to track, Jobloss.ai only logs layoffs that meet certain criteria: at least 100 jobs in one fell swoop, accompanied by some mention of AI (or proxy terms, like “automation” and “productivity”).

The dashboard aims to make the data we do have more accessible — not to shame any particular industry or company for its AI adoption choices.

“AI is a technology being adopted by companies for many reasons,” CAIS wrote. “Our goal is to make the costs of that adoption visible, so workers, communities, and policymakers can respond with real information instead of guesses.”

The Wage Loss Dashboard also includes a section on how well AI is completing remote freelance tasks. Using data from the Remote Labor Index (RLI) benchmark CAIS co-developed, which tests agents on real-world freelance tasks like web development and video production, CAIS shows how quickly agents are evolving.

Also: The top AI fear for 6,000 tech pros isn’t losing their jobs

“When RLI launched, the best AI agent completed just 2.5% of projects at professional quality. Eight months later, that number has risen to 15.8%,” researchers wrote. As of now, Anthropic’s Opus 5.5 can fully automate 21.25% of remote tasks, the highest in CAIS’ testing, followed by OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra.

But that doesn’t mean AI is gobbling up those projects from human freelancers. RLI measures what AI models are capable of doing, not what they’re actively taking on. That said, CAIS maintains alarm about what it sees as a trend: the convergence of AI-attributed job cuts with increasingly work-capable agents.

While legislation can’t explicitly prevent companies from opting for AI systems over hiring humans in certain cases, recent laws in a few states, including the tech-sector-heavy state of California, aim to make sure firing and other disciplinary decisions aren’t completely automated by AI systems.

Eine Infektion mit der Amöbe Naegleria fowleri ist selten, allerdings verläuft die Infektion fast immer tödlich. Deshalb haben die Behörden des mexikanischen Bundesstaates Yucatán sechs Seen gesperrt, in denen der Parasit nachgewiesen werden konnte. Auch in den USA sterben immer wieder Menschen durch die Amöbe. Laut der amerikanischen Gesundheitsbehörde CDC erkranken jährlich zwar weniger als zehn Menschen an der sogenannten Primären Amöben-Meningoenzephalitis (PAM), allerdings sind die Folgen einer Infektion fatal: 97 Prozent der Erkrankten sterben.

Naegleria fowleri ist thermophil, das heißt, die Amöbe mag warme Süßwasser und Böden am liebsten. Sie ist deshalb vor allem in den Subtropen und Tropen verbreitet, findet sich laut Robert-Koch-Institut (RKI) in den Sommermonaten aber auch in natürlich oder künstlich erwärmten Gewässern in gemäßigten Klimazonen. Laut CDC vermehrt sich Naegleria fowleri ab einer Temperatur von etwa 30 Grad Celsius bis hin zu 46 Grad Celsius am besten.

In seltenen Fällen gedeihen die einzelligen Lebewesen auch in nicht ausreichend gechlorten Pools oder verunreinigtem Trinkwasser. In Salzwasser hingegen kommt Naegleria fowleri nicht vor.

Normalerweise ernähren sich Amöben von Bakterien, die sie im Wasser und Boden entdecken. Manchmal findet die Amöbe allerdings ihren Weg über die Nase einer Person in den menschlichen Körper. Das kann beim Schwimmen, Tauchen oder auch beim Reinigen der Nase mit kontaminiertem Wasser passieren. Von dort dringt Naegleri fowleri über den ersten Hirnnerv (Nervus olfactorius), der für den Geruchssinn zuständig ist, bis ins Gehirn vor.

Die Inkubationszeit, also die Zeit von der Infektion bis zum Ausbruch der Erkrankung, beträgt zwischen drei und bis zu 14 Tagen. Die Infizierten bekommen Fieber, leiden unter Übelkeit, Erbrechen und Kopfschmerzen. Wahrnehmungs- und Bewegungsstörungen folgen. Es kommt zu einer sogenannten hämorrhagisch nekro­ti­sie­ren­den Menin­go­enze­pha­litis, einer Entzündung des Gehirns, die das Hirngewebe zerstört. Die Betroffenen fallen ins Koma und sterben in den meisten Fällen.

Laut CDC ist eine Infektion mit Naegleri fowleri ausschließlich über die Nase möglich. Eine Übertragung durch Tröpfchen oder Aerosole über die Luft findet nicht statt. Auch das Trinken von kontaminiertem Wasser ist ungefährlich - zumindest was eine Infektion mit der "hirnfressenden" Amöbe angeht.

Wer nach dem Baden in einem warmen See Kopfschmerzen, einen steifen Nacken und Fieber bekommt und sich übergeben muss, sollte sich schnellstmöglich in ärztliche Behandlung begeben.

Die Primäre Amöben-Meningoenzephalitis (PAM) wird mit einer Kombination von Medikamenten behandelt - darunter Antibiotika wie Rifampin und Antimykotika wie Amphotericin B, die für die Behandlung von Pilzinfektionen entwickelt wurden. Diese Medikamente wurden bereits erfolgreich zur Behandlung von Patienten eingesetzt, die überlebt haben. Miltefosin, ursprünglich zur Therapie von Brustkrebs angewendet, ist das neueste Medikament, das sich im Einsatz gegen die Amöbe vielversprechend zeigt.

Da die PAM so selten ist und die Erkrankung, einmal ausgebrochen, so rasant abläuft, gestaltet sich die Suche nach effizienten Therapien schwierig.

Die Klimakrise könnte das Problem verschärfen, weil sich mit steigenden Luft- auch die Wassertemperaturen erhöhen. Laut CDC infizieren sich mittlerweile nicht mehr nur Menschen in den südlichen US-Bundesstaaten, auch aus dem Norden der USA werden Erkrankungen gemeldet.

Vorerst bleibt es dabei, dass Naegleri fowleri bei weitem nicht die größte Gefahr ist, die in Gewässern lauert. Während zwischen 2012 und 2021 in den USA 31 Menschen an PAM erkrankten, starben in einem ähnlich langen Zeitraum (2011-2020) mehr als 4000 Menschen durch Ertrinken.

The helicopter-powered quest to save salmon in B.C.

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Jessica Hutchinson, executive director of conservation group Redd Fish Restoration Society, remembers the long, restless night in December 2025 listening to the thrum of a rainstorm in Tofino, B.C. Lying in her bed, she hoped the culmination of years of planning, weeks of construction and eye-watering helicopter bills would hold.

As Hutchinson lay awake, rain poured down from the steep hillsides surrounding Tranquil Creek like it normally did, sending a surge of water into the river below. But now the deluge had an obstruction: a tangled mass of around 50 trees, tufted like a giant bird’s nest and lodged into the riverbed, causing the flow to ricochet and swoop under and above. Pools and eddies had begun to form below the wooden mass of the river’s new intruder, also known as an engineered logjam, reflecting a years-long effort that is relatively uncommon in B.C.

Engineered logjams are large-scale interventions for places with large-scale problems. In Tranquil’s case, the watershed needed life support.

Redd Fish watershed restoration director Tom Balfour describes the approach as “open-heart surgery.”

“You don’t do this to rivers that need a little help,” he says. “You do it when things are really desperate.”

Logged extensively between the 1960s and the 1990s, the valley soon became a funnel for sediment flowing down from the steep hills. The river weaving through it had become a highway of fast-moving water, and sand and gravel, threatening to bury or drag salmon eggs out to sea. By 2017, Chinook were on the verge of local extinction, while chum, coho and steelhead were in severe decline.

The Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation, whose territory encompasses Hiłsyaqƛis (the Nuu-chuh-nulth name for Tranquil Creek), identified the region as an urgent priority, and Redd Fish subsequently launched their multi-year quest to figure out how to help it heal.

“Tranquil’s got a special place in my heart,” Tatt Charlie, lead guardian for Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation, says. Tla-o-qui-aht guardians are working with Redd Fish to restore the watershed. “It’s one of those amazing ecosystems where we could get almost all of our salmon spawning in that again and rejuvenate it for our people.”

The Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation worked in partnership with Redd Fish to develop a long-term plan for Tranquil. It required learning about what went wrong, and then gradually bringing the missing pieces back. It wasn’t cheap or easy, but ten years into their journey, Hutchinson says it’s been worth it.

“The dock is very slippery,” calls out Frank Witter as he steers the silver skiff up to the small wooden landing just outside the mouth of the Tranquil Creek inlet. Witter, Charlie and fellow Tla-o-qui-aht guardian Nathaniel Currie get ready to disembark.

The dock is remnant infrastructure from the watershed’s heavy logging days. So is the dusty sorting yard to our left, replete with metal loading skids angling into the ocean, as if the valley’s forests were still full of big trees to take away.

Hutchinson has come back from working on the riverbed to meet us, and we pile into one of Redd Fish’s company trucks. From the window, other signs of change soon follow: we pass a pile of pipes and metal planks near a recently decommissioned forest service road.

Witter, Redd Fish’s project manager, stops the truck as we reach a high point on the road, and we climb out to take a look. From here, the watershed’s history opens like a storybook.

What appear to be white, fuzzy scars carved into the mountainside are actually landslides, Hutchinson tells me. These are the places where disturbance-loving alder trees have lodged themselves onto the scree slopes, their foliage still bare from the winter.

After they lost the big conifer trees whose roots were anchors, the fine-grained sediment, gravel and jagged rocks that comprise the region’s hillsides started to fall. Logging roads made things worse, cutting holes in the hillside like a dispenser below a gumball machine. When Clayoquot Sound’s famous rainstorms bear down on the valley, they tend to pull the already-loose sand and rocks down with them.

The valley bottom, too, had lost its defences. Once fortified by big, steadying trees like cedars and spruce, the flat-lying ground is now peppered with daintily rooted species like alder. Over time, the river got wider and flatter.

For salmon, this layered onslaught of sediment spells trouble.

All five species of North American Pacific salmon have historically spawned in the Tranquil watershed, and for salmon, spawning is a miraculous event. It requires adult salmon to survive in the open ocean, replete with fishing nets, predators and pathogens, sometimes for up to around decade. If the salmon is fortunate enough to reach its elder years, it tracks the earth’s magnetic field and then draws on its keen sense of smell to return, sometimes over thousands of kilometres, to the same stream channel where it was born.

Under the right conditions, the salmon digs a nest beneath the gravel just deep enough for its eggs to stay covered during the river’s most turbulent months. If things go wrong, baby salmon can asphyxiate beneath a buildup of sand, or if the water moves too fast, they get washed out to sea.

It wasn’t always like this, Charlie says, as he gestures to the mouth of the estuary below us. It was once the bustling village of Hiłsyaqƛis, one of many village sites in the territory, where members of the Tla-o-qui-aht came to fish and camp each year.

Back then, surrounded by an intact forest, the now-rushing Tranquil Creek consisted of meandering paths of water braiding together. For millennia, the Tla-o-qui-aht people helped keep it that way, using various techniques to keep the populations in balance: cultivating strong-rooted crabapple trees that helped mitigate erosion in the estuary, for example, and building rock weirs to count and manage how much salmon swam up to spawn.

“You would allow the strongest of the river to go up, enough fish to make sure every square inch of gravel is spawned,” Saya Masso, Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation’s natural resources manager, says. Once enough salmon had travelled up the river to support the forest and its animals, Masso says the community would then begin their own harvest.

That stewardship was suppressed as Canada dispossessed the nation of its territory.

Over a century later, the Tla-o-qui-aht partnered with Redd Fish to conduct a years-long investigation to determine the damage caused in the interim, piecing together Tranquil’s history of disturbance through historic photos and terrain assessments. Their research determined logging strongly contributed to the watershed’s downhill slide. They also discovered that the watershed was unlikely to heal on its own in time for salmon to benefit.

“It’s not on a healing trajectory,” Hutchinson says. “It’s actually unravelling and becoming worse through the years.”

Witter’s team worked to stabilize the landslides, creating terraces and staking willow trees, known for their hearty roots, to prevent further movement. Throughout the lower watershed, Redd Fish planted 120,000 trees and restored 100 hectares of young, replanted forests by thinning out trees to create light to help big ones grow.

They also turned their attention to the river.

“Rivers take annual presence to become productive,” Masso says. “Our plan is to get to a system of abundance.”

Western science was slow to realize that wood and salmon need each other.

Since at least the last Ice Age, salmon’s decomposing bodies have fed and plumped the big trees of the Pacific Northwest. Those trees, in turn, feed the river, depositing big logs that create the smorgasbord of habitat types and pools salmon require to survive. Logs and logjams act like sorting hats for gravel, distributing patches of coarse and fine gravel that suit different species’ needs.

“It quickly became apparent to me in studying the rivers of this region that wood was as important as sediment and water,” geomorphologist David Montgomery says. He was one of the early contributors to a growing scientific recognition of the relationship between wood and salmon in the 1990s.

By then, much of the damage to these ancient Pacific rivers was done. Logging companies had clear-cut many watersheds, as they did in the Tranquil. Some fish biologists even encouraged wood removal from streams and rivers, thinking it would help fish travel upstream.

This antipathy to river debris had deep roots. Large-scale “log drives” in the late 1800s and early 1900s culled wood from the vast rivers, including in the Cowichan Valley, where people used explosives to clear off wood and other impediments.

In B.C. and throughout the Pacific Northwest, government-funded restoration crews gradually began bringing wood back in the 1990s. Early interventions were often targeted to smaller streams where a single log or two could make a meaningful impact. But big rivers like Tranquil Creek’s mainstem require something sturdier.

A short walk downriver, one of Redd Fish’s many engineered logjams comes into view, knotted and gnarled with trees piled atop one another, holding firm like a fortress as water passes dutifully around its edges.

“Frank, have you seen it?” Hutchinson says, getting Witter’s attention. River debris is accumulating in the logjam, a natural addition to the engineered effort. “That’s not even us, that’s just wood piled up.”

This is the promise of a well-built logjam: it takes up a life of its own, collecting wood the river throws at it. Ideally, it becomes self-sustaining, forming forested islands in the river that contribute their own source of wood.

To match the ferocity of the Tranquil’s rainy season flows, Redd Fish had to craft logjams with around 50 trees piled atop and between each other, lasagna-style, staked in place with tall logs piled deep into the riverbed.

“It basically is trying to mimic what an old growth tree would do if it rolled into the river with its giant root plate,” Hutchinson says.

“It would maintain the force of the river.”

These human-made jams are not cheap, requiring heavy equipment to temporarily isolate a spot along the riverbed with pumps and sandbags, and helicopters to haul wood and logs up to 15 metres tall. They also require teams of engineers working closely with traditional knowledge holders, geomorphologists and other scientists to ensure the investment and design will hold up.

“All those gears have to sing. But when they do, I’ve seen really good results.”

A shock of cold hits my legs as I venture forward into Tranquil Creek, in a channel that’s surprisingly deep and aquamarine blue.

Last year’s work is already visible, marked by the first crop of young willow trees clawing their roots into the gravel, one of many impediments Redd Fish and Tla-o-qui-aht Tribal Park Guardians have installed to slow the water down.

Redd Fish staff are here, hammering the new generation of stakes into the dry riverbed. For a moment, conversation lulls, and there’s just the sound of running water and syncopated tapping, then the conversation starts up again.

So far the outcomes of their efforts appear hopeful, though Hutchinson avoids drawing early conclusions. On snorkel surveys through the river, one of the organization’s efforts to monitor the completed work, Redd Fish has found salmon taking refuge in the pools created by the logjams, including during heat waves. In the last two years, those pools have gotten deeper.

Though costly and time-consuming, Redd Fish’s monitoring efforts and years spent studying the watershed are critical, Jason Hwang says.

“It’s an excellent example of a project in the current day that is doing all the right things,” Hwang, who is the chief program officer and vice president of salmon at the Pacific Salmon Foundation, says. “They’ve done all their homework.”

But the Tranquil faces pressures old and new.

Climate change is heating Tranquil’s waters, and fish farms nearby pose additional risks for the Tranquil river’s returns, potentially spreading pathogens that can infect migrating wild salmon as they move past. At the same time, restoration efforts across the province face constant funding challenges as government priorities shift.

This February, the mining company Imperial Metals received a permit to conduct extensive exploration work in the Tranquil watershed’s headwaters, in hopes of eventually opening a gold mine there. The Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation and representatives from the broader Tofino business community have voiced their opposition to the mine.

Many of Tranquil’s challenges remain, but for now, Hutchinson is glad to watch the river evolve on its own terms now that it’s had some help. She hopes it might be a model for what’s to come.

“We haven’t restored a single entire watershed, top to bottom,” she says.

“I was always hoping that Tranquil would be that watershed.”

Onigiri Gorichan serves delicate Koshihikari rice balls stuffed with salmon flakes, pickled plums or Spam and egg.

Not all stalls are focused on Japanese cuisine. Kyoto-based Pizzeria Da Naghino offers crackly edged Neapolitan pies and Gelateria Il Brigante, from the coastal city of Kamakura, serves scratch-made Sicilian gelato.

Orders are placed at self-service kiosks, allowing guests to mix and match items from various vendors without the hassle of separate checks. When the food is ready, the order number appears on a screen above the kiosks as well as on individual screens at each corresponding stall. Food is picked up directly at each stall.

The current generation of omakase chefs in Los Angeles are returning to the essence of the cuisine. A trip to Tokyo confirms what’s been driving their pursuit for excellence.

A wraparound bar anchors the massive food hall, with mounted TVs playing live sports. A full cocktail menu is available, including premium sake and drink specials such as $3.99 Bud Light and wine all day.

Upstairs is the Round 1bowling alley and arcade, with karaoke, rows of claw machines and other games imported from Japan. Billiards and ping pong tables will soon be added. Takahashi said the goal for the food hall and activity center is to become an all-day destination that will attract locals as well as day trippers from Los Angeles and the Central Coast.

YUU Japanese Food Hall plans to open in Escondido later this year with a fresh slate of concepts, including Shaved Ice Housekibaco, serving kakigori with ice that is slowly frozen over 72 hours, and Takoyaki Do-Raku Wanaka, a takoyaki shop founded in Osaka in 1986.

“It’s exciting that we’ve been able to bring so many Japanese restaurants to the United States,” Takahashi said. “It’s a long time coming.”

YUU Japanese Food Hall and Round1 Bowling and Arcade are at 3295 E. Main St., Suite 2, Ventura, and open daily from 10 a.m. to 2 a.m.
