New EU plan for Arctic warily eyes influence of Russia, US

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The EU unveiled its priorities in the Arctic as it vies with Russia, China and the US for influence. EU foreign affairs chief Kaja Kallas said drone incursions, GPS jamming and cable sabotage undermine Arctic security.

The EU on Tuesday presented a new Arctic Strategy that outlines the bloc's priorities in the key region amid challenges such as security threats, climate change and mining.

Russia is building up its military power in the Arctic, and US President Donald Trump has threatened Greenland, saying the Danish territory is needed for American security interests but later signed a deal with Denmark over US military access to the territory. China is also investing in the region and has shipping ambitions there.

The northern parts of Finland and Sweden, along with Greenland are located within the Arctic, meaning part of the region is in the European neighborhood.  Iceland and Norway, two other Arctic states, are also involved in the European single market.

"The Arctic is one place where the tectonic plates of geopolitics are colliding," European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said.

"When we see that sovereignty, territorial integrity and the rules that have underpinned security in the region can no longer be taken for granted, we have to act and change our strategic posture," she added.

EU High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy Kaja Kallas said Russia "has reopened Soviet-era bases and is testing new weapons" in the Arctic.

"Drone incursions, GPS jamming and the sabotage of undersea cables undermine Arctic security," Kallas continued. Russia's shadow fleet, which is an attempt to circumvent Western sanctions, is active in the Arctic.

On Arctic security, the EU plans to develop a maritime security hub in the region "to facilitate information sharing form seabed to space." The bloc also intends to expand European icebreaker capacity and extend its satellite surveillance program in the region.

Rising emissions and temperatures: The cost of Trump's climate retreat is now clear

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The world is set to breach the critical 1.5-degree temperature limit, with Donald Trump's US withdrawal from climate action worsening the outlook, according to a UN report. But there is a small silver lining.

For over a decade, scientists have warned the world must limit global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 Fahrenheit) to avoid the most severe consequences of climate change.

Decades of "insufficient" action on cutting the fossil fuel emissions heating the planet means overshooting 1.5-degree temperature threshold is now "imminent and unavoidable," according to the United Nations Environment Program's (UNEP) annual Emissions Gap Report.

If current climate policies continue, temperatures will rise by an average of 2.8 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels within this century. But even if countries meet their new climate pledges, global warming is still projected to reach 2.6 degrees Celsius. Both scenarios would tip the world into a period of catastrophic weather extremes.

The projections reflect a gap between what governments promise to do and the action they are taking to curb emissions. Some 167 countries have submitted climate action plans to the UN, covering 83% of global greenhouse gas emissions. But those pledges to cut emissions fall far short of what is needed to limit warming.

Among the world's largest emitters, Indonesia, China and the United States saw emissions rise in 2025, according to preliminary estimates. The increase in the US "far exceeded that of all other countries," driven by a resurgence of coal — the dirtiest fossil fuel source — and rising electricity demand, partly linked to massive AI data center expansion.

The outlook has worsened in part because of US President Donald Trump's withdrawal of the United States from the historic Paris climate accord in which the world came together in 2015 to agree to halt climate change. The US is the second-largest annual emitter of climate-warping greenhouse gases.

Since Trump entered the Oval Office for a second time, his administration has taken a sledgehammer to climate policy, gutting environmental regulations and expanding oil and gas production.

"The main takeaway is simple — governments need to get on with enacting policies that cut fossil fuel use as soon as possible," said Bill Hare, CEO of the global science and policy institute Climate Analytics, of the report.

Exceeding 1.5 degrees Celsius even temporarily sets humanity on a dangerous course, as it increases the risk of triggering potentially irreversible or difficult-to-undo "tipping points," like the loss of coral reefs, long-term retreat of ice sheets, and the Amazon Rainforest turning into a grassland.

As ecosystems disappear and sea levels rise, these changes could trigger a deadly chain reaction, releasing more greenhouse gases and accelerating warming, dramatically increasing the likelihood of deadly extreme weather.

This summer alone, Europe recorded more than 32,000 excess deaths related to heat and nearly 1,500 people died in a flash floods in Nepal and Tibet, after climate change laid the groundwork for a glacier to collapse.

But scientists maintain 1.5 degrees Celsius is not a magic number, and that every fraction of a degree of warming brings escalating risks. To reduce the risk of future damage, countries should still focus on rapidly transitioning away from fossil fuels, the largest contributor to global climate change, and toward renewable energy like solar and wind.

Now the best-case scenario is overshooting 1.5 degrees Celsius for a short time before bringing temperatures back down quickly.

"Overshoot is not surrender," UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said in a press statement. "We can still keep it as small and as short as possible — and bring temperatures back below 1.5 degrees before the end of the century."

UNEP lays out a scenario in which the world sees warming peak at 1.7 to 1.8 degrees Celsius around mid-century, before falling back to 1.5 Celsius by 2100. Getting there would require steep emissions cuts of 69% by 2040, relative to 2025 levels.

It would also require removing 330 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and storing it in the ground. But recent studies have cast doubt on the feasibility and costs of the technology, as well as the nascent carbon removal industry's ability to scale as quickly as required to meet climate goals.

The UN warns any delays will make the challenge harder, saying that every five years that emissions remain at current levels adds about 0.1 Celsius to projected peak warming.

"We must pull out all the stops to minimize peak warming and return to 1.5 Celsius by the end of the century," Inger Andersen, Executive Director of UNEP, said in a press release. "This is possible."

Still, climate policies have proven effective in reducing emissions. The UNEP report draws on more than 200 studies that analyze how measures like carbon taxes, government climate targets, building codes and subsidies for low-carbon technologies are working.

Carbon-pricing schemes have been the most widely studied so far. China's pilot schemes reduced emissions by about 11%, while the European Union's emissions trading system saw an 8% cut, compared with projected levels without these policies.

"There are more opportunities than ever to accelerate action," according to the report, pointing to the boom in renewable energy since the adoption of the Paris Agreement a decade ago.

Global renewable electricity capacity has grown three times faster than anticipated, and solar build-out grew seven times faster. In 2024, more than 90% of renewable projects were cheaper than fossil fuel alternatives.

While total energy supply from coal, oil and gas has continued to rise, that's happened at a slower rate than was projected in 2015. And coal use is likely to peak and decline by the end of the decade.

"We have a wealth of success stories showing that emissions can be cut quickly," Andersen said. "It is now up to nations to urgently put in place policies that spark fast, deep and sustained emissions reductions, and put the world on track for a more stable climate and more stable economies and societies."

Football: German coach Hürzeler flying high in Premier League

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Fabian Hürzeler is younger than his star player, a fellow Premier League German. But, contrary to his reputation in his homeland, Hürzeler has become a popular figure both on and off the pitch in England.

"We are absolutely in love with him. He's been an incredible manager. The results are extraordinary and we're flying."

Those words from Ben Parker will be familiar to fans of any club who have been on the kind of winning run Brighton and Hove Albion are currently on.

But all the signs on and off the pitch suggest that the man in question — Brighton's German coach Fabian Hürzeler — is more than just a flash in the Premier League pan.

Hürzeler has never coached in Germany's Bundesliga. After winning promotion to Germany's top flight with St. Pauli in 2024, he left to become the youngest man ever to take charge in England's Premier League at 31. His team currently sit third, having won six games in a row including a 5-0 thrashing of bitter rivals Crystal Palace last weekend.

It was the repeat of a fixture last season that marked Hürzeler's lowest Brighton ebb, with a 1-0 home loss making it one win in 12. "There are only two options," the German coach said at the time. "Give up or keep pushing harder, and that's always the option I choose in my life."

Parker, the co-editor of Brighton and Hove Albion fanzine, Dogma, admitted to DW that there were plenty who weren't sure if persisting was the answer. "There was some disquiet at the end of that game, there were people shouting: 'You're getting sacked in the morning.'"

While such sentiment may be difficult to swallow for many coaches, Parker said that Hürzeler told him in an interview soon after joining the club that he welcomed it when needed.

"He talked about wanting a fan relationship that had a two-way aspect. The club and him are really ambitious, so if the performances aren't up to scratch, we should be telling him that. And that's what happened on that day. I see the whole thing as being quite healthy."

Hürzeler never played at the top level. He represented Germany's youth teams and turned out for Bayern Munich's reserves but soon grew frustrated with second-team football. He then accepted a player-coach role at German fifth-tier side Pipinsried at the age of 23. From 2018, he was also an assistant coach for Germany's U20s.

He performed this alongside a job at an art dealership in the German state of Bavaria, where he moved with his family at the age of two having been born in the United States. After starting as an assistant coach at St. Pauli in 2020, he took over after head coach Timo Schultz was fired in late 2022. Since then, he hasn't looked back.

That unusual path to top-flight management and some innovative tactics at St. Pauli led to a perception in Germany that Hürzeler was a little aloof and something of a 'laptop manager' in the vein of former Bayern Munich and Germany boss, Julian Nagelsmann who had a similarly nondescript playing career. This, so the charge goes, meant Hürzeler wasn't able to forge the relationships and togetherness needed to make a team more than the sum of its parts.

That was rarely what was reported by his players and staff at St. Pauli, nor is it Hürzeler's belief. "I want to be myself, I don't want to play a role, and my age definitely helps regarding understanding the players," he said.

"I always say I speak their language and I understand their thoughts, because it wasn't that long ago when I played."

His antics on the sideline also earned him a reputation as a bit of a hothead. Hürzeler was booked seven times during St. Pauli's promotion push and since joining Brighton, the German has been booked nine times and been sent off once. When asked about this, he said: "Emotions are part of the game."

Parker said this authenticity has also been reflected in his relationship with Brighton and Hove, on the south coast of England.

"For most Brighton fans he comes across as quite a quirky, interesting, left-field figure. The mantra he set out when he first arrived was quite counter-culturalist, to challenge the establishment, and that fits well here," Parker said.

"You will see him walking along on the seafront, going up for coffees around town, and he travels back and forth on an ebike, not in a chauffer-driven car as a lot of managers might. He's also hosted fan events in pubs, which I believe he paid for. So I'd say he's very approachable."

But Hürzeler isn't the only 30-something German beloved in Brighton.

Pascal Gross, who rejoined the club from Borussia Dortmund in January, is also a firm favorite. The midfielder, who retired from Germany duty after the World Cup earlier this year, has scored three and made four in the club's opening seven league games. The 35-year-old's connection with Hürzeler is also key, with Groß admitting he stayed in touch with Hürzeler even before he resigned. The return is proving to look a great decision for both sides.

"I was here for seven years, got married here and both my kids were born in England. The most important milestones in my life happened here. I missed my friends and the feeling of being deeply connected to the club."

The feeling is mutual, said Parker. "Pascal Gross is everybody's favorite player. He's Brighton's first best signing, from Ingolstadt, and second-best signing, from Dortmund. He's a sensational player, and it's probably true to say there is a lot of warmth towards German football and Germany because of these hugely important characters at the football club."

The Premier League club's success has been built on a smart coach and player recruitment strategy guided by local owner, Tony Bloom. Part of the model is that those who shine brightest will often be snatched by the bigger fish.

"I think he's said he's a supporter of Bayern Munich, and he uses them as an example of the standards he wants," said Parker of Hürzeler. "I can see that he could be a good fit in a couple of years time. He won't go to a Liverpool or a Chelsea, I think there's a strong pull to Bayern in particular."

Bayern's current boss, Vincent Kompany, is in little danger at the moment, though Bayern coaches are only ever a few losses from crisis. For the meantime, Hürzeler is also happy where he is, having extended his contract to 2029. Again, the feeling is mutual.

Next up in the Premier League is a trip to Liverpool, where Jürgen Klopp became the first German coach to make a real mark on the Premier League. But before that, Hürzeler's team have an assignment in the Europa Conference League, a competition Brighton are among the favorites to win. Parker hopes a first major trophy is in sight.

"If he can be the person that wins us that first trophy, then he will go down as the greatest."

US sanctions target Iran's rail sector as Tehran shifts cargo from sea routes to overland corridors

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As US sanctions expand to Iran's rail sector, Tehran is relying more heavily on overland links with China and Central Asia. But capacity constraints, delays and costs limit rail's ability to replace maritime trade.

The United States has opened another front in its economic pressure campaign against Iran, this time targeting the railways Tehran increasingly relies on as maritime trade becomes more difficult.

On October 1, the US Treasury sanctioned three Iranian railway companies alongside seven automakers and five foreign suppliers of automotive parts as part of its Operation Economic Outcast.

Washington says Iran's rail and automotive industries provide revenue and logistical capacity to the state and are connected to networks involving the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC).

The timing of the move is significant. Since the US maritime blockade sharply restricted Iran's southern trade routes, Tehran has tried to move more goods through its land borders and railway connections with China, Central Asia, Russia and Turkey.

Rail transport has become more important just as Washington decided to target it.

Shahriar Naghizadeh, director general for foreign trade at Iran's railway authority, was quoted by the semi-official Mehr news agency as saying that the country has around 15,000 kilometers (9,300 miles) of main and secondary railway lines, more than 35,000 wagons and nearly 500 diesel locomotives.

The network carried around 44 million tons of freight in the Iranian year ending in March 2026, up from about 40 million tons the previous year, according to Naghizadeh. And authorities are targeting 54 million tons this year.

Iran is linked to neighboring markets through 13 rail crossings, providing overland access to Turkey, Azerbaijan, Afghanistan, Pakistan and onward routes to Central Asia and China via Turkmenistan.

The new US sanctions raise questions about how foreign railway operators, freight forwarders, banks and insurers will continue dealing with Iranian rail companies.

Bloomberg reported in May that freight trains traveling from the Chinese city of Xi'an to Tehran had increased from around one per week before the US blockade to roughly one every three or four days.

In September, a train left China carrying automotive parts, consumer goods and other cargo. It ran through Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan before entering Iran at Sarakhs with an expected transit time of 14 to 16 days.

For Iran, such services offer a route to Chinese suppliers that avoids the area around the Strait of Hormuz. But advertised transit times do not necessarily reflect what an importer experiences.

A Chinese freight company told DW that actual delivery times can stretch far beyond advertised schedules because of border inspections, customs procedures and train availability.

It estimated that rail transit along the main route from western China to Tehran would take 50-60 days under current conditions. Road transport along a similar route is around 22 to 25 days.

The gap between a scheduled 14-day transit time and commercial estimates of up to 60 days highlights one of the central challenges facing Iran.

Customs inspections, gauge changes, payment problems and sanctions can all be another potential point of delay.

Increasing the number of trains also requires more locomotives, wagons and cooperation from several transit countries.

Alireza Salavati, managing director of London-based Middle East Analytica, said rail is increasingly useful for Iran but should not be mistaken as a replacement for maritime trade.

"A workable overland route now links China and Iran, but I would not call it a replacement for sea freight," he told DW.

Under normal conditions, ships can move far larger quantities at lower unit costs. Rail becomes more attractive for goods where speed and continuity matter more than the cheapest transport.

That includes automotive components, machinery, electronics and industrial inputs, whose absence can stop an entire production line.

Salavati argues that the effect on companies cannot be measured only in freight charges.

For a manufacturer importing $1 million (€890,000) worth of inputs every 30 days, a 30-day journey means roughly a million dollars of inventory may be tied up in transit.

Cutting the journey to 15 days could reduce that amount significantly, depending on payment arrangements. The reverse can be much more damaging.

If input costs rise while delivery times stretch from 30 to 45 days, companies need more working capital simply to maintain the same production flow, the expert explained, pointing out that a factory can have customers and orders but still have to reduce production because too much cash is trapped in goods that have not arrived.

"Reliability matters as much as speed," Salavati said. "A 15-day train is little use if the container waits a week to depart, or if the bank or insurer will not touch it."

The new US sanctions add another source of uncertainty to that calculation.

Optimizing its railway use would give Iran more options than having to rely on southern ports alone, but the overall scale remains limited.

Washington's sanction strategy is designed to isolate Iran by closing maritime, financial, aviation and now railway channels.

Still, Salavati argues that the same pressure may accelerate Iran's integration with alternative networks.

"Washington's efforts to isolate Iran may also be pushing it deeper than at any point in recent years into China's economic orbit and closer to Russia's wartime strategic system," he said.

That does not mean China or Russia can make up for the economic cost of US pressure, since Iranian importers still need insurers, banks and customers able to absorb higher prices.

Container trains can only carry a fraction of what a large cargo vessel can transport, so for now, rail provides Iran with a limited alternative trade channel.

Its wider success depends on better financing and becoming more reliable — but even then it is not a wholesale substitute for sea freight.

The French police have drawn renewed criticism from rights groups, not only because of their methods but also because of their use of stun grenades and rubber bullets. The French police have a bigger arsenal than most European countries. For instance, officers use the so-called LBD-40, a type of gun used to fire rubber bullets.

"It is not a French specificity. But there are guidelines. There must be a certain distance," Anastassia Tsoukala, an associate professor of criminology at the University of Paris-Sud, told DW. After 24 months of protests sparked by the collapse of a concrete canopy at Novi Sad railway station, which killed 16 people in November 2024, Serbia is heading into an election unlike any other in the 14 years since the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) first came to power.

For the first time in years, the ruling party faces a serious political challenge, which this time comes from a student movement that has channeled widespread public discontent into an electoral campaign.

"These are the first elections since [Aleksandar] Vucic came to power in 2012 that are competitive and whose outcome is uncertain," political scientist Dusan Spasojevic told DW. Russia's aviation authority, Rosaviatsiya, has issued an emergency safety "carpet" protocol that is designed to immediately shut down the airspace over a specific region or around affected airports if an aircraft violates Russian airspace, weapons are used, or unidentified objects appear in the skies. Once the protocol is announced, drastic restrictions apply to all air traffic.

"If there is a military threat, a specific section of airspace is closed off, and this carpet is declared," Oleksandr Laneckij, the chief executive officer of the Friendly Avia Support consultancy, told DW. "Essentially, every drone poses a military threat."

Laneckij noted that Yemen's Houthis have managed to significantly disrupt Saudi airspace. "I think Ukraine's military capabilities far exceed those of the Houthis," Laneckij said.

The military analyst Jan Matvejev also said Ukraine would have little trouble severely disrupting Moscow-area air traffic. Though Ukraine has not yet managed to completely shut down Russian airspace as Zelenskyy plans, Matvejev said, its armed forces "remain committed to this idea."

"To close off the airspace and trigger the 'carpet' plan, all you need are simple drones that don't have to be equipped with a warhead or even have a long range," Matvejev said. Ukraine's arms industry can produce hundreds of combat-ready long-range drones in a day, he added. That is why he believes Ukraine could have the necessary resources to shut down Moscow's airports.

Indeed, Ukraine's efforts to shut down Russian airspace are "just getting started," Simon Shuster wrote in October in the US magazine The Atlantic, citing well-informed sources.

Matvejev said Russia's air-defense systems were not entirely effective in efforts to prevent Ukrainian drones from penetrating Moscow's airspace. "If it were possible to shoot them down, they would have done so by now," he said. He added that Russia would have deployed additional air-defense troops if they were available.

Laneckij said Russia might lower the threshold for declaring the carpet protocol, which could increase the risk of deadly incidents such as the 2024 Christmas day crash of an Azerbaijan Airlines flight. Investigators say the flight was struck by a Russian air-defense missile that was being used to defend against drones, killing 38 people.

Matvejev seconded the fear that Russia's defenses could again mistakenly target civilian aircraft instead of military objects. He said little had changed about Russia's air-defense protocol since the Azeri flight was shot in 2024.

In an operation against a low-flying drone, the target is visually identified and fired upon with a machine gun. In such a scenario, the danger to aircraft is extremely low, unless they happen to be flying in the immediate vicinity.

The risk to civil aviation increases significantly when anti-aircraft missiles are deployed, Matvejev said. A missile could have defects that are practically unavoidable in mass production and could veer toward an aircraft flying nearby. Matvejev said such defense measures could not guarantee the safety of civilian aircraft during operations.

He said drones did not have to carry explosives to be disruptive. A collision between an airplane and a basic drone can have serious consequences — and, he said, a drone sent from Ukraine all the way to Moscow must be relatively large, "weighting dozens of kilograms."

In the summer of 2023, Ukrainian armed forces attacked the airport in the Russian city of Pskov, where both military and civilian aircraft were parked. Earlier this month, drone debris fell on the Sochi airport, on Russia's Black Sea coast.

Russia also attacks Ukraine's airports. In September, Zelenskyy said Kyiv's airport had been struck, as well as the Boryspil airport near the capital.

He said issues could emerge around insurance. When insurers rate the security risk of flying to Russia as very high, they could significantly raise their rates for aircraft headed there. This would cause ticket prices to skyrocket.

Competition within Russia's aviation market has already declined because many airlines are no longer in business, Laneckij said. This has increased demand for tickets from the remaining foreign airlines.

"Whether they stay or not depends on the price they have to pay for the danger faced," he said.

EU moves to loosen rules on pesticide use as experts sound the alarm

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The European Union wants to relax rules for pesticides, saying it will be easier for farmers to adopt more environmentally friendly options. But health and environmental experts say the risks are far too great.

As part of its drive to "streamline and simplify" EU laws, the European Commission has proposed sweeping changes for agriculture and food safety legislation. These include a move to ease rules controlling the use of pesticides and herbicides, which help fight pests and weeds.

Under current EU rules, which date back to 2011, primary pesticide components, or active substances, are only granted approval for limited periods of no more than 15 years.

The European Commission's proposed revamp of the system would make indefinite greenlighting — including of the controversial glyphosate, which has been linked to cancer and kills almost all plant life — the default. Safety reviews would only be done in exceptional cases, and regulatory authorities, not the industry, would bear responsibility.

The commission has said its goal is to cut costs, simplify work for authorities and help farmers stay competitive. Scientists and doctors, however, warn that concerns over human health and environmental impact from these toxic substances are being brushed aside.

The European Commission has said it's responding to complaints from farmers and the agriculture industry to clarify EU rules that govern pesticide use.

A joint statement released in May by 19 food associations, including CropLife Europe, which promotes pesticides, said complicated administrative procedures and a lengthy approvals process meant farmers were "left without any effective solutions to protect their crops against insects, weeds and diseases," putting food security and livelihoods at risk.

"The existing system of periodic renewal assessment of every active substance has become unsustainable," the commission's press service told DW, citing "chronic delays" and "bottlenecks" in the approval of new, more sustainable products.

In recent negotiations, EU member states said open-ended authorizations should be restricted to "low-risk active substances."

But their stance is still wanting to extend initial approval periods for other substances to up to 15 years, and a maximum of 25 years for renewals.

Herbert Dorfmann, a center-right Italian member of the European Parliament, said there is currently a yearslong delay in approving new pesticides, including more eco-friendly options, meaning farmers are forced to rely on older substances. Or, if these are banned, go without altogether.

The commission wants to "make the system more efficient and responsive," while guaranteeing that "the protection of human and animal health and the environment remain the highest priorities."

Dorfmann, who as co-rapporteur is responsible for interinstitutional negotiations on the issue, backs the commission's stance, saying the EU needs "to open up the system for innovation."

He aims to bring the parliament's proposal on the new pesticide rules to a vote among EU lawmakers before the end of the year.

Ecologists and medical experts, however, have spoken out forcefully against the proposed changes, saying the potential implications for human health and the environment are being downplayed.

In September, more than 500 experts from across the EU signed an open letter calling on the European Parliament to suspend its consideration of the new regulations and conduct an independent investigation into health and environmental impacts.

On October 7, UN rights experts also backed the call for a full review into whether the proposed changes violated the EU's obligations under international human rights law, especially with regard to agricultural workers and children.

Pesticides can persist in the environment, contaminating water and degrading soils. They can also drive biodiversity loss and poison other animals higher up the food chain.

When it comes to humans, the European Environment Agency has said it was "not yet possible to estimate the burden of disease linked to current levels of exposure to all pesticides."

However, exposure to chemical pesticides, including banned substances that still persist in the environment, has been linked to various cancers, chronic respiratory disease, neurological disorders and developmental delays, as well as problems with fertility and behavior.

And exposure is widespread: A survey conducted between 2014 and 2021 found that 84% of urine samples from both children and adults in five European countries contained residue from two or more pesticides.

Martina Vijver, scientific director and ecotoxicology expert at Leiden University in the Netherlands, said experts were "deeply concerned" that the EU's current plan "will weaken rather than strengthen the level of protection for pesticide regulation."

Speaking at the European Parliament in September, she said regular reassessments were key to evaluating the safety of pesticides with new findings, pointing out that the perception of risk changes over time.

"The regulatory system must remain capable of correcting yesterday's decisions with tomorrow's knowledge," she said.

Salome Roynel, a policy officer at the Pesticide Action Network Europe, an NGO based in Brussels, said the harmful effects of pesticides aren't often fully understood until years later, when the product is up for reconsideration.

"New science emerges all the time," she told DW, highlighting how 56 active substances have been banned in the EU since 2011 as a result of periodic reviews.

That includes the insecticide chlorpyrifos, previously used in orchards and on vegetable and cereal crops, which has been tied to harmful effects on children's brain development.
