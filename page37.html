BC Ferries board cuts ties with CEO Nicolas Jimenez

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The BC Ferries vessel Spirit of Vancouver Island leaves Tsawwassen, B.C., bound for Swartz Bay in September, 2024.DARRYL DYCK/The Canadian Press

Nicolas Jimenez is out as CEO at BC Ferries in a “leadership transition” launched by the company’s board, just two days after the election of the B.C. Conservatives, who asked for his firing last year.

A news release from BC Ferries said Brian Anderson, the company’s vice-president of strategy and planning, has been appointed as the interim chief executive officer, effective immediately.

The release said the board of directors had also started a “revitalization review” to address growing demands for safe, reliable services.

The B.C. Conservatives, who secured a two-third majority in Saturday’s provincial election, called last year for Jimenez to be fired after the company awarded a $1-billion contract to a Chinese state-owned company to build four new vessels.

The party also called for the firing of board chair Joy MacPhail, saying she and Jimenez had shown “a complete failure of judgment” by pushing forward with the contract with China Merchants Industry Weihai Shipyards despite Canada’s trade dispute with China.

Eric McNeely, provincial president of the BC Ferry and Marine Workers’ Union, said on Monday that he saw indications last year that a review was taking place over the Chinese contract.

“I think there were some real questions around that time about why we wouldn’t build ferries in B.C. or in Canada, and then some questions sort of emerging about who’s accountable for decisions made by BC Ferries when there is so much government money in play,” said McNeely.

McNeely said about 4,500 union members at BC Ferries have lacked a collective agreement since November 2025, and he hoped the change in leadership could reset that labour relationship.

“I think with new leadership, there’s a potential opportunity to find labour peace as we go into the fall period, as opposed to what was happening under previous leadership where management was giving themselves increases while unionized members continue without,” said McNeely.

The BC Ferries board said in its news release that it appreciated Jimenez for his contributions to the company and a search for a permanent successor would follow the revitalization review.

Jimenez told the House of Commons transport committee last year that BC Ferries received six compliant bids to replace its four oldest ships, all from foreign countries.

While two Canadian shipyards pre-qualified for the competition, he said, neither chose to formally submit a proposal.

The organization chose the proposal that offered the best combination of value, quality, delivery, speed and protections for customers, Jimenez said.

Jimenez was previously president and CEO of provincial auto insurer ICBC.

Yandex Supercomputers Unscathed After Ukrainian Strikes, Company Says

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Yandex said Monday that all three of its supercomputers remained unharmed following a series of Ukrainian drone attacks against its data centers and have been moved to more secure locations.

“Once brought back online, the computers will rejoin Yandex’s service infrastructure,” the Russian tech company said in a brief statement. “They will also continue to be used for training foundation language models.”

It marks Yandex’s first comments about the three supercomputers — called Chervonenkis, Galushkin and Lyapunov — since Ukraine began launching air attacks against the company’s sites earlier this month.

The supercomputers, launched between 2020 and 2021, rank among the most powerful in the world. But as the global AI race has accelerated in recent years, their position on the list has gradually slipped.

Ukraine’s attacks against Yandex data centers have led to temporary outages of the company’s services across Russia, including ride-hailing apps, food delivery platforms and banking. Corporate clients on Yandex Cloud have also been impacted.

The Kremlin has said that, while it is too early to assess the financial losses, Yandex was taking steps to deal with the aftermath of the attacks.

Russia’s Digital Development Ministry said it helped Yandex migrate core systems to external backup servers.

The ministry also approved recommendations for protecting data centers against drone attacks, the newspaper Kommersant reported Monday, citing a government document.

The measures include building the most critical facilities underground, installing structures capable of withstanding blast waves, deploying electronic warfare systems and maintaining backup copies of data. They also call for regular coordination with the military and National Guard.

President Vladimir Putin has vowed retribution for the attacks on Yandex and earlier ordered officials to improve Russia’s ability to protect its digital infrastructure. Ukraine said it was responding to Russian attacks on its own data centers.

Russia Accuses Ukraine of Launching Strikes Against Oleshky Ahead of Ceasefire

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Russia's Defense Ministry on Monday accused Ukraine of repeatedly striking the Russian-controlled town of Oleshky in southern Ukraine with artillery and drones ahead of a planned local ceasefire this week, something it said had prevented the delivery of food and medicine and stopped people from leaving.

Ukraine and Russia said on Friday they had agreed to a ceasefire in Oleshky that would go into effect on Oct. 27, allowing for the delivery of aid and the evacuation of residents through humanitarian corridors.

Russia's Defense Ministry said in a statement on Monday that Ukraine had struck the town 18 times with artillery fire and carried out more than 120 drone attacks on it and the main roads around it in the week since Oct. 19.

Reuters could not independently verify that assertion, and there was no immediate response from Ukraine, which has in the past accused Russia of not letting aid in or people out of the town.

Also on Monday, the Kremlin said that the local humanitarian ceasefire agreed for Oleshky with the United States' help could set a precedent for similar ceasefires elsewhere in Ukraine.

The ceasefire comes as residents of the town, controlled by Russian forces since February 2022 and located close to the front line, have reported ​a deepening food crisis, with both sides accusing each other of blocking supplies.

Toronto fundraiser Elizabeth Tory was a maestro at working a room

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Elizabeth Tory and John A. Tory outside Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre Emergency Department. The couple provided funding for the Tory Trauma Program at Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, Canada’s first and largest trauma centre.Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre/Supplied

Elizabeth Tory, an intrepid volunteer with formidable powers of persuasion, tapped a vast network in support of various causes. She recruited surgeons and captains of industry to canvass for hospital fundraisers and once enlisted her hairstylist’s help to buttonhole an elusive donor as he settled into his chair for a trim.

Liz, as she was known by most, was legendary in Canadian charity and business circles for her drive, accomplishments and rapier wit. In the course of a life dedicated to community service, Ms. Tory played a strategic role on the boards of Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, the Toronto Symphony Orchestra and the Art Gallery of Ontario, among others, while also spearheading multimillion-dollar campaigns for donations. It was unpaid, enormously valuable work. At the grassroots level, for years she went door-to-door in the same Toronto neighbourhood collecting money for cancer research. Her daughter, Jennifer, tagged along when she was a child.

Ms. Tory died at her Toronto condo, in the company of her four children, on Oct. 3. At 94, she had succumbed to age-related ailments. The woman with six holes-in-one to her credit finally gave up golf two years ago and was active until the end as a member of the art gallery’s nominating committee vetting candidates for board service.

Her husband, John A. Tory, a trusted behind-the-scenes adviser in the Thomson and Rogers business empires, died suddenly of a hemorrhagic stroke in 2011. As a team, they were regarded as one of Canada’s most influential power couples, say those who knew them both.

Liz Bacon was a vibrant, outgoing nursing student when her sister Anna, a law school classmate of John’s, introduced them. He was as reserved as she was ebullient, their daughter Jennifer Tory says.

“My dad was very shy, notwithstanding he was a very successful businessman and had to be out there with all different types of people. Without her, he might have been a real hermit and stayed home at lot more. … I think that’s part of the reason he adored her.”

Elizabeth Tory, right, with the prestigious Sunnybrook Rose Award, which she won in 2008, presented by Virginia McLaughlin, who was the chair of the Sunnybrook board at that time.Doug Nicholson/Sunnybrook Hospital/Supplied

A voracious reader, Ms. Tory held her own at business events and was a maestro at working a room.

The late Peter C. Newman, author of The Canadian Establishment book series, made this observation: “Liz Tory, whose husband masterminds the exponential growth of the Thomson empire, is probably the most sought-after social animator, partly because she is so well-organized (serving on a dozen committees as well as running her own company, Tudor Travel) but mainly because she has an instinct for how group effort can be meshed into meaningful results.” She later sold the travel business to American Express, her daughter said.

Ontario PC Leader John Tory is consoled by his mother Liz Tory as he arrives to deliver his concession speech in Toronto on Oct. 10, 2007.Adrian Wyld/The Canadian Press

While the volunteer hours she put in were equivalent to a full-time job, none of this came at the expense of family, says her eldest son, former Toronto mayor John H. Tory. In addition to being a strong, supportive wife, she was a fiercely proud mother to her children, John, Jennifer, Jeffrey and Michael. She relished her role as grandmother and great-grandmother although she was not the sort of person one would describe as “cuddly,” her son John said at the funeral, drawing laughs from many in the church who had been at the receiving end of her zingers.

The Tory family’s community impact is perhaps most deeply evident at Toronto’s Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, where in addition to Liz’s – and now Jennifer’s – board service, the Torys have been major benefactors for more than five decades. Two weeks after the Tory family matriarch died, Sunnybrook celebrated the 50th anniversary of the Tory Trauma Program, which is the largest of its kind in Canada, chief executive officer Andy Smith said in an interview. It provides care for medical emergencies ranging from heart attacks and brain hemorrhages to accidents, burns and mass casualties.

As he spoke, the trauma team was on standby to receive patients from Alberta if needed following the devastating Wainwright military base fire that claimed four lives and injured dozens of others.

John A. Tory and Liz Tory, early in their marriage, consulted Sunnybrook doctors and executives on their most pressing needs and decided on a substantial donation to support trauma treatment. Jennifer Tory, a retired bank executive, has built on that philanthropy and followed in her mother’s footsteps as a member of the Sunnybrook Foundation board. Liz served on the hospital board for 14 years and subsequently sat on the foundation board.

Toronto Mayor John Tory accompanies his mother Elizabeth Tory to vote at an advanced polling election station in Toronto on Oct. 10, 2018.Nathan Denette/The Canadian Press

“Liz transcended to the highest levels of foundation leadership, where it was about much more than money. It was about the spirit that she brought and imparted,” Dr. Smith says. She had a way of inspiring others and bringing them on board.

Elizabeth Emma Bacon was born in Toronto on Jan. 15, 1932. She was the second of four children born to Howard Bacon, who owned a trucking business, and Helen Bacon (née Solomon). She attended Jarvis Collegiate in downtown Toronto, where she was an excellent athlete and sang in musical productions.

“Liz Tory came from modest beginnings to lead an extraordinary life of family and community contribution,” her children wrote in the death notice.

She was a student at the Wellesley Hospital School of Nursing when she met John A. Tory and did not resume her nursing studies after marrying him and having their first child at the age of 21. She was of a generation where women were not widely expected to work outside the home while raising families but volunteer work was encouraged.

The bold confidence she inherited from her mother served her in good stead when her mother-in-law, Jean Tory, introduced her to the worlds of wealth and charity. “She didn’t know she was releasing a tiger,” Liz Tory later told Globe and Mail society columnist Rosemary Sexton.

Her first volunteer job with Jean was with the Toronto Symphony Orchestra, where Liz Tory stayed on as a volunteer for more than 70 years. She was always in high demand from other not-for-profit organizations and charities wanting to boost their impact.

Alexander Younger, Dalton McGuinty, Sarah Richardson, Liz Tory.JENNA MARIE WAKANI/The Globe and Mail

Her children John and Jennifer say their mother was fearless and unabashedly direct in enlisting others to donate money or time. She was sharp, funny and made people laugh, although at times it was a somewhat nervous laugh.

They both recall a memorable exchange at a meeting of the Art Gallery of Ontario board, where a list of potential board nominees was being considered. The other board members seemed satisfied with the list, then Liz said, “I think my copy has a page missing.” When asked what she meant, she responded, “Well, I don’t know, the page that has the women’s names on it.” They had presented a slate entirely of men.

The zingers did not come quite as frequently toward the end of her life, although Liz firmly asserted her independence when her children took her on an exploratory tour of a retirement home. When informed of the menu and dining schedules, Liz quipped that that it was kind of like prison where you were told what time you were going to eat and who you were going to eat with, her son John recounted in an interview.

The “nice young woman” leading the tour gamely carried on the movie room where Liz asked, “Do you have porn night?” It was a joke, “and she would say it in a way that couldn’t be other than funny,” he added. “But it was a serious message for us that … she was going to live at home if she possibly could and we abided by her wishes.”

The condolences offered to the family after her death were fond and effusive. Ms. Tory had a remarkable ability to remember people’s names and the names of their children. When she volunteered at Sunnybrook, she would invite doctors home to dinner. CEOs, caregivers, hairstylists, the condo concierge and her golf caddy were among the friends who paid tribute. When the former mayor of Toronto goes to shops in his mother’s neighbourhood, he said, he is best known as Liz Tory’s son.

The editor-in-chief of Pepel, an online outlet covering news in the southwestern Belgorod region, has reportedly been charged by Russia’s FSB security service with publicly justifying terrorism.

Russia’s Justice Ministry designated Parmenov a “foreign agent” in February 2025.

Parmenov founded Pepel in the fall of 2022, months after President Vladimir Putin ordered the full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

‘Still Just a Child’: The Russian Teenagers Fighting and Dying in Ukraine

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“Why?! A young soldier who’d never seen combat … Still just a child,” Svetlana Serezhkina, a resident of the Moscow region, wrote on social media after news emerged of the death of Alisher Svirin, the first publicly identified Russian soldier born in 2008 to be killed in the war in Ukraine.

“It’s such a shame they let him sign a [military] contract,” wrote another commenter, Andrei. Dozens of similar comments written by residents of Svirin’s hometown of Pavlovsky Posad appeared under the same post.

According to his obituary, Svirin had served as a machine gunner in a motorized rifle brigade. He was just 14 when President Vladimir Putin launched the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

More than 200 Russian soldiers aged 18 have been killed in the war, according to a count by the BBC’s Russian service and the exiled news outlet Mediazona.

The real number may be higher, as the tally excludes soldiers listed as missing and fighters from the Russian-backed separatist territories in Ukraine’s Donetsk and Luhansk regions.

The pro-war Orthodox Christian VKontakte group Synodik, which has about 50,000 followers, carries dozens of obituaries for soldiers who were teenagers when the invasion began.

In Orthodox Christianity, a synodik is a list of names of the living or the dead who are remembered in prayers and church services. Likewise, Synodik’s members pray for Russian soldiers like they would in a real church. The comments, mostly posted by women, are filled with Orthodox prayers, animated images of icons and cross emojis.

One recent post commemorates Kirill Tretyakov, who was born Feb. 7, 2007, near the end of Putin’s second presidential term. He had just turned 15 when the full-scale war began.

Tretyakov grew up in Kamensk, a settlement of about 6,300 people roughly 100 kilometers (62 miles) from Ulan-Ude, the capital of Russia’s republic of Buryatia. After completing ninth grade at a local school, he enrolled in a vocational college to study construction. He enjoyed kettlebell lifting and football in his free time.

Tretyakov signed a contract with the Defense Ministry six days after his 18th birthday, according to his obituary.

“From an early age, Kirill strove to be a worthy citizen of his country. On Feb. 13, 2025, Kirill made the courageous decision to defend the Fatherland and signed a contract with the Defense Ministry,” the group’s administrators wrote.

The Moscow Times found identical obituaries in a VKontakte group about Ulan-Ude and on the website of Buryatia’s Kabansky district. Tretyakov served as a combat engineer in an obstacle-laying company of the 656th Separate Guards Engineer-Sapper Battalion. He was killed on Sept. 22 of this year.

“Kirill strove to learn everything a real man should know. He was a brave, determined and fearless warrior who always put the common cause above his own,” his obituary reads.

A psychologist in Russia who works with adolescents said that many young people struggle to grasp the reality of going to war.

“The risk of being killed is very high, but young people don’t think about that,” the psychologist told The Moscow Times, speaking on condition of anonymity for safety reasons.

“When you’re young, you feel like life will last forever, like you’re invincible. Teenagers lack the social circle, the word-of-mouth networks, that could help them think critically about the war.”

That illusion of invulnerability is compounded by a broader devaluation of life in Russia, the psychologist said, referencing television propagandist Vladimir Solovyov’s comment on air that “life is greatly overrated.”

The number of “newly departed warriors,” as the Synodik group calls them, rises almost every day. The group also publishes obituaries for soldiers from Russian-occupied Ukrainian territories.

“Nikolai was a simple guy. He finished school in our village and was open, cheerful and kind,” the head of the Voikovsky rural settlement in annexed Crimea, said of Nikolai Ruzov, 19. Ruzov was six when Russia annexed the peninsula in 2014 and 14 when the full-scale war began.

Like all draft-age men without a deferment, Ruzov completed a year of compulsory military service before signing a contract with the Defense Ministry and going to fight in Ukraine’s Donetsk region. He was killed on Aug. 18, 2026, less than three months after his 19th birthday.

A Crimean news outlet illustrated his obituary with an apparently AI-generated image presented as a portrait of Ruzov that was later reposted by other outlets. The soldier in the image wears a U.S. flag patch on his shoulder. The Moscow Times analyzed the image using Hive’s AI-detection software, which returned a 100% confidence score that it was AI-generated.

Ruzov had been the commander of an assault squad, state media reported.

“Can someone explain how a 19-year-old boy ended up commanding an assault squad?!” one commenter, Maxim Borisov, asked.

Among those offering condolences online were mothers whose own sons had been killed in the war.

“May Nikolai enter the Kingdom of Heaven, and may you find the strength to learn to live with this irreplaceable loss. Our children don’t die … they are joining the Lord’s army. My boy has been there for four years,” Tamara Romanova wrote on the social network Odnoklassniki.

Ruzov was posthumously awarded Russia’s Order of Courage.

Another 19-year-old soldier, David Fedorov, died on Oct. 20 after being sent on an assault mission, according to his mother.

Police stopped Fedorov on the street in Cheboksary, the capital of the Volga republic of Chuvashia, in May and forced him to sign a contract with the Defense Ministry, his mother told the independent news outlet Astra. He was officially assigned to serve as a driver despite having no driver’s license, she added.

The deaths of young soldiers also feature in state efforts to promote the war in schools.

On Sept. 21, materials for “Important Conversations,” a series of state-mandated patriotic lessons, highlighted two soldiers killed in the invasion at ages 20 to 25. One was Nurmagomed Gadzhimagomedov, a soldier from Dagestan who died in the first month of the full-scale war.

“When I see examples of heroism like this young man ... I want to say: ‘I am a Lak, I am a Dagestani, I am a Chechen, Ingush, Russian, Tatar, Jew, Mordvin, Ossetian’,” Putin said after Gadzhimagomedov’s death in March 2022.

Footage of Putin’s remarks was included in a seven-minute video shown to schoolchildren, while pupils were also offered an interactive lesson about Gadzhimagomedov’s life.

Military personnel in their early 20s also visit schools to deliver patriotic lessons. The children’s psychologist said in-person meetings with returning soldiers could have an even stronger influence on teenagers.

“Soldiers are surrounded by an aura of heroism,” the psychologist said. “The person who comes to speak to you is alive. He fought for three years and came back. Now he’s addressing an audience and has a stable position in society.”

“Young people have little to lose,” the psychologist continued. “They don’t yet have families of their own or things they would find painful to give up, so war can seem appealing.”

Award-Winning Production Designer Dies at Moscow Hospital After Arrest for Treason

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Alexander Kondratov, an award-winning production designer for the popular Russian TV series “Liquidation,” has died at a Moscow hospital after being transferred from a detention center where he was awaiting trial on treason charges, the rights NGO Perviy Otdel said Monday.

Kondratov, 52, was reportedly hospitalized at the Botkin hospital last Friday for an undisclosed ailment. The cause of his death has not been made public.

Russian law enforcement authorities charged Kondratov with treason on Aug. 5, though he had been held for more than 70 days under so-called “carousel arrests,” a practice of consecutive detentions on charges of disorderly conduct.

Born and raised in Moscow, Kondratov moved to Kyiv, Ukraine, in 2012, where his family currently resides.

Russian border guards at Vnukovo International Airport detained him on May 5, when he arrived to renew his international passport.

His defense attorney Natalia Tikhonova said the treason charges, which carry a maximum sentence of life in prison for men, stemmed from “at least six” online posts Kondratov had made. The nature of those posts has not been disclosed.

Kondratov was best known for his work as an art and production designer for the 2007 post-World War II detective drama “Liquidation.” His work on the series earned him two of Russia’s highest honors in television production — the TEFI and Golden Eagle awards.

Perviy Otdel reported that Kondratov’s death marks at least the 11th known case of someone dying in Russian custody this year while standing trial on charges widely viewed as being politically motivated.

Crown withdraws criminal charges against pro-Palestinian protesters arrested at Montreal sit-in

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Defence lawyer Barbara Bedont says most of the protesters have resolved their cases through community service.Christopher Katsarov/The Canadian Press

The Crown on Monday dropped criminal charges against pro-Palestinian protesters arrested during a sit-in at a downtown Montreal bank in 2024, ending a legal battle that protesters say raises broader questions about the right to demonstrate.

The Crown withdrew the remaining criminal mischief charges against 14 protesters after they agreed to plead guilty to violating a municipal noise bylaw and pay $300 fines.

The defendants were among 44 people arrested in April, 2024 after staging a sit-in at a Scotiabank branch, demanding that the bank divest from Israeli weapons manufacturer Elbit Systems amid the war in Gaza.

“We want to make sure people can continue to fight things like this,” protester Eli Tareq said at a news conference Monday at Montreal municipal court. “If a citizen gets treated this way, what will happen when people do anything that’s deemed more violent?”

Defence lawyer Barbara Bedont said most of the other protesters had already resolved their cases through community service.

She said the remaining defendants had a strong constitutional case and warned that criminalizing peaceful sit-ins threatens freedom of expression and the right to protest.

The case comes to an end months after Scotiabank’s investment management subsidiary disclosed it had sold its remaining shares in Elbit Systems, which protesters celebrated as a victory for their campaign.

“We set out to pressure a Canadian bank to stop profiting from genocide, and in that case we won,” protester Harar Hall said at the press conference.

But protesters said the resolution of their criminal cases fell short of the accountability they had sought from police and the justice system.

Protester Mara Farrokh said the defendants had challenged their arrests under the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, arguing that police had violated their constitutional rights.

But she said they eventually agreed to the fines in order to put an end to the proceedings. She alleged that the judge overseeing their case was treating them unfairly.

“We knew that getting justice out of this court system wouldn’t be possible,” she said in an interview on Monday.

These punks became city councillors in B.C. – and brought their progressive politics to the table

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

D.O.A.'s Joe Keithley won the most votes of any aspiring Burnaby councillor during the municipal election on Oct. 17.Clayton Keithley/Supplied

Joe Keithley has lived most of his life by a simple slogan: Talk Minus Action Equals Zero. He’s never much cared for rhetoric; making the world a better place requires work. The philosophy has coloured his nearly 50-year career as the front man of D.O.A., the trailblazing, innovative and deeply progressive B.C. punk band that has played and organized hundreds of benefit concerts for anti-racist, anti-war, environmental and many other causes.

So when Keithley was first elected to Burnaby, B.C.’s city council as a member of the local Green Party in 2018 by a margin of just a couple hundred votes, he was happy to see that voters understood his ethos. “At first, they were like, ‘Oh, it’s a punk rock guy,’” he says, “but then were like, ‘No, no, he actually takes these issues seriously.’”

The 70-year-old has since helped push the city to affirm housing as a human right and to boost its supply of affordable units, including when old buildings are knocked down for new development. During B.C.’s municipal elections earlier this month, Keithley’s reputation as a councillor took him even further than his reputation as a musician. Running this time as a member of the social-democratic Burnaby Citizens Association, he won the most votes of any aspiring councillor.

Keithley, pictured in 2014, formed D.O.A. in the late seventies.Clayton Keithley/Supplied

He wasn’t, remarkably, even the only punk-rock front man in the Lower Mainland to pull off that feat. Sean Orr, vocalist of the hardcore-punk band NEEDS – and a longtime civic-affairs writer, housing activist and artist – took home the most votes of any Vancouver council candidate on Oct. 17 as a member of the Coalition of Progressive Electors, or COPE, party.

Punk rock and progressive politics have been intertwined since the Ramones and Sex Pistols first brought the genre to the masses in the seventies. The connection only deepened at the turn of the eighties, when the genre’s rougher, tougher second wave more openly and deeply wrapped inclusivity into its scenes. It may have taken a few decades since D.O.A. helped christen that more aggressive wave and its descendants as hardcore punk, but the progressive politics of the genre are now firmly entrenched in two of the biggest municipal governments in B.C. (The provincial government is another matter, however, with the B.C. Conservatives winning a majority in last weekend’s vote.)

Vancouver elects Canada’s first Green mayor

“I really like the metaphor of the mosh pit – if somebody falls down, you pick them up,” Orr says. “Extrapolating that out to politics, into the world in general, it’s like the spirit of looking out for each other and protecting each other.”

His band NEEDS, which is now dormant, made both its politics and its sense of humour quite clear with several releases last decade. Its song titles include The Only Good Condo Is a Dead Condo and Eat the Rich... People’s Leftovers, but also We Don’t Know Why We Are Protesting Is Why We Are Protesting.

“I’ve written songs about limitations around punk rock and change, in terms of how it kind of gets co-opted by the big machine,” Orr says, acknowledging that there are “limitations to being a city councillor as well.”

Orr, 48, felt limited in his ability to push an agenda after first being elected to Vancouver council in a 2025 by-election. Not only was he COPE’s only councillor for that truncated term, he had a terse-at-best relationship with now-outgoing mayor Ken Sim after Sim falsely accused Orr, a supporter of harm-reduction approaches for drug users, of distributing illegal drugs. While Sim later apologized, Orr has since launched a defamation suit against him.

Despite being on the outside, Orr made serious efforts to get his voice heard in his brief first year-and-change on council. He co-introduced a successful motion for landlords to be fined for preventing tenants from running air conditioners; fought for more library funding and seven-day-a-week service; and, like Keithley in Burnaby, pressed to recognize housing as a human right.

He said that being part of COPE, now part of a largely progressive Vancouver council, keeps him grounded. “We’re a party that is connected to a lot of movements on the ground, and we are super aware of the limitations of we can do at City Hall – and that we have to kind of have that door open to those movements.”

Keithley ran for politics a half-dozen times before winning a Burnaby council seat. Before that, he was mostly known by a stage name that does not adhere to The Globe and Mail’s editorial style guide. And while he still performs with the band between council sessions, often in sleeveless shirts, these days you might catch him walking around Burnaby City Hall in a suit jacket – or at least a T-shirt with sleeves.

It’s only a slight lifestyle change for a man whose band once issued a record with a famed photo of Ojibwa warrior Brad Larocque staring down a soldier during the Oka Crisis on its cover, and which has also put out albums titled Something Better Change and, yes, Talk - Action = 0. (D.O.A. also released a song about American assimilation called 51st State – in 1987.)

From the archive: What happens when an old punk rocker enters politics

Keithley’s political career has caught the attention of everyone from documentarian Scott Crawford – whose film, also titled Something Better Change, chronicles the musician’s 2022 council campaign – to Beto O’Rourke, the Texas Democrat who came up in the same music scene as members of At the Drive-In and The Mars Volta. Punk didn’t exist when Keithley first got into music; he made it his own. “These people stood up and said something,” he said of his contemporaries. So did he.

Though both their past and present careers overlapped thematically, Orr and Keithley have only met briefly in passing. But the geographic coincidence of both winning the most votes among council candidates in their respective cities nonetheless highlights the way progressive politics emerges organically in the Vancouver area.

“If you look at Vancouver, we’re kind of isolated from the rest of the country, and we’ve prided ourselves on this long history of activism,” Orr says. “Look at Greenpeace, look at Adbusters, look at all these organizations that are now international – that started in Vancouver, because of that spirit of fighting back against institutions and power.”

Pushed to pursue safe degrees, students are shunning the humanities. At what cost?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Over the past three decades, the share of undergraduate degrees in health sciences, engineering, business, computer science and related fields jumped to more than 60 per cent from 42 per cent.Tuan Minh Nguyen/The Globe and Mail

Recent university graduates are still struggling in Canada’s unforgiving job market, with post-pandemic unemployment rates among young bachelor’s degree holders sitting at levels not seen since the early 1990s.

These struggles aren’t because young people are making irresponsible career choices; in fact, our graduates’ pragmatic mindset may be making the situation worse.

The youth employment numbers are sobering. Despite a rising population and the continuing retirement of the baby boomers, the number of job vacancies requiring a bachelor’s degree has fallen by 6 per cent in the last year, with 4.9 unemployed people for every job requiring a university degree. It is also taking young people longer to find a job, as the time they stay unemployed has nearly doubled since 2023.

One common misconception is that young people are choosing majors with little practical value. Florida Governor Ron DeSantis has spoken about young people taking degrees in “zombie studies,” while Ontario Premier Doug Ford told students worried about Ontario Student Assistance Program (OSAP) cuts that they’re “picking basket-weaving courses and there’s not too many baskets being sold out there.”

These criticisms aren’t just aimed at politicians; they’re aimed at them for creating systems that set young people up to fail. Mike Rowe, host of the TV series Dirty Jobs, is one such critic, lamenting that “we are lending money we don’t have to kids who can’t pay it back to train them for jobs that no longer exist.”

The data, however, tells a different story: Today’s young adults are taking a more pragmatic, career-oriented approach to education than previous generations.

Boomers and Generation X were far more likely than today’s grads to choose majors that politicians and TV hosts would dismiss as frivolous. In the early 1990s, when the latter half of Generation X started university, 13 per cent of bachelor’s degrees in Canada were awarded in the humanities. By 2024, that share had fallen to just 5 per cent.

Over the past 30 years, the share of undergraduate degrees granted in health sciences, engineering, business, computer science and related fields jumped to more than 60 per cent from 42 per cent, with no sign of slowing.

Often, the argument is not that young people lack pragmatism because they choose the wrong major while in university, but that they are being impractical by going to university at all, with Mike Rowe passionately arguing that young people would be better served by learning a trade.

Amid high youth unemployment, loss of early-career opportunities has lasting impact

However, given alarmingly low apprenticeship completion rates, young people may pragmatically choose programs where they have a higher chance of success.

The brutal reality is that our kids know they are entering a world where they cannot afford to make mistakes or explore their interests. Choosing the wrong major might lead to years of unemployment or underemployment, and not earning enough right away can put home ownership forever out of reach in much of the country.

This pragmatism starts early, as a high-school grade average of 96 is no longer a guaranteed entry into some university programs. No university in Ontario today would take a chance on someone with a high-school transcript as poor as mine was 30 years ago.

Once in university, students choose courses and majors to maximize their chances of getting a well-paying job upon graduation, which is typically interpreted as choosing a “practical” major and selecting courses based on the likelihood of earning a high grade. This safe approach prevents young people from taking risks and exploring their interests.

The undergraduate courses that have benefited me most in my career were challenging advanced philosophy courses, where I had little prior knowledge and often received relatively low grades because of it.

Students struggle to land career-boosting summer jobs after opportunities ‘fell off a cliff’

Like many virtues, pragmatism taken to excess becomes a vice. Rewarding students for taking subjects they already understand, and courses where they are likely to earn high grades, is like going to the gym and training muscles that are already strong, at weights that minimize the chance of failure.

Perhaps the worst part is that youth have been misinformed about which fields are safe options. Well-meaning parents and guidance counsellors have steered a growing share of students into just a handful of majors, such as business consulting and computer science, even after former president Barack Obama said everyone should learn to code. Ironically, these are now two of the most at-risk fields owing to the rise of AI.

We have done our young people a disservice by insisting they make safe, pragmatic educational choices. Pundits and educators have a terrible track record of predicting which careers will be most in demand, and steering students into a handful of occupation-focused majors increases risk if one of those occupations suddenly declines in demand.

Instead, young people should be encouraged to explore a wealth of their options and take risks, including the occasional course in zombie studies.

Mike Moffatt is the founding director of the Missing Middle Initiative and co-host of the Missing Middle podcast.

Canada backs UN push to treat environmental crime as serious organized crime

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Negotiations on the new protocol under the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime are expected to take years.Jeenah Moon/Reuters

Canada has voted to begin negotiations on a UN protocol that would treat environmental crime, including wildlife trafficking, as a serious organized crime.

It was one of 83 countries to vote in favour of the resolution on Friday, which establishes a committee to draft the new protocol under the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime. The protocol would also target timber, minerals, precious metals and waste, and illegal mining.

“Canada recognizes that crimes that affect the environment represent an increasingly concerning form of transnational organized crime,” said Rachael Dolan, a spokesperson for Global Affairs Canada, in a statement.

In many countries, including Canada, much environmental crime falls below the “serious crime” bar of the UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, which applies to offences punishable by four years or more in prison. That limits how readily the convention can be used for extradition, mutual legal assistance and joint investigations, the tools countries rely on to pursue criminal networks across borders.

In Canada, wildlife trafficking clears the bar, with a maximum of five years. Four of the country’s six main environmental protection laws, including the Fisheries Act and the Canadian Environmental Protection Act, do not, according to a UN legislative review published last year.

Canada’s baby eel fishery shows what is at stake. As The Globe reported in March, elvers fetch about $2,800 a kilogram, which has fuelled poaching and illegal trade. Last year, cracking down on illegal fishing, federal officers checked shipments at four major airports, seized 225 kilograms and made more than 100 arrests. But those arrests fell under the Fisheries Act, whose penalties are below the convention’s four-year threshold.

The Decibel podcast: Why Canada struggles to stop the illegal wildlife trade

The resolution, led by Brazil, passed in Vienna at the convention’s 13th Conference of the Parties. It cleared the required two-thirds majority, with 87 per cent voting in favour. Twelve countries, including the United States, voted against the resolution and 27 abstained.

It is the first time in 25 years that parties to the convention have agreed to develop a new protocol. Since the convention was created in 2000, it has had only three protocols, on human trafficking, migrant smuggling and firearms, the last adopted in May, 2001.

Alejandra Silva, a spokesperson for the UN Office on Drugs and Crime, said in a statement that the vote signals countries’ continued support for the convention but also “the need for the convention to be supplemented by specific provisions dealing with these forms of crimes that affect the environment, which are one of the most lucrative forms of transnational organized crime.”

Sheldon Jordan, a former director-general of wildlife enforcement at Environment Canada and a former Interpol official who co-founded the Canadian Environmental Crime Research Network, said the protocol would give enforcement agencies and prosecutors new tools to collaborate across borders.

“Criminals who violate environmental and natural resource laws will find it harder to hide behind borders,” he said, speaking from Vienna.

A Globe and Mail and Pulitzer Center investigation in June found that penalties for wildlife trafficking in Canada rarely match the crime. Most of the cases Environment and Climate Change Canada has publicly reported since 2011 ended in fines under $50,000, and fewer than one in eight included any custody. By comparison, a first-time courier importing fentanyl can expect eight to 18 years.

Mr. Jordan has described Canada’s environmental enforcement framework as more formidable on paper than in practice. “It’s just a scarecrow in many cases,” he said.

Ms. Dolan said Canada supported the resolution “in recognition of the serious and growing threat posed by environmental crime,” and called for “judicious consideration” of the resources needed to set up a negotiating committee.

“We are committed to working with others for a cost-effective, pragmatic and inclusive process, with the participation of relevant stakeholders including civil society and Indigenous peoples, recognizing they can be particularly affected by these crimes,” said Ms. Dolan.

U.S. amendments that would have weakened and delayed the talks were all voted down. They would have allowed a non-binding instrument instead of a protocol, tied the talks to available funding and postponed the work until the Convention’s lengthy implementation review is complete.

Talks cannot begin until the UN General Assembly approves funding for the negotiating committee from the UN’s regular budget, a process that runs through several budget committees, Ms. Silva said.

Negotiations on the protocol’s scope and terms are expected to take years. Canada’s federal wildlife penalties have not been updated since 2009.

Eric Bauza, the Toronto-born voice actor who breathed fresh life into some of Hollywood’s most iconic cartoon characters, has died at 46.

His family announced his death in a brief post on his official social media channels.

The Los Angeles Medical Examiner says he died of hypertensive cardiovascular disease on Sunday.

Bauza, who was born and raised in Scarborough, was known as a modern successor to Mel Blanc, who originated the voices of Bugs Bunny and Daffy Duck.

Bauza carried the torch in recent years. In 2018, he became the first non-white actor to voice Bugs Bunny. He went on to voice Bugs, Daffy and more in Looney Tunes Cartoons, which ran on HBO Max for six seasons starting in 2020.

He reprised the roles numerous times, including in the film Coyote vs. Acme, which came out this summer, years after it was filmed.

It almost didn’t see the light of day after Warner Bros. canned the finished film as a tax write-off; but a campaign to release it, led in part by Bauza, convinced the production company to put the project up for sale.

He pushed the hashtag #ReleaseCoyoteVsACME on social media, and at the 2024 Annie Awards made the case from the podium in the voice of Daffy Duck.

“He’s the go-to cartoon character above anyone else when it comes to begging and pleading,” Bauza said while promoting the film. “It seemed appropriate.”

Eventually, an independent distributor bought the film and released it to glowing reviews.

A lifelong lover of cartoons, Bauza described the surreality of becoming a pillar of the scene.

“We grew up watching this stuff as it was happening. I was buying those Transformers as they were being made,” he told The Canadian Press in 2022.

“Fast forward, and I’m auditioning for the reboots — I buy a toy that I’m voicing, which is a Transformer that my son’s now into, so it’s kind of like a cat eating its tail.”

Bauza’s lengthy list of credits also includes SpongeBob SquarePants and Bob’s Burgers.

Comedian Kristen Schaal, who voices Louise Belcher on Bob’s Burgers, paid tribute to Bauza on Instagram on Monday.

“When I found out he was coming to a Bob’s Burgers table read it honestly felt like we had been blessed by the Elvis of voice actors,” she wrote.

“I obviously didn’t know him that well. I only knew his immense talent.”

Bauza was a regular on the convention circuit, and spoke on a panel at Lightbox Expo in Pasadena, Calif., the day before he died.

Jerry Beck, an animation historian, moderated that panel and wrote about it on the website Animation Scoop after Bauza’s death.

“He was vibrant and alive,” Beck wrote. “To demonstrate a classic piece of Looney Tunes comedy he performed as Bugs Bunny doing the square dance call from ‘Hillbilly Hare’, while the classic cartoon ran on a large screen beside us, to an packed room full of hopeful artists, cartoonists and budding voice over performers.”

In an online tribute, fellow voice actor Josh Keaton said Bauza was a gifted and humble performer.

“He let his talent speak for itself and made everyone feel like he was their biggest fan,” Keaton said on social media.

“He will always be a legend but I’m devastated that I’ll never be able to give him a hug, or hear him joke or see him smile again.”
