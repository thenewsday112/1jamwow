Netflix relocated me to Japan. After I left the job, I found a new way to stay.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This as-told-to essay is based on a conversation with Matthew Kelson, now 43, who was transferred by Netflix from Los Angeles to Tokyo and is now the founder of CineYama, an independent cinema in Uenohara, Yamanashi Prefecture, Japan. His words have been edited for length and clarity.

Japan isn't new to me. In middle school, I spent two weeks living with a host family as part of an exchange program. I returned a couple of times as an adult.

I was working at Netflix in Los Angeles when the company relocated me to Japan in 2020. I was based in Tokyo for two years before moving to Uenohara, a small town about an hour and a half by train from Tokyo. I still spent time in the capital for occasional meetings and traveled extensively across Asia for screenings. Otherwise, I worked remotely from home. After a company restructuring, I left Amazon in 2024.

I wanted to stay in Japan. My daughter was six months old when my wife and I moved, and my son was born three years later. We thought it was a great place for them to grow up. I started thinking about how I could utilize my expertise in cinema and give back to the community.

While scouting for locations, I came across an abandoned kindergarten. In 2024, I started plans to turn the old school into a cinema. And in December, I got my permanent residency in Japan through the highly skilled professional fast-track program. A year later, I formed my company and spent another year renovating before opening a cinema in March.

I spent most of my childhood in Michigan, in the Detroit area. My father was a professor of international business. He traveled for work often. It got me curious about the world around me.

At 23, I came across the Detroit Film Center and took a filmmaking course. A year later, I became a teacher and worked as a projectionist there. Through that, I started working at an independent art theater in the Detroit suburbs. I started as an usher, then became the theater manager.

Within the two years of working there, I fell in love with the film world. At 25, I renovated an old school's auditorium into a theater in Michigan using my savings. The theater ran for two years, but after disputes with the landlord, it closed.

I was looking for a fresh start, so in 2011, I moved to Los Angeles. At first, I knocked on the doors of all the film distributors I'd worked with until I met some people at an independent film distribution company. I joined as an intern and worked my way up. I landed a job on Netflix's creative team two years later.

Getting the permit to renovate the abandoned kindergarten was a long process. I had to negotiate with officials at city hall and the residents about how I'd use the building, establish the company, deal with fire and health regulations, and construction in a language I don't speak fluently.

I learned that pushing harder is not necessarily what gets things done in Japan. It was about patience, building trust, and showing people that I was serious and that the project wasn't going to disappear in six months.

There were days when I wondered why I had chosen to make my life so difficult. But every milestone, from getting the building, the inspection, installing the projector, and finally opening the doors, made me more determined.

When the cinema first opened, the elderly residents seemed cautious. After a preview, they told me they were impressed to see the place cleaned up and put back into use. It's important for me to have screenings that kids and families can come to and enjoy the space.

Tickets cost 1,500 Japanese yen, or about $9.50. We offer a discount to seniors and residents. I stick with showing content that is harder to find elsewhere, so that it gives viewers a perspective on other ways of life and culture.

It's mostly family-run. I manage the operations, and my wife helps out sometimes. My kids stamp the tickets when they're around.

Opening the theater in the US was different from doing it in Japan. I was in my 20s, had little money, and was just figuring things out in Detroit. The one in Japan came after 15 years of working in film distribution, creative production, and international screenings.

It's been exciting living over here, having the cinema, and meeting people from all over the world. While life in the US would've been much easier, I like the challenges in Japan. That's why I was at Netflix for so long, because it wasn't an easy job either. I learned life in hard mode.

Australian murderer loses bid to eat Vegemite in prison

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Andre McKechnie argues the Vegemite ban in Victoria's jails denies him the right to "enjoy his culture"

A convicted murderer serving a life sentence in Australia has lost his bid to overturn a ban on prisoners consuming Vegemite - the salty yeast-based spread that has become a national symbol.

Andre McKechnie argued that the ban, in place since 2006, denied him the right to "enjoy his culture as an Australian".

Authorities in the state of Victoria say inmates may use the strong-smelling paste to disguise contraband substances or to brew alcohol behind bars.

And on Monday, a Supreme Court judge rejected McKechnie's appeal, saying the prisoner's "cultural rights are limited primarily by the fact he has been sentenced to a term of imprisonment".

In his lawsuit against Victoria's Department of Justice and Corrections, the agency which manages the state's prisons, McKechnie, 54, said the ban on the spread breached law requiring prisons to provide food that allowed inmates to maintain their health and well-being but the judge said that had not been proven.

McKechnie was handed a life sentence for stabbing a property developer to death in Queensland in the 1990s. He served part of his sentence in Queensland before being transferred to Victoria.

During the hearing, the court heard evidence from Corrections Victoria that the smell of vegemite can distract sniffer dogs trying to detect drugs being brought into prison.

The product is also banned in jails in Queensland, South Australia and Tasmania.

A much-loved breakfast spread for some, Vegemite has acquired controversial accolades since it first hit shop shelves in Australia in 1923.

The thick, brown spread was invented in the Victorian capital of Melbourne more than a century ago as an alternative to the British spread Marmite.

It sparked an international dispute in April last year when an Australian cafe owner in Canada was told to remove jars of the condiment from his shelves as it did not comply with local health regulations.

Australia's Prime Minister Anthony Albanese intervened in the row, saying it was "rather odd" that Canada allowed the sale of Vegemite's "rubbish" rival product Marmite. The cafe owner was later allowed to continue selling the spread.

Praca hybrydowa to nowa rzeczywistość. Ale nie wszystkim się to podoba

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Aktualizacja: 26.10.2026 07:29 Publikacja: 26.10.2026 05:05

W większości dużych firm obowiązuje model pracy hybrydowej

Pomimo nagłaśnianych w mediach przymusowych powrotów do biur znanych zachodnich korporacji praca hybrydowa (czyli częściowo zdalna) jest już zjawiskiem, które na stałe zagościło na rynku pracy. Również w Polsce ostatnie lata przyniosły umocnienie modelu hybrydowego – ocenili eksperci Colliers Define, omawiając w miniony piątek tegoroczną edycję jej raportu „Hybrid and beyond”.

Według badania, które latem objęło 270 członków zarządów i dyrektorów dużych firm zatrudniających głównie pracowników umysłowych, w 93 proc. z nich funkcjonują hybrydowe modele pracy. Wprawdzie w ostatnich latach widać stopniowy wzrost udziału stałej pracy w biurze – z 1 proc. w 2024 r. do 5 proc. w tym roku, lecz nadal jest on niewielki. Jeszcze mniejszy jest jednak udział firm pracujących tylko zdalnie, który zmalał teraz do 1 proc. (z 3 proc. przed rokiem). Zdaniem Doroty Osieckiej, partnera i dyrektora w Colliers Define, deklarowana i oczekiwana przez pracowników potrzeba elastyczności jest na tyle silna, że firmy nie zastanawiają się już, czy pracować hybrydowo, lecz jak to robić i jednocześnie uniknąć ryzyk, które niosą ze sobą praca w rozproszeniu.

Podczas gdy większość menedżerów dostrzega pozytywny wpływ pracy hybrydowej na dobrostan i work-life balance pracowników (73 proc.), na ich utrzymanie (62 proc.), a także na rekrutację nowych ludzi (64 proc.), to zdaniem ponad połowy badanych praca zdalna negatywnie wpływa na poczucie przynależności do zespołu i na przepływ informacji w firmie.

Prawie co drugi menedżer dostrzega też związane z nią problemy we współpracy w firmie. Jak zauważył Grzegorz Rajca, zastępca dyrektora w Colliers Define, pracując w rozproszeniu, mamy mniej interakcji z osobami, z którymi na co dzień nie współpracujemy, a które wcześniej spotykaliśmy w biurze. Dwukrotnie więcej badanych (40 proc.) dostrzega też negatywny wpływ pracy zdalnej na bieżące zarządzanie zespołami niż pozytywny wpływ. Z kolei ponad dwukrotną przewagę pozytywnych opinii (47 proc.) widać w ocenie wydajności osób pracujących zdalnie.

Wprawdzie dla większości pracowników umysłowych praca zdalna nie jest idealnym rozwiązaniem, ale tylko niewielka część z nich akceptuje pełny powró...

Mieszane odczucia top menedżerów wobec pracy zdalnej przekładają się na tendencję do ograniczania jej zakresu. W ostatnich trzech latach utrwala się zrównoważony model hybrydowy z 2-3 dniami pracy z biura (stosuje go 58 proc. badanych firm), podczas gdy spada popularność modelu z przewagą pracy zdalnej (z 25 proc. w 2024 r. do 15 proc. w tym roku), a także modelu z przewagą pracy z biura. Jak zaznacza Dorota Osiecka, coraz więcej firm (prawie co piąta) stawia na zróżnicowane modele pracy dopasowane do specyfiki poszczególnych działów i zespołów.

Jednocześnie od trzech lat maleje odsetek badanych pracodawców, którzy zachęcają pracowników do powrotu do biur – obecnie robi to 47 proc. firm wobec 64 proc. w 2023 r. Argumentują, że zależy im na większej integracji pracowników, na poprawie wymiany informacji i współpracy, a także na wzroście efektywności. Jednak 84 proc. firm napotyka opór pracowników przed przejściem na stacjonarny tryb pracy.

Zdaniem Doroty Osieckiej, w sytuacji, gdy większość badanych godzi się na zatrudnianie zdalnych pracowników, powszechny powrót do biur jest mało prawdopodobny. 34 proc. firm zatrudnia osoby, które mogą pracować zdalnie z dowolnego miejsca w Polsce, a tylko 30 proc. akceptuje wyłącznie kandydatów gotowych pracować w biurze.Jednak odsetek tych drugich rośnie – w 2024 r. wynosił 25 proc.

Jak zaznaczali eksperci Colliers Define, podczas gdy część z nich cierpi na tzw. choroby hybrydowe – w tym chaos informacyjny, zanik relacji i współpracy między pracownikami – to spora grupa sprawnie funkcjonuje w tym modelu. W ustaleniu, które z ich działań mają największy pozytywny wpływ, pomogło badanie przeprowadzone wśród ponad 1200 pracowników pod kątem pięciu wymiarów zdrowia hybrydowego (praca zespołowa, wsparcie organizacji, zaangażowanie w pracę, relacje w firmie i równowaga między pracą a życiem prywatnym).

Okazało się, że w rozproszonych zespołach najlepiej sprawdzają się regularne informacje zwrotne od bezpośrednich przełożonych (wdrożyło je 78 proc. badanych firm), a także dwie praktyki związane z docenianiem ludzi: zespołowe docenianie i niefinansowe metody doceniania, które stosuje dwie trzecie pracodawców. Natomiast tylko nieco ponad jedna piąta firm ma wdrożone reguły i praktyki umożliwiające pracę głęboką oraz rozwiązania zmniejszające obciążenie spotkaniami online.

Trump dials up Islamophobia against Abdul El-Sayed at Michigan Senate rally

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Al Jazeera looks at the key takeaways from the US president’s campaign event in Michigan ahead of the midterm vote.

Port Huron, Michigan – United States President Donald Trump has sharpened his anti-Muslim attacks against Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed, going so far as to suggest that Christmas would be cancelled if the progressive doctor won.

Trump visited Michigan on Sunday to shore up support for Republican candidates in the Midwestern state ahead of the midterm elections on November 3.

But from his podium, he levelled unfounded allegations against El-Sayed, including calling him a “jihadist”.

“He would be the only senator that will have to go through a metal detector before he’s allowed to go into the Senate,” Trump said of El-Sayed.

The president, whose popularity has been slipping in part due to the US-Israel war on Iran, spoke for nearly two hours to hundreds of supporters.

His remarks largely focused on Michigan’s Senate election, which is one of only about seven toss-up races for the upper chamber of Congress.

El-Sayed faces Republican candidate Mike Rogers for the state’s open Senate seat, and the race is considered crucial for Republicans to retain control of the 100-member Senate.

If Rogers wins, he would flip a seat currently held by retiring Democratic Senator Gary Peters, leaving Democrats with the difficult task of flipping five other seats to regain control of the chamber.

Currently, Republicans have a 53-seat majority in the Senate. Only 35 Senate positions are up for election in 2026.

Here are five key takeaways from the Republican event.

While Trump has repeatedly targeted El-Sayed’s Muslim identity in recent weeks, including by misrepresenting the Democrat’s name as “Mohammed”, the US president took his rhetoric to new heights on Sunday.

Throughout the speech, Trump returned to themes related to El-Sayed’s Muslim faith, falsely accusing the candidate of wanting to impose Islamic law in the US.

“So Abdul… also wants our country to go to Sharia law, and if he ever pulls that off, he should be the first one that’s tried. I’ll be a witness,” Trump said, apparently imagining El-Sayed being tried in a religious court.

Several times during the rally, some attendees blurted out “traitor” when El-Sayed’s name was mentioned.

“I’m here to keep a jihadist Muslim Brotherhood member from becoming a United States senator,” Trump said.

El-Sayed is a US-born citizen and former public health official who has pledged to uphold the Constitution. There is no evidence that he was ever a member of the Muslim Brotherhood or any foreign organisation.

Since Trump first ran for president during the 2016 election cycle, he has weaponised anti-Muslim bigotry for political gain.

In 2015, for instance – near the beginning of his first successful campaign – Trump said he would support “a total and complete shutdown of Muslims entering the United States”.

Less than two years later, Trump used the presidency to sign a short-lived executive order barring travel from multiple Muslim-majority countries, what became known as the “Muslim ban”.

In Sunday’s speech, Trump appealed once more to fears of Muslim immigration.

“If Abdul and the Democrats win, they will open the floodgates and invite hordes of jihadists to our shores, creating a nightmare of violence and terror until you cannot even have a Christmas,” Trump said.

El-Sayed has previously largely dismissed the anti-Muslim rhetoric against him as an attempt to create “distractions”, saying he is focused more on domestic issues, including lowering the cost of living and expanding access to healthcare.

With Trump’s approval ratings plummeting, some Republican candidates in tight races have kept their distance from the president.

Rogers has previously called for an end to the Iran war and expressed reservations about Trump’s tariff campaign, urging an end to the trade war with Canada, which borders Michigan.

But Rogers attended Sunday’s rally and took the stage alongside Trump in a display of mutual support.

A survey by the research firm Marketing Resource Group suggested earlier this month that only 35 percent of voters in Michigan view Trump positively.

Rogers set the tone for the anti-Muslim attacks against El-Sayed earlier in the rally, also claiming the Democrat is connected to the Muslim Brotherhood, a political movement in Egypt designated as a “terrorist” group by the US this year.

As a congressman, however, Rogers voted in 2012 to continue US aid to Egypt under Muslim Brotherhood President Mohamed Morsi.

In Sunday’s speech, Rogers also tried to paint El-Sayed as part of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), but unlike other progressives, El-Sayed is not a member of the left-wing group.

“This DSA-affiliated candidate is also taking a lot of money from organizations that support the Muslim Brotherhood, Hamas and Hezbollah,” Rogers said, without offering evidence.

All three groups are designated as “terrorist” organisations in the US, making it illegal to provide any material support to them.

On Sunday, the average price of a gallon (3.8 litres) of petrol in Michigan was $4.83, up from $2.91 before the start of the war on Iran.

Tehran has restricted passage through the Strait of Hormuz in response to the war, sending energy prices soaring.

Trump initially promised the conflict would only last four to six weeks.

But nearly eight months after the US and Israel launched the war on February 28, the conflict is showing no signs of concluding.

On Sunday, Trump said he has no regrets about the war.

“I made that decision, and I have no second guess,” Trump told the crowd.

The villagers who first spotted the five baby orangutans in a casuarina forest in India in early September were puzzled by their reddish hair and unfamiliar faces.

The three males and two females, aged six to eight months — were left on a plantation in eastern India’s Odisha, over 2,700 kilometers (1,700 miles) from their native rainforests in Borneo and Sumatra, evidence of a growing illicit trade of apes being trafficked into India.

They're now at an Indian zoo, where keepers reportedly have been playing classical music to soothe them.

Wild orangutans are native only to the islands of Borneo and Sumatra, where they sustain rainforests by spreading seeds across vast areas, allowing trees to regenerate and store carbon that would otherwise contribute to global warming. The species is critically endangered, threatened by severe habitat loss. There are about 119,500 orangutans left in the wild, according to conservation organization World Wildlife Fund.

The global illegal wildlife trade is estimated to be worth $34 billion annually, according to a 2026 study by National University of Singapore researchers, and India is emerging as an important market. Experts say seizures reflect a fraction of a booming trade in exotic or non-native animals sold to wealthy Indian collectors.

India’s wildlife laws were mainly designed to protect native species such as elephants, rhinoceroses and tigers from being trafficked overseas. Gaps in controls on imported animals complicate efforts to trace the origins of exotic wildlife.

In 2022, two juvenile Sumatran orangutans were found in boxes at a checkpoint between the northeastern states of Assam and Mizoram. Another was rescued in Mizoram in 2023, and a young orangutan was found in an apartment near Mumbai in November 2024. At least 23 gibbons were seized at Indian airports in 2025, according to an analysis by nonprofit Wildlife Trust of India. In May 2026, Egyptian authorities seized three India-bound, sedated baby chimpanzees.

“India has become the number one destination for the live trade of primates,” said Sinan Serhadli, a wildlife trafficking expert at the Orangutan Information Centre in Indonesia’s North Sumatra province.

India’s exotic wildlife trade and Southeast Asian trafficking routes

Wealthy collectors who prize rare animals as “status symbols” are driving the trade in trafficked animals from Southeast Asia, a regional hub for trade in species from elsewhere, said Uttara Mendiratta, a wildlife trafficking researcher in India.

Expanding tourism has led to more direct flights to India through cities like Bangkok and Kuala Lumpur, giving traffickers routes that are faster, albeit riskier, than overland border crossings.

“The profit margins are so high that they can afford to lose a few (animals) and still make a profit,” Mendiratta said.

Questions also surround legal trade. Most commercial trade in orangutans is banned, but exceptions exist for captive-bred animals. India imported 41 orangutans between 2021 and 2024, according to CITES, the global treaty regulating trade in endangered wildlife.

Sinan Serhadli, a wildlife trafficking expert at Indonesia’s Orangutan Information Centre, questioned whether the animals were legitimately bred in captivity.

“It just shows the loopholes. And ... corruption as well,” he said.

The CITES Secretariat declined to comment, saying the matter was under review

Loopholes in India’s wildlife laws enable wildlife trafficking

Concerned about diseases spreading from animals to people during the COVID-19 pandemic, India allowed people to register their exotic animals in 2020 without proving their origins.

They can report births, deaths and breeding of non-native species through an online portal. But the government lacks enough staff and expertise to adequately inspect collections and identify global species. The system also leaves room for trafficked animals to be misrepresented as registered ones, Mendiratta said.

India tightened its wildlife laws in 2022, giving authorities clearer powers to seize illegally traded exotic animals and prosecute traffickers. But by then, it already had “opened the floodgates,” she said.

India’s exotic pet trade “sits at the crossroads of biodiversity loss, unbridled community demand, and organized criminal activity,” said a 2026 report by Humane World for Animals India.

India's forest ministry didn't respond to AP's request for comment.

Indonesia renews push to repatriate orangutans from India

Indonesia’s Ministry of Forestry said it was taking “active steps” to bring home the five baby orangutans found in Odisha, sending experts to verify their origins and assess their health.

Their mothers were likely killed, traumatizing infants that normally would depend on them for up to nine years.

Conservationists have successfully returned orangutans to Indonesia from Thailand, but so far only one has been repatriated from India.

When one orangutan was transferred last year to a private wildlife facility known as Vantara, run by billionaire Mukesh Ambani’s family, India’s federal wildlife officials and India’s CITES authority were not informed, a federal wildlife official to New Delhi wrote in a report.

Officials concerned over the orangutan's care at a local public zoo ordered it transferred to Vantara, which describes itself as a wildlife rescue and rehabilitation center. The orangutan was treated for a respiratory infection and pneumonia before it was returned to Indonesia on Oct. 19 after 19 months at the private facility.

India’s CITES authority didn’t respond to AP’s request for comment.

Questions over the world’s rarest great ape in Indian zoo

Vantara, a 3,500-acre wildlife facility in western India, has over 150,000 animals and over 2,000 different species.

It has faced international scrutiny over its animal acquisitions. In November 2025, CITES questioned shipments to Vantara that included snow leopards from Germany, a bonobo said to have been bred in Iraq and a gorilla reportedly from Haiti. The primates are not native to those countries.

India’s Supreme Court cleared Vantara of wildlife trafficking and animal mistreatment allegations in 2025.

In September, 27 wildlife conservation and animal welfare organizations issued a letter urging the facility to end imports of wild animals, raising concerns about endangered species and animal welfare.

The groups said they had “never encountered anything comparable to the scale and diversity” of Vantara’s imports, citing more than 60,000 animals acquired in a short period.

One controversial acquisition, according to Vantara's 2024-25 inventory, is a Tapanuli orangutan, the world’s rarest great ape. Recognized as a distinct species only in 2017, it's only found in a small area of Indonesia’s Sumatra island, where 716 remain in the wild.

“The Tapanuli orangutan is hovering just above extinction,” said Serhadli, whose organization is one of the few that works with the species. The one at Vantara would be the only Tapanuli orangutan in captivity.

CITES trade data shows that the animal left Indonesia in 2024 and arrived in India via the United Arab Emirates.

“That can’t happen legally,” Serhadli said.

Indonesia’s Ministry of Forestry said it was taking “seriously” reports that a Tapanuli orangutan might be outside the country. It said it couldn't confirm the animal’s species, origins or how it got to India. An investigation would require checking permits and transfer records, along with scientific testing, the ministry said.

Neither Vantara nor Indonesia’s CITES authority responded to requests for comment from AP.

Milko reported from Islamabad, Pakistan. Associated Press journalist Edna Tarigan contributed from Jakarta, Indonesia.

По словам Ашрафи, неприятный запах, а также покраснение, болезненность и припухлость вокруг головки полового члена, особенно в сочетании с зудом и необычными выделениями, иногда могут указывать на инфекции, передающиеся половым путем.

Причиной неприятного запаха от пениса также может быть состояние, называемое баланитом, при котором головка полового члена воспаляется, добавил врач. Кроме того, таким симптомом могут сопровождаться бактериальные или грибковые инфекции и некоторые кожные заболевания, уточнил доктор.

Ранее Ашрафи рассказал, что эректильная дисфункция может сигнализировать о серьезных проблемах со здоровьем. По его словам, речь может идти о заболеваниях сосудов, диабете, высоком уровне холестерина и других опасных состояниях.

For a moment there, things were looking grim for NSW dining. The closure of two three-hatted restaurants – Quay and Oncore by Clare Smyth – in February was followed by two-hatted Bentley calling it a day in the CBD, and much-loved Ursula’s pulling up stumps in Paddington.

It was looking unlikely we would have 10 cracking spots to nominate as the Good Food Guide’s New Restaurant of the Year.

But then, bang! A volley of new bars, cafes and restaurants opened in June, some led by veteran operators pouring decades of experience into passion projects, others led by young guns making sure NSW will be the best Australian state for eating and drinking for generations to come.

Marrickville’s Ester Spirits is on martini duty today, but when it comes to the stiff drink, competition is stiff.

A normal martini ideally should be between -5C and -10C, but people have been getting competitive. The general rule of thumb is “as cold as humanly possible” but some bartenders are now taking that to extremes.

Take Alfie’s, for example. The high-energy chophouse in Sydney’s CBD served us the coldest martini of the year – a positively freezing -18C. Could have been colder, in my humble opinion.

(Today’s martinis are of the two-sip variety!)

From backstage: Carlisle Castle Hotel executive chef Cameron Votano and head chef Jimmy Walker held up their Pub of the Year frame high before a short speech on the podium.

Walker was still clutching it when we caught up with the duo once they walked off the stage.

A stunned Walker echoed his colleague, “It’s just crazy. I can’t believe how quickly it’s happened… we’ve only just opened and six weeks ago. The recognition’s insane in this amount of time, it’s just unbelievable.”

The 150-year-old Newtown pub, which had been family-owned-and-run for the past 60 years, was recently sold to Universal Hotels, who also owns Redfern’s Bat & Ball Hotel. The revamped, pokie-free community hub opened in September.

From the bar: Mark Best, asked who he thinks will win Chef of the Year, says it’s a tight race between Nicholas Hill of Porcine and South End’s Alex Tong and Hussein Sarhan. But he thinks the duo from Newtown will bring it home.

South End was awarded two hats earlier this year in its opening review by Sydney Morning Herald Good Food Guide editor Callan Boys. Sarhan previously cooked at produce-forward Fred’s in Paddington; Tong was sous chef at fire-licked Ester. Together, that’s one powerhouse package.

Best has picked up a few awards himself over the years, including the Guide’s Chef of the Year in 2009, and mentored last year’s Young Chef of the Year winner Jen Kwok Lee. Will his tip be the winning one? Stay tuned to find out.

When Quay served its final course on February 14 after four decades in business, it went out on top with the Good Food Guide’s highest ranking of three hats, a level it maintained since 2002, soon after Peter Gilmore took on the role of executive chef.

No other Sydney restaurant has come close to equalling that three-hat run, but Quay’s real legacy is its influence on Australian cuisine, produce and hospitality.

See the full timeline of Quay, and Legend Award winner Gilmore’s influence on its success, right.

The Trailblazer Award, founded in memory of Bill Granger and presented by SevenRooms, recognises an individual, team or business driving Australian food forward. The winner should have a unique point of view, embrace hospitality in its truest sense, and forge a path for others.

This year’s winner, Ahana Dutt, takes out the award for her work at her Newtown restaurant, Kolkata Social, which aims to flip the expectations of Indian cooking on their head.

The diner, which began as a social enterprise with Plate it Forward but is now under Dutt’s ownership, is less an Indian restaurant than a Bengali one, committed fiercely to the flavours and food of her heritage, yet also grounded firmly in place, spinning Kolkata through a third-culture lens. Think Davidson plum sharpening alu’r dom Hasselback potatoes (as served to tonight’s attendees), or kosha that sees retired Meredith Dairy goat cooked in a slow braise, dark and deeply spiced.

Trailblazing? Definitely. Australian diners may be increasingly fluent in the flavours of regional Italy or China, but Dutt still fends off questions about why she refuses to serve butter chicken. The chef recognises that there’s plenty of diversity in Sydney’s Indian food scene, but the broader public’s expectations, she says, can be paralysing.

Sticking to her guns at Kolkata Social is one more step to the food of Australia’s largest migrant population being celebrated in all its depth, nuance and glory.

The winner of the Legend Award has received a rock star’s reception inside Carriageworks.

As Peter Gilmore shook hands with fans and friends while approaching the podium, the entire room stood in applause.

A video played to the audience featured hundreds of photos of his food, including the famous Snow Egg dessert dish. “Seeing it like that is pretty crazy,” he said.

He thanked Leon Fink, and said he was lucky to work with hundreds of chefs in his restaurants. “God, it’s been a ride, absolutely. What can I say? This industry has given me so much, so much joy and so much love from everyone that works in this industry.”

“It hasn’t been work. It’s been my life … and an amazing amount of good fortune. And I’ve been lucky to work with some of the best people in this industry over the years.”

Gilmore paid special thanks to his wife, Kath Gilmore, “who has been my biggest support, and without her, I wouldn’t have had a normal life at all”.

In the year 2000, The Sydney Morning Herald published what could now be described as a historic food review. A young fellow running a restaurant in the furthest reaches of the northern beaches caught Terry Durack’s eye. This chap, wrote Durack, had “a real talent for sending out beautifully structured food with innate simplicity.”

With the benefit of 26 years of hindsight, we now know that Durack was onto something. Said young chap was Peter Gilmore, who went on to transform Australian dining with his tenure at Quay and Bennelong.

After earning three hats for 23 consecutive years, and winning Restaurant of the Year six times, Gilmore is now the winner of the 42nd Sydney Morning Herald Good Food Guide Legend Award.

As we are sure the hundreds of chefs who have passed through his kitchens and now carry on his legacy would agree, he has more than earned it. Congratulations to Peter Gilmore, whose new honour earns a standing ovation from the crowd.

Peter Fiander has applied for the Young Chef of the Year award before, so his origin story was already familiar to judges – and former winners – Lauren Eldridge, Dan Puskas, Louis Tikaram and Phil Wood: after leaving high school early, he started his career in Castle Hill at the age of 16 before going on to work at LP’s Quality Meats, Ragazzi and Porcine.

What impressed the panel this time around, however, was how he has evolved into a leader since stepping into the role of head chef at Osteria Luna.

Anyone can cook, after all, but not everyone can lead a team through the heat of service, and still find time to source whole beasts so his chefs can upgrade their butchery skills.

Fiander is a chef obsessed with the small stuff, a cook-it-from-scratch, do-it-yourself, learn-it-then-teach-it kind of cook, who wants all those steps to come together in a way that appears effortless on the plate.
