RFK Jr.’s Revised Vaccine Schedule May Cause 83% Surge in Hepatitis B Infections

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s proposed changes to the childhood vaccine schedule have raised alarm among public health experts and researchers.

Concerns include a rise in preventable infectious diseases such as measles, polio, and hepatitis B.

A new study suggests that delaying or foregoing the hepatitis B shot for newborns could increase chronic infections among children.

In December 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) ended its recommendation for universal hepatitis B vaccination at birth for infants whose mothers test negative for the virus. Instead, the federal health agency recommends individual-based clinical decision making.

After reviewing the evidence, the CDC, which is overseen by the HHS, concluded that newborns “face little risk of contracting hepatitis,” which deemed the vaccine, typically administered at birth, unnecessary.

Experts say this shift in public health policy wasn’t based on science, and researchers warn of rising infection rates.

“Hepatitis B immunizations have been proven to protect individuals against a serious and significant disease,” said Graham Tse, MD, pediatrician and chief medical officer of MemorialCare Miller Children’s & Women’s Hospital in Long Beach, CA.

“The hepatitis B vaccines have several decades of history and have been proven safe and effective,” Tse said. “There has been no new data or evidence presented or uncovered to support delaying the newborn dose of hepatitis B.”

Here’s what parents should know about making informed decisions with their child’s healthcare team.

A U.S. modeling study published in Annals of Internal Medicine on October 20 projected that delaying the first vaccine dose until 2 months could increase chronic hepatitis B infections among infants by 19% under one scenario.

With shared decision making and lower vaccine uptake, projected increases ranged from 83% to 225%, compared with universal birth-dose vaccination.

The researchers also estimated that up to 1.8 million young adults could remain susceptible to hepatitis B by age 20 under shared decision making, compared with 232,000 under universal birth dosing.

Another study published in JAMA Pediatrics in late April projected hundreds of additional chronic infections annually if the hepatitis B birth dose were delayed or omitted.

However, these figures are model-based projections, rather than verified outcomes. Actual results will depend on vaccination rates and other factors.

In December 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) adopted a recommendation shifting away from universal hepatitis B vaccination at birth for infants whose mothers test negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).

Under the new approach, parents and healthcare professionals can decide together whether to give the first dose at birth or defer it until the baby is 2 months old.

The change followed an 8 to 3 vote by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), marking a departure from longstanding U.S. guidance.

However, infants born to mothers who test positive for hepatitis B or whose infection status is unknown should still receive the hepatitis B vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG) within 12 hours of birth, according to the updated guidelines.

Hepatitis B is a viral infection that affects the liver.

Although some infections resolve, newborns and infants face a higher risk of developing lifelong hepatitis B infection compared with adults.

Up to 90% of infants infected during their first year develop chronic hepatitis B.

“We have actual world evidence that vaccinating newborns for hepatitis B prevents hepatitis B infections later in life and the serious complications of that disease,” Tse said.

Over time, chronic infection can lead to cirrhosis, liver failure, or liver cancer.

“The danger of delaying the newborn hepatitis B shot is that individuals do not get vaccinated and thereby do not have protection against hepatitis B,” Tse said.

Early vaccination helps protect young infants before potential exposure occurs, including situations in which maternal screening misses an infection.

“Newborns can be infected with hepatitis B from a mother who is not aware they have a hepatitis B infection or from exposure later in life,” Tse said.

“The risk is developing a hepatitis B infection, which can potentially lead to liver cancer, fulminant hepatitis, or severe end-stage liver disease.”

In contrast to the CDC, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) still recommends a universal hepatitis B vaccine dose within 24 hours of birth, followed by additional doses during infancy.

The leading pediatric medical organization has raised concerns about the potential consequences of reduced vaccination rates.

The hepatitis B vaccine has decades of safety and effectiveness data.

Tse said the AAP’s vaccine schedule and recommendations are based on decades of study, use, and expert input, and provide the best protection against vaccine-preventable diseases.

Parents who have questions about potential side effects, timing, or their baby’s individual risk should discuss them with a qualified healthcare professional.

“Parents are confused, given the recommendations from CDC, HHS, and Kennedy differ from established and respected groups such as the American Academy of Pediatrics, the Infectious Disease Society of America, and several states,” Tse said.

“Parents desire to keep their children safe, and having differing opinions and confusion will lead to reduced vaccination rates as parents experience doubt as to whom to listen to or believe.”

While the updated CDC policy offers more flexibility for some families, lower vaccination uptake could lead to an increase in chronic hepatitis B infections.

Ask your pediatrician about the benefits of vaccination against hepatitis B at birth, the risks of delaying the first dose, and the recommended schedule for completing the series.

You can also ask how maternal screening results affect your baby’s protection.

An informed conversation with your healthcare professional can help you weigh the evidence and choose a vaccination plan for your baby.

“I see no argument whatsoever for changing the established vaccination schedule for hepatitis B,” Tse said.

“There have been no new studies, no data, nor evidence to justify Kennedy’s proposed changes to the childhood immunization schedule. Delaying vaccinations or skipping vaccinations puts your child at risk for serious and significant diseases, which are all preventable,” Tse said.

Christina Applegate shared an unexpected health update on her return to the MeSsy podcast with co-host Jamie-Lynn Sigler.

The two actors have detailed their experiences with multiple sclerosis (MS), a neurological disorder that can lead to debilitating symptoms affecting daily functioning and quality of life.

Applegate, 54, has been raising awareness about the condition since her initial diagnosis in 2021.

In two podcast episodes released October 20, Applegate revealed that doctors had told her she was misdiagnosed with MS.

“She came into my room and said, ‘Congratulations, you don’t have MS,’ ” Applegate said as she fought back tears. “I was, like, not happy at all. Everybody else was so happy.”

The “Anchorman” and “Dead to Me” actor explained that her symptoms have continued to worsen, leaving her without an explanation for what she’s experiencing as doctors investigate the cause.

Being misdiagnosed with MS is relatively common. Applegate’s revelation highlights a difficult reality for people living with unexplained symptoms resembling MS. Her experience raises questions about how MS is diagnosed, why misdiagnoses may occur, and what people can do when their symptoms don’t match their diagnosis.

While MS can be difficult to diagnose, an accurate diagnosis can help people with the condition gain access to appropriate treatment. A misdiagnosis, on the other hand, may lead to unnecessary interventions and medication, and take an emotional toll over time.

“MS can be challenging to diagnose because there is no single test that confirms it in every case,” said Steve Allder, MD, consultant neurologist at Re:Cognition Health.

“An incorrect diagnosis can have significant consequences. Someone incorrectly diagnosed with MS may experience considerable anxiety, undergo unnecessary investigations, or receive treatments with potential risks and side effects. Equally, if MS is missed, treatment may be delayed, potentially allowing further neurological damage,” Allder told Healthline.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis underscores that MS misdiagnoses remain a concern.

Research suggests that around 15% of people diagnosed with MS may actually have another condition.

However, estimates vary across studies, with some reporting rates ranging from 5–41%.

While an accurate diagnosis can guide patients toward appropriate treatment, a mistaken one may lead to unnecessary medication, emotional distress, and years of uncertainty.

New diagnostic guidelines offer hope for greater accuracy, but they also raise important questions about how doctors can adequately distinguish MS from other conditions.

“Research has shown that MS can be both overdiagnosed and underdiagnosed,” Allder said.

“Diagnosis relies on a combination of symptoms, neurological examination, MRI findings, and sometimes, additional tests. It is also important to recognize that diagnoses can evolve as new evidence emerges. As neurologists, we must remain open to revisiting a diagnosis when symptoms or test results do not fit, ensuring patients receive appropriate treatment and support.”

There is no single test that definitively confirms every case of MS.

Diagnosis typically involves reviewing symptoms, conducting neurological examinations, and evaluating MRI scans and laboratory results.

“A second opinion or follow-up assessment may be appropriate. The priority is an accurate diagnosis that leads to the right treatment and support,” Allder said.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) published updated recommendations for menopause management in Obstetrics & Gynecology on October 15.

These expanded recommendations are the most comprehensive guidance to date. They include new clinical guidelines for menopause hormone therapy (MHT), nonhormonal medications, and nonpharmacological treatment of vasomotor symptoms (VMS), which include hot flashes and night sweats, and sleep and mood changes.

According to the ACOG guidance, 50%–92% of females experiencing menopause report VMS.

Around 16%–66% of females in the United States report menopause-related sleep issues, and more than one-third of perimenopausal and postmenopausal females report experiencing depressive symptoms.

“In recent years, misconceptions about the risks of hormone therapy have been widespread, which has made some clinicians reluctant to prescribe it and some patients hesitant to use it,” Bruce Pier, MD, clinical professor and associate fellowship director at the University of North Carolina and collaborator on the ACOG clinical practice guidelines, told Healthline.

“This guidance offers a comprehensive review of the evidence to support individualized counseling and shared decision making. Our hope is that it clears up those misconceptions and expands access to treatment.”

The ACOG is also developing companion guidance for genitourinary syndrome of menopause, expected in 2027.

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of MHT with estrogen and progestogens (or estrogen alone for people without a uterus) for both VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbance, in individuals who can use hormones.

It also conditionally recommends conjugated estrogen plus bazedoxifene (Duavee) as an alternative for these issues.

According to a meta-analysis from 2021 that was cited by the guidelines, estrogen therapy, with or without progestogens, significantly reduced VMS frequency and severity compared to a placebo or no treatment. A 2017 meta-analysis ranked transdermal estradiol plus a progestogen as the most effective option for reducing VMS frequency.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis, also cited by the guidelines, found that MHT was associated with improved self-reported sleep quality. Once again, transdermal therapies showed some of the largest effects.

A Handful of Pistachios May Help You Sleep Better, Review Says

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Could a handful of pistachios before you go to bed really help you sleep better?

A review of studies published in Nutrition Research Reviews examines how the nutrients and plant compounds in pistachios might influence sleep health.

Pistachios contain several nutrients linked to sleep-related processes, prompting an investigation into whether they might offer benefits beyond their nutritional value.

While the findings suggest that pistachios may support sleep, the evidence is inconclusive and warrants further study.

“Pistachios are compelling for sleep research because they bring together several nutrients and plant compounds connected to sleep physiology within one whole food,” said study author Marie-Pierre St-Onge, PhD, professor of nutritional medicine and director of the Center of Excellence for Sleep & Circadian Research in the Department of Medicine at Columbia University Institute of Nutrition, in a news release.

“Rather than focusing on a single ‘sleep nutrient,’ this review provides a rationale for studying how the complete pistachio food matrix may affect sleep-related pathways,” she continued.

The narrative review examined existing research on pistachios and sleep-related biology.

The researchers explored whether the combination of nutrients in pistachios could promote quality sleep.

Rather than identifying pistachios as a sleep remedy, the researchers outlined biological pathways through which their nutrients and bioactive compounds might influence sleep.

“We were particularly interested in pistachios because they are particularly high in phytomelatonin, polyphenols, and carotenoids, which we noted were plant-derived components that had been shown to have sleep benefits in preclinical studies,” St-Onge told Healthline.

“We also noted their high content of tryptophan and vitamin B6, which are relevant to melatonin production in humans,” she continued.

Pistachios contain several nutrients associated with sleep, including melatonin, which helps regulate the sleep-wake cycle.

Other studies examining the effects of some of these nutrients on sleep offer further context.

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of tryptophan supplementation found that it may reduce wakefulness after sleep onset, particularly at doses of at least 1 gram (g) daily. However, an ounce of pistachios supplies only about 71 mg, which is much less than the doses examined by the researchers.

Additionally, a 2023 review of dietary polyphenols led by St-Onge identified potential links between plant compounds and sleep outcomes, although results varied across studies.

Neither review established that polyphenols or tryptophan from pistachios specifically affected sleep quality.

“I was not surprised that pistachios may help support sleep, given their combination of nutrients involved in sleep regulation,” said Michelle Routhenstein, MS, a preventive cardiology dietitian at EntirelyNourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“Pistachios provide tryptophan, an amino acid the body uses to produce serotonin and melatonin; vitamin B6, which supports these pathways; and magnesium, which plays a role in nervous system function and sleep regulation,” she told Healthline.

The new review highlights the concept of the “food matrix,” or how nutrients and bioactive compounds interact within a whole food.

Instead of investigating tryptophan, magnesium, or melatonin individually, researchers were curious about the combined effects of consuming them together in pistachios.

Laboratory and simulated-digestion research offers clues about how fiber and plant compounds in pistachios and peanuts interact with gut microbes. However, these findings do not establish that eating pistachios changes sleep quality in real-world conditions.

Broader nutrition research also points to a relationship between diet and sleep.

A 2021 review in the Annual Review of Nutrition examined evidence linking dietary patterns and individual nutrients to sleep outcomes. The findings suggest a relationship between sleep and diet, but note that more research is needed to determine cause and effect.

In a nutshell, the current evidence builds a compelling case for pistachios as a sleep aid.

However, it’s too soon to recommend pistachios as a sleep remedy, as many factors may affect sleep quality and insomnia.

“Sleep difficulties can have different underlying causes,” Routhenstein noted. “If inadequate intake of these nutrients is a contributing factor, pistachios may help, but they may not address sleep issues related to other nutrient deficiencies or other causes.”

Much of the supporting evidence cited in the review comes from studies of individual nutrients, other foods, laboratory experiments, or biological mechanisms rather than from trials involving whole pistachios.

Researchers have also reported significant variations in the melatonin content of pistachios, making the amount in a typical serving difficult to pinpoint.

Additionally, the review received support from American Pistachio Growers, and the corresponding author, St-Onge, serves on its advisory board. Larger studies and clinical trials are needed, ideally without industry funding, to ensure well-rounded results.

Indeed, the authors call for randomized controlled trials examining whether eating pistachios affects sleep outcomes, including sleep quality, duration, and melatonin levels.

“This was a review; we noted that the complete profile of pistachios would make them well-suited to support healthful sleep, but research is needed to evaluate this,” St-Onge told Healthline.

“This is the next step, and something we are actively working on. We also need to better understand what dosage and frequency of consumption would be optimal.”

Regardless, a handful of pistachios is still considered a healthy choice as part of a balanced diet.

“A small handful, about 1 ounce of shelled pistachios, is an appropriate portion,” Routhenstein said. “Enjoy them during the day or as a light afternoon or evening snack when you’re hungry.”

If nutrients like tryptophan help promote sleep, it’s possible that other nuts could yield similar results.

“Other nuts, such as almonds and walnuts, may offer similar benefits because they also contain nutrients involved in sleep regulation,” Routhenstein said.

“Early research is encouraging, but it’s too soon to say how much they might help.”

Other foods, like turkey, also contain tryptophan.

“For someone allergic to nuts, foods such as tofu, beans, oats, eggs, dairy, and fish can provide tryptophan, which the body uses to make serotonin and melatonin,” Routhenstein said. “The goal is to meet their nutrient needs rather than rely on one food to improve sleep.”

Sleep health depends on multiple factors. As such, Routhenstein recommends a personalized approach to getting better sleep, from what you eat and when you eat to addressing any nutritional gaps.

“I would also encourage regular, balanced meals throughout the day to support blood sugar management, especially for those with diabetes, while avoiding large meals close to bedtime,” Routhenstein said. “Caffeine later in the day and alcohol in the evening are also worth considering as possible sleep disruptors.”

Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Increase Dementia, Stroke Risk

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Your hub for heart health management, insights, and solutions

Healthy adults who eat high levels of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) may be more likely to show signs of damage to the brain that could increase the likelihood of stroke and future memory problems, according to a study published October 15 in JAMA Network Open.

UPFs are often defined using the NOVA classification: These foods typically have 5 or more ingredients and include substances not typically found in culinary preparations. This category of foods includes packaged snack foods, sugary cereals, soft drinks, and frozen ready-to-eat meals.

UPFs account for more than 50% of daily energy intake in high-income countries, including the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom.

Previous research has linked these foods to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes. This new study adds to growing evidence linking high UPF intake to adverse health outcomes.

This was a cross-sectional analysis of 8,783 adults between the ages of 35 and 69 without cardiovascular disease. The participants were all enrolled in the Canadian Alliance for Healthy Hearts and Minds (CAHHM) and Prospective Urban and Rural Epidemiology-MIND (PURE-MIND) cohorts.

The researchers used MRI scans to evaluate the participants’ brains. They also set out to analyze the cross-sectional relationship between UPF consumption, body fat, vascular brain injury (VBI), and cognitive performance.

They measured the participants’ habitual UPF intake using a food frequency questionnaire. Intake was expressed as servings based on a 2,000-calorie-per-day diet.

UPFs accounted for an average of 25% of participants’ daily diets. For the individuals with the highest intake, this increased to 45% of everything on their plates, compared to 16% for those who ate the least.

The researchers also noted that UPFs were not just an added part of individual diets. These foods appeared to replace whole foods.

Among people with the highest UPF intake, fruits, vegetables, grains, and other minimally processed foods accounted for 41% of their daily food intake. Among those with the lowest UPF intake, minimally processed foods made up 68% of their daily intake.

When the researchers compared the highest intake to the lowest, they found that those with a higher intake of UPFs had 1.7% more body fat. They also found that people with a higher UPF intake had higher odds of VBI.

“These findings are concerning, but it is important to remember that an association does not prove cause and effect,” said Michelle Routhenstein, Preventive Cardiology Dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“From my perspective as a preventive cardiology dietitian, these findings reinforce the importance of nourishing our blood vessels to support both cardiovascular and brain health.”

Although researchers found no significant differences in cognitive performance, the signs of vascular brain injury observed in the study have been associated with an increased risk of stroke and cognitive decline.

These findings suggest a possible link between UPF intake and signs of brain changes. However, participants were only assessed once and weren’t followed over time. This means the researchers were unable to determine whether UPFs directly caused the changes.

“While we can’t conclude from this study that limiting UPFs would improve brain health, we have decades of literature demonstrating that higher intakes of green leafy vegetables, brightly colored fruits, whole grains, and lean protein sources are good for brain health,” said Suzanne Judd, PhD, Director of the UC College of Medicine’s Center for Public Health, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“So focusing more on what can be brought into the diet rather than what should be eliminated might be a good way to start,” she added.

The preservatives [in UPFs] may have a direct impact on the vascular system, while the loss of antioxidants results in a less robust response to inflammation in the body.

“Additionally, sugar or salt is often added to make the food a bit tastier so people consume it in higher quantities. Over-consumption of food can lead to weight gain and diabetes, which can negatively impact brain health,” Judd said.

Judd told Healthline that it is virtually impossible to completely avoid UPFs. Even some “health” foods, like protein powder and some yogurts, are considered ultra-processed.

It is more important to look at diet overall and emphasize consuming foods as close to their natural state as possible, such as fruits, vegetables, grains, and meats purchased raw or frozen and then cooked at home.

Routhenstein recommended starting simply with one manageable swap rather than trying to overhaul your entire diet.

She said that you should identify an ultra-processed food you eat regularly and consider a more nutrient-rich alternative. It is important to account for convenience, food preferences, budget, and lifestyle so that the change feels realistic and is sustainable over the long term.

“I want people to feel empowered, not fearful, when making food choices,” said Routhenstein. “Healthy eating does not mean having to cook everything from scratch. What matters most is building consistent, nourishing habits that support both heart and brain health.”

Physical activity may help support mood and improve quality of life during the menopause transition, according to a study published on October 13 in Menopause, the journal of the Menopause Society.

This review of 12 clinical trials involving over 1,000 people ages 40 to 65 found that both aerobic and mind-body exercises may be associated with reduced depressive symptoms and improved well-being.

It is common for individuals to experience psychological symptoms during menopause, including depression, anxiety, low mood, stress, and difficulty with thinking and memory. These symptoms can have a significant effect on quality of life, with an estimated 1 in 10 women leaving work due to menopause symptoms.

This study found that regular physical activity may help improve certain psychological symptoms and overall quality of life during menopause.

The types of physical activity in the review were broken into two categories: aerobic-based exercise (ABIs) and mind-body activities (MBAs).

The researchers found that ABIs showed the strongest effects on reducing depressive symptoms and improving overall quality of life. This may be particularly relevant during perimenopause when women may be more vulnerable to mood instability due to estrogen fluctuations.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, pointed out that there are various ways that ABIs can have positive effects.

Ross explained that aerobic exercise may help improve menopausal symptoms through several mechanisms, including the release of endorphins, reduced inflammation, improved cholesterol levels, and better regulation of body temperature and sleep cycles.

MBAs were shown to have a greater effect on improving psychological quality of life. These exercises involve mindfulness, body awareness, and breathwork. They may help improve emotional regulation.

ABIs tended to require fewer sessions. This may mean these types of exercises are easier to fit in, especially if you are facing time or energy constraints. For example, in one trial, participants were instructed to walk for 30 minutes, three times per week. In another trial, participants attended three hour-long Zumba classes per week.

In contrast, one trial using MBAs instructed participants to attend two 75-minute yoga classes each week, and another had participants attend daily one-hour yoga classes.

Stephanie Faubion, MD, Director for the Mayo Clinic Center for Women’s Health and Medical Director for The Menopause Society, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that this was a relatively small body of evidence. “While physical activity is an important component of a healthy lifestyle during midlife, I would be cautious about interpreting these findings to mean that exercise is a treatment for depression related specifically to menopause,” she said.

Research has shown that physical activity can positively affect mental health. Studies have also found that regular exercise can help improve symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Faubion told Healthline that, broadly speaking, regular physical activity may support mood through multiple pathways, including better sleep, improved physical function, and overall well-being.

However, she also noted that this study cannot tell us precisely why the improvements occurred or whether aerobic exercise is superior to other forms of activity.

Faubion said that recommending physical activity during and after menopause extends well beyond mood.

“Physical activity is important for cardiovascular and metabolic health, maintaining muscle mass and physical function, and supporting bone health,” she said.

“These become particularly important at midlife as women experience age- and menopause-related changes in body composition, bone, and cardiometabolic health,” Faubion added.

Ross agreed that physical activity is important for more than just mood and mental health improvement. She also noted that there are other types of exercise beyond ABIs and MBAs that can benefit people during menopause:

“These types of activities help with weight loss or allow women to maintain a healthy weight. If you are not ready for aerobic exercises, stretching and simple exercises help with balance and stability,” Ross said.

Faubion told Healthline that physical activity is an important part of healthy aging and should be encouraged during the menopause transition, but it should not be suggested that women can simply exercise their way out of significant depression or other bothersome menopause symptoms.

“Women experiencing persistent depression, anxiety, or symptoms that interfere with their quality of life should seek evaluation and discuss the full range of evidence-based treatment options with their healthcare clinician,” Faubion said.

Women With Autoimmune Diseases More Likely to Be Misdiagnosed Than Men

A new international study found that women with autoimmune diseases often experience longer delays in getting a correct diagnosis compared to men.

The research, published in eClinicalMedicine on October 12, also shows that females report higher rates of being misdiagnosed, especially with mental health or psychosomatic conditions.

These findings highlight challenges in diagnosing autoimmune diseases and suggest that sex differences affect patients’ experiences in healthcare.

This is significant given that autoimmune diseases, conditions in which the body’s immune system attacks its own healthy cells, are estimated to affect around 4.6% of the U.S. population. Additionally, despite the observed disparities in diagnoses, women are almost two times as likely as men to be diagnosed with an autoimmune disease.

Researchers conducted an online survey between December 7, 2024, and March 7, 2025, inviting adults ages 18 and older who had a confirmed autoimmune or rheumatic disease.

The survey was carefully designed and tested with input from patients, charities, and medical experts to ensure it captured important information about patients’ diagnostic journeys.

Participants were asked about the time it took from when they first noticed symptoms to when they received their autoimmune diagnosis.

They also reported how many healthcare visits they made before diagnosis and whether they felt their symptoms were ever misdiagnosed as mental health issues, psychosomatic problems (physical symptoms caused or worsened by psychological factors), or another physical disease.

The survey was widely shared on social media, patient support groups, and community organizations to reach a broad audience.

Special efforts were made to include underrepresented groups such as males, younger individuals, and people from minority ethnic backgrounds.

The survey was available in five languages, and participants could complete it with help from researchers if needed.

To analyze the data, the team used statistical methods, adjusting for factors like age, type of autoimmune disease, continent of residence, and financial situation.

These methods helped compare the experiences of males and females while accounting for differences in disease types and other factors

The study included 6,607 participants, with 91.2% identifying as female and most living in the United Kingdom. On average, women were younger than men in the study.

Autoimmune thyroid disease was the most common condition reported. Non-autoimmune conditions like anxiety were more commonly reported by women, while men reported higher rates of hypertension.

One of the main findings was that women reported longer delays before getting a diagnosis. Nearly 32% of females experienced delays of four years or more from symptom onset to diagnosis, compared to about 26% of males.

On the other hand, men were more likely to be diagnosed within a year of symptom onset (nearly 50% of males versus 40% of females).

Women also made more visits to healthcare providers before diagnosis, with 26.2% having 11 or more visits compared to 18.4% of men.

When it came to misdiagnoses, women were significantly more likely to report that their symptoms had been mistaken for mental health issues (43.3% of females vs. 32.7% of males) or psychosomatic causes (33.9% of females vs. 21.5% of males).

However, there was no significant difference between men and women in being misdiagnosed with another physical disease.

The type of autoimmune disease also influenced misdiagnosis rates. Diseases with clear physical symptoms or definitive lab tests, like type 1 diabetes or rheumatoid arthritis, had lower rates of mental health and psychosomatic misdiagnoses.

In contrast, conditions such as autoimmune encephalitis or undifferentiated connective tissue disease had higher rates of these misdiagnoses (over 50%).

After adjusting for disease type and other factors, women still had higher odds of reporting mental health or psychosomatic misdiagnoses compared to men.

Other factors affected misdiagnosis rates, too. People living in continents outside the United Kingdom, those with lower household income, and certain disease groups were more likely to report psychiatric or psychosomatic misdiagnoses. Age, however, was not linked to the risk of being misdiagnosed.

Muhammad Qasim, MD, a pulmonologist and medical writer with MySleepApneaTeam, told Healthline that autoimmune diseases are often difficult to diagnose. Qasim wasn’t involved in the study.

“Early symptoms can be vague, fluctuate over time, and affect several parts of the body,” he said. “Fatigue, pain, sleep problems, brain fog, and mood changes do not immediately point to one diagnosis.”

Qasim echoed the study’s findings, explaining that similar symptoms may be interpreted differently in women versus men, with complaints being attributed to anxiety, stress, or psychosomatic causes.

However, there are probably multiple factors contributing to this phenomenon, he said.

“Differences in disease presentation, gaps in research, fragmented care, and unconscious bias can all contribute,” said Qasim. “Once a psychiatric explanation enters the medical record, there is also a risk that later clinicians view new symptoms through that same lens.”

Regarding unconscious bias, the study’s authors noted that women have historically been treated by doctors as “less credible” and more “emotional” in reporting their symptoms, which can influence how doctors interpret them.

These disparities in diagnosis are important, however, because longer waits and erroneous diagnoses can delay proper treatment and increase women’s frustration, leading them to stop asking for help.

The researchers suggest that healthcare providers need to be aware of these concerns and consider autoimmune diseases carefully when patients, especially females, present with more complicated symptoms.

But when October ends, and the wall-to-wall campaigns move on, what has awareness actually changed for those impacted by breast cancer?

For Marisa Weiss, MD, the answer is that there is always more to be done, and she’s uniquely placed to hold that view.

Dr. Weiss has treated breast cancer patients for more than 30 years. In 2000, she founded Breastcancer.org, a not-for-profit that provides expert-reviewed medical and lifestyle information on breast health and breast cancer to millions of people globally.

Sixteen years ago, she was diagnosed with stage 1 breast cancer, becoming, in her own words, a “dual citizen” in that moment, with lived experience on both sides of the doctor’s desk.

I had the pleasure of sitting down with Dr. Weiss as part of Healthline’s Breast Cancer Awareness Month coverage to talk about how awareness has changed care for the better for millions of people around the world, but why critical gaps remain, and how access to trusted information and community can help close those gaps.

“It’s good that awareness is high, but knowledge remains low,” she said. The misconception she sees most is the belief that breast cancer is something that happens to other families.

“Most people think they’re off the hook because it doesn’t run in their family,” Dr. Weiss explained. In fact, 85%-90% of people who get breast cancer have no known family history and no known gene in the family, “so we’re all at risk,” she warns.

For most, a diagnosis arrives as a shock. “They’re completely surprised, flattened by it. Their life is upside down, and they scramble to find what they need to know,” Dr. Weiss noted.

That aftershock caused by a diagnosis is at the core of why Breastcancer.org exists. Because the person whose life is at stake is increasingly expected to direct their own care and advocate for their needs, access to accurate and empowering information is critical in that process. “Decisions aren’t being made around the patient and for the patient in a bubble,” Dr. Weiss said. “Decision-making is supposed to be shared.”

With appointments hard to get and clinicians stretched, she said patients have to do more of the work.

In practice, Dr. Weiss says that means preparing by writing down your family history on both sides and making sure your care team knows who you are. “What’s important to you? Who’s in your life? Do you want more children? Are you planning a vacation? All those decisions can have an impact on the care you receive.”

Where Dr. Weiss sees the widest gap is after treatment ends. Speaking from personal experience, she said: “I had the best care possible, and there were still gaps. You can find yourself alone with lots of questions and fears.”

Understandably, a great deal of focus and attention is centered on diagnosis and treatment, she said. Then it stops. “When your life is supposed to go back to normal, it never does. You have to find a new normal. That quiet phase can be really lonely and difficult.”

For Dr. Weiss, the questions that fill that silence are often as practical as they are emotional: “How do I know my treatment is working? How do I tell leftover side effects from new ones? How do I go back to work, and who do I tell? How do I talk to my kids? What about that new treatment I heard about on TV last night?”

That post-treatment period can also be the first time anyone has really had a chance to stop, take stock, and start asking “what next?”

“You might think, what happened to me? Who do I want to be moving forward? How do I have a long life full of wonderful things, and remain as independent as possible?”

Some of what patients do post-care never reaches their doctor at all, with Dr. Weiss pointing to a growing amount of personal care happening outside the hospital: GLP-1s, peptides, hormonal therapies, supplements, medical cannabis, to name a few. Dr. Weiss says the stakes are real, because some of those choices can interact with cancer medications with potential for adverse or unexpected side effects. “That is why people turn to Breastcancer.org with questions that are personal, private, and important, including “what do I need to share with my doctor, and who’s on a need-to-know basis?”

Her advice to patients, which she took herself: “Put up your hand and say, ‘thank you so much for caring, but I cannot hear another story right now.’”

On the topic of AI and its impact on treatment and care, Dr. Weiss is cautiously optimistic. On the treatment front, she points to tools that help radiologists read mammograms more thoroughly and assess future risk as game changers in the fight against breast cancer.

Breastcancer.org also uses AI for tasks such as finding citations and interpreting pathology reports. Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Not Be Linked to Colorectal Cancer

Ultra-processed foods as a group do not increase the risk of colorectal cancer, according to a new study.

Researchers from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) report that they found no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer incidence among the more than 400,000 adults they studied.

They said their findings are important because ultra-processed foods now account for more than half of adults’ energy intake in the United States. They published their research on October 15 in PLOS Medicine.

The study comes amid recent research that has connected ultra-processed foods to an increased risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity.

However, the link between colorectal cancer and ultra-processed foods has been less clear.

A 2025 study reported that ultra-processed foods may increase the risk of colorectal cancer in females under 50.

A 2022 study found that males who consumed high rates of ultra-processed foods had a higher risk of developing colorectal cancer.

NCI scientists noted in their new study that ultra-processed products represent a wide range of foods.

They pointed out that foods such as whole grains and dairy products were associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer among study participants, while foods such as processed meats were associated with a higher risk.

“We did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence,” said Erikka Loftfield, PhD, a former investigator with the NCI, in a news release. “However, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention.”

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the NCI scientists appear to be on the right track.

“While the study did not find that overall ultra-processed food intake was associated with colorectal cancer risk, the findings suggest that simply classifying foods by their level of processing may not tell the whole story,” said Marissa Shams-White, PhD, a senior principal scientist in population science at the American Cancer Society.

“One of the challenges with studying ultra-processed foods is that the category includes a very wide range of foods and beverages, and we don’t yet know whether they all relate to colorectal cancer risk in the same way,” she added.

Ketan Thanki, MD, a colorectal surgeon specializing in benign and malignant diseases of the colon, rectum, and anus at the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California, agrees.

“Just because it is ultra-processed doesn’t mean it is inherently bad or good for you,” he said. “The source of the food and the additives like preservatives, flavorings, sugars, and pigments that are added to increase palatability probably play a much larger role in increasing cancer risk.”

“A whole grain cereal and a hot dog are both UPF, but they push risk in opposite directions,” Thanki noted.

In their study, the National Cancer Institute researchers analyzed data from 461,682 adults aged 50 to 71 years who enrolled in the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study between 1995 and 1996.

The participants recorded their eating habits using a food frequency questionnaire. Researchers then used that data to estimate each person’s intake of ultra-processed foods. The median consumption of ultra-processed foods among participants was 43% of daily median intake.

The participants were then followed until 2018, for a median of nearly 19 years. The scientists reported that 10,075 cases of colorectal adenocarcinoma were diagnosed during that period.

The researchers reported no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and the incidence of colorectal cancer.

However, they said their findings varied by food type. Higher intake of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium derived from UPF sources was associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence, while meat from UPF sources was associated with higher incidence.

Researchers also found that replacing 10% of gram weight from UPF intake with minimally processed foods was associated with a 2% lower incidence of CRC.

The researchers acknowledged limitations to their study.

They noted that the participants were primarily older, white Americans. In addition, UPF intake was only measured once at the beginning of the study period, so the findings cannot account for changes in eating habits or the increasing availability of ultra-processed foods.

Nonetheless, Ashkan Farhadi, MD, a gastroenterologist at MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center in California, who wasn’t involved in the study, said he found the results “very interesting.”

“The definition of ultra-processed foods is so broad,” he told Healthline. “It’s important to designate what type of ultra-processed foods you’re talking about.”

“You shouldn’t just go by whether a food is designated as ultra-processed,” Farhadi added. “You should go by what’s on the [ingredient] label.”

Shams-White shared a similar approach. “When it comes to diet, it is important to follow an overall healthy dietary pattern,” she told Healthline. “Emphasize a variety of fruits and vegetables and whole grains and limit red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, ultra-processed foods, and refined grain products.”

“Eating a diet rich in fiber and whole grains, dairy and calcium, and limiting processed and red meat and alcohol is what really reduces your risk of developing cancer,” added Thanki.

The American Cancer Society estimates there will be about 108,000 new cases of colon cancer and nearly 50,000 new cases of rectal cancer in the United States in 2026.

The incidence rate is slightly higher in men than in females.

The organization reports that the rate of colorectal cancer cases in the United States has declined since the mid-1980s, but they note that most of that decrease has occurred in older adults.

The incidence rate among people under 50 increased by nearly 3% from 2013 to 2022. Colorectal cancer is now the leading cause of death from cancer for U.S. adults under the age of 50.

Colorectal cancer occurs when cells in the large intestine grow out of control. About 70% of cases begin in the colon.

Farhadi said genetics, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and a sedentary lifestyle can also increase a person’s risk of developing colorectal cancer.

Shams-White noted that the American Cancer Society Guideline for Diet and Physical Activity for Cancer Prevention recommended achieving and maintaining a healthy weight, being physically active, following a healthy eating pattern, and avoiding alcohol and tobacco.

Thanki points out that there are risk factors for colorectal cancer that can be modified and others that can’t.

Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: Lung Cancer Can Look Like Anyone

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Picture someone with lung cancer. Now picture a 24-year-old soccer player in the prime of his life. A filmmaker in her early 40s with a camera in her hand, doing what she loves best. A busy mom of two, with a shoulder so sore she can’t pick up her newborn, yet struggling to get a diagnosis.

The gap between the picture in our heads and the people actually being diagnosed is costing time. And time is the one thing with lung cancer you cannot get back.

We know that lung cancer is the most common cancer worldwide and the leading cause of cancer death. And while lung cancer incidence rates are falling in many countries as fewer people smoke, lung cancer in people who have never smoked is on the rise globally, both as a proportion and in absolute numbers.

According to the CDC, as many as 20% of lung cancers diagnosed in the US are in people who have never smoked. Data suggests that non-smoking lung cancer is now among the most common causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide.

The numbers are stark. Lung cancer can affect anyone. And that includes the young – like Alex, one of the subjects of a powerful new short documentary commissioned by the charity EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK for their Our Lungs: Time to Breathe campaign.

Alex was a 24-year-old soccer player who died just weeks after receiving a diagnosis of Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) positive lung cancer, following several misdiagnoses born of the fact that no one suspected lung cancer in one so young, fit, and with no smoking history. The film was made by filmmaker Sarah Li, who herself was diagnosed with Stage 4 EGFR+ lung cancer in April 2024, just a month after Alex died from the same mutation of the disease, and charts her journey with survivor’s guilt, what an earlier diagnosis may have meant for Alex, and what that fact means for her own future.

Ahead of Lung Cancer Awareness Month in November, I sat down with my friend, Gini Harrison, to talk about the film, her own journey, and why catching lung cancer sooner matters so much. Gini is a Professor of Psychology at The Open University, a trustee of EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK, and is living with stage 4 EGFR-positive lung cancer. She’s also the mom at the top of this piece, whose sore shoulder was initially attributed to breastfeeding posture.

This interview has been lightly edited for clarity and length.

Gini Harrison: I’d had my son, Michael, in February 2021 and was on maternity leave. My daughter, Emily, was five. I think the shoulder pain started around March, but that was it. No cough, no breathlessness… nothing that made me think about my lungs.

Initially, they gave me painkillers, which helped a bit. But the pain kept getting worse. Eventually, I started losing movement and strength in my arm, and it got to the point where I couldn’t pick up my son anymore.

This was all happening during COVID, so my appointments were over the telephone. Eventually, after months without being examined face-to-face, I paid to see a shoulder specialist. As soon as he touched my shoulder blade and I practically leaped out of the chair, he knew something wasn’t right. He ordered an MRI, which showed a tumor in my shoulder blade and another in my upper right lung. In December 2021, I was diagnosed with stage IV lung cancer.

Gini Harrison: I wasn’t thinking, “This must be cancer.” I was just thinking, “Why isn’t this getting better?”

And I think that distinction matters. Patients shouldn’t have to identify their own disease before they can get help. You should be able to say that something is wrong and be listened to.

Gini Harrison: It was devastating. So many of my first questions were about the children: “How are we going to tell them?” “Am I going to be here to see them grow up?” “Will they remember me?” Suddenly, there was a question mark over a future I’d taken for granted.

I was also completely shocked by the type of cancer. If someone had said breast cancer, I don’t think I would have been anywhere near as surprised. But lung cancer?! I’d always associated it with smoking, and I’d never smoked.

I hadn’t imagined that this particular cancer could ever happen to me. So yes, I had some assumptions that I now challenge. I understand how deeply rooted they are because I had them too.

Gini Harrison: People are often shocked, and one of the first questions is whether I smoked.

And they often look healthy. That doesn’t mean everything is easy, or that there aren’t side effects. It just means you can’t tell what someone is dealing with by looking at them.

I think we’ve given lung cancer such a narrow public image that people outside it can become almost invisible.

Gini Harrison: Oh, yes! Once, I spoke to a doctor about a breathing problem after radiotherapy. He asked, “How much did you smoke?” When I said I’d never smoked, he replied, “Well, how did you get lung cancer then?”

On another occasion, I went to a hospice for help managing side effects, and someone there asked, “How are you still alive?” That’s quite a thing to hear when you’ve come looking for support!

When people ask about smoking, I try to explain that I’ve never smoked and I’m certainly not alone. Never smoking doesn’t make you immune. But I’m also very clear that nobody deserves lung cancer, whether they’ve smoked or not. We shouldn’t be creating a hierarchy of who deserves compassion.

Smoking history matters medically. But in a personal conversation, “How are you doing?” is a much kinder place to start.

Gini Harrison: Lung cancer isn’t one single disease. Two people can both have lung cancer, but different changes in their cells may be driving it.

Genes contain the instructions that tell our cells how to work, including when to grow and divide. Changes in those genes (called mutations) can cause cells to keep dividing when they shouldn’t, forming a tumor. In my case, there’s a mutation in a gene called EGFR.

EGFR-positive lung cancer is a type of non-small cell lung cancer caused by a genetic mutation in the EGFR gene, which keeps the EGFR receptor switched “on,” leading to uncontrolled cell growth and enhanced cancer cell survival.

Biomarker testing looks for these changes, as well as other features of the cancer that can guide treatment. If we know what’s helping the cancer grow, there may be a medication that specifically targets it.

The word “genetic” can sound scary, particularly when you have children. But these changes usually develop in cancer cells over a person’s lifetime. They aren’t necessarily inherited.

Gini Harrison: Please get it checked. And if it persists, worsens or doesn’t improve with treatment, go back. Explain what’s changed and how it’s affecting your life. Ask, “What happens next if this doesn’t get better?”

The symptoms don’t always look like lung symptoms. Mine was shoulder pain.

Most symptoms will turn out to be something other than cancer. But when a symptom continues to worsen, the original explanation needs to be reconsidered. Lower risk doesn’t mean no risk.

And I don’t want all the responsibility to fall on patients to keep pushing. Reassurance should come with a next step.

Gini Harrison: Once I had recovered from my initial treatment, I realized that I wanted to help others in my situation, so I became a trustee of the charity EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK. Through this work, we produced a film, Our Lungs: Time to Breathe. At the heart of it, it challenges the perception of what someone with lung cancer looks like.

The hardest part is how close to home it feels. Alex was so young, and that worked against him. Watching his family talk about what happened is heartbreaking. It’s just so unfair.

Gini Harrison: Sarah’s story shows life continuing alongside lung cancer. She’s a filmmaker with ambitions, people she loves, and things she wants to do.

Through Alex’s family, you see the enormity of the loss. I hope people feel that, rather than simply hearing another statistic.

Gini Harrison: For me, the biggest thing is that my children are growing up and I’m here to see it. Michael is now five (the age Emily was when I was diagnosed), and Emily is about to turn ten. I can’t tell you how grateful I am to be here with them.

It wasn’t about big bucket-list things. It was about being here every day. Seeing Michael start school. Seeing Emily reach double figures. Those milestones are enormous when you’ve wondered whether you’d get to see them.

After chemotherapy and radiotherapy, I’ve had no evidence of disease since May 2022, and my scans remain stable. But a big change has been seeing the progress in available treatment. It gives me more hope about what might be available if I need treatment again.

The public image of lung cancer still needs to catch up. We need better recognition of symptoms, access to appropriate testing and treatment, and support for the whole person. As a psychologist as well as a patient, I’m particularly aware that psychological well-being and family life need attention alongside the cancer itself.

Gini Harrison: I’d love for people to share the film and help get it in front of those who wouldn’t normally think lung cancer has anything to do with them. And take something from it into everyday life: book the appointment you’ve been putting off, ask your doctor about testing, or respond with kindness when someone tells you about their diagnosis.

Just being willing to rethink your assumptions could make a difference.

Gini Harrison: “You don’t have to put a brave face on with me. How are you really doing?”

When you look well, or your scans are good, people can assume you’re “fine now”. But I’m not sure the fear ever completely goes away. Every scan is terrifying. It feels like there’s a sword of Damocles hanging over my head, and part of me is waiting for the bad scan. I also still have lasting side effects from treatment, and probably always will.

My life has fundamentally changed. Some of that has brought good things, but it doesn’t make the difficult parts disappear. It helps when someone gives you room to be honest about both, without expecting you to reassure them or turn everything into a positive story.

Gini and I have been friends for nearly 30 years, and, while she’d probably cringe to hear me say it, I remain in awe of the way she approaches life with cancer. I am so grateful for her sharing her experiences with me for this column. What Gini asks of us is simple: look again, ask the question, listen, and act on your instincts. Our Lungs: Time to Breathe is where that starts for her. You can learn more about the Our Lungs: Time to Breathe campaign and the wider work of EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK by clicking the links. — Robin Hough

Taking Blood Pressure Meds? DASH Diet Likely Safe for Potassium Levels

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

People who adhere to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet do not experience significant changes in potassium levels while taking blood pressure medications or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) drugs, according to a new study.

Researchers also reported that people taking these medications do not experience significant changes in their kidney function.

Both types of medications are known to increase potassium levels. High potassium levels can lead to hyperkalemia, a condition that can cause issues with the heart rhythm. People with impaired kidney function due to chronic kidney disease are at increased risk of hyperkalemia.

“The DASH diet is a well-known, proven nutrition pattern that is known to improve blood pressure,” said Marian Budu, MD, a clinical research coordinator on the GoFresh Trials at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston and the presenting author of the study, in a news release.

“Knowing that the DASH diet did not increase blood potassium levels in this population with normal baseline potassium levels provides assurance that the DASH diet can be prescribed to a wide range of people,” she continued.

The preliminary findings are being presented at the American Heart Association’s Hypertension Scientific Sessions 2026 meeting this week in Virginia. The research has not been published yet in a peer-reviewed journal.

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the research provides medical professionals with important information.

“While we have traditionally exercised caution before recommending the DASH diet in patients who are prescribed ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or potassium-sparing diuretics, these results show that those patients could likely adopt the DASH diet without significant concern for harm,” said Cheng-Han Chen, MD, an interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA.

“DASH diets do not appear to increase potassium levels to any significant degree in patients taking medications that are known to potentially raise serum potassium,” added Kevin Volpp, MD, the founding director of the Penn Center for Health Incentives and Behavioral Economics in Pennsylvania and the scientific lead of the American Heart Association’s Health Care by Food initiative.

“This is important because it is a theoretical concern that diets rich in potassium could cause high levels in potassium in some patients.”

The new study was a secondary analysis of the GoFresh and GoFresh Rx randomized trials that enrolled Black adults in the greater Boston area with high blood pressure.

Some of the participants were taking medications to lower their blood pressure, and others were not taking any hypertension drugs.

The study focused on four classes of blood pressure medications: angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), and potassium-sparing diuretics and beta-blockers, as well as NSAIDs.

The analysis took place in 2026 and included 356 Black adults with an average age of 53. About 70% were women.

At the start of the study, the participants had systolic blood pressure readings between 120 mm Hg and less than 150 mm Hg. They also had a diastolic blood pressure reading of less than 100 mm Hg.

The participants also had varying degrees of kidney function. In addition, they had limited access to grocery stores.

Half of the participants received three months of weekly DASH-patterned groceries and weekly nutrition counseling sessions. The other half received a cash stipend every four weeks to buy groceries, with no nutrition counseling.

The participants’ blood potassium levels were measured at the beginning of the study and again after 12 weeks.

The researchers reported that eating more potassium-rich foods in the DASH diet did not increase potassium levels to dangerous levels among participants taking blood pressure medications.

They noted no significant increase in potassium levels, regardless of whether participants were assigned to the dietary intervention or the monetary stipend group.

In a news release, the researchers acknowledged that their study did not include people with stage 4 or stage 5 kidney disease or those with higher-than-normal potassium levels.

They added that the study population comprised only Black individuals living in Boston, so the results may not generalize to the broader population.

The study authors said that future research should focus on participants with suboptimal kidney function and those with higher potassium levels.

Volpp agreed that more research is needed.

“This study was conducted in patients with non-elevated potassium levels at baseline and does not answer the question of whether they are safe in patients with elevated potassium levels or significant impairments in renal function,” he told Healthline.

Chen noted that potassium levels should be taken seriously.

“Certain classes of blood pressure medications can potentially cause high potassium levels, especially in people with kidney disease,” he told Healthline. “If potassium levels become too high, this can lead to symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and muscle pain, or even serious heart problems such as arrhythmias.”

The DASH diet is a dietary plan that emphasizes fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins such as chicken, fish, and beans.

The plan also includes whole grains, low fat dairy products, and cooking with vegetable oils. The diet discourages the intake of red meat, salt, fat, and added sugars.

Previous research has indicated that the DASH diet can provide numerous health benefits.

A preliminary 2022 study concluded that the dietary plan can help lower blood pressure and improve heart health, but those results haven’t been published in a scientific journal. Still, the findings were consistent with a 2019 study that found the DASH diet can help lower the risk of heart disease.

In addition, a 2020 study reported that adults aged 65 and older with obesity lost body fat while following the DASH Diet. A 2023 study suggested the diet can help reduce belly fat.

A 2019 meta-analysis suggested that the DASH diet may also help lower the risk of certain cancers.

Many of these protective effects are attributed to the diet’s high fruit and vegetable content.

Chen said the DASH diet has many benefits, but the eating plan might not be for everybody.

“The DASH diet has been shown to be beneficial in lowering blood pressure, losing weight, and controlling blood sugar and cholesterol levels,” he said. “However, the amount of planning and portion tracking required to follow the diet may be too much for some people.”

Chen recommended that people ease into the DASH diet.

“The best way to start on the DASH diet is to slowly introduce the changes to your meals,” he said. “This can be as simple as including a few extra servings of fruits and vegetables each day as well as swapping some whole grain products for refined grains. Over time, more of the diet can be incorporated.”

Volpp agreed that introducing the DASH diet in increments is a good strategy.

“For many people, it is easiest to make small incremental changes sequentially,” he said. “One way to get started is to increase fruit by a couple of servings a day. Another is to pay attention to the amount of sodium in packaged foods and consciously choose products lower in sodium. A third is to make a conscious effort to eat more vegetables.”

“Make changes first that are easiest for you to make; it will be easier to make those changes stick,” Volpp added.
