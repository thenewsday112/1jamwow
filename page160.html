A pollster admitted this week to using fabricated data in its surveys that were picked up by leading publications across the U.S., claiming the stunt was an “experimental project” intended to showcase the “vulnerabilities” of the polling industry. The announcement has shaken trust in polling infrastructure ahead of the midterms, especially as some candidates say their campaigns were directly harmed by the fake numbers.

After election analysis website FiftyPlusOne submitted an inquiry to Wedgewood Polls regarding the authenticity of its data and methodologies, the pollster put out a statement across social media on Tuesday, saying it had been conducting a test to show "how easy is it for an entirely fabricated polling firm to gain traction, be taken seriously, and expose how little scrutinization exists in media and public commentary?" In the statement, which has since been deleted, the group claimed it had been “far too easy” to distribute false data and insisted it "highlights a systemic breakdown in media vetting and data aggregation.”

Wedgewood's polls had been used by multiple national outlets for their midterm reporting, including the New York Times, Newsweek, MS NOW, and POLITICO. The Times, which cited around 15 Wedgewood polls between June and October, has since removed the data from its stories. In an accompanying editor’s note, the publication detailed its checking process for polls and said Wedgewood had answered several of their inquiries prior to the data being published, including questions on the identities of the group’s pollsters, the methods used to draw their samples, and the source of their funding.

TIME's email to Wedgewood Polls bounced back.

In another fraudulent polling operation uncovered in August, a company called Median Strategies fabricated polls for races in Wisconsin, Nevada, and California and similarly called the ploy a “social experiment” in a statement to the Los Angeles Times.

“It's very troubling that we've had two examples of this within a very short period of time during election campaigns, creating a lot of problems for legitimate polling,” says Michael Traugott, a professor emeritus of political science at the University of Michigan, who previously served as president of the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR).

Here’s what to know about the Wedgewood scandal, and what it says about polling ahead of the pivotal midterm elections.

The polling group called itself a “fictional entity run by a small group of industry veterans” in its statement. Its X account, which contained many of its polls, no longer exists.

G. Elliot Morris, a pollster and journalist who works at FiftyPlusOne, tells TIME that he and his colleagues had been suspicious about a “crop of new polling firms” that have emerged and released anonymous data. An individual eventually tipped off FiftyPlusOne to Wedgewood's polling, which was “the straw that broke the camel's back for us,” he says.

FiftyPlusOne said in a public statement that Wedgewood “operated as if it were a legitimate polling firm” by collecting money from donors to carry out crowd-funded polls and share its survey data.

The poll analysis group said Wedgewood disclosed two names in connection to the company: Jack Sims and Andrew Prater. According to FiftyPlusOne, Sims had prior experience in the polling industry. SoCal Strategies, another polling firm, this week acknowledged in an email to an NPR affiliate in Michigan that Sims had worked there, but is not currently employed by the company and “has not been for a year.”

“It is not atypical for a pollster to leave an existing firm, form their own, and self-fund some surveys to build reputation; we had no reason at the time to believe this case was any different,” FiftyPlusOne said regarding Sims.

“This event does not help what is a decades-long trend of declining confidence in the polling industry,” Morris added, when asked about Sims’ involvement with Wedgewood. TIME has been unable to reach Sims for comment.

The polling scandal has sparked a flurry of reactions within the politics and government sector, with one former Senate hopeful claiming the numbers circulated by Wedgewood contributed to the collapse of her campaign.

Democratic Michigan state Sen. Mallory McMorrow recalled how a poll from Wedgewood had shown her “plummeting into single digits.”

Wedgewood released polling on June 29 that placed McMorrow as having the support of only 9% of registered voters, leaving her trailing behind Haley Stevens and Abdul El-Sayed. Other polls also showed McMorrow in third place with single digits. A Susquehanna Polling survey conducted June 9 to 14 put McMorrow at 9%, although 49% of Democratic primary voters surveyed were undecided. A July 1 Quantus Insights poll put her at under 8%. McMorrow suspended her campaign on July 5, just one month before the primaries.  In a statement posted to social media on Oct. 20, McMorrow said donations “dried up” and she was “hounded” with calls to exit the race after the circulation of the “infamous” Wedgewood poll numbers.

Advising others not to trust the polls, McMorrow said she hoped Democrats are ready to “blow up the system with campaign finance and transparency reforms.”

Jennifer Holdsworth, a Democratic strategist who ran a SuperPAC that supported McMorrow, claimed the fake poll had been “used by several donors to pull back funding at a time we were being outspent by $80 million.”

Amid widespread discussions over fabricated data, Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Chair Brendan Carr said, without naming any specific instance, that there has been a “significant surge” in fake polls and blamed news outlets for “amplifying or airing” them “without regard to basic journalistic ethics.”

Democratic FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez quipped back and shifted the onus onto fraudulent pollsters themselves.

“Pollsters who intentionally mislead the public should answer for it, but broadcasters who report publicly available polling data should never be punished when a poll is wrong,” she said in response to Carr. “FCC rules on news distortion or hoaxes do not apply to a station that reports this data in good faith.”

While documented fake polls are rare, according to experts, polling in general often plays a large role in shaping the narrative of hotly contested races.

Traugott tells TIME that polls can affect voting behavior and support for campaigns “because by and large, the average citizen is interested in backing the winner, and backing means not just votes; it also means financial contributions, contributing time and effort to the campaign, and the like.”

Research has demonstrated what is known as the “bandwagon effect,” in which voters become more likely to vote for candidates or issues that are more favored in polls. A 2023 study from the Journal of Elections, Public Opinion and Parties showed that polls discouraged support for less popular ballot items, or as the paper’s authors put it: “polls demobilize the minority and mobilize the majority.”

It also found that moderate voters, who often represent a crucial demographic of voters and can swing tight elections, were the most impressionable when it comes to being influenced by polling. “Social conformity is strongest among ideological moderates,” the study said.

This effect has been documented going back decades.

A 1996 experiment showed that participants who were shown a fabricated poll ahead of that year’s primary had a “significantly greater tendency” to vote for the candidates favored by the poll.

This phenomenon has similarly been demonstrated in prediction markets, which have been shown to sway voters almost as much as polls. A study released on Oct 20 by policy think-tank the Brennan Center showed that voters were 10% more likely to say that a candidate would win in an election if that person had higher odds of winning based on a prediction market.

Likewise, voters were less likely to believe that a candidate would win if their odds were predicted to be lower. These prediction market odds, the study said, had a similar effect on perceived electability as traditional polls.

Traugott says that generally speaking, voters who identify with a specific party are unlikely to change their vote as a result of polling, but that they might not show up to the polls at all if they see their party’s nominee is disfavored to win.

“It's often the case in the general election that the polls have an influence in terms of turnout,” he says.

Michael Dimock, the president of the Pew Research Center, says that in tight races, of which there are many in the upcoming midterms, “it's very easy for polls to ‘pick the wrong winner.’”

“I do think that people should be very cautious in over-interpreting pre-election polls' ability to project an election outcome, whether it's at a national level, state level, a district level, [or] local level,” he tells TIME.

One of the main reasons for this, Dimock contends, is that even if participants in a survey say who they would vote for, they still might not show up to cast their ballot. He refers to this immeasurable but important factor as “enthusiasm differences.”

“What polls are really bad at capturing is who's going to actually show up and cast a ballot. It's really hard,” Dimock adds. “Human beings are bad at predicting their [own] future behaviors.”

Recent weeks have brought red flag after red flag for investment in AI and the related data center boom. Bond yields soared for AI infrastructure giant CoreWeave, a sign that investors view the buildout as increasingly risky. And Meta’s reporting of negative free cash flow for the quarter to fund its infrastructure buildout while saying it might need to raise even more capital to fund its AI buildout not only hit its own stock but contributed to a price decline across the big tech companies.

These developments raised questions across Wall Street about whether too much is being spent too quickly on data center expansion. How questions over data center investment play out has big implications for how companies and investors approach the future of energy and, by extension, climate. A pullback in the pace of new data centers could ease pressure to build fossil generation, but it would also weaken the commercial case for clean energy technologies that need big, reliable buyers who are willing to pay a premium for power.

Most obviously, a dent in the AI buildout would translate directly into a slowdown in related electricity demand growth. In advanced economies like the U.S., data centers will account for 20% of that growth through 2030. By then, data centers are expected to account for 3% of global electricity demand, according to data from the International Energy Agency.

Making sense of the climate and energy economy.

Some climate advocates may initially rejoice at slowing electricity demand. Even as renewable energy expands, grids around the world still rely on fossil fuels. In the U.S. in particular, AI demand has sparked a wave of building new natural gas infrastructure. In the short term, slowing demand would mean lower emissions from the existing system than today’s baseline assumptions and less investment in new natural gas infrastructure.

But a slowing data center build out leaves less mature technologies at risk. From advanced geothermal to new ways of producing nuclear power, big tech companies have been willing to pay more to lock in reliable power sources, thereby accelerating the market for these new efforts. Even unproven technologies like nuclear fusion have received a big financial boost from tech firms speculating that they might help solve their power challenges and give them a competitive leg up in the AI race. In a tighter financial environment with lower projected electricity demand, both the incentive and the financial capacity to make bold bets diminish.

To be clear, data centers are not the only source of growing electricity demand. New manufacturing continues to create the need for power. The need for air conditioning has ramped up in many emerging markets, particularly in the Global South, creating new grid pressures. And electric vehicles continue to take off in many markets outside the U.S.

Even as gas remains resilient, much of the power built to meet demand will come from clean sources, including and especially solar and battery storage. Solar is increasingly cost competitive. And this year’s spike in fuel prices—particularly natural gas—has made local power more appealing. But the companies behind growing sectors like air conditioning and EVs are less involved in local power systems and lack the capital to really shape them like the world’s wealthiest tech companies have. In short, clean energy will take a hit without the backing of big tech.

It’s too soon to tell whether, in the long run, this week’s AI infrastructure recalibration will be a blip in time or part of a much broader realignment. But it’s something that anyone invested in clean energy and climate should be watching closely.

ChatGPT can give decent financial advice — to the people who need it the least.

A complaint I hear a lot from my millennial peers is that our parents didn’t teach us enough about money. I can say that personal finance wasn’t dinner table conversation in my house, and even as I went off to college and started fending for myself, it was almost a taboo subject. When it came to money, people either had it or they didn’t, and you definitely didn’t talk about it.

Maybe this is why the average American isn’t exactly fluent in personal finance, and the situation is getting worse. Americans can only answer about 50 percent of basic questions about borrowing, savings, and risk on a financial literacy survey, according to a 2026 study by TIAA, a retirement services company. About a third of those surveyed said they have a hard time making ends meet and are debt-constrained. These figures have remained relatively unchanged for nearly a decade, but dropped to their lowest level in this year’s study.

A weekly dispatch to make sure tech is working for you, instead of overwhelming you. From senior technology correspondent Adam Clark Estes.

It makes sense: It’s hard to learn about money when it’s not taught in schools, and it’s such an intensely private topic. It’s also hard to make good financial decisions when you’re one of the growing number of American households struggling to pay bills, repay debts, and save for the future — and feeling very stressed about it.

In a perfect world, each of us could sit down with a financial adviser to get help — but most of us can’t afford that. Instead, average Americans tend to find personal finance tips on TikTok, in Reddit threads, by watching Suze Orman on YouTube or How to Get Rich on Netflix, or by Googling their questions.

With access to an internet’s worth of information and financial advice, ChatGPT, Claude, and other AI chatbots can answer any question you ask. They can help you put together a basic budget or, armed with your bank statements and the details of your student loans and your retirement accounts, tailor solutions to your specific situation. Or, at least, that’s the promise.

AI could create a more utopian, more equitable future, where reliable, hyper-personalized advice is just a prompt or two away for people who can’t afford to meet with an expert in real life. But machines are built by people, and there’s good reason to be concerned that these machines will simply replicate the biases and disparities built into our current system.

Here’s what experts say about how to make the most of these tools — and how to think about all of the inherent risks of uploading a lot of personal financial information to a chatbot or, worse, giving an AI agent the keys to your bank account.

Nearly 20 percent of Americans have used AI tools like ChatGPT for financial advice, and close to 25 percent for Gen Z adults and millennials have tried it, according to an August Gallup poll. These numbers could tick up too: In October, OpenAI started rolling out a dedicated personal finance tool to its ChatGPT Free and Go users in the United States, which effectively means anyone can try it. ChatGPT Finances lets you securely connect your bank account to the tool using a platform called Plaid, and then you can basically have a conversation with the chatbot about your real-time finances down to the penny. It’s like an AI-powered Mint (RIP) that you can talk to directly.

Regardless of where you encounter a chatbot or how much data you give it, experts say these tools could genuinely help improve financial literacy. You can talk to them about money problems you’re too embarrassed to discuss with friends and family, or ask questions and receive actionable, personalized information. There are real upsides to using AI this way.

Taha Choukhmane, an associate professor of finance at the MIT Sloan School of Management, has been studying outcomes when people turn to chatbots for financial advice. In general, these AI tools give pretty good advice, and that advice gets better as people become more practiced in asking the right question, according to research he published earlier this year.

“We were actually quite surprised that relative to the status quo, the average American would be better off if they were to follow the advice,” Choukhmane told me. But he added a caveat: “Just following the advice without understanding the advice puts you at risk of not fully understanding the risk.”

But you shouldn’t trust everything a chatbot tells you and should verify everything before making any decisions.

Chatbot advice has some significant limitations. First, it’s not impartial. The finance industry has a vested interest in steering you toward certain ways of managing your money, and that typically means selling you products, like loans. If you’ve ever searched for personal finance tips on Google, you’ve probably noticed that a lot of the top results come from banks and other financial institutions. This is by design, according to John Campbell, an economics professor at Harvard. But because AI is aggregating all of that advice, the specific influences and possible bias is less obvious.

“AI is very promising for providing low-cost financial advice in plain English that will arguably be better than what many people would be able to do on their own,” said Campbell, who’s also the co-author of Fixed: Why Personal Finance Is Broken and How to Make It Work for Everyone. “One caveat is that chatbot advice is quite likely — unless a model has been carefully trained — to reflect the average of whatever’s being posted on the internet, and that may or may not be first-rate advice.”

An example Campbell gives is the question of when to start taking Social Security benefits. While you’re eligible at age 62, many economists agree that you should delay your benefits, which, based on the way the government calculates your monthly payouts, will allow you to enjoy much higher benefits later in life. But paid financial advisers can recommend starting as early as possible, since waiting longer reduces the amount of money that needs to be managed, which reduces the adviser’s fees. That means there’s a lot of conflicting advice online, much of it supplied by the finance industry. And that’s the information used to train AI models.

“The advice is much more mixed,” Campbell said. “And so if a chatbot is learning from that, it may not actually give the best advice.”

It can be hard to recognize bad advice anywhere in life, but especially when you’re talking to a machine that’s programmed to please you. A chatbot could encourage you to consider a balance transfer, to lean in on credit card rewards, or to refinance your mortgage — all decisions with upsides and downsides for you, but definite upsides for banks — without you realizing how much of its training data comes from the finance industry selling these products. What’s worse is that if you do end up taking bad advice, it might be years before you realize it. You won’t know that you refinanced a mortgage at a bad time until a better time comes along.

That leads me to the other major issue with using chatbots for money advice: People who lack financial literacy are the most likely to fall for bad advice, because they simply don’t recognize it as such. They also tend to be less wealthy, which means they can’t afford big mistakes.

One way to mitigate risk, oddly enough, is to give the AI chatbot more personal information. The more details about your situation it has to consider, the more it can offer personalized advice. More context also means better advice, according to Choukhmane.

“There’s a trade-off there,” Choukhmane said. “There’s real risk around privacy, and so I think everyone needs to find their level of control.”

Any time you’re talking to a chatbot, you’re putting a lot of trust in tech companies to take care of the personal details you’re sharing. Sending sensitive financial data is an especially risky proposition. While tech companies, including OpenAI, promise to keep your data secure, there’s already a long list of breaches, leaks, and privacy violations that serve as cautionary tales.

It’s also impossible to know what tech companies might do with your data in the future. Most of them will use it to train their models, unless you tell them not to. Some of them will use the data to serve you personalized ads.

Then there’s the risk of bad actors getting into your ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini account. If you’ve been using a chatbot for financial advice and uploading your bank statements, a hacker could gain access to a stunning amount of information that could enable them to do all kinds of terrible things to your life.

“They could steal your identity and open accounts and then quickly bankrupt a person,” said Delicia Reynolds Hand, a senior director at Consumer Reports. “It’s not even so much the primary underlying accounts that could be at risk, it’s more just access to this information.”

The steady march of AI progress is making this situation even more complicated. The previous era of chatbots offering financial advice is quickly bleeding into a new phase of AI agents logging into bank accounts on users’ behalf. If you’re using a tool like Claude Cowork or even an AI assistant like Muse, you can give an agent the login credentials to your bank account, and because the agent is operating its own computer, it can actually make transfers, apply for loans, or really do anything in your account. This is reportedly driving major banks wild, since they don’t know which logins are coming from humans and which are agents. And because we know that AI agents are unpredictable, it’s hard to know how that access might go wrong.

Beyond data security and privacy, you should also be aware of the simple fact that nobody’s really at fault if you end up losing money using these tools. Chatbots are not regulated in the way that human financial advisers, who have a fiduciary duty to act in your best interest, are.

“If I get bad tax advice from H&R Block or a tax adviser, there are a clear set of rules and guardrails in place to manage what I can expect in that relationship and where the service they use has liability and where that liability ends,” Hand said. “Currently, that’s not the case for these tools.”

In the final years of the Cold War, Ronald Reagan popularized an old Russian proverb: Trust, but verify. The phrase is somewhat nonsensical, since trusting someone implies that you don’t need to verify what they’re saying, and verifying what’s said implies you didn’t trust them in the first place. But if you are willing to shoulder a bit of risk, consider approaching AI this way, especially when your life savings are on the line. I’d also suggest adding a coda to the phrase: And then delete your data.

Chatbots are not stand-ins for licensed financial advisers, he said, and you shouldn’t let them make decisions for you.

The great paradox with tools like ChatGPT and Claude is that they’re very good at generating truthy text. It sounds authoritative and looks correct — and usually is. Except when it isn’t. AI is famously unpredictable and prone to hallucinations. You can ask the same question twice and get two different answers. That doesn’t mean these tools are useless. It just means you have to approach them with caution. You have to interrogate these chatbots, challenge whatever advice they give you, and then crosscheck that with other, more reputable, sources.

“If you’ve never used these tools, I would not encourage you to just change all your financial plans based on your first conversation,” Choukhmane said. “You want to be quite careful. Start using these tools, start understanding, ask a lot of follow-up questions.”

When it comes to mitigating risk, consider giving ballpark figures for your income or spending when you’re talking to a chatbot about your finances. Uploading financial documents, even if they’re redacted, is inevitably filling a tech company’s vault with your sensitive information without necessarily knowing what will happen to it next. You should also dig into your privacy and security settings to make sure you’ve turned on multi-factor authentication and that your personal data is not being used to train AI models and gets deleted after 30 days.

Ethan Bloch, the product lead for ChatGPT for Financial Services, made a similar set of recommendations when I talked to him recently. Chatbots are not stand-ins for licensed financial advisers, he said, and you shouldn’t let them make decisions for you. They’re designed to inform and help you think through complex topics.

“You should liken it to just talking to a friend who has some very informed opinions about finance,” Bloch told me. “But if you’re actually making some large financial decision — your portfolio or retirement — you still need to seek out the help.”

Regardless of how experienced you are with chatbots or how much data you’re prepared to hand over, remember what Campbell, the Harvard professor, said. If your financial literacy isn’t great, AI chatbots can be trouble. And if you’re like most Americans, you could probably stand to learn a little bit more about things like mortgages and interest rates.

Yes, Chatbots can be a good way to brush up on financial literacy. You can ask them the money questions you’re too embarrassed to discuss with friends and family. But you can also learn about money the old-fashioned way: Reading books and websites written by reputable, independent sources. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. has a portal of financial education resources, including games and quizzes. Khan Academy has a course that covers everything from credit scores to college savings. There’s a personal finance subreddit that I joined years ago and read almost daily. There are budgeting apps, like YNAB which has its own huge Reddit presence.

Whatever you do to learn more about personal finance, simply doing something is progress. Talking about personal finance isn’t the taboo it once was in the US, and it’s even a required course in the vast majority of American high schools now. And while AI won’t magically solve your money problems, it can help get you started. Just be careful. Your livelihood could be on the line.

How I learned to stop worrying and love the culatello

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When I met Scott Lem — a hobbyist charcutier, or meat curer — at his small, makeshift prep space in South Pasadena last week, I had about a million questions for him.

I was there to help break down a pig leg, which we would then salt and turn into culatello and fiocco, two Italian cured meats. Before we could even pick up our knives, I started grilling him on every step of the process.

What salt percentages do you use? Do you have to cure it in plastic bags? How do you control the humidity during drying?

Truth is, I’ve wanted to cure meat at home for a long time, but I’ve always been too intimidated to take the plunge.

Why? Well, I think I’ve never totally understood what’s going on when you preserve meat — the levers to pull, the variables to control and how to control them, the safety.

Preservation was really my entry point into cooking (“The Art of Fermentation” by Sandor Katz was one of the first cookbooks I ever owned), though my first at-home fermentation experiments were hit or miss.

I turned apple scraps into apple cider vinegar (a fly got in and left a school of revolting little wrigglers swimming through my brew), attempted mead (used bread yeast instead of wine yeast and it exploded) and made kimchi out of cauliflower and then used that to inoculate cream for cultured butter (tasted like pizza, kind of awesome). I made gravlax, hot sauce, ginger bugs, bad mustard, mushy pickles.

I fell in love with the fermentation process and soon my kitchen was cluttered with bubbling, odorous jars. When it worked, it was magical.

More than just the magic, I was chasing this romantic vision of myself as the type of person that always has something unique, delicious and homemade tucked away, ready to be shared with guests.

Early in my preservation journey, I met someone just like that.

Some close friends and I were staying at an acquaintance’s old stone house in a small, forgotten village in northern Italy. One night, we made dinner for our host, and as we sat down to eat he ducked down to the cellar and returned with a gallon of local wine, a hunk of mite-infested cheese from a neighbor, and a mysterious, thick, milky-white block wrapped loosely in parchment.

It was lardo — cured pork fat — which I’d never had before. He sliced it transparently thin and told me to stick out my tongue. Never one to say no to a new friend’s request, I obliged, and he gently laid a slice in my mouth. It melted immediately into pure, porky, salty, Hitchcock-zoom-inducing richness. That might have been the moment I became determined to cure my own meat.

That was 10 years ago, and though it’s long been at the top of my culinary bucket list, I still haven’t done it.

This is the year that I overcome my fears.

Enter Scott Lem. Lem is mostly self-taught and has made a variety of salumi, forcemeats and cured whole muscles, which he shares at occasional Lem Party pop-ups, which he started with Cynthia Wong, his wife and business partner.

I asked Lem to explain the whole process to me. He did so very patiently.

For whole muscle curing, Lem butchers the meat, rubs it with 2% salt and 0.25% curing salt (nitrate for long-curing meats, nitrite for shorter curing) by weight, then vacuum seals it and puts it in his fridge for a couple weeks to a month to allow the salt to penetrate evenly through the muscle. Then he cases the meat (often in what can only be called a “beef bung”), dunks the whole thing into a mold solution and allows it to air dry at room temperature for a day. Then, he dries the meat, hanging it in a refrigerator he rigged to control the temperature (aiming for 45 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit) and humidity (aiming for 75% to 80%) until it has lost 35% to 40% of its weight, which might take four to six months. Then it’s often vacuum sealed again to allow the remaining moisture content to balance throughout the entire piece of meat. Finally, it’s ready to eat.

When I’d finished interrogating Lem, I felt like I was finally starting to understand the principles of meat curing — the variables, the levers, the safety.

Basically, you’re trying to get enough salt into the meat so that it’s protected from bad bacteria. The curing salt (which contains nitrates) helps to prevent botulism, and the mold on the outside helps draw out moisture and outcompete other unwanted surface bacteria. The drying process and humidity slowly reduces the water content of the muscle, which aids in preservation, as excess water would make it more likely to spoil.

After we’d finished breaking down the pig leg (Lem let me do this part — I did … OK), then salting and bagging the culatello and fiocco, Lem and Wong invited me to sit down for a meal.

Lem cut thin slices off a couple finished culatelli, some fiocco and some coppa. The meat — truly — was incredible. Among the finest cured meats I’ve had in recent memory.

As we ate together, I realized I’d found myself in another one of those beautiful moments where someone had welcomed me into their space and shared their work and pride and homemade creation with me.

I learned a lot from Lem. Am I going to buy a whole pig’s leg next week? Probably not. But maybe I’ll buy a coppa. Or it might be more appropriate to start with a nice big chunk of lardo.

Por qué los jóvenes dan el salto de nenúfar

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Cambian más de trabajo para ganar experiencia y ascender.

En los últimos 13 años, Simone Stolzoff ha tenido muchos empleos. Antes de su puesto actual, trabajó en márketing para una empresa de tecnología, fue director de comunicaciones en una start up, jefe de diseño en un estudio, redactor en una revista y reportero. Cada cambio le ha aportado nuevas experiencias, aunque no siempre haya supuesto un ascenso. “En lugar de peldaños en una escalera, [mis cambios de carrera] se asemejan más a una serie de hojas de nenúfar; cada salto representa una nueva oportunidad de crecimiento”, afirma. “Algunos cambios conllevaron aumentos salariales, y otros recortes. He estado dispuesto a ganar menos dinero a cambio de encontrar un trabajo más significativo”.

Su caso no es una excepción. En el actual entorno laboral marcado por la incertidumbre, muchos jóvenes recurren al cambio frecuente de empleo como una alternativa estratégica al trabajo para toda la vida. Este enfoque –denominado en recursos humanos salto de nenúfar– prioriza la adquisición de nuevas competencias y experiencias mediante desplazamientos laterales a través de puestos de corta duración en diversas organizaciones y sectores. Esta estrategia parte de la premisa de que una trayectoria profesional gratificante no es algo que ofrezca un empleador a largo plazo, sino algo que cada trabajador debe buscar por su cuenta.

La permanencia media de un empleado de la Generación Z durante sus primeros cinco años de carrera es de 1,1 años, frente a los 1,8 años de los Millennials y los casi tres años de las generaciones de mayor edad, según una encuesta de Randstad.

Izzy Clements, de 30 años, confía en que una mayor variedad de experiencias le ayude a progresar, tras pasar del área de ventas a los departamentos de eventos y márketing en su empresa. Aunque al cambiar de puesto aceptó una rebaja salarial, ocho meses después consiguió un ascenso. “No descarto estudiar oportunidades para cambiar de sector o de empresa”. Una gran aspiración, comenta, es quizás llegar a ocupar un puesto de dirección en el área de marketing o de campañas.

Sander van ‘t Noordende, consejero delegado de Randstad, señala que los trabajadores jóvenes están mostrando una mayor decisión con respecto a sus trayectorias profesionales, buscando empleadores que inviertan en su desarrollo y mirando oportunidades en otros lugares si un puesto no ofrece posibilidades de progreso. “Tienen un fuerte deseo de crecimiento continuo y son muy conscientes de la rapidez con la que evolucionan las competencias”, afirma. “Estamos viendo cómo la tradicional escalera corporativa está dando paso a trayectorias profesionales diversificadas, en las que las personas deciden activamente ampliar sus habilidades y funciones en lugar de seguir un único camino lineal”.

Los empleadores se están adaptando para responder a estas prioridades cambiantes, señala Dan Black, líder de estrategia global de talento en EY. A medida que más empleados buscan cambiar de puesto para desarrollar sus propias competencias, la consultora ha tenido que esforzarse más por “mantener su relevancia”.

“Somos más conscientes de la necesidad de ofrecer [a los empleados] espacio y tiempo para aprender cosas nuevas, ya sea mediante clases formales de formación, orientación informal o actividades centradas en el bienestar general”, afirma Black. “Queremos fomentar oportunidades para que las personas exploren y realicen tareas diferentes”.

En la empresa británica de cosméticos Lush, Karen Blackburn, responsable de talento, comenta que los empleados más jóvenes son ambiciosos y tienen un gran deseo de aprender. La compañía cuenta con una base de datos interna de talento con las competencias de los empleados, que permite asignarles proyectos a corto plazo en otras áreas de la empresa. “Vemos cómo los empleados más jóvenes dejan Lush para aplicar sus conocimientos o seguir desarrollándose en otros lugares. Sin embargo, bastantes regresan más tarde a trabajar en Lush en áreas que les apasionan o en las que han adquirido nuevas competencias”.

Para algunos jóvenes, esta práctica de ir saltando de un puesto a otro es una forma de sacar el máximo provecho a su situación. Danielle Nicholson, una orientadora profesional de 29 años residente en Detroit, lo interpreta como un intento de “recuperar aunque sea una pequeña cantidad de poder en una economía implacable”.

Al realizar una serie de movimientos profesionales laterales y deliberados en un periodo de tiempo relativamente corto, los trabajadores pueden acceder a oportunidades que, de otra forma, no estarían al alcance de personas ajenas a la organización. “La Generación Z está respondiendo a una economía que nos trata como si fuéramos prescindibles”, afirma. “En lugar de dar por sentado que un solo empleador brindará seguridad a largo plazo, muchos profesionales están tratando de crear esa seguridad por sí mismos”, concluye Nicholson.

Scientists Identify a New Type of Depression That May Feel Like Permanent Jet Lag

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new study suggests nearly one in four depression patients may experience chronic body clock disruption.

For some people with depression, every day feels like waking up with jet lag but without the flight. They sleep longer, wake exhausted, and find themselves most alert when the rest of the world is winding down. Their problem may not simply be how much they sleep or how poorly, but when their internal clock tells them to.

In an analysis of 11,380 Australians with a history of depression, researchers identified 2,604 people, or 23 percent, whose sleep habits and symptoms fit a proposed new subtype of depression called circadian depression. The patients developed depression younger, experienced more severe symptoms, and found common antidepressants less helpful.

In other words, a large portion of the population may experience depression due to their body’s internal clock, which perhaps is not in sync with the tune of modern life, rather than due to anxiety.

Our internal timekeeping systems, or circadian clocks, respond to light and help regulate sleep, appetite, alertness and mood.

“When it’s off, you feel jet-lagged all the time,” Ian Hickie, a University of Sydney psychiatrist and senior author of the study, told ScienceAlert.

Depression can look strikingly different from one patient to another. Some cannot sleep; others sleep excessively. Some lose their appetite; others gain weight. Yet psychiatrists often begin treatment with similar medications, sometimes trying several before finding one that helps.

Only about 30% of patients experience full relief from their very first antidepressant medication. Around one-third of people do not respond to standard antidepressant drugs overall.

Researchers at the University of Sydney wanted to investigate whether a subset of patients might share a particular vulnerability owed to disruption of their internal clocks.

The investigation goes well beyond poor sleep making people miserable. Someone whose internal clock runs late may feel wide awake at midnight but exhausted at 8 a.m. When school or work demands an earlier schedule, the mismatch can resemble living in a different time zone without ever traveling.

To investigate, the team examined questionnaires and genetic data from the Australian Genetics of Depression Study. The 11,380 participants predominantly had severe or recurring depression.

Researchers looked for six clues: a strong preference for evenings, unusually late sleep timing, sleeping more than usual during depression, feeling very tired on waking, seasonal changes in mood and behavior, and social jet lag.

The latter refers to the discrepancy between biological time, determined by our internal body clock, and social times, mainly dictated by social obligations such as school or work. Someone might force themselves awake at 7 a.m. during the week but naturally sleep until 11 a.m. on weekends.

Participants who met at least three of the six criteria entered the circadian group. The most common combination involved excessive sleep during depression, pronounced morning exhaustion and an evening preference.

Within this proposed subset, depression had begun at an average age of 18.7 in the circadian group, versus 23.2 among the others.

More than half reported at least ten depressive episodes, compared with just over a third of other patients with more typical depression. They also reported greater psychological distress, more suicidal thinking and more experiences resembling mania or psychosis.

This may be mostly related to genes. Participants in the circadian group had somewhat higher inherited risk scores for ADHD, bipolar disorder and depression, alongside traits associated with inflammation and insulin resistance.

Some symptoms overlapped with atypical depression, a recognized subset that can involve excessive sleeping and weight gain. But more than 1,400 participants with atypical depression did not meet the circadian criteria.

That suggests the two groups are not interchangeable, despite the overlap sometimes.

People in the circadian group rated two common antidepressant classes, SSRIs and SNRIs, as somewhat less effective and reported more side effects.

Previously, research linked evening chronotypes to poorer antidepressant responses. Separately, clinical trials have suggested that bright-light therapy can help some people with depression. Another review of 58 studies found evidence linking improvements in mood to better circadian alignment, particularly among people with delayed internal clocks.

Co-author Jacob Crouse argues that clock-focused treatments, including light therapy and melatonin, deserve consideration earlier in care.

“Because circadian depression is linked to people’s internal body clock, treatments targeting the circadian rhythm, such as light therapy and melatonin, may be much more relevant, and clinicians should reach for these much earlier in people with this presentation,” he said. But no trial has yet shown that people selected by these six criteria benefit more from such treatments

For now, circadian depression remains a promising proposal rather than an established diagnosis. Its practical message is simpler: when depression persists despite treatment, the timing of someone’s sleep may deserve as much attention as its duration.

Some independent experts outside the study question whether the so-called circadian depression group is biologically distinct. Finding genetic markers for late sleeping, for instance, is less surprising when late sleeping helped define the group.

“That means these genetic associations do not independently establish a distinct biological subtype of depression,” Ronan Lordan of the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland told the Science Media Centre.

The practical lesson is not to replace antidepressants with melatonin or a light box. It is to take sleep timing seriously when assessing depression, particularly when treatment fails, and to discuss possible interventions with a clinician.

The findings were reported in The British Journal of Psychiatry.

The Trump administration this week announced an overhaul of the nation’s food stamp payment system, aimed at modernizing benefit cards to protect families from electronic theft. The move comes a week after ProPublica published an investigation showing how $470 million in Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, benefits were stolen from recipients last year alone, in large part because these cards are technologically outdated.

ProPublica’s story highlighted victims’ experiences and examined repeated failures by Congress and the U.S. Department of Agriculture to upgrade Electronic Benefits Transfer, or EBT, cards nationally. ProPublica’s reporting was shared around the department, according to two USDA officials.

The new proposed rule, officially issued by the USDA’s Food and Nutrition Administration on Friday, has been in the works since 2023 — but it was continually delayed until now. Reasons included understaffing at the USDA, especially in the wake of the Department of Government Efficiency’s cuts last year, and a backlog of other regulations that the White House budget office had prioritized. White House and USDA officials did not immediately respond to a request for comment on the sudden shift.

The rule will mandate that all 50 states transition from EBT cards with just a magnetic stripe on the back — which are easy for criminals to skim data from — to ones with digital microchips, which credit and debit cards have had for more than a decade, and tap-to-pay technology. Other changes include creating new federal “SNAP EBT Operating Standards” to be revised on an ongoing basis, so that the feds and states together can do a better job of keeping up with best practices in the credit card industry and the changing tactics of fraudsters.

A handful of states have already transitioned to chip cards on their own, including California, where the theft of SNAP and other public benefits was reduced by 83% in two years after the technology was adopted. But more than 40 states haven’t added chips, and hundreds of thousands of lower-income Americans continue to show up at grocery store checkout lines only to find that their food assistance for the month has been drained by thieves. NPR and other news outlets have also recently reported on this crisis.

Members of the public, including state SNAP administrators and grocery store owners who may have logistical questions about how to overhaul their EBT systems, now have 60 days to submit comments on the new USDA regulation; these will be reviewed by agency officials before the rule is formally enacted. After that, states will have two years to switch to chip cards, although their ability to meet that deadline will depend on several factors. For one, many are budget-strapped right now, due in large part to the fact that President Donald Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act slashed states’ funding for administering their SNAP programs — dollars that they could have used to upgrade their EBT systems and print and mail out the new cards.

Multiple current and former FNA officials, as well as state program administrators, told ProPublica that they are worried the agency is mandating expensive changes for states but that Congress so far has shown little sign it will provide federal funding for the chip card transition. As ProPublica reported last week, the Senate’s version of this year’s Farm Bill includes money for this purpose, but the House version does not; Democrats and Republicans remain at loggerheads about overall SNAP spending, and it is unclear what will happen. The estimated total federal and state cost of the changes is $283 million, as compared with the more than $600 million in SNAP and other benefits that are stolen from existing cards every year.

Still, the mandate that all states implement chip EBT cards “is a big deal for low-income households,” said Justin King, a longtime safety net advocate who is now the policy director for Propel, a company that helps SNAP recipients access their benefit information. He added that he was “legit surprised” by the administration’s decision.

“Theft rings don’t care about state lines. You’re just squeezing a balloon if you update one state at a time,” King said of the prospect of individual states continuing to gradually adopt chip cards without a federal requirement. “A national standard means there’s nowhere left to go” for the criminals, he said.

For the moment, at least, there are signs that the Trump administration’s posture toward this issue has shifted more broadly. On Wednesday, while barnstorming in Ohio for Republican candidates, Vice President JD Vance (whose roles include serving as the administration’s “fraud czar”) called a press conference and made a point of emphasizing that low-income families are often the victims of SNAP fraud.

This was at odds with how Vance has portrayed the problem in the past. “There are people who have Lamborghinis who currently receive SNAP benefits,” he has previously said, in a typical comment. For ProPublica’s previous article, research reporter Gabriel Sandoval reviewed dozens of speeches, social media posts and press accounts of statements made by Vance and other Trump officials, and found that they have consistently portrayed SNAP recipients as the perpetrators of fraud.

Vance also took a startlingly different tone this week toward the food stamp program itself. In his 2016 memoir “Hillbilly Elegy,” which first propelled him to fame, he wrote that SNAP recipients in Kentucky and Ohio “live off of government largesse”; he said that people in that part of the country are largely “hardworking, except of course for the many food stamp recipients who show little interest in honest work.”

But this week from the stage in southern Ohio, he said, “Step back for a minute and realize that the [SNAP] program is one of the most important social service programs that we have in the United States of America.” He went on at length: Food stamps ensure that “all of our fellow citizens, particularly children, are able to have a full belly at the end of every single night, that regardless of whether they can pay for a meal, we don’t let American children and American families go hungry. That is one of the most important and sacred things that our tax dollars go to.”

The White House and Vance’s press office did not immediately respond to a request for comment on his rhetorical about-face.

Vance then had Attorney General Todd Blanche and other top officials give more details on multiple recent SNAP theft prosecutions. These included several ongoing EBT card skimming cases in Oregon in which criminal groups had allegedly pilfered the SNAP account numbers and PINs of legitimate beneficiaries and then used that data to buy bulk quantities of goods like energy drinks and baby formula — which the scammers resold for over $3 million. This is exactly the type of case that ProPublica reported on last week.

“The simple reality is that it is actually a very tough thing to go through a [grocery store checkout] line with kids,” Vance said. “To present that card, to expect to be able to take that food home to your family, to be able to cook it and provide a meal for your kids, and to be told that because of the theft of a fraudster, that you are being rejected. And there are hundreds of thousands of families all across our country, all across the state of Ohio who have had that experience.”

What Did Denisovans Look Like? Scientists Have Their Best Answer Yet

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Ancient proteins are helping scientists put a face to a long-lost branch of humanity.

For years, we’ve mostly known this mysterious human lineage from fossil bits and pieces. Scientists have found fossilized bones, skull parts, they’ve even found traces of their genes in people alive today. But what did the Denisovans look like?

Now, a new study is finally bringing their appearance into focus. By analyzing ancient proteins preserved in fossils from China, researchers have identified more Denisovan remains and pieced together a remarkably detailed picture of their features including massive brow ridges, sloping foreheads, and broad, relatively flat faces.

The findings also suggest something surprising: several ancient human groups that scientists thought were distinct may have been Denisovans all along.

The Denisovan story began in a cave in Siberia. In 2010, scientists analyzed DNA recovered from a tiny finger bone and discovered that it belonged to a previously unknown group of ancient humans. They named them after Denisova Cave, where the bone was found.

Denisovans were close relatives of Neanderthals. They had also interbred with our species, leaving traces of their ancestry in people alive today.

Unlike Neanderthals, whose skeletons have been found at numerous sites, Denisovans were initially known from only a handful of fragments. Scientists could read their genetic history more easily than they could picture their faces.

That began to change in 2025, when researchers showed that a nearly complete skull from Harbin, northeastern China, belonged to a Denisovan. The fossil, nicknamed “Dragon Man” and previously described as Homo longi, offered a rare look at the shape of a Denisovan head.

The latest discovery adds another crucial piece.

At Xujiayao in northern China, archaeologists excavated 20 human fossil specimens between 1976 and 1979. The roughly 199,000–138,000-year-old remains included skull and jaw fragments whose place on the human family tree remained disputed.

The DNA didn’t survive. But the new team, led by researchers in China, turned to something else: proteins preserved inside bone.

They recovered 18 ancient proteins from two fossils. In both specimens, they found a distinctive version of a collagen protein previously identified in confirmed Denisovans, but not in Neanderthals or modern humans. A separate analysis of the protein sequences also grouped the Xujiayao fossils with Denisovans.

Taken together, the Xujiayao remains and the Harbin skull suggest a few traits. Denisovans had long, low skulls, heavy brow ridges and foreheads that sloped backward. Their faces were relatively broad and flat through the cheeks, with little forward projection around the mouth. Their nasal openings were broad, and their skull bones thick.

So, they weren’t just Neanderthals living farther east. They shared some features with Neanderthals but differed in the back of the skull and parts of the jaw. Some features also resembled the much older Homo erectus, while others looked surprisingly modern.

Of course, these facial reconstructions can never be perfect; there’s a lot of information you can’t derive from bones, DNA, and proteins. For example, we can only infer their skin tone, hairstyle or exact facial appearance. Any lifelike image necessarily involves artistic interpretation. But it’s the first time we have a more complete idea of what Denisovans looked like.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia, for Nature.

The bones now reveal far more than scientists could have inferred from the original Siberian finger bone. And they may explain another longstanding mystery.

For decades, researchers encountered ancient human fossils across China that seemed to fit nowhere neatly. Some were classified as early modern humans. Others were treated as Neanderthal relatives or members of separate species.

The Xujiayao fossils themselves were recently placed in a proposed species called Homo juluensis, partly because of their enormous estimated braincase capacity of about 1,700 cubic centimeters. Meanwhile, the Harbin skull had been assigned to Homo longi.

The new study argues that differences such as skull size may not justify dividing these fossils into separate evolutionary branches. After comparing the bones from these regions, the scientists suggest we’re simply dealing with a varied Denisovan lineage.

This is still far from a settled issue, and the conclusion is stronger for the specimens identified through proteins or DNA than for those linked only by resemblance. But this would explain why scientists just weren’t finding any Denisovan remains; they were, but they didn’t realize they’re Denisovans.

The accumulating discoveries also suggest that Denisovans were remarkably adaptable.

Fossils and molecular evidence now place them across a huge swath of Asia, from Siberia to the Tibetan Plateau, northeastern China and as far south as the Taiwan Strait. The authors suggest they may have occupied parts of East Asia over hundreds of thousands of years, across radically different environments.

They weren’t a small group confined to a Siberian cave. They were a widespread part of humanity’s history.

And their story didn’t end entirely with extinction. Ancient interbreeding left Denisovan DNA in modern humans, particularly among populations in Oceania and parts of Asia. One inherited Denisovan-related genetic variant even helps some Tibetans cope with oxygen-poor air at high altitudes.

We still can’t know exactly what Denisovans were like. But scientists are no longer working with a faceless genome.

A population once revealed by a tiny finger bone is gradually emerging as a group of real people, with distinctive faces, varied lives and a place in our own ancestry. Real people who are now a part of our own genetic heritage.
