Президент Соединенных Штатов Дональд Трамп выразил уверенность, что договоренности с Россией о продаже зарубежных активов компании «Лукойл» и сотрудничестве в сфере добычи природного газа на Аляске рано или поздно будут достигнуты. Об этом он заявил журналистам на военной базе Эндрюс перед вылетом в Мичиган, передает ТАСС.

Говоря об украинском кризисе, президент США добавил, что конфликт необходимо завершить. При этом конкретных сроков заключения энергетических сделок он не назвал.

Не так давно руководство Министерства финансов Соединенных Штатов продлило генеральную лицензию на продажу зарубежного бизнеса российской нефтяной компании «Лукойл». Теперь в качестве крайнего срока поиска покупателя обозначено 21 ноября.

Ранее официальный представитель Кремля Дмитрий Песков заявил, что президент России Владимир Путин обозначил тему трехсторонних переговоров по украинскому кризису, которые состоятся в Абу-Даби. Он уточнил, что это вопросы безопасности для России и Украины после выхода на мирное урегулирование.

William Diaz has a mantra: His only political party is his Venezuelan community.

He doesn’t abide by strict partisan rules, and doesn't believe in only voting for Democrats or Republicans. He supported Republicans Sen. Rick Scott and Secretary of State Marco Rubio when they were running for office in Florida, where Diaz has lived for 37 years.

For these midterm elections, however, the Orlando resident hopes to make a clear statement: He does not support President Donald Trump’s immigration policies and is unhappy with how Republican-backed initiatives have affected his community.

“I voted already, and what I did when I was voting and when I was with my ballot, I was really thinking and praying for all those that has no voice, those that can not vote, those immigrants that are right now victim of actions on the streets,” Diaz tells TIME, referring to Trump’s mass deportation agenda and the expansive influence of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). “My people have been suffering that much.”

Diaz is one of more than 2.6 million people who have already cast ballots in Florida, either by mail or through in-person early voting, according to state figures released Sunday. In-person early voting began in some counties Oct. 19 and expanded statewide Oct. 24.

Diaz’s community is one part of a Florida Latino electorate with varied national origins and political loyalties. Taken together, however, Latino voters are a swing vote in several closely watched races statewide. They were key to Trump’s Florida victory in 2024, with 56% supporting him over Kamala Harris, according to the 2024 American Electorate Voter Poll.

But new polls ahead of Election Day suggest that Latino support for the GOP may be on shaky ground—a shift that could put Republicans to the test in Florida and shape the political landscape beyond November.

Dissatisfaction among Florida’s Latino voters reflects a broader national mood, according to Stephanie Valencia, a co-founder of Equis Research, an organization that studies Latino voting behavior.

Concerns about the economy and immigration enforcement tactics are fueling dissatisfaction among Latino voters, who “make up a huge part of the electorate in Florida,” she says, pointing out that both of these issues are “disproportionately impacting” them.

A Univision/YouGov poll, conducted Oct. 2–15, showed that 41% of registered Latino voters in Florida were “very or somewhat worried” that they, a family member, or someone close could be detained or otherwise directly affected by immigration enforcement, compared with 27% of Florida voters overall.

The same poll showed that 4 in 10 Latino voters in the state felt that their finances had worsened over the past year, with 70% disapproving of Trump’s handling of the cost of living in the U.S. That stands in comparison to a 63% disapproval rate from Florida voters overall.

For Johanna Chavez, a Dominican immigrant living in Kissimmee, Florida, the economy is top of mind.

“Many people are concerned about the economy and the price of gasoline … and then there’s the cost of rent, it’s become incredibly, overwhelmingly high,” Chavez, who speaks Spanish, tells TIME through a translator. “All the things they promised would be done—nothing has happened.”

In the past few years, Chavez says it has felt like the United States has started “regressing,” and the opportunities she was looking for when she arrived here have not materialized in the way she had hoped.

“It’s starting to feel just like the situation back in my home country, the Dominican Republic,” she says. “We all came here hoping for change, seeking a better life, but it’s ending up being the same thing all over again. Things just aren’t what they’re supposed to be.”

Jared Nordlund, the Florida State Advocacy Director at UnidosUS, the largest nonprofit Latino rights organization in the U.S., says this is a common refrain among Floridian Latinos; people are upset over how the economy has been affected by tariffs and the war in Iran. Florida was hit particularly hard by rising gas prices after the closure of the Strait of Hormuz squeezed the global energy supply, he says.

“Florida is not a state friendly for public transportation, so people are highly reliant on the cars, and so what they would be paying for food now is having to kind of get broke between … paying to get the gas and then going to get food,” Nordlund says.

The question now is whether that dissatisfaction will translate into votes against Republicans—and whether it will be enough to tip the state’s close races.

Trump’s approval rating has plummeted with Latino voters in Florida, dropping from 58% approval at the start of his term to 36% ahead of midterm elections, according to Equis Research.

According to an AP-NORC poll from October, about 6 in 10 Latino voters disapprove of how Trump is handling the presidency, and more than 7 in 10 disapprove of how he is handling the economy.

A Pew Research Center poll found that Trump’s job approval rating among Latino voters dropped from 38% in February 2025 to 24% in September and October. Of those who backed him in the last election, approval dropped to 57%—down from 93% in early 2025.

The shift may be resonating with Republicans like Rep. Maria Elvira Salazar, who is seeking re-election in Florida’s 27th District in Miami-Dade—a majority Latino district that Trump carried by some 15 points in 2024. She publicly broke with Trump in her campaign and criticized his immigration policies.

In one ad in September, Salazar said that Trump has “gone too far" with his deportation agenda.

“In July, 50% of the 50,000 people that were detained had no criminal record,” she notes in the ad.

“Be careful what your advisers are telling you about immigration policy,” Salazar continues, addressing Trump. “The same Hispanics who helped you get to the White House in 2024 feel betrayed today.”

Guillermo Grenier, a Cuban American professor of sociology at Florida International University (FIU), agrees that many Latino voters are “ticked off” at Trump.

“The population, if you look at the whole Latino conglomerate, is turned off pretty strongly with Trump,” he says.

But South Florida’s Latino electorate is unique; Grenier points out that the state is home to 1.9 million Cuban Americans—the largest concentration in the United States.

As a voting bloc, the community has significant sway. In 2024, FIU’s Cuba poll found that 68% of likely Cuban American voters in Miami-Dade County supported Trump, compared to 23% for Harris—representing his highest-recorded support level in the poll's history.

A shift away from Republicans among Cuban American voters could therefore have significant consequences in South Florida. But disillusionment doesn’t always turn into action, according to Grenier.

“I think a lot of Cubans are pissed off at Trump, but they're still Republicans,” he says.

The Democratic Party’s response to this potential opening may unfold over several election cycles.

“Democrats are going to be in a probationary period with Latinos and with all voters, quite frankly, on whether they can deliver or not,” Valencia says. “One election is not durable support among a particular group.”

Both Valencia and Grenier suggest that it will come down to Democrats proving that they are willing to reinvest in Florida.

There are several key races at stake in Florida. Although the state has leaned reliably red for the past three election cycles, Democrats are investing late in the race through campaigning and financing.

Florida Democrats posted a larger monthly net gain in active registered voters than Republicans, according to figures the party highlighted Oct. 1. The Florida Democratic Party touted the gains as a sign of its growing base in the state.

“We’ve spent years rebuilding this party from the ground up. Our organizers are in communities year-round, our volunteers are knocking doors across this state, and Democrats are having real conversations with Floridians about what’s at stake,” Florida Democratic Party Chair Nikki Fried said in an October statement. “The momentum is real.”

Gov. Ron DeSantis is term-limited and cannot run for re-election, leaving the role open for Byron Donalds, the Republican candidate who has been endorsed by Trump, and David Jolly, who is running as a Democrat after leaving the GOP in 2018.

A poll released Oct. 21 by the Public Opinion Research Lab (PORL) at the University of North Florida (UNF) found that Donalds leads 49% to 47%—within the poll's almost 3-percentage-point margin of error.

Latino voters could be important to Jolly’s chances. The Univision/YouGov poll found that they favored him over Donalds, 48% to 43%, even as Donalds led 50% to 42% among Florida voters overall.

Democrats are taking note. Weeks after Jolly beseeched the Democratic Party to send money to his campaign, the Democratic Governors Association announced Oct. 20 that it will invest $1 million to help close the narrowing gap in the weeks before Election Day.

That trend follows in certain congressional races downballot.

A September poll found Salazar leading Democratic challenger Eliott Rodriguez, a former local news anchor, by just 1 point.

Angie Nixon, a member of the Democratic Socialists of America, is an underdog for Senate, taking on Republican incumbent Sen. Ashley Moody. A recent UNF poll has Moody leading by 49% to 45%, halving the advantage she had in July.

National Democratic leaders and allies have made the trip to Florida to bolster these close races.

Independent Sen. Bernie Sanders, a Democratic ally, rallied in Tampa for Nixon on Oct. 17. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries campaigned with Rodriguez on Oct. 16; they held a campaign event at a Cuban coffee shop and bakery.

The decision received some blowback, with one conservative Cuban influencer taking to Instagram to slam the business for hosting Jeffries. He argued that associating with the Democrats equated to supporting socialism.

For Grenier, the incident shows that Democrats have some serious work to do to gain trust in Florida—and while it might be “too late” to invest in candidates in this election, the party can lay the groundwork for 2028 and beyond.

He said that if Democrats want to build on the momentum after the midterms, they can’t get “gun shy.”

“This new beachhead that [Democrats] are trying to establish is the way to go for the next elections,” Grenier says.

“You know, it was 10 years of investing before Republicans flipped the state in 1996,” he continues. “So, they need to just keep it up, and have national leaders support local leaders.”

Has Healey’s military-style budget secrecy really played out so differently to past budgets?

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Chancellor was said to be worried about market-sensitive leaks, yet significant policies have been heavily trailed in the media

The public has been told repeatedly that John Healey is so concerned about budget leaks that he has set up defence-style “compartmentalisation” to prevent the Treasury’s financial secrets escaping.

The macho military analogies began in mid-August when Healey was said by the Telegraph to be “on a war footing” to stop the drip of information that he believed had beleaguered Rachel Reeves.

The Financial Times added that “military-style protocols” were being deployed, while the Spectator was excited about the use of “classified computer systems” and the i newspaper described a process where officials could sign in to only their parts of the document and could not access the sections they were not working on.

This weekend, the Observer reported that Healey was “flabbergasted” by the lack of security around financial information when he became chancellor and “it was so different to how we did things in the MoD”.

The chancellor is known to have been worried that market-sensitive leaks cost Britain billions in the run-up to the last government’s budgets. But has this time around been so different after all?

Despite the heightened “security” around Wednesday’s budget, many of its announcements have been leaked already from a clear mixture of authorised and unauthorised sources.

It emerged well in advance that there will be a higher tax on banks – a deeply market-sensitive policy – and a package of energy support for the most vulnerable.

There will also be billions of pounds in extra defence spending, directed towards the British Business Bank and National Wealth Fund, as well as a £1bn crackdown on online VAT sales, with big retailers such as Amazon, eBay and Temu to be responsible for collecting what is due from smaller merchants selling on their platforms.

On top of that, there have been stories indicating the level of headroom the chancellor will be looking at – in the region of £15bn – known in the political trade as “pitch rolling” to avoid alarming the markets or the public.

The Treasury and No 10 have also undertaken their traditional stream of embargoed announcements by press release in the days before the fiscal event, such as a new £1.5bn borrowing facility for local authorities and £1bn for social housing, as they seek to build a sense of action and momentum about what the government is trying to achieve.

Former Treasury advisers believe the rigamarole around budget security is partly designed to deter civil servants and gossiping MPs from thinking they can embark on unauthorised chatter before a policy is ready.

But they also say it is unhelpful for the chancellor to produce a hermetically sealed budget, which could cause a backlash over surprise measures – in the markets, the media, among the government’s own MPs and the wider public.

It is often the less-trailed policies that have tended to go wrong, from George Osborne’s pasty tax in 2012 to Philip Hammond’s surprise national insurance hike for the self-employed in 2017 that had to be abandoned within seven days after a furore among Conservative MPs about breaking their manifesto promise.

Aides say there is a balance to be struck – and it’s not always a good idea to hoard information until budget day. “If everything is kept tight, then you end up surprising people. And that’s called a Liz Truss situation,” says one former strategist from the Tory years.

Healey has been shutting down speculation about various tax rises, from higher capital gains tax on certain assets to lowering the mansion tax on properties worth more than £1.5m – showing he has begun to see the virtue in expectation management and limiting surprises on the big day.

There may still be a few fiscal “rabbits out of the hat” on the day, as Healey seeks to combine a “steady-as-she-goes” budget with some eye-catching measures designed to show the public that the government is on their side.

But if the Treasury really has aimed for military levels of secrecy around its budget, the Ministry of Defence might have something to worry about.

Albanien, Belgien, Frankreich, Italien: Spanien: Die Protestwelle erfasst Europa erneut

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Fast 1000 Festnahmen gab es in Frankreich, Gewalt und Verletzte einmal mehr in Albanien oder auch in Barcelona. Hunderte Festnahmen gab es bei Protesten in Italien, wo Menschen auch ins Kolosseum eingedrungen sind.

Bei Protesten in mehreren europäischen Ländern ist es erneut zu Krawallen gekommen. Gewalttätige Zusammenstöße gab es am Wochenende in Frankreich, Albanien, Spanien, Italien und Belgien. Bei Ausschreitungen in Frankreich wurden 1.314 Menschen festgenommen. 53 Polizeikräfte seien verletzt worden, teilte das Innenministerium in einer morgendlichen Bilanz mit. In Italien wurden neun Haftbefehle erlassen und 317 Menschen festgenommen, in Barcelona gab es 128 Festnahmen.

Einsatzkräfte seien gegen 75 versuchte Brandstiftungen vorgegangen und es habe 24 Plünderungen gegeben, hieß es aus dem französischen Innenministerium weiter. Die Nacht sei ruhig verlaufen, hieß es. Frankreichweit waren 70.000 Beamte im Einsatz.

Den seit Wochen für eine bessere Ausstattung von Frankreichs Schulen demonstrierenden jungen Leuten schlossen sich weitere Gruppen an, um etwa gegen zu hohe Lebenshaltungskosten zu demonstrieren. Innenminister Nuñez sagte am Samstag, die Zahl der Festnahmen werde noch steigen, weil noch Bilanz gezogen werden müsse. Es sei abermals zu Sachbeschädigungen und Plünderungen gekommen.

Die eigentlichen Demonstrationen verliefen nach Worten des Innenministers reibungslos. Dennoch kam es am Rande der Protestzüge erneut zu Ausschreitungen. Es hätten sich bei beinahe jeder Versammlung „Risikogruppen“ gebildet, die sich von den Umzügen absetzten. Bilder aus Paris zeigten Wolken von Tränengas und Feuerwerkskörper sowie den Einsatz von Wasserwerfern. Auch auf Bildern aus anderen Städten wie Lyon, Bordeaux und Marseille waren Tränengaswolken und Feuer zu sehen. Der Minister machte insbesondere die „radikale extreme Linke, die Ultralinke“ dafür verantwortlich.

Auch in Albanien kam es bei Protesten gegen Missstände im Hochschulwesen und gegen die Regierung von Ministerpräsident Edi Rama erneut zu Gewalt. Demonstrierende in der Hauptstadt Tirana warfen mit Steinen auf Regierungsgebäude und die Polizei, wie die staatliche Nachrichtenagentur ATA berichtete. Die Lage sei eskaliert, als Protestierende das Umweltministerium in Brand setzten. Auf Fernsehbildern und in sozialen Medien waren chaotische Szenen zu sehen.

Laut ATA hatte der unter anderem von Studierenden organisierte Protest am Samstag vor dem Bildungsministerium begonnen und sich auf andere Ministerien und Teile der Stadt ausgeweitet. Laut dem privaten Fernsehsender Klan warfen Demonstrierende auch Feuerwerkskörper und Molotowcocktails. Die Polizei setzte den Angaben zufolge Wasserwerfer, Tränengas, Pfefferspray und Gummigeschosse ein, um die Menge zu zerstreuen. Demnach gab es Verletzte auf beiden Seiten. Beamte hätten auch das Kamera-Team von Klan News angegriffen, hieß es.

In Barcelona kamen Berichten zufolge Hunderte Jugendliche und junge Erwachsene am Samstagabend bis in die Nacht hinein zu Protesten zusammen. Junge Leute hätten Straßenmobiliar, Fahrräder und Motorräder beschädigt, Geschäfte geplündert und Müllcontainer angezündet, berichteten unter anderem die spanische Tageszeitung „El País“ und der katalanische Fernsehsender Betevé. Das genaue Ziel der Proteste war demnach unklar.

Nach den nächtlichen Ausschreitungen wurden in Barcelona 128 Menschen festgenommen, die meisten davon Minderjährige. Die Festnahmen erfolgten wegen „Vandalismus“, „Beschädigung öffentlichen Eigentums“ und „Plünderung“, teilte die katalanische Polizei am Sonntag mit.

Polizeichefin Sílvia Catà sagte im Sender 3CatInfo, 73 der Festgenommenen seien minderjährig, ihre Akten würden an die Jugendstaatsanwaltschaft weitergeleitet. Nur zwei der 128 Festgenommenen seien schon zuvor straffällig geworden. Insgesamt 15 Beamte wurden Catàs Angaben zufolge bei den Ausschreitungen verletzt.

Der Ministerpräsident der wohlhabenden Region im Nordosten Spaniens, Salvador llla verurteilte die Gewalttaten: „Gewalt hat in Katalonien keinen Platz. Niemals. Protestieren ist ein demokratisches Recht. Angriffe, Einschüchterungen und Sachbeschädigung hingegen nicht“, sagte er laut „El País“.

Auch in Belgien gab es kleinere Demonstrationen in Brüssel und Lüttich. Diese waren laut Innenminister Bernard Quintin ohne größere Zwischenfälle zu Ende gegangen, wie er auf der Plattform X schrieb. In der Hauptstadt Brüssel nahmen rund 400 Menschen daran teil, wie die Nachrichtenagentur Belga berichtete. Demnach gab es neun Festnahmen wegen Störung der öffentlichen Ordnung sowie Waffenbesitz und Anstiftung zu Gewalt. Am vergangenen Wochenende waren es demnach 150 Festnahmen.

Tausende Menschen demonstrierten auch erneut in Italien. Bei einigen Protesten kam es zu vereinzelten Zusammenstößen zwischen Demonstranten und Polizisten. Die Polizei sprach am Samstagabend von neun erlassenen Haftbefehlen und 371 Festnahmen. In 110 Fällen habe es sich um Minderjährige gehandelt. Insgesamt seien 5.000 Personen kontrolliert worden. Die Proteste umfassten ein breites Spektrum an Themen, darunter hohe Lebenshaltungskosten, hohe Benzinpreise sowie Aufrüstung.

Tausende Menschen demonstrierten auch erneut in mehreren italienischen Großstädten. Bei einigen Protesten kam es zu Zusammenstößen zwischen Demonstranten und Polizisten. Neun Haftbefehle wurden erlassen und 317 Menschen festgenommen. Die verschiedenen Proteste umfassten ein breites Spektrum an Themen, darunter hohe Lebenshaltungskosten, Benzinpreise sowie Aufrüstung und den Nahostkonflikt.

In der Hauptstadt Rom kam es am Samstagabend im Zentrum zu Spannungen: Die Polizei setzte Wasserwerfer ein. Über dem Gebiet des Hauptbahnhofes Termini kreisten Polizeihubschrauber. Im historischen Zentrum drangen zwischenzeitlich Protestteilnehmer in das Kolosseum ein.

In der norditalienischen Metropole Mailand, in der es vergangenes Wochenende zu schweren Krawallen gekommen war, verliefen die Proteste größtenteils ohne größere Zwischenfälle. Aus Sorge vor Ausschreitungen waren viele Luxusgeschäfte verbarrikadiert worden. In Bologna setzte die Polizei bei Zusammenstößen mit Demonstranten Tränengas und Wasserwerfer ein. In Turin drängten Sicherheitskräfte Teilnehmer der Proteste mit Schilden zurück.

In Portugal protestierten zahlreiche Menschen in Lissabon und Porto für das Recht auf Wohnen. Die Polizei nahm nach Angaben der Nachrichtenagentur Lusa in der Hauptstadt 25 Menschen fest. Dort war es bei den Protesten rund um den Rossio-Platz zu Auseinandersetzungen zwischen Demonstranten und Sicherheitskräften gekommen. (APA/dpa)

Pour sa quatrième candidature, la patronne du Rassemblement national a lancé sa campagne dimanche lors d'un meeting à Orléans, accompagnée par Jordan Bardella et Eric Ciotti.

"Levez les mains ! Je veux voir tous les drapeaux !" Le chauffeur de salle ne ménage pas ses efforts pour faire chanter le public sur Edith Piaf, Céline Dion ou Michel Delpech, avant l'arrivée de Marine Le Pen lors de cette seconde journée du congrès du Rassemblement national. La quadruple candidate à la présidentielle a choisi de lancer sa campagne dans une ambiance de karaoké, à l'Arena d'Orléans, dimanche 25 octobre. A six mois de l'élection suprême, les mines sont réjouies, sous les stroboscopes. "On grappille d'année en année, l'espoir est plus marqué, on sent un courant qui se lève", souffle David, venu en famille du Loiret. "On y croit cette fois !", sourit Sophie*, en attendant le début du meeting.

A l'extérieur, des camionnettes bleues et de nombreux policiers et gendarmes gardent les lieux. Le congrès se déroule dans une ville placée sous très haute surveillance : la préfecture a déployé 3 000 policiers et gendarmes et interdit toute manifestation dimanche, ce qui n'a pas empêché une centaine de militants antifascistes de se rassembler, à bonne distance de l'Arena. "C'est un peu inquiétant, et plus on va s'approcher du vote, plus ça va être tendu", souffle Vincent, 22 ans, qui a fait la route depuis Nîmes.

"Ils pensaient vous intimider, ils pensaient vous dissuader de venir, vous êtes 8 500 rassemblés ici", se réjouit Jordan Bardella, confortablement réélu la veille à la présidence du parti lors d'un scrutin sans suspense. Mais son discours, axé sur l'autorité, est interrompu par une altercation dans les gradins. Pour couvrir l'incident, le président du RN fait entonner La Marseillaise au public, tandis que le service d'ordre évacue plusieurs militants de l'Union des étudiants juifs de France (UEJF) venus dénoncer "l'antisémitisme et le racisme" du parti. Deux journalistes qui filmaient la scène sont à leur tour expulsés brutalement, selon Mediapart.

Numéro 2 sur le ticket présidentiel, le trentenaire a été éclaboussé par des révélations de Mediapart sur des propos antisémites passés qui lui sont attribués. "Ça n'a aucun impact auprès de nos électeurs", balaie Jean-Lin Lacapelle, membre du bureau national du RN. Mais samedi, les journalistes de Mediapart ont été brutalement exfiltrés de la salle du congrès par le service d'ordre du RN, après avoir été pris à partie par des militants. "Anecdotique", évacue un cadre. "Mediapart a provoqué des militants, notre service d'ordre a préféré les sortir pour les protéger. On continuera d'accréditer tous les médias". Les journalistes ont pu ensuite regagner la salle.

Ces incidents et les manifestations récurrentes d'opposants ponctuent une campagne que les lepénistes souhaitent "positive". "On n'est pas du tout bunkérisés, assure un parlementaire. L'extrême gauche veut faire penser ça et nous faire passer pour un groupuscule de cinglés alors qu'on est à 35% dans les sondages."

Samedi, entre 13 000 et 26 000 personnes ont défilé dans le calme de l'autre côté de la Loire pour s'opposer au RN, dans un centre-ville quasi bouclé, où les commerces étaient fermés et barricadés. En tête de cortège, le patron des socialistes, Olivier Faure, a accusé le parti de Marine Le Pen d'être "responsable" d'un "climat délétère, raciste, xénophobe depuis très longtemps", au lendemain d'un attentat raciste, qui a fait un mort et plusieurs blessés. "Faire un lien entre ce qui s'est passé à Trappes et nous n'est pas digne, mais la gauche continuera à le faire car elle n'a rien d'autre à dire", a réagi Marine Le Pen devant des journalistes samedi.

Porté par de bons sondages, le RN estime que la victoire n'a jamais été aussi proche de la victoire, grâce à la stratégie de normalisation et au contexte politique. "On a gagné la bataille des idées et de la crédibilité, il reste celle des urnes", résume Jean-Lin Lacapelle. "Mon optimisme vient du fait que je pense qu'on a eu raison sur tout, estime Marine Le Pen, en privé, tout en restant prudente, après trois élections perdues. Rien n'est jamais acquis. Il faut faire campagne comme si on était à 13 ou 14%" dans les sondages.

Pour l'emporter, le RN doit séduire au-delà de son socle électoral et lorgne les électeurs de droite. Sur scène avant les deux leaders du RN, Eric Ciotti, chef de l'UDR (Union des droites pour la République), veut incarner ce rapprochement, deux ans après avoir quitté avec fracas Les Républicains pour rallier Marine Le Pen. J'appelle solennellement mes anciens amis des Républicains à nous rejoindre !", harangue-t-il sous les applaudissements.

La salle est chauffée à bloc lorsque Marine Le Pen arrive sur scène, peu après 16h20. "Ce qui fait notre force, c'est que nous avons toujours refusé le déni et osé affronter la réalité en face, sans tabou, sans interdit", clame-t-elle. "Tout le monde en France est maintenant d'accord avec nous !" Pendant une heure, la fille de Jean-Marie Le Pen convoque l'image de Jeanne d'Arc, déclinée sur les produits vendus à la boutique du RN, pour promettre "la renaissance de la France", dans un discours aux accents positifs. "Notre seule boussole, c'est la France, son redressement et le bonheur des Français", lance la députée du Pas-de-Calais. Sans pour autant faire l'impasse sur ses fondamentaux, comme la lutte contre "l'immigration qui submerge nos villes" ou encore "l'Etat prédateur" qui "spolie" les héritages, devant un public acquis à sa cause.

"Quand l'extrême gauche prône le chaos, le parti de l'ordre, c'est nous", affirme Yoann Gillet, directeur adjoint de la campagne. "La violence des pilleurs a certainement servi de révélateur à certains de nos compatriotes qui étaient peut-être dans le déni", affirme la candidate en privé, alors que le parti assure enregistrer un pic d'adhésions depuis, et revendique 160 000 militants.

Cet enthousiasme se heurte toutefois à quelques difficultés. La candidate n'a toujours pas décroché de prêt bancaire pour financer sa campagne. Elle reste par ailleurs soumise à une échéance judiciaire : la Cour de cassation doit se prononcer juste avant le premier tour dans l'affaire des détournements de fonds du Parlement européen, pour laquelle elle a été condamnée en appel le 7 juillet. Elle risquerait alors de devoir purger sa peine d'un an de prison ferme, aménageable sous bracelet électronique. Une perspective que personne n'envisage aujourd'hui au RN. “President Trump is not trying to take over a state election in any way, shape, or form,” Blanche told CNN’s State of the Union programme. “We’re not laying any groundwork for the president to do that.”

Election officials around the country have told Reuters they are preparing ​for potential federal interference in the Nov 3 midterm elections that will decide control of the US Congress, including legal challenges and disputes over voting that could disrupt certification of the results.

In addition to the Alaska lawsuit, the Trump administration has taken other vote-related actions against states, including a lawsuit to stop California from counting certain mail-in ballots and a mostly unsuccessful attempt to force 30 states and the District of Columbia to provide complete voter rolls.

The Alaska action is specific to that state, Blanche said, reiterating the Trump administration’s contention that voters will be “confused” because two Republicans on the ballot have almost identical names: incumbent US Senator Dan S. Dan J. Sullivan has denied in interviews with US ​news outlets that his aim is to cause confusion or divert votes from Dan S. Sullivan. His campaign did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The lawsuit was filed to give officials “the ability to determine whether there was confusion that violates the law,” Blanche said.
