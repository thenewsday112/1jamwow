Why is the Plaza Hotel Closing? The Story Behind the Façade

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Of the 700+ hotels in New York City, The Plaza Hotel stands apart. Surrounded by skyscrapers, the stout, eighteen-story building at the corner of 59th Street and 5th Avenue, just south of Central Park, is more of an icon in the cityscape than any of its super-tall neighbors could hope to be. It hosted The Beatles just before their breakthrough performance on The Ed Sullivan Show, welcomed F. Scott and Zelda Fitzgerald, inspired the eponymous Neil Simon play Plaza Suite, acted as a backdrop in Home Alone 2, and was the home of the fictional Eloise, the little girl who roamed its elegant halls. Now, nearly 120 years after it first opened, it’s shutting its doors—at least for a few years.

The Plaza's façade and the interior of the hotel are registered landmarks, recognized by both the national and city government.

Starting in 2027, the Plaza Hotel will embark on a four-year-long renovation of its marble façade. First designed by Henry Janeway Hardenbergh in 1907, the hotel’s base and lower levels are crafted from rusticated marble, with the upper levels being made from a polished, smooth marble exterior. Rusticated marble deteriorates more quickly when exposed to the elements, and can often need to be repaired and restored within twenty years. Coincidentally, the 2027 renovation comes just 19 years after the last major renovation to the hotel in 2008.

Restoring the hotel’s façade will require more than just throwing up the scaffolding and calling in a team. The façade and the interior of the hotel are recognized as registered landmarks both nationally and by the city. The Landmarks Preservation Commission is working closely with the hotel to help with the restoration efforts and preserve the architectural beauty of the building.

Interior View of the Palm Court Restaurant, 1957

“Designated as both an individual and interior landmark, the Plaza Hotel’s elegant French Renaissance-style architecture, distinctive roofline, and prominent location have made it an iconic feature of the New York City skyline,” says Courtney Metakis, from the New York Landmarks Preservation Commission. “Its grand, public interiors have made it an enduring part of the city's cultural identity for more than a century." The commission did not comment further on the plans for the renovation or guidelines to keep in line with the commission’s protected status.

While this oversight is meant to preserve the history of the hotel in the event of, say, billionaire real estate developers encroaching in the vicinity of the building, it also can make restoration projects like this vastly more complicated. The commission will require the restoration process be carefully managed to preserve the integrity of the marble and any historical details. The hotel has enlisted Allison Doyle Partners (ADP) Architects for the job—the same firm that worked on the previous renovation of the interior architecture. Design firm HLZAE will oversee the façade’s masonry restoration in particular.

Plaza Hotel, Fifth Avenue Entrance, New York, New York, 1929. The iconic hotel has been open for 120 years.

“The undertaking reflects the complexity of maintaining a property of such architectural and historical significance, with careful consideration given to preservation requirements and the building’s long-term care, as well as the safety of our guests, employees, and the wider community,” the hotel’s management said in a statement. “The scope and complexity of the façade works require detailed planning and coordination with relevant stakeholders, alongside adherence to applicable historic preservation requirements. Further considerations relating to the planning and execution of the works remain under review as the process advances.”

While the restorations are only to the marble surface of the building, the meticulous nature of the job would have disrupted all aspects of the Plaza experience for would-be guests. If you spent $1,500 for a room (or upwards) and woke up not to the sun cresting over Central Park, but because a man on a scaffold was drilling just outside your window, you would likely be upset about it.

The Plaza Hotel entrance in New York City, 1988. The last major renovation to the hotel was in 2008.

“With a building of that age, the level of architectural significance and importance to the city, requires a great deal of care and planning and execution for any restoration,” explains Frank Mahan, principal at SOM, the architecture firm that worked on a similar restoration of the historically protected façade and interior of the Waldorf-Astoria hotel in midtown over the course of ten years. “Any Landmarks building requires a certain degree of engagement with the LPC staff and sometimes at a public hearing.” Similar to the expectations of the Plaza Hotel, the Waldorf Astoria’s owners wanted to restore the façade rather than reinvent it in a new, modern image, so the LPC was involved through the process.

There’s never a good time to announce a multi-year closure, but the hotel is now working to make good on many of its long-term plans guests have made. Last April, bride-to-be Christine Carney booked the Plaza for her wedding, and was still sorting out down payments for the booking as of just a few weeks ago. Since the announcement of the closure, she's had to start looking for an alternative venue in time for the big day. The hotel is also working with union The Hotel & Gaming Trades Council to place the Plaza’s 378 staffers into new jobs for the interim of the restoration.

The real question is: Will four years be enough time? Or will the Plaza need to extend the expected closure once unforeseen needs arise, as they so often do with older buildings. “Any project with an existing building involves unexpected existing conditions,” Mahan adds. “It’s rather inevitable that once you start working on the building, you uncover things you may not have expected or that may differ from what the original drawings predicted. There’s a certain amount of flexibility and nimbleness expected on the design side.”

Luckily, the neighborhood has no shortage of historic hotels from a bygone era of the city. If you were planning a trip to stay at The Plaza (or had one booked and this is how you’re learning that it’s been canceled…) consider going just across the street to the Sherry-Netherland, up 5th Avenue another block or two to The Pierre, or around the Eastern side of Central Park for a night at the Lowell or the Carlyle.

National Park Service names 10 new American World War II Heritage cities

Published Oct 16, 2026 3:16 PM EDT

On October 16, the National Park Service (NPS)announced the latest additions to a growing list of designated World War II Heritage Cities. The park service began the program in 2020, as a way to honor local ties to history. Officials have since celebrated locations across 46 states and two territories, but there are many more places worthy of distinction.

The10 new cities and counties announced today span Alaska all the way to the U.S. Virgin Islands.

“These designations highlight the enduring legacy of service, sacrifice, innovation, and industrial achievement…and continue to shape our national story,” NPS Jessica Bowron said in a statement.

According to Bowron, this year’s selections include hubs for military manufacturing, training facilities, and logistical centers integral to Allied campaigns. In Aurora, Colorado, Fitzsimons General Hospital cared for wounded returning service members, while the city of Pendleton, Oregon, featured an airfield that trained B-25 crews that later participated in the Doolittle Raid. Meanwhile, South Charleston, West Virginia’s Naval Ordinance Plant supplied invaluable inventories and was largely staffed by local women and Black workers.

Where Is the Outrage Over Trump’s Racist Targeting of Ilhan Omar?

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The establishment press and some Democrats are strangely mute over the president’s flagrant abuse of the legal system.

Donald Trump has long fantasized about deporting Representative Ilhan Omar. In 2019, Trump lashed out against Omar and her congressional colleagues Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Rashida Tlaib, and Ayanna Pressley—all left-wing Democrats and women of color and part of the “Squad.” Deploying a familiar xenophobic jibe, Trump taunted, “Why don’t they go back and help fix the totally broken and crime infested places from which they came.” For three of the Squad, Trump’s threat was vile but empty: Ocasio-Cortez, Tlaib, and Pressley were all born in the United States.

But for Omar, Trump’s words carried a direct intent of harm. Omar was born in Somalia, came to the United States at age 8 as a refugee, and was eventually granted citizenship at age 17 in 2000. On Thursday, The Washington Post reported that in 2019, the same year Trump was telling Omar to “go back” to where she came from, his administration ordered immigration officials to comb through her personal records to find evidence of fraud that could lead to her removal from the US. This fishing expedition came up empty-handed.

Never one to be defeated by a mere lack of evidence, Trump has persisted in calling for Omar’s deportation ever since—calls that have ramped up over the last year. In January, he said Omar should be sent “back to Somalia,” a call he repeated the following month. In March, Vice President JD Vance joined in, bluntly declaring, without evidence, that Omar “definitely committed immigration fraud.”

There are ample reasons why Omar has been one of Trump’s favorite punching bags. As a leading leftist in Congress, she is a natural antagonist. But, more importantly, as a fearless Somali American woman, she excites all the passions of racism, xenophobia, and misogyny that animate Trump and his MAGA movement.

Given the long history of Trump’s vendetta against Omar, it was ultimately not surprising when the Department of Homeland Security raided her home this week in search of evidence of immigration fraud, a move that The Wall Street Journal reports is motivated by a goal of stripping her of her citizenship.

The campaign against Omar stinks to high heaven of partisanship and bigotry. It is also part of a larger pattern of Trump’s weaponization of law enforcement to punish perceived enemies. As The New Yorker documented recently, Trump’s persecutions have done immense harm to the Department of Justice:

Numerous attorneys have been pushed out or fired after refusing to prosecute such Trump enemies as the former F.B.I. director James Comey and the New York attorney general Letitia James. When these vindictive prosecutions eventually fell under scrutiny in courtrooms, they didn’t fare well. It is extremely rare for grand juries to dismiss charges brought by federal prosecutors, but the case against James, who was accused of committing mortgage fraud, has been rejected twice. Indictments of James and Comey were initially secured by Lindsey Halligan, a lawyer and a former beauty-pageant contestant whom Trump had appointed as the U.S. Attorney for the Eastern District of Virginia, even though she’d had no experience as a prosecutor. But a magistrate judge subsequently reprimanded her for a “disturbing pattern of profound investigative missteps,” including repeatedly misstating the law to grand jurors. Another judge then dismissed both cases, ruling that Halligan had been appointed unlawfully.

But there’s a striking fact that sets the malicious prosecution apart: Unlike earlier victims of Trump’s lawless abuse of the justice system, Omar isn’t getting a full-throated defense from the political and media elite.

Omar does have people fighting on her side. As my Nation colleague John Nichols reports, she remains popular with her constituents in Minnesota, and the state’s Democratic Party has rallied to her side. House minority leader Hakeem Jeffries decried Trump’s “vile personal attacks” and the fact that Trump’s “violent mass deportation machine has descended upon the home of a duly elected Member of Congress and her family.” Even former Republican representative Marjorie Taylor Greene was critical of Trump. In a post on X.com, Greene asked how “pretending to get ready to deport Ilhan Omar is going to bring gas down under $2, make health insurance freaking affordable, make a family’s weekly grocery bill make sense, and stop sending our beloved military off to fight for another fucking foreign war???”

Greene’s pointed rebuke of Trump stands in sharp contrast to Senate minority leader Chuck Schumer, who in the five days since the raid has yet to say one word in defense of Omar.

Schumer’s silence is hardly surprising. Because Omar has been outspoken in her criticism of Israel and the Israel lobby, establishment Democrats such as Schumer have often kept their distance from her.

Omar’s criticism of Israel and her larger anti-war stance are overwhelmingly popular with Democratic Party voters and have majority support with the general public. According to a recent poll by Project 270, 67 percent of the public want to end overseas war, and 51 percent believe Israel has committed genocide in Gaza (as against 29 percent who deny that genocide has occurred). But these positions are less popular with the political and media elite.

Other victims of Trump’s wrath have been turned into media heroes. But Omar is being treated differently. Writing in The Intercept, Adam Johnson drew a pointed contrast between the Omar raid and the August 2025 raid on the home of John Bolton, Trump’s former national security adviser turned critic.

The very day of the Bolton raid, we saw scathing editorials from the New York Times, Washington Post, and Wall Street Journal. Over the next week, each of these newspapers also published separate pieces from their opinion staff opposing the raid, casting doubt on its legitimacy, and otherwise leveling outrage at the obvious power grab. The raid was explicitly condemned in Politico, The New Yorker, and The Atlantic, which published four different indignant columns.

The raid on Omar’s home has thus far received zero of the above media interventions. No editorials, no opinion columns in the Times, Post, or Journal; no preening in The New Yorker or Politico or Atlantic about the erosion of “rule of law.” The Atlantic did run an article on the Omar raid but it was equivocal and Both Sides in its tone…

As Johnson notes, the reason for the difference in treatment is simply one of identity: Omar is a critic of the establishment, while Bolton is a fixture of the national security state.

On his Unpopular Front Substack, John Ganz also noted that the response to the Omar raid has been “muted” and “tepid.” He suspected the reason might be complacency, due to the “repeated failure of this regime to prosecute its enemies.” But Ganz rightly notes that the attack on Omar is one of the most disturbing events of Trump’s presidency, since it upturns the core principles of liberal democracy:

[T]he attack on citizenship itself is both the very essence of the Trump movement and its most dangerous feature. From birtherism to the stolen-election myth, Trumpism can be understood as the notion that some Americans are not really Americans, that their political rights are illegitimate, and that the nation must be purged of these interlopers.

Ganz is right to highlight the dangers of Trump’s actions. One could add that there is a dual threat: Trump’s subversion of liberal democracy is aided and abetted by an establishment that is so committed to fending off outsiders that it can’t be bothered to defend Ilhan Omar at this crucial moment.

Leqembi, Kisunla: Die neuen Alzheimer-Medikamente und ihr großer Haken

„Presse“-Gesundheitsexperte Köksal Baltaci spricht im Podcast über erste Anzeichen von Alzheimer, neue Therapien und was jeder von uns zur Vorbeugung tun kann.

In Österreich leben 100.000 bis 150.000 Menschen mit Demenz, bis 2050 könnten es mehr als doppelt so viele sein. Die häufigste Form ist Alzheimer. 125 Jahre nachdem der Arzt Alois Alzheimer die Krankheit erstmals beschrieben hat, gibt es nun Medikamente, die den Verlauf tatsächlich bremsen: die Antikörper-Therapien Leqembi und Kisunla. Doch sie wirken nur im Frühstadium und kommen nur für einen kleinen Teil der Patientinnen und Patienten infrage. Warum erklärt „Presse“-Gesundheitsexperte Köksal Baltaci in dieser Folge.

Woran erkennt man erste Anzeichen, und warum fallen sie oft als Erstes den Angehörigen auf? Was können Bluttests und Gentests, und für wen eignen sich die neuen Medikamente Leqembi und Kisunla? Warum hat sich die Hoffnung zerschlagen, dass Abnehmspritzen wie Ozempic gegen Alzheimer helfen? Und was kann jeder selbst tun? Laut Med-Uni Wien ließen sich rund 40 Prozent der Alzheimer-Fälle durch Vorbeugung verhindern. Manche Faktoren wie Bildung oder Einsamkeit sind aber schwerer zu beeinflussen, ist die Wahrscheinlichkeit an Alzheimer zu erkranken also eine soziale Frage? Leqembi und Kisunla im Überblick: Seit 2025 sind in der EU die Antikörper Leqembi (Wirkstoff Lecanemab) und Kisunla (Donanemab) zugelassen. Sie werden über ein bis eineinhalb Jahre als Infusion verabreicht und bauen schädliche Eiweißablagerungen (Amyloid) im Gehirn ab.

Heilen können sie Alzheimer nicht, aber den Verlauf bremsen. Sie wirken nur im Frühstadium und werden nur in spezialisierten Zentren eingesetzt. Ausgeschlossen sind etwa Menschen mit schweren Herzerkrankungen, nach größeren Schlaganfällen oder unter Blutverdünnung. Infrage kommen derzeit nur fünf bis zehn Prozent der Patienten.

The employees have been given Anthropic hardware and office space, and can access “internal tools and systems that are roughly on par with what our own risk assessment teams have access to,” Anthropic said. “Outside a small number of clearly defined categories (e.g., data about customers, attorney-client privileged information, or information about our model architectures), Anthropic employees are encouraged to share information with them just as they would with a fellow employee working on alignment risk.”

COP17: Which countries have released new pledges to tackle nature loss?

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Some 58% of countries have met a UN call to submit new pledges on addressing nature loss – two years after the original deadline – according to Carbon Brief analysis.

Countries were asked to submit new pledges, known as national biodiversity strategies and action plans (NBSAPs), by the start of the COP16 biodiversity summit in October 2024.

After only 15% of nations met the original deadline, countries agreed at the summit to a new text that “urges” countries to release their NBSAPs “as soon as possible”.

Carbon Brief analysis shows that 113 countries have now come forward with new NBSAPs – up from 55 nations a year ago.

Countries will gather in Yerevan, Armenia, next week for COP17.

COP17 marks the halfway point of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF), a landmark agreement made in 2022 to reverse nature decline by 2030.

The summit will provide the stage for the first global review of countries’ progress towards meeting the targets of the GBF.

In Montreal, Canada, in 2022, nearly every country in the world agreed to the GBF. The overall “mission” of the framework is to “halt and reverse” biodiversity loss by 2030.

The GBF includes a list of 23 targets for 2030. They cover an expansive range of topics, including restoring ecosystems, addressing climate change and providing developing nations with finance to help cover the costs of protecting nature.

As part of the GBF and its underlying documents, countries agreed to a schedule for monitoring their progress towards achieving the 2030 targets.

This included the preparation of a “global report” of progress coordinated by the UN, which will inform a “global review” undertaken by countries at COP17.

The global report draws on countries’ NBSAPs and national targets. It also draws on countries’ seventh national reports, which were due to be submitted to the UN in February of this year.

Carbon Brief analysis shows that 113 out of 196 parties (58%) have now met the call to produce a new NBSAP – up from 55 nations just a year ago.

Since last year, many African nations have now published new NBSAPs.

African countries that have released new plans in the last year include: Algeria, Benin, Botswana, Cameroon, Comoros, Congo, Côte d’Ivoire, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Egypt, Equatorial Guinea, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Gabon, Gambia, Guinea, Lesotho, Liberia, Madagascar, Malawi, Mali, Namibia, Rwanda, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Somalia, Sudan, Zambia and Zimbabwe.

More South American nations have also published new NBSAPs. This includes Brazil – the world’s most biodiverse nation – as well as Belize and Chile.

COP17 host nation Armenia published its new NBSAP in April.

According to the GBF and its underlying documents, countries that were “not in a position” to meet the deadline to submit NBSAPs ahead of COP16 were requested to instead submit national targets.

Unlike NBSAPs, national targets simply list biodiversity goals that countries will aim for, without an accompanying plan for how they will be achieved.

Four of the world’s megadiverse nations – countries that account for the majority of Earth’s species – have not yet published new NBSAPs. These countries are Papua New Guinea, Peru, South Africa and the US.

The US is the only UN member country that is not a signatory to the UN Convention on Biological Diversity.

The country with the most land on Earth, Russia, has also not produced a new NBSAP.

By the end of COP16, some 119 parties had produced at least one national target. Two years later, this figure has risen to 175, or 89% of countries.

In addition, around half of countries met the deadline to produce their seventh national reports by February of this year. This figure has now risen to 73% of countries.

Over the past two years, many developing countries have expressed concerns that a lack of available funding has prevented them from publishing their NBSAPs.

A spokesperson for the Global Environment Facility (GEF), the multilateral fund that provides funding to help with the preparation of NBSAPs, told Carbon Brief in 2025 that 120 out of 139 countries that had requested financial support since COP16 had been able to access it.

The spokesperson added that the UN Environment Programme was “working to resolve outstanding issues” to allow the remaining 19 countries to access financial assistance.

Your eyes dilate depending on your thoughts

Your eyes really can betray your thoughts, but don’t get too self-conscious about it. Researchers in Japan believe that the subtle signs our eyeballs make are more useful to neuroscientists than the interrogation room. Based on their findings recently published in the journal iScience, examining the difference physiological responses from “inner” versus “overt” speech may help us better understand everything from linguistic development in children to the efficacy of silent pep talks.

To learn more, a team at the University of Tsukuba in Honshu enlisted dozens of volunteers for a series of simple counting exercises. After observing the participants at rest while thinking about nothing in particular for a control, researchers then asked them to count sequentially (1, 2, 3…), in number-subject combinations (one dog, two dogs…), and in specific intervals (1, 3, 5…). They also completed each task both silently and aloud. In each phase, the team carefully monitored their pupil dilation.

The results were subtle, but striking. Participants’ pupils dilated during each overt speech task, but this same muscle movement depended across each inner speech directive. Pupils constricted while volunteers silently counted sequentially, dilated for interval counting, and didn’t change at all for number-subject counting. But regardless of the inner speech task, pupil size changes were always smaller than they were during overt speech.

“The results of the present study do not support the initial assumption that inner and overt speech produce equivalent pupillary responses,” the team summarized. “Across the three experiments, overt speech consistently elicited pupil dilation, whereas inner speech did not.”

This collective data indicates that our inner speech isn’t simply the nonverbal counterpart to talking aloud. Instead, it’s a form of communication that appears to correspond to lower levels of brain arousal. If true, measuring pupil dilation may become a valuable tool when attempting to assess internal language and thinking. This could be particularly useful for studying language development in young children and infants who can’t properly describe their experiences to adults yet.
