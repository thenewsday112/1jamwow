Krawalle in der Finanzmetropole Mailand sowie Proteste der Landwirtschaft setzen Italiens Regierung in Zeiten knapper Kassen stark unter Druck. Schärfere Sicherheitsmaßnahmen und Millionenhilfen sollen die Wogen glätten.

Mit neuen Hilfen für die Landwirtschaft und den Transportsektor sowie einer Verschärfung der Sicherheitsmaßnahmen versucht Italiens Premierministerin Giorgia Meloni die Lage im Land wieder unter Kontrolle zu bringen und eine weitere Ausweitung der Proteste zu verhindern. Gewalttätige Demonstrationen und Plünderungen, vor allem durch Minderjährige mit Migrationshintergrund aus dem Mailänder Umland, hatten Mailand und andere norditalienische Städte in den vergangenen Tagen lahmgelegt.

Für dieses Wochenende rufen Akteure in sozialen Medien zu weiteren Aktionen auf. Das Zentrum von Mailand wird teilweise abgeriegelt. In einem Sonderdekret hat die Regierung das präventive Polizeigewahrsam auch für Minderjährige ausgeweitet, strengere Strafen verabschiedet und 500 Soldaten in die Stadt beordert.

Die Auseinandersetzungen am stark frequentierten Mailänder Domplatz und in umliegenden Einkaufsstraßen sowie Plünderungen von Kleidungsläden, des Kaufhauses Rinascente und anderer Geschäfte verursachten nach Einschätzung von Wirtschaftsverbänden Schäden von mehr als einer Million Euro. Noch viel größer ist jedoch der Imageschaden. Denn Mailand ist eine Touristenmetropole und die Unruhen fanden mitten im Zentrum statt. Einige Luxusläden blieben tagelang geschlossen.

Der starke Gewaltausbruch hat auch tieferliegende Ursachen. Die Jugendlichen, es waren bereits Elfjährige dabei, leben in Parallelwelten. Angeheizt werden die Proteste von Rappern, die für viele der Jungen ein Vorbild sind. Dazu kommen Aufrufe in sozialen Medien, möglicherweise über Künstliche Intelligenz generiert, die dazu auffordern, sich „Markenschuhe und Fahrräder“ zu holen.

Schulen und Universitäten sind vielfach marode. Italien gibt mit insgesamt vier Prozent vom Bruttoinlandsprodukt deutlich weniger für Bildung aus als der EU-Durchschnitt. Auch die Zahl der Schulabbrecher und der Inaktiven ist unter den Jugendlichen überdurchschnittlich hoch. Die Jugendarbeitslosigkeit liegt bei über 20 Prozent. Und die Reallöhne sind seit 2019 um fast vier Prozent zurückgegangen. Dazu kommen extrem hohe Mieten in Mailand und eine Inflationsrate von inzwischen über vier Prozent.

Die Regierung kommt zunehmend unter Druck, denn auch der Transportsektor und die Landwirte mobilisieren. In Süditalien blockieren Lkws noch immer viele Hauptverbindungsstraßen. Supermärkte konnten in den vergangenen Tagen nicht mehr beliefert werden und blieben ohne Waren. Tankstellen ging der Treibstoff aus. Nachdem die Regierung nun 175 Millionen Euro locker gemacht hat, davon 105 Millionen Euro für den Transportsektor und 70 Millionen Euro für Landwirtschaft und Fischerei, sind die Blockaden teilweise aufgelöst worden. Die Hilfen werden überwiegend in Form von Steuergutschriften geleistet. Finanziert werden soll das durch Umschichtungen im Haushalt.

Die beiden Wirtschaftszweige leiden unter stark gestiegenen Treibstoffpreisen. Obst und Gemüse verrottete zuletzt teilweise auf den Feldern, weil es nicht abtransportiert werden konnte. Bauern zahlen außerdem deutlich mehr für Düngemittel.

Ein Jahr vor den Wahlen verschärft sich die Lage für Meloni. Denn die ausbezahlten Hilfen sind nur ein Tropfen auf den heißen Stein. Doch der vor wenigen Tagen vorgestellte Budgetentwurf ist auf Kante genäht. Die EU-Kommission genehmigt für zusätzliche Ausgaben für Rüstung und Energie 2027 einen Anstieg des Defizits auf 3,4 Prozent – also über die Defizitgrenze von drei Prozent hinaus. Meloni fordert wegen „außergewöhnlicher Umstände“ von Brüssel mehr „Flexibilität“, um die Ausgaben erhöhen zu können. Außerdem will sie eine deutliche Ausweitung des EU-Budgets ohne Kürzungen des Agraretats. Damit stellt sie sich gegen Österreich, Deutschland, die skandinavischen Länder und die Niederlande, die eine Ausweitung des EU-Haushalts ablehnen.

Es kommt hinzu, dass die stark gestiegenen Zinsen die Refinanzierungskosten des Landes in die Höhe treiben. Italiens Verschuldung liegt bei über 138 Prozent des Bruttoinlandsprodukts, es ist das am höchsten verschuldete Land in der EU. Die höheren Zinsausgaben schränken den Handlungsspielraum ein. Dabei profitierte Italien mit insgesamt 194 Milliarden Euro am meisten vom europäischen Wiederaufbauprogramm NextGenerationEU, das jedoch ausgelaufen ist. Nach Meinung vieler Beobachter hat Rom die Mittel schlecht ausgegeben.

Dagegen wehrt sich der Bankenverband ABI massiv. Carlo Messina, Chef der italienischen Bank Intesa Sanpaolo, verweist darauf, dass sich die Geldinstitute 2025 dazu verpflichtet haben, binnen drei Jahren „freiwillig“ zusätzlich 10,5 Milliarden Euro zum Budget beizusteuern. „Ich habe an diese Vereinbarung geglaubt“, sagte Messina. „Die Banken dürfen nicht die Einzigen sein, die immer dann herangezogen werden, wenn es Probleme gibt.“ Er verwies darauf, dass die Institute bereits 25 Milliarden Euro von ihren Jahresgewinnen von 50 Milliarden Euro an Rom abführen. Trump's executive order targets mortgage insurance, refinance costs. What it means for borrowers

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

President Donald Trump has signed an executive order that aims to reduce mortgage costs as rising interest rates and inflation make homeownership less affordable.

The order, titled "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People," directs federal agencies to proactively cancel mortgage insurance on eligible future loans and expand a program to make refinancing cheaper, among other actions.

In an X post on Thursday, Federal Housing Finance Agency director Bill Pulte said "millions of Americans will save thousands and thousands of dollars."

But it's difficult to know the future consumer impact without more details, according to Keith Gumbinger, vice president of mortgage website HSH.

Trump's executive order comes less than two weeks before the Nov. 3 midterm elections, with rising costs and housing affordability a major concern among voters. It builds on previous orders to restrict Wall Street firms from buying single-family homes, among other actions.

Homeownership has become less affordable in recent years. In 2025, the monthly payment for a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage on a median-priced home was roughly 33% of the median family's income, up from 26% in 2022, according to the Federal Reserve's latest Survey of Consumer Finances published in early October.

Whether you're a current homeowner or future buyer, here are some of the key things to know about the new executive order.

Conventional loans — mortgages not insured or backed by U.S. government programs — typically require private mortgage insurance, or PMI, when a down payment is less than 20% of the home's purchase price. By contrast, Federal Housing Administration mortgages require mortgage insurance on all loans.

Trump's executive order directs the FHFA to create a pilot program that cancels eligible borrowers' mortgage insurance "proactively" once their home equity reaches a certain threshold, according to the White House. This would apply to mortgages originated after the pilot program launches.

Currently, conventional loan borrowers can generally remove PMI once their loan balance is below 80% of the purchase price or once they have reached 20% equity, according to Fannie Mae. Once your balance reaches 80% of the original value, you can ask your loan servicer if you're eligible to remove PMI, or they may remove it automatically when your balance reaches 78% of the original value.

In a statement Thursday, Seth Appleton, president of U.S. mortgage insurers, an industry trade organization, said: "We commend the President's focus on lowering costs for future homeowners, and the private mortgage insurance industry is proud to already be delivering on that goal."

Typically, the cost of PMI can be 0.2% to 2% of the loan annually, but varies based on your down payment size, credit score, amount borrowed and other factors, according to Fannie Mae.

For example, if you purchased a $600,000 home with a 10% down payment and a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage at 7.3%, your monthly mortgage insurance could be $351, according to a private mortgage calculator from Freddie Mac.

"Most mortgages ... are carrying PMI at this point in time, especially with the way home prices have been and down payments being as small as they've been in recent years," Gumbinger said.

During the second quarter of 2026, the median home down payment was 13.7% of the home's purchase price, or $27,100, according to a September report from Realtor.com.

Trump's executive order also aims to reduce mortgage refinancing costs by making permanent the FHFA's pilot program that waives the lender's title insurance policy and allows "cost-friendly alternatives," according to the White House.

"I don't know that it's necessarily a game-changer in terms of the cost of housing, which is dictated by home price and mortgage rates at the moment," Gumbinger said. "But you know, every little bit certainly helps."

As of Oct. 23, mortgage interest rates for 30-year fixed-rate mortgages were 7.32%, according to Freddie Mac, and refinance demand was down 59% in mid-October, compared with the same week the previous year.

Originally planned through November 2027, the FHFA pilot was "limited in scope," for refinances with a loan-to-value ratio below 80%, in a handful of states, former FHFA director Sandra Thompson said during a congressional hearing in April 2024.

Thompson estimated that consumers could save $500 to $1,500 in closing costs for loans sold under the pilot program.

Making the program permanent could save borrowers because the lender's title insurance policy, which is usually required for new loans and refinances, "has always been fairly pricey," Gumbinger said.

The lender's fees related to title and title insurance generally make up the largest share of mortgage closing costs, according to a 2025 Urban Institute report.

Brazil’s rapid election count puts spotlight on voting security ahead of US midterms

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Footage from a polling station in Rio de Janeiro showed electoral officials retrieving the voting results reports and disconnecting the machines after the first round of voting on Oct. 4, 2026. (Reuters)

SAO PAULO, Brazil— About 125 million Brazilians voted Oct. 4 on machines that print no paper ballots. Less than four hours after polls closed, the country knew its presidential election was headed to a runoff between conservative challenger Flavio Bolsonaro and leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva.

That process has been in place for over three decades, and Brazilians have become accustomed to its speed, simplicity, accuracy and routine. The system is robustly audited, completely disconnected from the Internet, and is the largest digital voting system deployed on the planet.

Eleven days before the U.S. midterms, Brazil’s system, analysts say, could offer Americans a more secure way to vote.

The system has been widely praised and was also mentioned in President Donald Trump’s 2025 executive order on elections, where he cited Brazil’s biometric voter identification as an example. Yet Brazil’s count runs partially on the kind of paperless machines that the president has vowed to scrap.

STATE DEPARTMENT CONSIDERS SHARING PASSPORT RECORDS FOR VOTER CITIZENSHIP CHECKS

Election workers prepare and seal electronic voting machines ahead of the upcoming general elections in Brasilia, Brazil, on Sept. 23, 2026.  (Mateus Bonomi/Anadolu via Getty Images)

Over the years, Brazil merged federal, state and municipal databases into a digital voting record, whereby voter identity, eligibility and single ability to vote can (and usually must) be verified biometrically. Nearly 9 in 10 voters unlock the machines with a fingerprint.

Brazil’s Superior Electoral Court, or TSE, which designs the machines and oversees elections, says there has been no proven fraud since their 1996 debut. The 499,248 machines used on Oct. 4 never connected to the internet. Their software was open to inspection by parties, universities and watchdogs for 11 months before being digitally signed and sealed in public. Last December, the Brazilian government invited a record 149 outsiders to try to break the system; evaluators concluded nothing they found could alter results or reveal how anyone voted.

An electronic voting machine at a Regional Electoral Court (TRE) facility in Brasilia, Brazil, on Wednesday, Sept. 23, 2026.  (Ton Molina/Bloomberg via Getty Images)

On election day, randomly drawn machines are fed paper ballots on camera as federal auditors and foreign observers watch and specifically track results.

Meanwhile, at closing, every machine used at every polling station prints its tally, which is then posted in front of the polling place with a QR code, so anyone can check it against the national count. In an email to Fox News Digital, TSE said each file’s digital signature "confirms that the result was produced by the electronic voting machine registered for that polling station."

Karina Stange Calandrin, a political scientist and professor at Instituto Brasileiro de Mercado de Capitais (IBMEC), says the system combines three traits rarely found together: voting is electronic, votes hashed to voter eligibility are stored locally in the machine and tallying is automated.

"It is an extremely secure and safe system. The machines never connect to the Internet and even the central database system used to transmit tallied votes to TSE must match results issued physically and locally by each individual machine without human interference," she said. Corrupting it, she added, would require breaking the seal of each machine and installing software that wouldn’t match the TSE’s version, automatically disqualifying its tally — a scale of collusion so large that it "simply could not pass undetected."

The machines passed their toughest political test in 2022, when former President Jair Bolsonaro, now serving a 27-year sentence for plotting to overturn his 2022 defeat, spent years attacking their credibility without evidence. Bolsonaro is presently serving time under house arrest.

This year, for the first time, a Bolsonaro appointee ran the TSE: Justice Kassio Nunes Marques. Bolsonaro’s eldest son, Sen. Flávio Bolsonaro, finished first with 47% to President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s 45%. The machines at the center of the senior Bolsonaro’s controversy may yet hand victory to his son.

Florencia Ferrer, CEO of e-stategiapublica, which has audited elections in 17 countries, has no doubt the Brazilian system is the most modern and secure in the world. "In India votes are electronic, but each voter gets a printed version of their vote to take home, which weakens voter safety and augments coercion rates," she says, adding that Estonia’s internet voting and the Philippines’ electronically tallied paper ballots carry their own risks.

An engineer from the University of São Paulo’s Polytechnic School who audited the machines in 2014, and declined to be named due to the sensitive nature of his work, agreed. "While no system is completely infallible... we have found no traces of tampering, wrong-doing or malicious intent," he told Fox News Digital.

Still, some defend higher scrutiny. Security researchers say a clean record is not proof enough, and the Carter Center said voters should at least see a printed version of their ballot deposited into a separate area of the machine; otherwise, results integrity rests on software alone. Germany’s top court ruled in 2009 that voters must be able to check their ballot without expert help, effectively barring a Brazilian-like system.

Brazil’s machine has traveled mostly on loan to countries in Latin America, Africa and Asia. Paraguay adopted it permanently in 2006, with machines that now print each individual vote.

Brazilians go to the polls on Oct. 4, 2026,  (Luis Robayo/AFP Via Getty Images)

"It’s obvious Washington and the remainder of the U.S. are way behind both technology as well as security when it comes to polling on voting day," Ferrer said. U.S. elections are run in thousands of local jurisdictions under 50 sets of state rules and about 30% of 2024 ballots came by mail, which is slower to process. Brazil has one national election authority, compulsory voting and no mail-in ballots.

Brazilians will vote this coming Sunday on Oct. 25. The winner is expected to be known and certified in under five hours.

"The question is not whether Brazil has invented a perfect voting machine. No voting system is perfect. It is whether democracies can combine the speed and consistency of digital tabulation with independent verification, public scrutiny and rules that citizens across the political spectrum have reason to trust," Ferrer said.

Scientists Built a 3D Cloak That Makes Objects Almost Invisible to Thermal Cameras

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An intricate metal-and-rubber lattice steers heat around objects, concealing their presence from thermal detection.

An object doesn’t have to be visible to give away its location. Sometimes, the way it disrupts the flow of heat is enough to reveal that something is hiding there, much to the despair of camouflaged soldiers who have to endure thermal vision drones on the battlefield. Now, engineers have developed a device that can make that thermal signature almost disappear (although sadly it won’t work for infantry).

Researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and the Technical University of Denmark have built a three-dimensional thermal cloak that redirects heat around hidden objects, leaving the surrounding temperature pattern nearly undisturbed. Unlike earlier designs restricted to simpler shapes or only blocking infrared in a single plane, their cloak can accommodate complex geometries and work regardless of the direction of heat flow.

The breakthrough could eventually help protect sensitive electronics, improve heat management in computer chips, and potentially conceal equipment from certain forms of thermal detection.

There’s a catch, however. This isn’t the kind of invisibility cloak that could make a person disappear from a thermal camera. The device redirects heat flowing through solid materials, whereas the human body continuously generates its own heat and emits infrared radiation into the surrounding environment

Heat naturally flows from warmer regions to cooler ones. But if you place an obstacle in the gradient’s path, then the heat flow changes. A poorly conducting object, for instance, forces heat to spread around it, creating distortions that betray its presence.

A thermal cloak must do two things simultaneously: steer heat around the hidden object and restore the original temperature pattern on the other side.

Researchers have pursued this idea for years. In 2014, scientists in Singapore demonstrated a three-dimensional thermal cloak that concealed an air bubble inside metal. More recently, a 2024 study by some of the same researchers produced intricate thermal cloaks, heat rotators and concentrators, primarily using two-dimensional designs.

The challenge was making the effect work around genuinely complex three-dimensional objects, regardless of the direction of heat flow.

“A real thermal cloak should work no matter where the heat comes from,” said Shelly Zhang, an engineering professor at Illinois.

“Our device can hide a complex 3D object in an infinite number of directions while keeping the temperature inside stable and protected.”

To accomplish this, Zhang and her colleagues developed a lattice made from tiny intersecting aluminum bars, surrounded by a rubber-like material called polydimethylsiloxane.

Aluminum conducts heat readily; the rubber conducts it poorly. So by changing the thickness and orientation of the metal bars in three dimensions, the researchers could control precisely where heat traveled.

They used mathematical models to calculate the required properties throughout the cloak, then translated those figures into a continuous structure that could actually be manufactured.

The researchers used metal 3D printing to manufacture the aluminum framework, then cast the rubber-like material into the remaining spaces.

To test their design, they created an apple-shaped core concealed inside a pear-shaped shell. They placed the completed specimen between two aluminum plates. One plate was heated to 40 degrees Celsius (104 degrees Fahrenheit), while the other was cooled with iced water.

After an hour, an infrared camera recorded the temperature distributions across the specimen.

Outside the cloak, the temperature bands remained nearly straight and evenly spaced, as though the hidden core were absent. Inside, temperatures varied much less, indicating that relatively little heat crossed the protected region.

The team also fabricated and successfully tested a different, heart-shaped cloak.

But perhaps the most striking demonstration involved human faces. The researchers designed a lattice that could conceal a detailed face-shaped object, complete with glasses, inside another face-shaped structure.

The researchers even 3D-printed intricate, face-shaped cloaks, but only tested their ability to hide heat using computer simulations, not physical experiments. The cloak works by redirecting heat around an object, not by hiding heat produced by the object itself. That means it can’t yet conceal a person or other heat-generating object from a thermal camera.

Still, the work suggests that engineers could eventually route heat around delicate electronic components or maintain more stable temperatures in demanding environments.

“Any field that needs precise control of heat or needs to protect something from being detected thermally could benefit from this work,” Zhang said.

The 3D thermal cloak was described in the journal Nature Communications.
