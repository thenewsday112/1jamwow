Justice is elusive, and there are few clearer examples of this than when people who commit acts of sexual violence are given rote tasks as a means of atonement.

Recently, the “Cornell 7” case has inspired new conversations about what accountability and atonement involve. When college administrators investigated a report of sexual violence, some of the alleged perpetrators were reportedly assigned to write “reflection papers,” along with other punishments. The writing assignments drew such ire that Cornell issued a statement stating that "none of the individuals charged were offered the opportunity to write essays as a sole consequence of their involvement."

I am a survivor of childhood sexual abuse, which started when I was 11 years old. Abuse from my step-father went hand-in-hand with punishment. He knew how much I loved words, reading, and writing, and began to use them to discipline me.

Our household was governed by an elaborate set of rules. We were not allowed to speak during dinner without permission. We were required to stand while playing board games. Our bedrooms were inspected every evening, and our beds had to be made with hospital corners.

Any infraction could result in punishment. One of the preferred methods was to require me to write an essay outlining what I had done wrong and how to correct my misdeeds. If I didn't wash the dishes carefully enough, I would have to write an essay about why I needed to take better care when cleaning up after dinner.

Other times, I was ordered to write the same sentence hundreds of times.

If I complained about dinner, I had to write 100 times, “I will appreciate my mother’s cooking.”

If I made fun of one of my siblings, I had to write 250 times, “I will not make fun of my brother.”

And if I resisted attempts to assault me, the punishment could be a thousand repetitions of “I will listen.”

I was being punished for trying to protect myself from the person who was abusing me. In doing so, I learned that writing the correct words was not the same as believing them. I could produce an essay explaining what I might have done wrong and promise to be better, but it didn’t mean that I believed that I misbehaved. It meant that I understood what was required of me. The punishment was complete when the writing was complete, but whether I had learned anything was beside the point.

More than 50 years later, I find myself wondering what we believe an essay can accomplish when it is assigned in response to allegations of sexual violence. Cornell’s disciplinary proceedings have resulted in different sanctions for different students, including expulsions and suspensions. “Reflection papers” were among the educational requirements imposed on some students.

But what, exactly, can an assigned essay demonstrate?

An essay can be thoughtful and persuasive; it can express regret, recognize harm, and promise change. But how do we know whether the person who wrote it believes any of what is written? What happens when completing an assignment becomes a substitute for demonstrating genuine understanding or changed behavior?

I have spent my adult life writing, teaching, and interviewing people about their lives. I believe in the ability of language to help us understand ourselves and one another, and I know how easily language can be used to conceal the truth.

My abuser understood the power of words, and used them to dictate the terms of my obedience. He controlled what I was allowed to say. He warned me that no one would believe me and threatened violence against those I love if I spoke out. I believed him, and I remained silent and ashamed.

I was required to write about my smallest infractions while being forbidden, through fear, to speak about the most terrible thing happening in my life. And the person who demanded that I explain my wrongdoing was the person committing the most serious wrongdoing of all.

This is why the relationship between language and power matters so much to me. The person with authority often gets to decide what constitutes an acceptable explanation, a credible account, or a sufficient expression of remorse.

Jane Doe, the young woman at the center of the Cornell case, reported the incident to university police in 2024. Since then, the timing and content of her disclosures have become subjects of intense public scrutiny. Public debate has focused in part on differences between her initial statements and her subsequent allegations.

I cannot speak for her, and our experiences are different. But I understand how fear can determine what someone is willing to reveal, even when she desperately needs help.

There is something troubling about how much we expect words to prove in cases of sexual violence. We scrutinize the accounts of those who report abuse, searching for inconsistencies. Now we are asking those found responsible for misconduct to demonstrate understanding through written reflection.

I believe writing can lead to meaningful change. But an essay is evidence that someone has completed an assignment. It cannot establish whether that person understands the harm they have caused or whether their behavior will change.

Why are we putting so much faith in the words people are required to write, while questioning the words that others struggle to say?

As a Food Editor, 'Food Is Fuel' Never Resonated. Here's What Changed My Mind

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Editor’s note: The following story references disordered eating.

“Food is fuel.” I’ve heard the cliché my entire life, and it never resonated. Food has always represented much more to me: a reason to gather, a way to celebrate holidays, a source of nostalgia and novelty. To see food as proverbial gas in the tank seemed clinical.

But food has also been a source of deep personal anxiety, and in moments, downright pain. For many years, I was obsessive and compulsive with my eating behavior. A binge-and-restrict cycle became my norm, with food acting as the currency for doling out punishment and rewards.

As I worked with a therapist and mended my relationship with food, I started seeing it as nourishment for my spirit. This was actually fairly easy—I’ve always loved to eat. But it was finding the resolve to intentionally fuel my body, especially as I’ve established a deeper practice of strength training and running, that remained a struggle.

So used to underfueling, I assumed everyone was so tired after a jog or lift that they also literally fell asleep an hour later. Not the case! Many people with robust movement routines eat enough carbs, protein, and fat—not just to achieve a baseline level of physical nourishment, but a quantity necessary to build stamina for heavier sets, increasing mileage or speed, what have you.

This kind of nutrition was new to me. It was more than salad and a plank of salmon. As I educated myself on truly energy-enhancing and recovery-supporting foods, I found ingredients I’d been conditioned to see as “unhealthy,” like bread and cheese, or white potatoes and red meat, actually offered incredible nourishment.

Throughout my teens and early adulthood, I approached health with an all-or-nothing mentality. When I learned that red meat was higher in saturated fat than legumes, I limited my intake of the former. After reading that too many carbohydrates could spike my blood sugar, potentially leading to more hunger and weight gain, I swapped my morning oatmeal for yogurt. Even though I had no medical predispositions to warrant slashing these foods, I saw the ingredients as barriers to optimal health.

At the time, my definition of “healthy” was misguidedly synonymous with “being thin.” I logged hours of endless, directionless cardio, the only intention for which was to burn the minimal calories I consumed the rest of the day.

After a brief respite in the 2010s when body-positivity and neutrality were in vogue, the pendulum has swung back with a vengeance. Today, I have the opportunity to buy a get-skinny-quick scheme in the form of certain peptide therapies, which can result in fat loss and decreased appetite. Myriad celebrities who once touted body-positivity are taking and becoming advocates for the FDA-approved versions of these weight-loss drugs, often known as GLP-1 drugs—choices that are, of course, theirs, and none of my business, but ones that are public and influential.

This is not to bash properly taking a prescribed medication: Many users report that, since taking a GLP-1 drug, they are able to comfortably exercise, or found it easier to avoid alcohol and stay sober, or even manage a binge-eating disorder. My main concern with these medications is how easy it is to access and abuse them. I’ve been in several social settings in the past months where someone has mentioned they are “microdosing” a GLP-1 drug. These are folks who do not medically need the prescription, but take the shots or pills purely to lose that “last” 5 or 10 pounds. And where things get especially sticky for me is when these conversations shift to food noise. And they almost always do.

Though there’s not a formal definition, the concept describes when thoughts of food become so intrusive they disrupt the regular flow of the day. Yet I fear many people, especially those who have a history of disordered eating, conflate food noise with any thoughts of food, even as a result of hunger—something your body should feel throughout the day, especially if it’s expending energy. When someone praises a drug for helping them forget about food altogether, something is wrong. And blaming the medication alone is a Band-Aid on a gushing wound. What we should really be focusing on is why people are ashamed to fuel themselves.

This has become something of a mantra for me lately: It is human to feel hungry, and what a privilege it is to be able to satiate myself. Look at what I can accomplish when I am truly nourished.

My strongest, most capable body might not be its most lean version. What’s more, I don’t want to rid myself of thoughts of food at the expense of losing my appetite. I love food. And I want to learn why certain ingredients, even those I’ve been conditioned to fear in the past, are beneficial to my body.

This doesn’t mean I’m switching my protein sources to exclusively chicken breast or beef because the gym rats swear by it. In fact, I probably only cook meat once or twice a week. Seafood and plant-based proteins are incredibly nutritious, each offering plenty of macronutrients—not that I’m tracking anything religiously.

If there aren’t personal dispositions from an ethical or medical perspective, there is no reason to completely omit any ingredient from one’s diet. A russet potato filled with ground beef appears in this column alongside legumes and grains, chicken breast, and sardines precisely to show that a healthy diet is a varied one, especially for active people.

Do I eat more on particularly lively days, when I can really reap the benefits of the extra energy? You bet. Do I force myself to curb my appetite on lower-activity days because I haven’t “earned” my sustenance? Not anymore. In fact, intentionally underfueling can slow metabolism as the body attempts to store nutrients. Diet culture may have turned calories into something to fear or avoid, when in reality they are simply units of measurement for energy. And we all need energy!

Sometimes it’s as simple as that. Reorienting my perspective to see food not as a reward, but a power source, changed everything. And fueling both my body and brain are equally worthy. I need complex nourishment after finishing a 10K just as much as I will after writing this essay. And it won’t just be a collection of the appropriate dose of macros to keep me going. It will be salty (or sweet) and rich, crunchy and colorful, something I genuinely crave.

Ein Grund für die schwindenden Reserven ist die gestiegene Nachfrage aus den USA. Wie die Nachrichtenagentur «Canadian Press» berichtet, deckten sich US-Kunden im vergangenen Jahr wegen des Zollstreits zwischen den USA und Kanada mit Ahornsirup ein. Laut Regierungsangaben stiegen die Exporte in die USA zwischen 2024 und 2025 um 17 Prozent. Bereits im Vorjahreszeitraum hatten sie deutlich zugelegt, also vor dem Amtsantritt von US-Präsident Donald Trump. Die USA sind mit einem Anteil von mehr als 60 Prozent der wichtigste Abnehmer von kanadischem Ahornsirup. Deutschland folgt mit gut 7 Prozent.

Wie viel Ahornsirup die Produzenten gewinnen können, hängt stark von der Witterung ab, sagt SRF-Wirtschaftsredaktor Klaus Ammann. Für eine gute Ernte braucht es frostige Nächte und milde Tage. Beides sei im vergangenen Jahr in Kanada ungünstig gewesen.

Ahornbäume wachsen meist in Wäldern. Im Winter bohren die Produzentinnen und Produzenten Löcher in die Stämme und setzen Zapfen ein. Folgt auf eine Frostnacht ein wärmerer Tag, steigt zuckerhaltiger Saft im Baum auf. Dieser wird aufgefangen und über Schläuche zur Produktionsstätte geleitet. Dort wird er stundenlang eingekocht, bis das Wasser verdampft ist und ein goldbrauner, dickflüssiger Sirup entsteht. Für einen Liter Ahornsirup braucht es rund 40 Liter Baumsaft.

Fällt auch die nächste Ernte im Frühjahr 2027 schlecht aus, könnte Ahornsirup knapper und teurer werden. Bislang konnte die Branche schwächere Ernten mithilfe ihrer strategischen Reserve ausgleichen.

Die strategische Reserve soll Schwankungen bei der Ernte ausgleichen und Angebot sowie Preise stabilisieren. Sie ist eine Massnahme der Branche in Québec und unterscheidet sich von den Pflichtlagern in der Schweiz, die der Bund für Krisenfälle vorschreibt, etwa für Zucker, Reis und Weizen.

Ohne diesen Vorrat fehlt der Ahornproduktion ein Teil des bisherigen Puffers. Die Produzenten hoffen nun auf eine gute nächste Ernte, um die Reserve wieder auffüllen zu können.

The ranking "America’s Growth Leaders 2027" is the result of a comprehensive analysis conducted to identify the top performing publicly listed companies in the US. The analysis is based on three primary dimensions: Growth Performance, Financial Stability and Stock Performance.

The first dimension, Growth Performance, was assessed using data from Statista’s revenue database, which contains company growth data for the last five years. To be considered for the evaluation the companies had to be headquartered in the USA, publicly traded and demonstrate strong revenue growth in the last five years. Relative growth and the number of years in which growth was achieved were considered in the evaluation.

The second dimension, Financial Stability, was investigated by analyzing the companies’ profitability as well as using classic metrics such as the Piotroski F-Score and Altman Z-Score, which refer to a company’s overall financial health and the probability to file for bankruptcy. Industry specific KPIs were utilized to portray financial stability of companies within the banking, insurance and REIT sector.

The third dimension, Stock Performance, was evaluated based on multiple KPIs, that underline the companies’ strengths as a stock investment. The analysis process focused on the average annual growth rate, recent returns, a comparison to the overall market and the stocks’ volatility for the past 5 years.

Once the data was collected and evaluated, it was consolidated and weighted within a scoring model. The scores of all three dimensions were added on an equal percentage basis to form the final ranking score of a maximum of 100 points. The 500 companies with the highest score were awarded as “America’s Growth Leaders 2027” by TIME and Statista.

Treefrog ‘fingerprints’ are on their eyeballs

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 1:06 PM EDT

Our fingerprints, patterns on whale flukes, and fur patterns on cheetahs are examples of the natural ways we can tell one individual animal from another. For an Amazonian treefrog called the egg-eating spiny-backed frog (Osteocephalus oophagus), we need to look into their eyeballs. Fine black radial lines cross their golden irises, and form a pattern similar to our fingerprints. A study recently published in the journal Herpetozoa details the discovery.

The project began with study co-author Mélissa Peignier, a behavioral ecologist at the University of Bern in Switzerland, admiring frog eyes.

“At the time, I was doing my PhD, studying Allobates femoralis in the field in French Guiana,” Peignier said in a statement. “My first reaction was simply something along the lines of, ‘Wow, what beautiful eyes!’ That initial curiosity gradually turned into a genuine interest in the species, and I realised how little we actually know about them.”

There was also an ethical component. One of the standard ways to tell amphibians apart from one another is toe-clipping, where a section or all of a single toe is removed. This procedure can affect their survival, physiology, and behavior.

“Other studies have used toe-clipping, which involves removing some of the digits. Personally, I was uncomfortable with this approach, especially given that these are arboreal frogs that rely on their limbs to climb trees.”

Looking for other markings could offer a more humane solution and a way to observe the animals natural behavior unimpeded.

For this study, fieldwork took place over three rainy seasons (2021 to 2023) on a river island in the Nouragues Nature Reserve in French Guiana. They identified 103 individual frogs over the course of the study.

During their later encounters, the team took pictures of each animal’s eyes without touching it and then compared the images with a reference library. This way, the recognised frogs were left undisturbed and their eyes appeared in their more natural state. The identifications were then independently verified using Wild-ID, a software package that analyzes photographic markings in animals.

They found highly distinctive iris patterns. Within one season, between 56 and 80 percent of the frogs were recaptured and reliably re-identified. According to the team, this indicates that the patterns on their eyes remain stable enough to monitor the frog in the short-term. Photographs could even be taken of frogs while in their mating embrace (called amplexus) without disrupting them. The team hopes that this method will spread.

“In a world where biodiversity is declining at an alarming rate, it is essential that we minimise our impact on the animals we study,” added Peignier. “If we can reliably identify individuals using naturally occurring features, we may be able to reduce the need for more invasive marking techniques while still collecting the data needed to answer important scientific questions.”

While the reliability of the eye markings will vary between species, and should be done case by case, the team sees considerable promise for amphibians with complex irises. And working with them offers its own benefits.

“Honestly, the frogs themselves are incredibly beautiful and surprisingly chill, which makes working with them a real pleasure,” Peignier said.

Meet Mori, a Cute Robot Mushroom That Doesn’t Really Say (or Do) Anything

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You've had a long day. You finally get home, fling the door open, plonk on the couch, and let out a big sigh. In the corner of your eye, you notice your mushroom lamp perk up. Its microphone must have heard you come in. Its head raises to reveal round, animated eyes, and a little diamond-shaped nose—which actually houses a camera.

It does a little jiggle because it sees you're back. Its name is Mori.

As you move around the space—maybe it's time to start cooking dinner—the little robot's face follows you, observing your movements and making soft sounds that are a cross between a bird and R2-D2. The day's stress catches up, and you need a moment to relax, so you head over to Mori, touch its head, and its eyes and camera-nose dip back inside its body. It goes back to being a lamp, except now it plays nature soundscapes so you can feel at ease.

That's the pitch from OKA, or One Kind Artifact, a new startup based in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and Shenzhen, China, focusing on experimental technology. CEO Hua Wang tells WIRED he wants his company to create five experimental “Artifacts” over the course of OKA's lifetime. Mori is the first.

“There are certain relationships in this era we want to fix between humans and our surroundings—human with nature, human with one another—every product is an experience trying to reconnect some of those relationships,” Wang says. Before cofounding OKA, he was a general manager at Meipai, a video-sharing app from the Chinese phone brand Meitu. He also cofounded Bondee, a now-defunct social media app that briefly went viral in Asia.

According to the company, the point of Mori, the nearly 12-inch-tall home robot lamp, is to reconnect its owners with nature. It can play nature soundscapes. It will glow different colors—inspired by rain, forests, fire, and wind—while reacting to your movements. It can guide you through a calming breathing session.

It also uses its built-in camera and microphones to serve as a fun little interactive companion in your home. Ask it how your outfit looks, and Mori will jiggle excitedly. If you're playing music or watching TV, Mori can dance to the tunes and react to the action on your TV screen.

It intentionally does not do anything else. Unlike an Amazon Echo, it isn't an assistant: You can't use voice commands to control smart lights or cue up Spotify tracks, and it won't answer if you ask about the weather.

The Founder's Edition is a staggering $649 and is available starting today, though stock is limited to a few hundred units. That price also includes a two-year warranty and two years of Mori's subscription service ($17 per month or $80 a year). If you miss out on the Founder’s Edition, you can still buy a standard edition Mori, but that costs $849 (there's a preorder price of $799), and that higher price shockingly does not include any of the subscription features.

Wang says today's home robots have mostly taken on “machine-like” forms that don't mesh well with personal environments, so OKA designed a lamp that blends in, but is also anthropomorphized to make being at home “feel better.”

OKA's AI algorithms (powered by multiple local large language models) let Mori understand the room's context through the camera and microphone, so it knows when to interact and when to stay quiet. The company calls this “Attentive Intelligence.” It has memory, so it remembers you and interacts with you a certain way over time, whereas it might act a little more shy if you bring home a guest. If it doesn't see someone for a month, it will forget them.

It might notice, for example, that you've been on your phone doomscrolling for two hours straight on the couch. Mori might then try to get your attention so you come over, listen to some wind rustling through the leaves, and give your eyes a break. When you're on a phone call, or maybe having an argument with a significant other, it understands what’s happening and goes silent so as not to distract you.

“All gadgets try to capture more of your attention,” Wang says. “Mori gives your attention back to you. It invites you to do meditative experiences. Nature has this restorative power to humans.”

The robot can connect to Wi-Fi, which should give you pause from a security standpoint, but Wang says the internet connection is only used for firmware updates.

Mori processes raw images and audio locally, and then discards the files. Facial feature data—which is collected so it knows to react to you versus a guest—is encrypted on the device, and never uploaded. The camera turns off when the robot retracts into a lamp, and the microphone turns off when you double-tap the top of Mori's head. It is battery-powered, so you can take it from room to room, or just leave it plugged in.

Wang says Mori's skill set is limited for now, though it could expand in the future. He suggests it could connect to your Oura Ring data in the future so it knows exactly when you need some relaxing sounds, or to your Spotify account to play different content. Voice commands could even come later—like asking Mori to play nature sounds instead of having to touch it.

“People feel isolated and want this kind of presence in their home. A lot of people leave their TV on to fill the emptiness of the room,” Wang says, pointing to a time when he was feeling isolated while studying abroad in the US. “It's not particularly for the elderly or children, but for younger generations.”

Cute robot pets that interact with you have been welcomed into our homes before. Chris Harrison, director of the Future Interfaces Group at Carnegie Mellon University, points to Sony's Aibo dog from 1999 as an early digital companion. However, he says the crucial ingredient they have been missing is what artificial intelligence can now provide. These previous robots were too simplistic, but now that the modern versions can understand and react to human actions and emotions, they could become more impactful.

“Humans have always wanted to anthropomorphize everything—they love making things cute,” Harrison says. “We want to make robots cute; we want to put cute little cases on our iPhones.” He points to Meta's new Muse agent, which has a cuddly avatar. “It is almost like a pet, but a hyper-capable pet.”

When I point out that Mori doesn't seem very capable with its limited feature set, Harrison likens it to people owning a goldfish or a hamster.

“Are we holding [Mori] to an unrealistic bar? People do extract a lot of pleasure out of taking care of their pets and snuggling with their pets and interacting with their pets that I wouldn't say are necessarily more sophisticated.”

Whether people will shell out hundreds of dollars for a robotic pet that can’t fetch—or even tell you the time—is up in the air. But if this mushroom-shaped, camera-wielding lamp can fool overstimulated brains into feeling a little less alone at the end of the day, maybe that’s enough.
