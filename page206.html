Training AIs to Solve CAPTCHAs

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Interesting research:

Abstract: Vision-language models (VLMs) can solve visual CAPTCHAs without task-specific training, but the agents built on them approach every challenge from scratch. For such an agent, the hundredth instance of a familiar puzzle costs as much time and compute as the first. Specialized detectors invert the trade-off, answering in milliseconds but only for categories they were trained on. Neither improves with exposure. We study what changes when a solver improves with use. Our system pairs a fine-tuned YOLOv8 detector with an open-weight VLM behind a confidence-based router, and runs entirely from screenshots and operating-system input events, with no browser automation or DOM access. It reaches 85.4% overall and 84.2% macro accuracy across 16 classes, exceeding either component alone. Every answer VLM produces also serves as a training label, so the detector absorbs categories it was never trained for, typically after one or two encounters and without human annotation. The same loop also repairs it. A CAPTCHA operator can perturb images against the publicly released detector and drive its accuracy to 0%, but the perturbations leave VLM untouched, and its labels let the detector recover. Under a year-long simulated arms race in which the CAPTCHA operator re-crafts its perturbations each month, the solver recovers every round, and a cheap ~70%-accurate open-weight teacher hardens it as effectively as a perfect oracle. Visual CAPTCHA defenses that assume a failing bot stays failing therefore understate how quickly an adaptive solver returns...

Wie ein Obdachloser vor einer Londoner Luxusvilla lebt

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mitten in London lebt Anders Fernstedt - völlig ohne Geld. Der gebürtige Schwede hat sich unter dem Vordach einer der teuersten Immobilien eingerichtet. Dass er da bleiben kann, hat er einem Zufall zu verdanken.

In Londons Nobelviertel Knightsbridge gibt es viel zu sehen. Das Viertel im Zentrum der britischen Hauptstadt liegt nur einen Steinwurf vom Buckingham Palace, dem Hyde Park und dem Kaufhaus Harrods entfernt und zieht täglich Scharen von Touristinnen und Touristen an.

Außer vor den berühmten Sehenswürdigkeiten bleiben viele von ihnen aber auch hier stehen: Vor dem Eingang eines prächtigen Stadthauses stapeln sich Pflanzen, Sonnenschirme, Einkaufstaschen und Kinderspielzeug. Mittendrin lebt Anders Fernstedt. Der Schwede, der nach einer schwierigen Lebensphase auf der Straße landete, hat sich in der Vorhalle des Gebäudes ein provisorisches Zuhause eingerichtet und wohnt dort inzwischen seit mehr als drei Jahren.

Ausgerechnet diese Villa war vor wenigen Jahren noch Londons teuerstes Wohngebäude. Die Luxusimmobilie am Rutland Gate 2-8A wurde 2020 für die damalige Rekordsumme von 210 Millionen Pfund (rund 245 Millionen Euro) an Hui Ka Yan, den Gründer des chinesischen Immobilienkonzerns Evergrande, verkauft. Der Milliardär wurde im vergangenen Monat zu lebenslanger Haft verurteilt.

Hui hat das Anwesen nie bezogen. Das Haus ist auf den Namen seiner Frau registriert. Da ihre Vermögenswerte eingefroren wurden, kann die Immobilie nicht von Insolvenzverwaltern verwertet werden und befindet sich seither in einem rechtlichen Schwebezustand.

Als Fernstedt damals darüber stolperte, dachte er sich nichts dabei: "Ich dachte einfach, das wäre ein Unterschlupf für eine Nacht", erzählt er der DW. "Ich bin begeisterter Camper und interessiere mich für Pflanzen. Vor der Natur habe ich keine Angst."

Fernstedt hat das Gebäude selbst nie betreten, das inzwischen einen deutlich vernachlässigten Eindruck macht. Stattdessen richtete er sich nach und nach in der Vorhalle ein. Hinter den mächtigen Säulen des Eingangsbereichs liegt eine Villa mit 45 Zimmern, eigenem Innenpool und 116 Fenstern.

Der Schwede schläft das ganze Jahr über draußen. Ein großer Sonnenschirm dient ihm zusätzlich als Schutz und schafft eine abgeschirmte Nische, in der er Matratzen und Bettzeug untergebracht hat. "Ich kann mich nicht erinnern, wann ich zuletzt schlecht geschlafen habe", sagte er. "Und das gilt für alle vier Jahreszeiten. Im Winter wird es nicht kalt, und weil ich keine Wände habe, zieht im Sommer eine angenehme Brise durch."

Zu Beginn habe er sich eine schöne Illusion geschaffen, erzählt Fernstedt. "Ich habe mir vorgestellt ich sei ein Junge, der mit seinen Eltern in diesem Haus wohnt, und der diese gefragt habe, ob er auf dem Balkon zelten dürfe."

Fernstedt wurde von seiner Mutter in Schweden großgezogen. Später arbeitete er in verschiedenen Berufen in Schweden und den USA und machte sich in der Technologiebranche einen Namen. 2016 zog er nach England. Dort arbeitete er unter anderem als Journalist für verschiedene Medienanstalten, darunter auch für den Economist. Doch rund um die Corona-Pandemie gingen die Aufträge immer weiter zurück.

Eine Beteiligung an einem Technologieunternehmen aus seiner Zeit in den USA verschaffte ihm zunächst noch finanzielle Reserven. Doch das Polster schmolz mit der Zeit dahin. Fernstedt lebte schließlich in einem Zelt und später in Übergangsunterkünften, wo er nach eigenen Angaben Gewalt erlebte und mit Menschen in Kontakt kam, die unter schweren Suchterkrankungen litten.

"Das Kurioseste an meinem Lebensstil ist für viele Menschen, dass ich völlig ohne Geld auskomme", sagt er. Fernstedt hat sich ein Netzwerk aus Bäckereien, Cafés und Blumenläden aufgebaut. Dort erhält er übrig gebliebene Lebensmittel, Blumen für sein Zuhause und gelegentlich Zugang zu Strom. Für Duschen, Kleiderspenden und soziale Kontakte nutzt er zudem die Angebote einer Obdachlosenhilfsorganisation im nahegelegenen Stadtteil Victoria. Für viele Anwohnende gehört er inzwischen ganz selbstverständlich zum Straßenbild von Knightsbridge.

"Das ist ein sehr gehobenes Viertel", sagt Fernstedt. "Schon nach wenigen Wochen war mir klar, dass alle wussten, dass ich hier lebe." Er habe sich dann gefragt, wie er Präsenz zeigen könne, ohne ständig präsent zu sein. Deshalb richtete er seine Aufmerksamkeit auf die Kinder und Enkelkinder der Anwohnenden. "Wenn sie sich am Küchentisch für mich einsetzen, verbessern sich meine Chancen", so Fernstedt. So erklärt sich seine Spielzeugsammlung vor dem Haus.

Ob er keine Sorge habe, seine Habseligkeiten offen liegen zu lassen? Fernstedt lacht. "Das ist ein bisschen wie der Nussspeicher eines Eichhörnchens", sagt er. "Ich habe so viele Sachen angesammelt, dass ich es gar nicht bemerke, wenn etwas verschwindet."

Es gibt Nachbarn, die seine Anwesenheit kritisch sehen. Doch der Schwede bemüht sich, sein provisorisches Zuhause ordentlich zu halten und möglichst unauffällig zu leben. Andere sehen sogar einen Vorteil darin: Denn solange er dort lebt, gilt das leerstehende Anwesen als weniger anfällig für Einbrecher oder Vandalismus.

Insgesamt habe er die Nachbarschaft jedoch als offen und hilfsbereit erlebt. "Manchmal stellen sich Leute vor und fragen, ob ich etwas brauche", erzählt er. "Meine Standardantwort lautet dann: eigentlich genau das, was Sie mir gerade gegeben haben: ein bisschen normale Zeit und ein freundliches Gespräch unter Nachbarn." Manche brächten auch Essen vorbei und er habe auch schon "elegante italienische Abendjacken" und andere Kleidungsstücke geschenkt bekommen.

Doch nicht alle obdachlosen Menschen in London haben ein so stabiles Umfeld gefunden wie Fernstedt. Nach Schätzungen der Hilfsorganisation Crisis gelten in der britischen Hauptstadt mehr als 200.000 Menschen als wohnungslos. Allein zwischen April und Juni dieses Jahres wurden in London 4.392 Menschen erfasst, die tatsächlich auf der Straße schliefen. Das sind vier Prozent mehr als im gleichen Zeitraum des Vorjahres und 58 Prozent mehr als vor zehn Jahren.

Nach Regierungsangaben standen 2025 in London 105.138 Wohnungen und Häuser leer. Das entspricht etwa jeder 37. Immobilie in der britischen Hauptstadt. Premierminister Andy Burnham, der im Juli sein Amt antrat, versprach, bis Weihnachten jedem Menschen, der in England auf der Straße lebt, einen Weg aus der Obdachlosigkeit aufzuzeigen.

Angesichts des enormen Werts des Anwesens weiß Fernstedt, dass er jederzeit gezwungen sein könnte, sein ungewöhnliches Zuhause zu verlassen, auch wenn die Villa seit Jahren leer steht. Dennoch hat ihm dieser Ort etwas gegeben, wonach er sich lange gesehnt hatte. Zuvor war er mit seinem Zelt von einem Platz zum nächsten gezogen oder in Unterkünften gelandet, in denen er sich oft unsicher fühlte. "Ich habe nicht mehr ständig die Sorge, um die nächste Ecke zu fahren und feststellen zu müssen, dass plötzlich alles weg ist", sagt er. "Ich bin schon so lange hier, dass ich aufgehört habe, mir ständig Sorgen zu machen. Denn wenn wir uns zu viele Sorgen machen, können wir nicht glücklich sein."

This snake cartwheels to escape danger

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 19, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

Snakes will bite, rattle their tails, and even spit venom to escape danger. But of all the world’s species, southeast Asia’s dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) relies on an especially fascinating escape method. When threatened, the diminutive serpent rolls away like a speeding wheel.

An adult dwarf reed snake typically measures less than 12 inches long, but its unique locomotive maneuvers allow it to retreat as fast one foot per second—about four times faster than it travels by simply slithering on the ground. It’s not a flailing getaway, either. The snake also easily rolls along a straight path while somehow keeping its head both stable and facing forward.

Now, researchers finally have a better understanding of how the dwarf reed snake achieves such dizzying acrobatics. In a study published today in the journal Current Biology, a team at Japan’s Toho University detailed the specific physiology that enables its locomotion after filming the snakes with high-speed cameras. Essentially, the snake doesn’t rotate its entire body in a singular, forward motion—it whips one side of its body forward towards the ground while an arched portion swings down by its head in what the study’s authors call a “stroke.” The snake then alternates between its left and right side coming into contact with the ground for each successive stroke. Essentially, it’s like twirling a rope in a lateral circle while holding one end in your hand—except that end is the snake’s head.

The cumulative efforts bypass the traditional idea of a wheel requiring something like a central axle. Instead, this ingenious, highly controlled movement allows the animal to keep its head and sensory organs upright and facing ahead while traveling at such high speed. This is compounded by the dwarf reed snake directing regions of its body to engage in large, alternating rotations while stabilizing its head.

By gaining better insight into these biological mechanisms, researchers also highlight the difficulty in trying to predict an animal’s capabilities based solely on its physiology. A snake may have primarily evolved to slither—but that doesn’t mean it can’t get creative when necessary.

Bob Woodward reveals "dream source" in memoir, "Secrets," out next month

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Bob Woodward — in an eye-opening excerpt from his forthcoming memoir, "Secrets," that's previewed in the new issue of The New Yorker — reveals that one of his best friends, the late Richard Darman, was also one of his best sources during the Ronald Reagan and George H.W. Bush administrations.

  • "Darman was willing, even eager at times, to talk about the most sensitive intelligence matters, including N.S.A. intercepts and covert C.I.A. operations," Woodward writes in his book, out Nov. 15. "He was always careful not to talk about matters that would harm national security, I believed, but he would go right up to the edge — a reporter's dream source."

Why it matters: Woodward, 83 — who followed the blockbuster Watergate reporting of his youth with a shelf of revelatory books about American presidents — gives a searingly honest account of the collapse of his friendship with Darman after a Post series that was damaging to Bush. Woodward details a disappointment in his Washington Post career and, in the case of a story where he was the responsible editor, a "moral failing."

  • The New Yorker calls the Darman excerpt "A Dangerous Friendship," revealing the danger of "getting too close to a source." Woodward and Darman never reconciled, although "Secrets" describes their deathbed encounter.

Woodward writes in his New Yorker debut that he and Darman, who controlled the paper flow to Reagan as staff secretary and was budget director for Bush 41, "had countless long lunches together — Saturday or Sunday — at the Madison Hotel, across the street from the Post, discussing government, journalism, and ourselves":

"I felt like I was getting a master class in how government really operated. Plus, many scoops. In later years, Darman would drive out on weekends to our country house near Annapolis, Maryland, to spend large parts of the day with me and my wife, the journalist Elsa Walsh, who was particularly fond of him. Some people found him arrogant, but I loved his comfortable confidence as a friend and a source. It was part of what made him so extraordinary and valuable. He knew how to turn on the charm, and he did for both Elsa and me."

Behind the scenes: "From critical national-security information to the colorful office tales he shared, Darman provided me with a gold mine of information," Woodward recalls. "I interviewed him in at least fifty-eight meetings or phone conversations. I have boxes filled with hundreds of pages of notes on our discussions. After our most important conversations, as is my practice, I typed a memo of one to six pages, laying out in detail what he'd said."

  • Detailing Darman's "hatchet man" role, Woodward reveals that the "hardest of Darman's targets to dislodge was the First Lady, Nancy Reagan. While Darman poured scorn on her in private, describing her to me as a 'mean bitch,' he could not find a way to drive a stake into her heart."

After a hard-won legislative victory, Darman told Woodward, Reagan said: "Whew! Ever feel like you shit a pineapple?"

Pourquoi l'Internet de Starlink joue un rôle clé dans la guerre des drones qui oppose l'Ukraine à la Russie

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Les satellites Starlink, exploités par la société SpaceX d'Elon Musk, offrent un accès Internet haut débit qui s'avère très précieux dans les conflits modernes.

Dans le cas de l'Ukraine, ce service est essentiel pour les communications sur le champ de bataille et pour garantir l'efficacité de sa guerre des drones contre la Russie.

Grâce à Starlink, les drones ukrainiens peuvent rester connectés à leurs opérateurs à des distances plus grandes qu'avec les réseaux radio classiques, ce qui les rend également moins vulnérables aux interférences.

Mais mardi soir, Starlink a soudainement et de manière inattendue limité la vitesse de vol à laquelle les drones peuvent se connecter à son réseau Internet.

Cela a perturbé certaines « missions » sur le champ de bataille, selon un haut responsable ukrainien, tout en entravant la capacité de Kiev à se défendre contre les attaques russes.

La limitation de vitesse à 160 km/h impliquait que tout drone volant plus vite que cela perdait la connexion, ce qui les rendait pratiquement inutilisables.

« Starlink donne à l'Ukraine la capacité de voir ce qui se profile à l'horizon et de protéger sa population », a déclaré Kurt Volker, ancien ambassadeur des États-Unis auprès de l'OTAN, à l'émission Today de BBC Radio 4. « Si cette communication est interrompue, davantage de personnes mourront, et la ralentir comme cela a été fait est une décision très cynique et insensible. »

Des sources du ministère ukrainien de la Défense ont indiqué à la BBC qu'une partie du service militaire Starshield de Starlink avait été rétablie mercredi soir, même s'il n'est pas encore clair si les restrictions concernant la vitesse à laquelle les drones peuvent voler sans perdre la connexion ont été entièrement levées.

Ces sources ont ajouté que les autorités continuaient à travailler pour faire la lumière sur « les raisons et les circonstances » de cette mesure.

Mais cette mesure a démontré une fois de plus à quel point Starlink peut influencer le conflit qui ravage le pays depuis que la Russie a lancé son invasion à grande échelle en Ukraine en février 2022.

Des informations vérifiées à portée de main

Starlink est un réseau Internet qui utilise des satellites en orbite terrestre basse pour fournir une connexion Internet, notamment dans les zones reculées et sur les champs de bataille.

Il compte actuellement plus de 10 000 satellites actifs, ce qui en fait la plus grande constellation de satellites au monde.

Il appartient à SpaceX, l'entreprise d'Elon Musk, et constitue la principale source de revenus de la société.

Alors que Starlink est destiné à un usage civil, Starshield est son service de niveau militaire, conçu pour le Pentagone.

Starshield s'appuie sur le réseau Starlink, mais offre une sécurité renforcée, notamment grâce à un cryptage amélioré, afin que les informations confidentielles puissent être transmises sans être interceptées.

Le gouvernement américain a autorisé l'Ukraine à utiliser Starlink et Starshield pour se défendre contre la Russie. Musk a coupé l'accès de Moscou à ce service en février, ce qui a donné à Kiev un avantage dans le conflit.

En bref, Starlink utilise ses milliers de satellites pour fournir un accès Internet haut débit dans presque tous les coins de la planète, grâce à des terminaux terrestres que les utilisateurs peuvent facilement acheter et installer.

Comme le dit Musk, il suffit de suivre deux instructions : « pointer vers le ciel et se connecter ».

Cela les rend idéaux pour une utilisation sur le champ de bataille.

« C'est une connexion… qui, en gros, peut être utilisée très rapidement ; elle ne nécessite aucun équipement particulier, aucune préparation : il suffit de se rendre sur le terrain et, en général, ça fonctionne », a déclaré à la BBC un soldat prénommé Neo, membre de l'une des unités de reconnaissance par drones de l'Ukraine.

"Simplifica nuestro trabajo porque podemos traducir o transmitir en directo nuestras operaciones de reconocimiento", añadió.

Según George Barros, del Instituto para el Estudio de la Guerra (ISW), la red se ha convertido en "la columna vertebral de las comunicaciones militares en Ucrania" y es utilizada por las fuerzas navales, terrestres y aéreas de Kyiv.

En Ukraine, on utilise aussi bien Starlink (le service civil) que Starshield (le service militaire), en fonction des besoins en matière de communication.

Comme l'a expliqué M. Barros à la BBC, cela est essentiel pour une utilisation efficace d'armes telles que les drones, qui se connectent au réseau afin que les opérateurs puissent visualiser leurs cibles en temps réel, réduisant ainsi au minimum le délai entre la détection et le tir.

La Russie n'étant pas autorisée à utiliser Starlink, lorsque l'Ukraine lance des missiles à longue portée à l'intérieur du territoire russe pour attaquer des raffineries de pétrole, le réseau ne fonctionne pas.

En ce qui concerne la légitime défense de l'Ukraine, le pays dépend du réseau Starlink pour protéger son espace aérien contre les attaques russes, en particulier les drones Shahed à réaction, a ajouté M. Barros.

Les Shahed volent à des vitesses supérieures à 319 km/h, ce qui signifie que la restriction imposée par Starlink a rendu pratiquement impossibles les efforts ukrainiens pour les localiser.

Pour le soldat Neo, cela signifie que toute restriction délibérée imposée au réseau Starlink constitue « une trahison ».

En l'absence de solution de remplacement immédiate au réseau Starlink, toute limitation de vitesse ou perte de connectivité a des implications importantes pour les défenses de l'Ukraine, et les récentes restrictions ont démontré la forte dépendance de Kiev à l'égard de ce réseau.

Même si Starlink peut fournir un accès presque partout dans le monde, cela ne signifie pas pour autant qu'il soit accessible partout, et les pays doivent obtenir une licence pour l'utiliser.

La Russie fait partie des pays où il n'est pas autorisé, bien que Kiev ait déclaré par le passé que Moscou continuait à se procurer des terminaux et à les utiliser sur le territoire ukrainien occupé.

« Si SpaceX apprend qu'un utilisateur non autorisé utilise une borne Starlink, nous mènerons une enquête sur cette plainte et prendrons les mesures nécessaires pour désactiver la borne si cela se confirme », a déclaré Starlink en 2024.

Mais avec des dizaines de milliers de bornes rien qu'en Ukraine, il pourrait s'avérer pratiquement impossible de déterminer quelles bornes sont autorisées et lesquelles ne le sont pas.

Lors d'une visite à Londres mercredi, le président Volodymyr Zelensky avait déclaré qu'il espérait qu'il ne s'agissait que d'une erreur ; cependant, dans une interview accordée par la suite au Financial Times, il a affirmé : « Ce n'était pas un problème technique, c'était un choix délibéré. »

Musk a affirmé que Starshield était géré par le gouvernement américain et a laissé entendre que le Pentagone pourrait en être responsable.

« Les systèmes Starshield appartiennent au gouvernement américain et sont sous son contrôle », a-t-il écrit sur X. « Toute limitation de vitesse ou d'utilisation est décidée par les forces armées américaines et, en dernier ressort, par le président des États-Unis. »

« Starlink est un service commercial détenu et contrôlé par SpaceX. L'utilisation de Starlink est limitée aux activités civiles ou de défense et ne peut être employée à des fins militaires offensives, car cela serait inapproprié pour une entreprise commerciale », a-t-il conclu.

La BBC a tenté d'obtenir des commentaires de la Maison Blanche et du Pentagone.

Les restrictions imposées mardi soir ont coïncidé avec les attaques meurtrières menées par la Russie contre l'Ukraine, qui ont privé d'électricité 600 000 personnes et endommagé les infrastructures de communication et d'énergie utilisées par l'armée ukrainienne.

Les Ukrainiens ont affirmé n'avoir reçu aucun avertissement concernant l'arrivée des drones avant qu'ils ne frappent.

« Il est clair que cette action ne peut pas être une coïncidence et qu'elle est liée à la situation politique actuelle », a écrit Serhiy Beskrestnov, conseiller de Zelensky, sur Telegram mercredi matin.

Les relations entre les États-Unis et l'Ukraine se sont récemment détériorées, notamment depuis que les États-Unis ont annoncé un accord avec la Russie visant à importer son diesel sur le marché américain.

Zelensky a qualifié cela de « cadeau » pour le président russe Vladimir Poutine, et Donald Trump a répondu qu'il estimait qu'« il était temps que l'Ukraine ait un nouveau président ».

Dimanche, Trump a annoncé un cessez-le-feu concernant les infrastructures énergétiques, mais trois jours plus tard, l'Ukraine a repris ses attaques, accusant la Russie de ne pas respecter l'accord et les États-Unis de lui retirer un soutien crucial.

Le Kremlin a déclaré n'avoir jamais accepté de cessez-le-feu.

Zelensky a accusé les États-Unis de menacer de retenir des renseignements si l'Ukraine continuait à attaquer les installations pétrolières russes. Trump a nié ces allégations, mais il a déclaré la semaine dernière à la presse que Zelensky « ferait mieux de mettre fin » à ces attaques.

Lors de son séjour à Londres, Zelensky a reconnu se sentir trahi par les États-Unis.

L'accord conclu par Trump avec la Russie constitue la dernière tentative de son gouvernement pour faire baisser les prix du carburant aux États-Unis avant les élections de mi-mandat de novembre, après des mois passés à gérer les conséquences politiques de la guerre avec l'Iran.

Ce conflit, qui a débuté en février, a fait flamber les prix, ce qui a nui à sa popularité auprès des Américains.

Un plan de pensiones o proteger la salud: el mejor regalo de Navidad de las pymes para sus trabajadores

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Tu negocio asegura el futuro de todos: encuentra aquí la mejor ayuda profesional para tu empresa

La relación entre empresas y trabajadores ha cambiado. Y no se trata de tendencias, sino de una realidad que va en crecimiento y de la que se extraen beneficios para ambas partes. La Previsión Social Colectiva (PSC) se ha convertido en el canal idóneo donde establecer y conformar una situación que va más allá de los estrictamente laboral.

La PSC ofrece un conjunto de soluciones de protección y de ahorro que las empresas contratan para sus empleados para mejorar su retribución emocional y obtener ventajas. Por ejemplo, de cara a la jubilación se pueden establecer planes de pensiones de empleo, o distintos programas de ahorro y salud. Ramón Ramos, responsable de planes de pensiones y seguros colectivos de vida de AXA, explica de qué forma se articula: “La empresa impulsa el ahorro de sus trabajadores a través de un salario diferido mediante los seguros de jubilación y planes de pensiones para complementar la prestación de la Seguridad Social, o de un salario en el momento actual mediante el pago de la prima de los seguros de vida, accidentes y salud con los que complementar los servicios y prestaciones públicas. Todo ello con el apoyo de ventajas fiscales”.

Louise Glück’s Life in Restaurants

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

If Maria Rondeau and JuanMa Calderón were literalists, they might have put nasturtiums on the menu at Rosa y Marigold, the Peruvian restaurant they opened in April, in Boston. They took the name from the novella “Marigold and Rose,” the final book published by their friend Louise Glück, the Nobel Prize-winning poet, before she died, in 2023. The story is told from the perspective of infant twins with divergent personalities—Rose is unguardedly buoyant, whereas Marigold is watchful and introspective. “The garden relied on nasturtiums, which Mother and Father ate,” the narrator observes. “It was strange to see the grilled fish with nasturtium butter melting over it. . . . But once you try it, Mother said, the taste is magic. The twins knew what magic was. It made the sun rise. Now they knew it had a taste.”

Rondeau, who grew up in Guatemala, is an architect; Calderón, her partner in life and in restaurants, is a filmmaker and chef from Peru. In September, I sat at Rosa y Marigold’s sleek U-shaped bar with Rondeau and the poet Dan Chiasson, a regular contributor to this magazine and another close friend of Glück’s, sharing thin strips of charred beef heart, tendrils of squid blanketed in a silky, lavender-hued olive mayonnaise, and a formidable mound of chaufa de mariscos—Chinese Peruvian seafood fried rice. To drink, Chiasson ordered his usual, a gin Martini, recalling fondly how Glück would fish the olives out of his glass. As an epileptic, she abstained from hard liquor, but she loved the flavor.

“The whole book is about this idea of duality,” Rondeau said. “It’s an allusion to her two grandchildren”—Glück’s only child, Noah Dranow, is the father of twins—“but it’s also Louise. I think many people are two sides.” Rondeau designed Rosa y Marigold’s dining room to have two sections, each with a mirrored-steel slab hanging overhead, slightly angled to offer a glimpse of the other side.

That afternoon, we’d visited Celeste, the couple’s first restaurant, which they opened in the spring of 2018 in a tiny triangular storefront in Somerville. Glück, whose apartment was nearby, soon became a regular, with a regular order: a cold beer, the arugula salad with lime and Parmesan, and the ceviche, sometimes followed by the sudado de pescado, a Chinese Peruvian dish of steamed fish and vegetables. She always came with a friend, occasionally two, and often requested that the music be turned down.

The couple knew little about her until, one day, Glück left behind an envelope return-addressed to Yale, and Rondeau looked her up, to get it back to her. After that, Glück made her reservations with Rondeau directly. “If anything was off—the salt, the lime, the fish—I would get a text,” Rondeau said affectionately. “She had a perfect palate!” Chiasson added. “She was our in-house critic,” Rondeau continued. “And I would always be extremely grateful, because she kept it real.”

One evening, Rondeau and Calderón mentioned that they’d been approached about writing a cookbook. The next time they saw her, she handed them a typewritten foreword, detailing how she’d first encountered Celeste in Boston magazine, which had published a photo of the ceviche. “It was a summer day and I wanted that ceviche immediately,” she wrote, admiring how it was “not drowned by the chef’s noisy creativity in shards of coconut or a confetti of pomegranate seeds / A very pure looking dish of glistening blue cod.” At the restaurant, Glück became “more like a citizen” than a regular, she wrote. “And Celeste is a world. But a world so much more wonderful and reliable than our real world.” Glück ate dinner at Celeste on the day she won the Nobel, surrounded by friends of Rondeau’s and Calderón’s, whom they’d invited to insure her privacy.

I first learned about Glück’s passion for restaurants in the spring of 2020. At the time, I was this magazine’s weekly restaurant critic, and I had shifted, suddenly, from dining out all over New York City to chronicling the industry’s attempts to stay afloat during lockdown. Glück e-mailed me to commiserate, and to ask if I had ideas for how to help. “Among so many horrors, the collapse of these enchanted worlds seems somehow desperately poignant,” she wrote.

I was lightly shocked to hear from her. I knew and revered her as one of the great living poets, and imagined her as particularly godlike, too high-minded to care about something as earthly as food. There was also the fact that she had struggled with anorexia as a young woman, which she wrote about in her 1989 lecture “Education of the Poet.” We struck up a sporadic correspondence. “I love to cook and have been a terrific and fastidious home cook,” she wrote with refreshing candor, but she yearned for the ritual of dining out. (Later, I learned that she’d bought a heat lamp for Celeste so that she could eat there outdoors.)

In “Education of the Poet,” Glück, who was born to a wealthy family in New York, in 1943 (her father, a Hungarian Jewish immigrant, helped invent the X-Acto knife), described her anorexia as an attempt to “establish a self with clear boundaries.” “The conflict played itself out most fiercely with my mother,” she wrote, who “only wavered when I began to refuse food, when I claimed, through implicit threat, ownership of my body, which was her great accomplishment.” By all accounts, Glück’s mother, Beatrice, was a wonderful cook; Glück’s papers, archived at Yale’s Beinecke Library, make clear that she grew up among people who loved to eat. In one letter from summer camp, addressed to her younger sister, Tereze, Glück wrote, “I miss—sundaes, shrimp cocktails,” next to tiny sketches of each.

Glück recovered from anorexia through years of psychoanalysis—years during which she also studied poetry with Léonie Adams and Stanley Kunitz. She published “Firstborn,” her début collection, in 1968, when she was twenty-five. Later letters detailed lavish hotel meals in Europe with her first husband and dinner parties in Plainfield, Vermont, where she taught writing at Goddard College and worked part time at a café, baking pastries in exchange for child care. (Noah, her son, grew up to be a sommelier.)

In 1980, John Dranow, Glück’s second husband and the director of the writing program at Goddard, co-founded the New England Culinary Institute, a small cooking school that operated restaurants in Montpelier. Glück sat on the board of directors until the nineties, when they divorced and she moved to Cambridge. In one poem from “Meadowlands,” an unsparing, darkly funny collection from that time, an unidentified husband and wife argue over food. “I stopped liking artichokes when I stopped eating / butter. Fennel / I never liked,” he says. “Living with you is like living / at boarding school: / chicken Monday, fish Tuesday.”

“Why do you call it rigidity?” she retorts. “Can’t you call it a taste for ceremony? Or is your hunger for beauty / completely satisfied by your own person?” The husband has the last word, accusing her of joyless pleasure-seeking: “When you make artichokes, / make them for yourself.”

The word “poet,” Glück wrote, “must be used cautiously; it names an aspiration, not an occupation. In other words: not a noun for a passport.” She could go years without writing, though she rejected the notion of writer’s block, perceiving instead a liminal state of waiting for poems to arrive. During fallow periods, Glück occupied herself with teaching, which she loved, gardening (she claimed to have read nothing but catalogues for two years), and eating at restaurants. “Dinner takes a certain shape,” the poet Katie Peterson, who ate with her frequently at Chez Panisse when Glück taught at Stanford, observed. “A familiar menu is like a rosary.”

In Cambridge, Chiasson pointed out Koreana, the restaurant where he, as a worshipful Harvard grad student, had first spotted Glück, in 1997, having dinner with the poets Robert Pinsky and Frank Bidart. (Bidart, Chiasson’s mentor, eventually introduced them.) Chiasson showed me an unpublished poem that he’d written for Glück after she died: “back before anything happened / yet it was still the bitter end / three poets sat around a lazy susan / kimchi, Tsing Tao, bibimbap, gossip.”

Earlier that day, we’d dropped in on the poet Henri Cole, in Boston’s South End. The two men, who had each shared countless meals with Glück, but never together, delighted in comparing notes, remembering her habit of sampling several wines before inevitably choosing something crisp and dry; her insistence that no meal was complete without greens; and the decisiveness with which she could turn on a place she’d loved. She almost always ordered the fish. She almost never ordered dessert, but would happily pluck the raspberry off of someone else’s crème brûlée. She preferred to eat early, and would wait outside her home, ready to be picked up.

Chiasson and Cole had last seen each at La Royal, another of Rondeau and Calderón’s restaurants, at a memorial for Glück, packed with her dinner companions. When Rondeau first told Glück, in 2021, that she and Calderón were considering opening a new place, Glück advised against it, then immediately changed her mind on a visit to the empty storefront, in a beautiful brick building from 1910, facing Royal Avenue in Cambridge. Not long before she died, Glück weighed in on the menu—both the dishes served and the printed layout—and chose the restaurant’s name.

Chiasson theorized that his friend’s love for both restaurants and poetry stemmed from her interest in “primal urges.” “We build these institutions to contain them and express them,” he said. “Marriage, restaurants, homes—they’re frames for containing all this passion.” In 1999, Glück imagined a creation story of sorts, describing small worlds that appeared in the fissures of a larger, shattered one. “On Huron Avenue they became / a block of stores,” she wrote, “whatever / they were or sold, they were / alike in their function: they were / visions of safety. Like / a resting place.” She called the poem “Formaggio,” after her favorite cheese shop, in Cambridge. ♦

“The Brightness,” Reviewed: Chad Harbach’s Sequel to “The Art of Fielding”

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Pella and Irma, two classmates at Westish College, in Wisconsin, are the best of friends. Pella, a newly engaged divorcée and former wild child, has just graduated. Irma, several years younger, is a rising academic star, a scholarship student from a rural Catholic family, so unhip that her lack of touch is its own form of cool. Together, they’ve been in weekly independent-study sessions, reading authors such as Edith Wharton, for the past three years. But if they’re such good friends—why aren’t they talking? Sure, Pella is planning her friend Owen’s wedding, and Irma is gearing up for her senior year. But that doesn’t explain the sudden distance between them, or why the reclusive Irma made the uncharacteristic decision to go to the big parties that open Westish’s school year without telling Pella.

“The Brightness” (Little, Brown), Chad Harbach’s new novel, clocks in at nearly seven hundred pages, but the bulk of it follows Pella and Irma over the span of a few late-summer days in 2015. At Owen’s wedding, Pella’s engagement falls apart and her friendship with Irma seems to reach a breaking point. At Westish, Irma participates in a prank that is meant to draw awareness to sexual assault on campus. The rest of the book covers the subsequent nine months, in which the two women navigate the fallout of these events, Pella from New York and Irma from Westish, only to be reunited toward the end.

By this point, they have assumed the same aimlessness. Pella isn’t getting married, Irma isn’t going to grad school, and neither of them has a job, let alone a career. Unlike other people they encounter throughout the book—people who have shaped themselves and climbed professional heights—there is something unfinished about Pella and Irma. They’re always becoming, never being. Usually, when people don’t grow up, something has gone awry: they didn’t get to, they didn’t plan to, or they have simply delayed as long as possible. With others, though, resistance to a sleek and defined persona is fundamental. Like curly hair that must be ruthlessly flat-ironed into submission—both our heroines have such hair, though Irma’s is “frizzy,” which means curly but unhappy—these people push back against any form in which they find themselves, even if they chose it.

Readers of Harbach’s first novel, “The Art of Fielding” (2011), will recognize some of these names: Pella, Owen, Westish College. That’s because “The Brightness” is its metafictional sequel, with the premise that the previous book was written by Owen as a roman à clef. This conceit allows Harbach to trace the development of not just his characters but his own work, through the reception of Owen’s book and through the way Westish students discuss his other authorial proxy, Edith Wharton. Irma, for instance, adores Wharton but nonetheless engages in a little trash talk: “Wharton’s world is too narrow. . . . I mean, Wharton is flawless at describing the world she sees—but the world she describes is restricted to what she can see, and see readily. She never gropes around in the darkness—she never reaches toward the numinous.”

Throughout “The Brightness,” characters dismiss ambitious realist novels, whether historical or contemporary in concern, as “not exactly the news about how we live” or as bland, “mashed-potato realism.” The middlebrow realist is a master technician, a superb replicator of experience, but neither more nor less than this. These statements—coming as they do in the pages of a large, enjoyable novel that could be called quasi-historical and even accused of having a “whole boring ‘plot’ ”—begin to suggest a preoccupation with whether such novels are worth anyone’s time. The existence of the book says yes; the characters within it say maybe.

Irma has been avoiding her friend for a simple reason: she’s been busy planning her prank. At the parties that ring in the school year, two of her accomplices apply temporary tattoos that say “RAPE VICTIM” onto passed-out athletes’ foreheads. Then Irma publishes a manifesto in the campus newspaper under the moniker Witches of Westish. Once a video of one of the athletes freaking out goes up online (“I’M A DUDE! I WAS NOT SEXUALLY ASSAULTED!” he screams), what starts as a feminist protest becomes a national story, with one of the victims appearing on a Fox News show hosted by a floppy-haired man named Hunter Stroman.

Perhaps because it avoids a situation in which someone is actually raped, “The Brightness” portrays this particular kind of youthful political action with a touching mixture of the high-mindedness, impishness, bravado, self-aggrandizement, and pointlessness that such events really contain. The Witches of Westish want attention, which they get but are unprepared to handle; the athlete who goes on Fox News wants his pride back but instead swings a national spotlight onto Dahlia, a pranker who is suspended; the college administration just wants this whole problem somehow not to be a problem, which is impossible. Celebrities get involved—Lana Del Rey wears a sweatshirt that says “FREE DAHLIA”—as do feminist media startups (such as the titular Brightness), but, as far as campus-rape prevention goes, nothing is accomplished. Some people make money, some people use the buzz to shore up a brand, and some people get their lives wrecked, but who falls into this last category comes down to bad luck more than to justice.

Irma views both herself and her friends as bystanders to the main issue, campus rape; they are raising awareness but otherwise not involved. As events get out of control, though, Irma realizes that her friend Dahlia wants them to. Whenever offered a chance for rapprochement, Dahlia escalates. Her chosen victim, earnestly seeking to understand why she did what she did, tries to have a conversation with her, but she refuses so that he will take his complaints to the administration. Irma’s slow recognition that Dahlia has something different at stake here, as well as the inevitable reveal of what that “something different” happens to be, is delayed because Irma, for all her intelligence, can’t put together why an upbeat, confident classmate might suddenly become moody and self-destructive. Irma lives in a world in which people are fundamentally kind and good. There are things she cannot see and does not understand. Instead, what she sees when she looks at Dahlia is somebody who is fearless to the point of neglecting herself, and part of their friendship lies in how comforting this lack of comprehension is for Dahlia.

The fictional models Harbach cites, both within the text (such as Wharton’s story “The Pelican”) and in interviews (such as “Anna Karenina”), are attentive to social judgments and situations. They are about just and unjust ostracism, the delicacy of one’s reputation, who gets away with things and who doesn’t. They understand that human relationships and human interiority both exist within a world, and this world constrains not only what people do but what they can even regard as possible. Harbach is a gifted observer of undergraduates, their pathos and their comedy, but his Westish is a gentle and perhaps too-good-natured institution, where even the jocks are nerds. This means that the larger social dynamics created by rape—even the prospect of rape—in small, intimate communities go unconsidered. There is no one genuinely malicious at Westish, and thus none of the moral compromises one finds at an actual college: the friends who take sides, which usually means picking the person who’s less trouble; the real but unspoken feeling that sexual assault may be an inevitable part of freewheeling social life; the surreal experience of being in a class about “Tess of the d’Urbervilles” with someone you know to have hurt other people. The singular strangeness of studying at a small college is striving not to know what you know about somebody else, for the sake of higher learning, or at least for the peace of the classroom. These kinds of forces, which explain why students behave in the self-warping ways that they do, are missing, as is the sense that people are ever constrained by something beyond their own quirks of personality or individual delusions.

Meanwhile, back at Owen’s wedding, old friends and enemies reunite, dance, drink too much, and go to bed with the wrong people. Here, again, Harbach demonstrates a profound gift for observing individuals and their relationships. His depiction of the wedding is replete with the sort of tiny triumphs and failures of these occasions: comparing yourself with an old classmate and feeling like a loser, comparing yourself with a younger person and feeling like you wasted your potential, ending up in a group at the end of the night you’d never have chosen and will never see again. Pella, for her part, spends the night obsessed with proving herself to be a flourishing adult. She helped Owen write his book, and helped him plan this wedding, but knowing that she “had once again played the supportive editorial role” means that she is filled with a sense of resentment. She fawns on an old high-school friend, then steals the attention of a guy the friend likes. She is embarrassed by her fiancé’s physical unself-consciousness—he’s at peace with his balding and weight gain, his sunscreen is applied all wrong—as if this somehow reflects poorly on her. Can she really be impressive if she’s with a man who just wants “a grilled cheese of a woman, salty, filling, and cheap”?

Collapsing relationships are the bread and butter of many an insightful novel. But Harbach also captures a more delicate thing in his depiction of Owen and Pella: the frustration that exists between a successful artist and a less successful friend who feels instrumental to that success. Pella envies Owen and secretly enjoys things like a negative review that compares his book to morphine, even as she lives vicariously through the praise and attention he receives. She knows that Owen hasn’t done her dirty, professionally speaking, but still feels neglected.

Once again, though, there’s a social aspect to the proceedings that Harbach avoids, this time not by leaving things out but by insufficiently considering what he’s put in. As mentioned, “The Art of Fielding” is a real novel in “The Brightness.” Various lines make clear that it should be regarded as nearly identical to Harbach’s book. That means it’s a book about two things: first, Henry Skrimshander, a talented Westish shortstop who gets the yips, and, second, Owen’s love affair with the president of the college (who was also Pella’s father). Owen seems to have made no changes to events as they happened. Nevertheless, his “Art of Fielding,” like Harbach’s, is treated by critics as a “roseate” work of old-fashioned realism.

The problem is that Owen’s book would not be received as comforting and old-fashioned. It would be seen as a work of merciless autofiction in the vein of Karl Ove Knausgaard, and it would be discussed, as his books are, as aggressive, antisocial, and spiteful. Despite almost everyone at the wedding having read Owen’s novel, he faces no social fallout: there is no frustration at his depiction of the school and his peers, and no paranoia about whether he’ll expose them further. More to the point, such a book would simply not be described by critics as full of “comforts,” and “The Brightness” is too concerned elsewhere with the way discourses play out online to ignore the debates a work of barely disguised fiction about sleeping with your closeted college president would create. The only person who responds to it in a way that seems even remotely accurate is, oddly, Hunter Stroman, who calls it a novel about being “groomed for gay sex by the sixty-year-old president of [Owen’s] college.”

“The Brightness” suffers from its own problems with thin disguises. The Times is the Times in this book, Vice is Vice, the TV show “Girls” is the TV show “Girls,” and Lana Del Rey is Lana Del Rey. On the other hand, there are women’s websites named the Termagant and the Napkin, a designer label named Jane and Janet, a publication named the SoHo Review, and, of course, a Fox News host named Hunter Stroman. Every time a new, media-adjacent name appears, it’s unclear whether what you’re encountering is a fictional invention or an inside joke. In a conversation before Owen’s wedding, a character dismisses a forthcoming novel as a “period piece” by quipping, “The characters are always humming old advertising jingles. And the price of everything is repeated ad nauseam: ‘Oh goodness! Three shillings six for a jiggery-joo!’ ” But if such an effect was what Harbach sought to avoid by not saying “Jezebel,” “The Hairpin,” or “Tucker Carlson,” he sacrificed a much more important sense of social reality. The titular Brightness, for instance—a women’s media startup that was founded by an actress, and that publishes a mixture of glossy fashion content, aggregated slop, and barely edited confessional pieces—represents a kind of corporate girl power that the reader, sitting at the lofty vantage point of ten years later, knows isn’t heading anywhere except off a cliff. For the characters, though, it’s just a website, and, as glamorous as the media world it represents seems, neither Pella nor Irma wants to join it. It is neither an aspiration nor a temptation; it is not a friend or an enemy; it is just indistinctly there.

These flaws are all the more frustrating because so much of “The Brightness” is beautiful and perceptive. Irma, in particular—who has abandoned her rural Catholic roots but still appears to others as a confusing aesthetic mix of Fox News and flower child, “starch and eccentricity . . . shrewdness and innocence”—is a wholly convincing portrait of someone who is fiercely loyal to her family and their values (despite being at odds with them) but who also feels alternately freed and imprisoned by her own singularity. She is depicted without a trace of condescension, and her struggles with her identity can’t simply be chalked up to a restrictive childhood. They are existential problems that are represented seriously:

You couldn’t be a sexual being and also be Irma; you couldn’t be a woman and also be Irma; you couldn’t slash your hair in the darkness and also be Irma. You couldn’t be an intellectual and also a sexual being, or a woman, or a slasher of hair in the darkness. You could be an intellectual and yourself, but not without an irksome sense of narrowness, penury, and incompletion. You couldn’t enjoy, relax, have fun, take pleasure, cut loose, party, whatever you wanted to call it, embrace certain deep and Dionysian aspects of your being, without suspending your moral sense, and all too often it seemed your moral sense was all you had.

Passages like this one are not only a joy to read—they convey something fundamental about people, who are always in conflict with themselves, managing a jumble of desires that pull in wildly different and sometimes mutually exclusive directions. Irma is not going to resolve her dilemma by having sex or cutting loose, nor by renouncing such pastimes and doubling down on intellect. Her need to be multiple represents the tension that will run through the rest of her life, not something that can be reconciled to create a new, final, and perfect Irma. When she does have sex, she experiences the aftermath, too, as doubled: “a kind of grief-in-love, a losing-in-the-having. . . . A new kind of unloneliness, which now she could never unknow.”

The accusation levelled within “The Brightness” against novels that are stuck in the past, whether formally or through their subject matter, is that they are “not exactly bringing the news.” The proper response to this complaint is that novels do not bring the news; the news brings the news. This is as true of art that is experimental and political as of art that is old-fashioned. While in New York, Pella becomes smitten with the work of a feminist artist named Sylvie, especially a film called “Death of My Husband.” In it, Sylvie talks directly to a man standing behind her camera, who asks, “Would you consider killing one’s husband a revolutionary act?” Sylvie replies, “Revolutions just go round and round. I’ve been trying to become an artist.” At the end of the film, pregnant by a man she despises, Sylvie says simply, “There’s no way out.”

Art like Sylvie’s inherently contains two messages. Even if there’s “no way out,” the film’s existence says, “You’re not alone.” An artist is always becoming an artist, the same way Pella and Irma will always be becoming Pella and Irma. All works of art are addressed in part to people they cannot know, with problems they cannot understand. To prove that an old-fashioned book can be excellent, it isn’t necessary to dramatize arguments about the form. You need only a prose style, an interest in people, and a sense of society. In art as in sports, the best defense is a good offense. ♦

On a cold February morning at West Virginia’s Capitol, children in winter jackets, holding signs and their homework, clogged the hallways outside the House Finance Committee room. Delegate Vernon Criss, the committee’s chairperson, popped outside his office, then promptly walked back in.

Five days earlier, the Republican lawmaker had proposed to limit payouts from a voucher program that diverts public tax dollars to private education. Since then, families had shown up each morning, and with them lobbyists and organizers posting inflammatory assertions that the proposal would kill the program.

Criss didn’t want that. Five years earlier, he voted to create the Hope Scholarship program, the nation’s first educational savings account program to have no income requirements. Since then, 11 other states have passed similar legislation.

In the first year of the Hope program in West Virginia, it cost the state $9 million. Now, as 2026 was beginning, it was set to expand, with estimates that it could cost $200 million or more a year. And Criss was concerned that unchecked growth of the program, coupled with a spree of tax cuts, could put the state budget into a tailspin. The state and its lawmakers needed “to face the music,” as Criss would later say.

On that February day in Charleston, a staffer unlocked the committee door. Parents and media filed in, taking seats along the narrow benches lining the committee room’s walls. Kids lay on the floor. A delegate asked if the Hope Scholarship bill would be discussed. When Criss said no, the parents and their kids stood up and filed out.

Within the next few days, lawmakers would stop the effort to curtail spending on vouchers. And Criss would soon be fighting for his political life.

The money and organizing behind the demise of the bill, as well as the targeting of Criss, can be traced to “school choice” advocates at the local and national levels and their ability to spend huge sums to have their way.

When Republicans assumed control of the West Virginia Legislature in 2015, school choice — their preferred terminology for voucher-style programs — became a rallying point for an array of political nonprofits. Through lobbying and supporting sympathetic lawmakers, they helped establish the Hope Scholarship six years later.

The first year, a little more than 2,300 students enrolled in the program, which was designed to permit more students over time. Today, 26,000 students are taking part, more than the enrollment in the state’s largest public school district.

West Virginia has been part of a nationwide push, partially funded by billionaire-backed political groups, to use taxpayer dollars to fund private schools.

Among the proponents of these programs are the influential Koch family network and Pennsylvania billionaire Jeffrey Yass, who has spent more than $350 million since 2015 on political campaigns, including a push for public funding for private education.

In West Virginia, Arkansas and Texas, proponents have been eager to challenge anyone who got in the way, including fellow Republicans who voiced concerns. Basic guardrails or efforts to cap spending have been met with intense resistance.

During the 2026 West Virginia primary, three political action committees spent heavily on independent advertisements, targeting 10 incumbent Republicans. They succeeded in ousting four, all of whom had disagreements with the governor; most expressed reservations about the Hope Scholarship.

Their biggest victory was unseating Criss, the long-serving incumbent and high-ranking Republican.

One of the three PACs working to defeat Criss is affiliated with the Koch family. Yass has contributed to the two others: School Freedom Fund and the Sugar Maple PAC. David McIntosh, president of School Freedom Fund and the affiliated Club for Growth, was jubilant afterward.

“These elections are a warning to Republican leaders like Vernon Criss — if you oppose school freedom, expect to be held accountable,” he wrote in a press announcement at the time.

Criss had been unopposed in his 2024 primary. In 2026, the three PACs spent more than $145,000 to help a 23-year-old defeat him.

Criss’ proposal to put financial limits on the Hope Scholarship program came as West Virginia public schools arrived at a dangerous crossroads. The program directs dollars away from public schools, adding to funding shortfalls created by enrollment drops.

Since 2019, 70 public schools have closed throughout the state, with the system losing more than 30,000 students. By the end of this school year, up to 20 more schools could close.

During the same legislative session this year, when lawmakers passed emergency legislation to help one school district stay open, there was little drama — no crowds in the hallways or the galleries.

In 2021, as West Virginia lawmakers worked on the bill that would create the Hope Scholarship, they got some help from the outside: a lobbyist and political operative named Jason Huffman.

Huffman has been the director of Americans for Prosperity-West Virginia since the mid-2010s. The group is part of the larger Americans for Prosperity, which was set up by the billionaire industrialist David Koch and supported by his brother Charles, longtime donors to conservative causes. Americans for Prosperity advocates for low taxes, school choice and loosened environmental regulations throughout the country.

In shaping the West Virginia legislation, Huffman said, one goal was to create a school voucher program that other states could model. While other states had already passed voucher-style programs, they hadn’t made them open to all residents regardless of income.

Among those advocating in West Virginia were members of Cardinal Institute, a homegrown conservative think tank. Founded in 2015, the group made public funding for private schooling one of its first policy planks. Over the last decade, Cardinal has received significant donations from organizations that also have received funding from Koch entities and Yass.

To support the Hope legislation, Cardinal’s leaders distributed research, published columns in newspapers and linked up with other groups, like Americans for Prosperity and the Institute for Justice, which litigates libertarian causes, to push the issue. They also found allies in parents who wanted the financial flexibility to send their children to private schools or teach them at home or in small clusters of like-minded families.

Katie Switzer, a mother of six, became one of Cardinal’s most reliable advocates. She had started looking into schooling options after her second child was diagnosed with a learning disability.

“I just felt like this doesn’t feel good to send my nonverbal child to a school that you know isn’t really collaborative, and I don’t know what’s going on when she’s there because she can’t tell me when she gets home,” she said. The experience made her a proponent of alternatives to public school.

Her story appeared in some of Cardinal’s promotional copy for the bill. She sat in on a video call presentation featuring state Sen. Patrcia Rucker, a Hope Scholarship proponent. She took to Facebook to tout the program.

And she stepped up when the Hope Scholarship was challenged in court by other parents who argued the program would undermine funding for public schools.

Hope’s opponents in the case gained support from the state Board of Education, which argued the program siphoned money away from public schools. On the other side was the West Virginia attorney general, Patrick Morrisey, defending the law and the state.

The Institute for Justice was looking to get involved, but it needed to find somebody with standing in the case. Switzer and another parent signed up, giving the firm the ability to file motions and argue in court. The Institute for Justice has received $8.5 million from charities controlled by Yass over nearly a decade.

The case went to the West Virginia Supreme Court, where the justices ruled 4-1 in favor of the program.

The ruling meant the Hope program had survived. It was good timing for Morrisey. He was about to make a run for governor. When Morrisey waded into a tough four-way primary battle, the Club for Growth Action, a super PAC Yass has donated heavily to over the years, was ready to help.

“Our goal in all of this is to elect people who will be champions for school freedom, lower taxes, less regulation, so businesses can thrive,” McIntosh, the group’s president, said in an interview.

The Club for Growth Action pumped $10.8 million into that race. Morrisey won the plurality in the Republican primary and went on to trounce his Democratic opponent in the general election.

On a cold morning in January 2025, Morrisey stood bundled up in a black wool coat on the stairs of Capitol, its gold dome towering above him. He took the oath of office and gave his address.

“We will expand the Hope Scholarship, putting more control in the hands of parents to choose the best educational path for their children,” he said.

Hours before Morrisey gave his State of the State address earlier this year, the House Education Committee called a meeting to hear about a report from the Rand Corporation, a nonpartisan think tank.

Rand called on the state to fix what was becoming abundantly clear: Education in West Virginia wasn’t just in transition; it was in crisis.

Initially the Hope Scholarship was limited to students attending public school or children who would enter kindergarten. Participation grew over time. And as the 2026-27 school year began, the program opened to any student in West Virginia, including those who had spent their entire academic careers in private school or in homeschooling.

That translated into tens of millions of additional state dollars to private schools and homeschooling. Estimates for the 2026-27 school year ranged from $230 million to more than $300 million in the months leading up to the legislative session. The program costs for the year are still being tallied but are currently projected at an unprecedented $150 million.

The Rand report suggested placing an income cap on the Hope Scholarship and increasing funding for public schools.

While the Education Committee would work on public school funding, Criss’ Finance Committee would tackle the ballooning costs of the Hope Scholarship. He was not interested in Rand’s solutions but found his own. By capping the scholarship amount and limiting certain expenses, Criss was hoping he could help prolong the program for families well into the future. But families would soon be rallying against him.

In the time since the Hope Scholarship had withstood the court challenge, Switzer’s reach had only grown. On Facebook, she administered a group that offers practical advice to hundreds of parents, or “Hope Schoolers” as she called them, on questions like what items counted as qualifying expenses and how to properly file for the program.

Before Criss’ plan rolled out, Switzer said she received word from a member of the Finance Committee — though she did not disclose who — that the Hope Scholarship would be discussed.

Writing a lengthy Facebook post, she warned that the move would severely damage the program. “The bill takes away 19,000 children’s access to online education, tutoring, private schools across state borders, and many other beneficial academic programs,” she wrote.

Huffman, the Americans for Prosperity state director, took to radio and social media to decry what he described as an existential threat to the program. Parents with kids in tow echoed those fears.

Criss’ proposal would’ve capped the award amount for the Hope Scholarship at a little more than $5,000 a year. He said his intent was to prolong the program for the long run, not kill it.

Huffman countered that by capping the award amount, inflation would eventually erode families’ abilities to use the program for private education or homeschooling.

Looking back, Criss said he anticipated some pushback, because the Hope Scholarship is a “giveaway program.” He blamed political forces for riling people up.

At a caucus meeting with the Republican supermajority — which holds 91 of the 100 seats in the lower chamber — Criss said he couldn’t muster the votes. A bill to fix public schools that came out of the Education Committee died entirely.

Election season was right around the corner, and Americans for Prosperity-West Virginia and other groups hadn’t forgotten the Hope Scholarship battle. Criss had failed what Huffman called “a litmus test” in trying to restrict public money for parents looking for an alternative to public school.

Meanwhile, McIntosh, the president for Club for Growth, said he received word from his allies in the Morrisey administration. Morrisey was concerned about lawmakers who had opposed him on budget issues and school choice. The governor was looking to replace those he saw as not aligned.

While the legislative backlash had been about one issue, the campaign against Morrisey’s foes went well beyond education issues.

Ads depicted Criss as a liberal who wanted to open borders for illegal immigration. That was based on a no vote Criss had cast against a piece of legislation that would’ve mandated employers run a citizenship check on every employee.

Fliers mailed by the School Freedom Fund, a PAC affiliated with Club for Growth, depicted candidates as supporters of drag queens. Americans for Prosperity-West Virginia sent volunteers door to door to rally for their candidates, including in Criss’ district.

The newly created Sugar Maple PAC — headed by a former state delegate and Morrisey ally — ran ads accusing opponents of being Republicans in Name Only.

One mailer, depicting Criss driving a garbage truck, accused him of taking “West Virginia’s values to the dump.” On the back, it reiterated his vote against the citizenship check for employees and his vote against West Virginia’s abortion ban.

“Vernon Criss’ liberal trash,” the flier read, with an image of black trash bags.

“I think that the fact that they called me a liberal, it just stuck out,” Criss said. “You know, as the chairman of finance, I know that it’s a targeted job, obviously, that’s part of the deal. Those types of things happen, that’s just part of the politics involved here.”

The School Freedom Fund poured nearly $1 million into statehouse races across West Virginia to both retain Morrissey’s supporters and oust detractors, like Criss. Americans for Prosperity did the same, again targeting Criss, and spending around $700,000 across the state.

A spokesperson for Morrisey said in an email that the governor is a supporter of both public schools and the Hope Scholarship and “is also grateful that organizations that share his worldview of helping West Virginia children having the best educational opportunities in the country weigh in.”

Sugar Maple PAC and Yass did not respond to requests for comment. Yass contributed $450,000 to Sugar Maple in the lead-up to the 2026 primary. Among the three PACs, Sugar Maple spent the most on the Criss race: $86,000 to attack Criss and $15,000 to back his opponent, Charles Hartzog, a 23-year-old gas station cashier.

Criss didn’t just lose the primary; Hartzog beat him with 61% of the vote. Criss is now running a long-shot campaign as a write-in for his district in the general election.

Musk pledges 1 SpaceX share for every child in America

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Elon Musk said Monday he'd give one of his shares in SpaceX to every "kid" in America next year.

Why it matters: It'd be a roughly $11 billion giveaway at current stock prices, nearly double Michael Dell's record-breaking Trump accounts gift.

What they're saying: "Next year, I will give one of my SpaceX shares to every kid in America. This will be the first of many," Musk said in a post to X just before 4 a.m. ET Monday.

  • He did not specify if "kid" meant all minors under 18 or some other status, nor did he elaborate on what "first of many" meant.
  • He also did not reply to X accounts asking if he meant all kids present in the U.S., or just citizens.

By the numbers: Per the Census Bureau, there are just over 72 million people under 18 in the U.S.

  • If each got one SpaceX share, at Friday's closing price of $146.03, that'd be a market value of a little over $10.5 billion.
  • Musk owns billions of SpaceX shares, so the giveaway would make only a small dent in his holdings.

Zoom in: Musk's pledge isn't the first from SpaceX.

  • In July the company's COO Gwynne Shotwell said she'd gift a share of the stock to each of 2 million Trump accounts, a gift now worth nearly $300 million.

Zoom out: Musk's announcement comes one day after Anthropic said it was setting up a new public benefit fund with 5% of the company's equity, an entity potentially worth $100 billion or more.

  • Musk responded to Anthropic's news by saying "I recommend giving the shares directly to the public," later adding "This is what I will be doing."

How AI giants will reshape global philanthropy

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI boom is about to rewrite the rules of global philanthropy, shifting its center of gravity to Silicon Valley, where the IPOs of AI giants Anthropic and OpenAI will create the world's largest charitable organizations.

Why it matters: Rather than massive donations earmarked for renaming hospital wings or university graduate programs, expect the new wave of giving to mirror startup culture: flexible capital, ambitious bets and a search for measurable impact.

Driving the news: Anthropic announced Sunday that it plans to launch a massive benefit fund with 5% of its shares.

Zoom in: The company's pledge — which would amount to $100 billion if it reaches a value of $2 trillion in its IPO — will go to a public benefit fund and is set to grow as its valuation rises.

  • Anthropic says the fund will be a wholly owned LLC, giving it flexibility to make large donations to nonprofits or distribute money directly to specific populations.
  • The commitment builds on the pledges of co-founders to give away 80% of their equity. A program available to early Anthropic hires and used by a large number of company staffers matched donations at a 3:1 ratio. The company says roughly half its employees participate in donation matching.
  • The company says the fund's shares will not be used for lobbying or political giving in the U.S. or abroad.

Mamdani Can No Longer Avoid the Issue of the NYPD

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The mayor has been able to put difficult questions about policing on the back burner—until now.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch arrive to attend the New York City Police Department Memorial Wall on October 13, 2026.

Zohran Mamdani’s first year in Gracie Mansion has—unusually for a New York City mayor—mostly been an unquestionable PR success. Thanks to tangible quality-of-life improvements, some early wins on his agenda, and Mamdani’s world-class communications skills, his constituents actually seem to like him.

But no elected official can avoid rough patches forever. The bill always comes due. And, in New York City, that bill often comes attached to perhaps the single most powerful, divisive entity in the five boroughs: the New York City Police Department.

Mamdani, both during his mayoral campaign and now in office, has tried, and mostly succeeded, in reaching a broad truce with the NYPD without alienating a leftist base restless to rein it in. Movement advocates have so far given him grace on his reluctance to deliver on their demands for police reform, which largely predate Mamdani’s time in office. But in recent weeks, events have pushed that uneasy peace to a breaking point, brought the NYPD to the forefront of the political conversation, and underscored the challenges and contradictions inherent in moving from socialist outsider to the executive of the largest city in America.

First, there was the backlash following a protest outside the United Nations against Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu—a man Mamdani thinks should be sent to The Hague. The NYPD arrested dozens of protesters, including some elected officials who, like Mamdani, are members of the Democratic Socialists of America. Some of the police officers were members of the Strategic Response Group, an NYPD unit ostensibly founded as a counterterrorism force but that mostly functions to arrest and brutalize protesters. Mamdani has vowed to abolish the SRG, but has yet to follow through on his promise.

That controversy was followed by an even bigger one over the horrific ICE shooting of 28-year-old Oscar Belgal in the Marble Hill neighborhood last week. The presence of ICE in the city, along with scenes of NYPD officers roughing up protesters who had mobilized in the wake of the shooting—including DSA-affiliated congressional candidate Darializa Avila Chevalier—pushed the dissatisfaction of the left to new heights.

The national leadership of DSA called on Mamdani to abolish both the SRG and the Community Response Team, an anti-crime unit formed during Eric Adams’s tenure that has a pattern of brutalizing New Yorkers. And, most contentiously, DSA demanded that Mamdani fire NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch, the conservative billionaire heiress who was appointed by Adams and whose approach to policing would be familiar to anyone who lived under Rudy Giuliani’s “broken windows” regime. (The New York City chapter of DSA was more circumspect, saying that Mamdani should fire Tisch if she did not “end NYPD complicity with ICE and ensure that the NYPD is not used as a weapon against New Yorkers exercising their right to free speech, or as a shield for ICE’s unchecked violence.”)

Firing Tisch is not a new demand, nor one restricted to the socialist left; nonprofits like the NYCLU were strongly critical of her tenure long before Mamdani took office. This week, city councillors Julie Won and Carmen De La Rosa—neither of whom identify as socialists or are affiliated with DSA—called on the mayor to replace Tisch. But the controversy around Tisch’s presence in the role had mostly lain dormant until the past few weeks.

When asked by a reporter at a press conference this week if he would be open to replacing the police commissioner, Mamdani reiterated that he would be keeping Tisch where she was, and went on to praise her record of “uprooting corruption” and “lowering crime across our city.”

So where does all this leave Mamdani, the city’s left, and, most crucially, the prospects for reform of the NYPD?

Mamdani largely shied away from debates on crime and public safety on the campaign trail and was clear that he would not run on defunding the NYPD, but he ran on a public-safety platform that was at the very least interested in curbing the agency’s most egregious excesses and creating more humane civilian alternatives to police in addressing issues like homelessness. He promised to abolish the SRG and get rid of the NYPD’s controversial gang database, which unconstitutionally surveilled tens of thousands of Black and Latino New Yorkers.

Thus far, he has mostly not delivered on those promises, and even walked some of them back; just a few days after his swearing-in, Mamdani stood with Commissioner Tisch at a January 6 press conference as she praised the gang database for helping deliver the safest year in New York’s recorded history. According to testimony from Public Advocate Jumaane Williams in front of the New York City Council Committee on Public Safety, individuals could end up in the database for actions as mundane as “wishing a gang member a happy birthday on social media, being outside late and wearing a certain color, and living in the same public housing complex as accused gang members.” At a press conference in April, Mamdani signaled his intent to reform rather than abolish the database, noting that the NYPD “has also implemented a number of reforms as per the recommendation that came through.”

And he has set up a clear dividing line between his administration and some of his base by rejecting the calls to oust Tisch. That is not necessarily because he fears backlash from within the NYPD. The police unions are hostile to anything they perceive as reform measures, but keeping Tisch should be seen more as a bid to manage elite perception than an effort to keep the NYPD in line. While Tisch has spent much of her public-sector career at the NYPD, she is not exactly a creature of the department. Unusually for a police commissioner, she has never actually served as a police officer, holding a variety of civilian positions in the department, and has since held other executive positions in city government across multiple administrations. Less than two years ago, she was Adams’s sanitation commissioner, and said publicly many times that the position was her “dream job.”

Tisch’s value proposition to Mamdani is that she is well-connected. A daughter of one of the wealthiest families in New York City and a personal friend of Ivanka Trump, she is well-regarded among New York’s elite. Governor Kathy Hochul advocated for keeping Tisch on as police commissioner behind the scenes in 2025, and has gone on the record praising Mamdani for “bringing in real pros.” Both Hochul and House minority leader Hakeem Jeffries cited his keeping Tisch on as a factor in their decision to endorse Mamdani in the general election last year.

So far, Mamdani has shown a willingness to engage with some of the movement’s demands, and this week he indicated future action on the SRG. Whether that means he will abolish the unit or simply reform it remains to be seen. But there is no indication that, even if the SRG were to disappear, other NYPD units would not simply adopt its anti-protest functions—as well as its aggressive and sometimes unconstitutional methods. Abolishing the SRG would also set up a direct clash with Tisch, who supports maintaining the unit.

Tisch’s remaining in her position embodies some of the inherent dissonance of a democratic socialist’s being the chief executive of New York City, overseeing a police force of over 33,000 officers. The mayor comes from activist spaces, but is now at the heart of the political establishment. His campaign appealed to New Yorkers interested in a vision for city government that put them, not the ultra-wealthy, first, but a politician in a city as large and complicated as New York has to navigate the often competing demands of lots of different groups, not just that of his base. He is a supporter of Palestinian liberation whose police commissioner, a self-described “proud Zionist,” apologized to a synagogue hosting an illegal land sale that was the target of pro-Palestine protesters. He has made concrete promises around policing, but is also eager not to become a target of the NYPD’s ire, as confrontation with the police unions or the threat of a police strike could derail his administration.

These contradictions inevitably force someone in Mamdani’s situation to make divisive choices, like the choice he has now made to keep Tisch. There are many who see that as a politically sensible way for the mayor to maintain momentum on the other key parts of his agenda for the benefit of the working class without getting drawn into the NYPD quicksand. But there are others who will see this as a sign that New York’s wealthiest are still calling the shots on public safety—and that fundamental police reform remains as out of reach as ever. It’s too early to see how all of this will play out. But one thing seems certain: The days of Zohran Mamdani’s being able to avoid the NYPD-shaped elephant in the room are over.

Ken Paxton’s Anti-Abortion Influence Already Extended Beyond Texas. He Could Take It Further in the Senate.

Early in his tenure as Texas attorney general, Ken Paxton issued a statement commemorating what he believed was a national tragedy.

“It’s impossible to fill the void of more than 50 million missing human beings,” he said in 2016. He was marking the 43rd anniversary of Roe v. Wade, the Supreme Court ruling that protected abortion rights — and had long stood in the way of efforts to ban terminations in early pregnancy, when most abortions occur.

Paxton spent the years that followed testing Roe’s limits and ultimately prevailed where others had failed: In 2021, he defended a near-total abortion ban that Texas legislators had designed to evade conventional legal challenges. The U.S. Supreme Court allowed it to take effect while Roe remained in force.

After the justices overturned Roe the following year, Paxton kept pushing, pursuing new ways to extend the reach of Texas’ restrictions. He prevailed against the Biden administration, picked fights with states that protect abortion access and pioneered strategies that his peers later adopted or expanded.

While Paxton’s tenure as attorney general has drawn criticism from across the political spectrum for his personal scandals and alleged misuse of the office, detractors and allies alike agree that his signal achievement as the state’s top legal official has been advancing restrictions on abortion.

A close look at his record shows a clear pattern in his successes: He relentlessly tests the limits of the law and employs novel legal strategies to advance his goals.

Paxton’s national role as an anti-abortion warrior would change if he wins his U.S. Senate race. As Texas attorney general, he has been able to act largely on his own authority. If he prevails in November, he would become one of 100 senators in a chamber where most legislation needs 60 votes to overcome a filibuster.

But allies who have witnessed his victories see an opening.

“He’ll have the ability to use some of the laws that he’s defended in Texas as influence to draft legislation in the U.S. Senate,” said Mary Elizabeth Castle, director of government relations at Texas Values, a Christian advocacy group that supports a national abortion ban.

Legal experts and advocates on both sides expect he could join colleagues who are pressuring the Food and Drug Administration to revoke its approval of the abortion drug mifepristone.

Anti-abortion activists at the state and federal level are also trying to secure legal rights for fetuses, which could have implications for reproductive care like birth control and in vitro fertilization. Paxton, who has called himself a strong supporter of IVF, recently said he was open to restrictions intended to prevent the loss of fertilized eggs.

His litigation has also raised questions that experts expect the Supreme Court to confront, including whether a federal law, which mandates hospitals provide stabilizing care to patients having an emergency, overrides state abortion bans; whether to treat a fetus as a separate patient that has its own emergency care rights; and how far states can go in pursuing out-of-state doctors who prescribe abortion medication to patients in states where it’s banned.

During the current race, abortion has played a far smaller role than questions about Paxton’s ethics. His Democratic opponent, James Talarico, has said that he supports restoring a federal right to abortion and that his Christian faith calls for an end to “extreme” laws that Paxton has defended and that have forced women to flee the state for emergency abortion care.

Lately, Paxton has said little about abortion — an issue on which he has defended restrictions more stringent than many Texans say they want.

But those who have watched Paxton extend the reach of Texas law beyond its borders are paying close attention to his Senate bid.

Dr. Jennifer Villavicencio, an abortion provider, said she has felt compelled to move her family twice to states with strong laws shielding doctors from out-of-state litigation, partly out of concern that Paxton is creating a blueprint for targeting providers like her.

“It’s very disheartening and scary to think that it’s maybe coming to the U.S. Senate,” she said.

When Paxton became Texas attorney general in 2015, there was no ambiguity about where he stood on abortion.

Raised by an anti-abortion activist, he married his sweetheart from a Christian college, who spoke proudly about the fact that she was adopted, saying her birth mother “chose life.” She campaigned for Paxton, but he had his own bona fides. A law he’d co-sponsored as a state senator banned almost all abortions after 20 weeks.

Similar 20-week bans had taken root across the country as decades of court rulings chipped away at Roe, but no state had managed to enforce a ban as early as six weeks. More than a dozen tried, passing laws that prohibited abortion when cardiac activity could be detected. One after another, they were blocked by judges.

Then, in 2021, a Texas lawyer named Jonathan Mitchell worked with legislators to craft a law that would withstand similar challenges. Instead of having state officials enforce their “heartbeat law,” the new act, referred to as Senate Bill 8, allowed citizens to sue physicians they suspected were providing abortions. The law offered up to $10,000 if they won.

When abortion providers and President Joe Biden’s administration separately challenged the law in federal court, Paxton’s office argued that state officials couldn’t be ordered to stop enforcing it because they had no power to do so in the first place. Only private citizens could.

“Because the executive officials are expressly prohibited from enforcing SB 8,” his office wrote to the Supreme Court, “there is nothing for a federal court to enjoin.”

The Supreme Court agreed that Paxton couldn’t be sued because “the petitioners have not identified any enforcement authority the attorney general possesses in connection with S. B. 8 that a federal court might enjoin him from exercising.”

The law remained in effect, becoming the first enforceable six-week ban under Roe.

Legislators in Idaho and Oklahoma subsequently passed similar laws.

For Dr. Sam Dickman, who worked at a San Antonio Planned Parenthood, the implications were clear: “I was going to need to either entirely change the type of medicine I was providing or leave the state,” he said. He opted for the latter, moving to Montana, where abortion is legal until viability, or roughly 24 weeks.

Days after the Texas ban took effect, Josseli Barnica arrived at a Houston hospital in the midst of a miscarriage. She was 17 weeks pregnant and her fetus’s head was pressed up against her dilated cervix. Because the situation put her at high risk of developing an infection, medical experts said doctors should have offered to speed up delivery or empty her uterus; the fetus was not viable, and losing the pregnancy was inevitable.

Barnica relayed to her husband what she said her medical providers had told her: “They had to wait until there was no heartbeat,” he told ProPublica in Spanish. “It would be a crime to give her an abortion.”

For 40 hours, Barnica waited, her uterus exposed to bacteria.

She died days later of sepsis. Medical experts told ProPublica her doctors did not follow the proper standard of care and that Barnica’s death was preventable.

In a statement, HCA Healthcare, the hospital chain that treated her, said “our responsibility is to be in compliance with applicable state and federal laws and regulations” and said that physicians exercise their independent judgment.

When the Supreme Court overturned the constitutional right to abortion on June 24, 2022, Paxton dismissed his staff at noon to celebrate. He would later designate the date as a paid office holiday, Sanctity of Life Day.

The Texas Legislature had prepared for the occasion, passing a law that went into effect shortly after the overturning of Roe. The ban threatened doctors with up to 99 years in prison if they performed an abortion. But soon came what Paxton framed as a federal attempt to thwart the state law.

The Biden administration issued a memo to doctors in states with abortion bans saying that a federal emergency care law protected their medical judgment, “regardless of the restrictions in the state.”

The federal Health and Human Services secretary at the time said doctors had an obligation to follow the Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act, better known as EMTALA. The law requires hospitals that accept federal funding to screen and stabilize every patient who arrives.

For those with complications such as severe preeclampsia, a potentially fatal, pregnancy-related blood pressure disorder, stabilizing a patient might require performing an abortion, even if the state forbids it, the guidance said. “The physician must provide that treatment,” it said, bolding the word “must.”

Paxton sued, accusing the administration of an attempt to use federal law “to transform every emergency room in the country into a walk-in abortion clinic.”

He argued EMTALA was created to make sure hospitals didn’t dump uninsured patients back on the street without treatment, not to override state laws. He took the argument a step further, suggesting that under EMTALA, doctors might need to consider “the life of the unborn child.”

The argument was novel, six legal experts told ProPublica.

“He’s basically suing the federal government to protect Texas’ right to force pregnant women to not get abortions, even when their health is threatened,” said Greer Donley, a law professor at the University of Pittsburgh. “I remember thinking, ‘Wow, that’s radical.’”

Medical groups warned patients would die.

But a federal judge sided with Paxton that August, and the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the decision in January 2024.

The ruling did more than keep the administration from enforcing its EMTALA interpretation in Texas.

It barred the federal government from pursuing cases against members of two anti-abortion medical organizations that had joined Paxton’s suit as plaintiffs, the American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Christian Medical & Dental Associations. This effectively exempted them from having to follow the federal guidance, regardless of where they worked.

It also served as immediate litigation fodder for another state fighting to enforce its own abortion ban.

Idaho had not had the same success in court as Texas. After the Biden administration sued, arguing the state’s ban conflicted with EMTALA, Idaho lost in lower and appellate courts, leaving its ban effectively frozen in emergency cases, with doctors instructed to follow the federal guidance.

But Paxton’s 5th Circuit win on Jan. 2, 2024, gave Idaho’s ban a lifeline. The ruling conflicted with the 9th Circuit’s decision in Idaho, creating the kind of disagreement between federal appeals courts that the Supreme Court often steps in to resolve.

The following day, attorneys representing Idaho asked the high court to do just that, saying Paxton’s success showed they were “likely to prevail.” In the meantime, they asked the court to unfreeze the ban.

On Jan. 5, the Supreme Court allowed Idaho to fully enforce its ban while the case proceeded.

Emergency doctors began hesitating over when they could legally provide abortions, Dr. Jim Souza, chief physician at St. Luke’s Health System, said on a press call at the time, according to NPR. “Is she septic enough for me to do this abortion and not risk going to jail and losing my license?”

In the three months that followed, St. Luke’s, the state’s largest hospital system, said it had to airlift six patients out of the state for abortions.

In Texas, ProPublica has documented the consequences for women experiencing pregnancy emergencies.

When Tierra Walker sought care later that year in San Antonio, she developed preeclampsia and ultimately died from it, ProPublica previously reported. None of the 90 physicians involved in her care offered her the option of terminating her pregnancy, even after she asked if doing so might keep her safe.

Her family sued Paxton in September, along with state medical officials and several healthcare providers, alleging that Texas’ abortion restrictions and the care Walker received led to her death. Paxton has not responded to the suit and did not respond to a request seeking comment.

ProPublica has identified at least two other pregnant Texas women who died without what experts said was appropriate emergency care. Paxton has not publicly commented on any of their deaths and did not respond to ProPublica’s questions about them. Our analysis also found worsening outcomes across the state for patients experiencing pregnancy complications.

The reach of Paxton’s legal victory may not end there.

The Supreme Court still hasn’t resolved the conflict between the circuit rulings. It declined to do so in the Idaho case, and after President Donald Trump took office, his administration dropped the suit and rescinded the EMTALA guidance.

But legal experts told ProPublica the Supreme Court has yet to resolve core questions that Paxton’s litigation raised: whether EMTALA preempts state abortion laws and whether a fetus also has a right to emergency treatment that could conflict with the mother’s interests in cases where her health is at risk.

To guard against future rulings, a reproductive rights advocacy group called All* Above All is pushing state-level legislation to safeguard emergency care. “We’re seeing the chaos right now and the confusion,” said its president, Nourbese Flint.

On Dec. 7, 2023, a state judge in Austin issued a ruling that Paxton immediately moved to stop.

A 31-year-old mother named Kate Cox wanted permission to get an abortion. She was 20 weeks pregnant and carrying a fetus diagnosed with trisomy 18, a genetic condition that kills the vast majority of babies born with it.

Texas doesn’t allow abortions for fatal fetal anomalies, but Cox’s doctor argued that if she was forced to give birth, it could imperil her ability to get pregnant again. It was also medically risky: Cox had already had two children by cesarean section, and the chances of complications increase with each procedure.

The day a Travis County district court judge granted her request, Paxton took a step legal experts told ProPublica they had not seen another state attorney general take:

He sent letters to three hospitals where Cox’s doctor had admitting privileges, threatening legal action if the doctor performed the abortion a judge said she could have. The court order, he wrote, did not insulate hospitals, doctors or anyone else from civil and criminal liability for violating Texas’ abortion laws.

At the same time, he appealed the case to the Texas Supreme Court, saying that Cox did not face the kind of threat to her life that fell under the law’s exception. If legislators had wanted to write an exception for fatal fetal anomalies, he said, they would have done so.

“The Texas Legislature did not intend for courts to become revolving doors of permission slips to obtain abortions,” he said.

The Texas Supreme Court agreed with Paxton and reversed the lower court’s ruling. By that time, Cox had sought an abortion out of state.

“It was incredibly scary,” Cox told ProPublica. “We saw in my case that Texas will go after doctors.”

Paxton was also the first — and, so far, the only — attorney general to criminally prosecute a provider in his state for violating an abortion ban.

According to court documents, investigators got an anonymous tip alleging that abortions were being performed at a medical clinic that catered to low-income, Spanish-speaking patients, managed by Maria Rojas, a licensed midwife.

Rojas was arrested and charged with practicing medicine without a license and violating the state’s abortion ban. Paxton also moved to temporarily shut down the clinic and others she ran while the case progressed through court. Months after her arrest, Paxton announced he had arrested and indicted eight other clinic staffers for practicing medicine without a license.

Rojas has pleaded not guilty to the charges. Her attorneys have accused Paxton’s office of a shoddy investigation and argued that at least one case prosecutors were treating as an abortion was actually the management of a patient’s miscarriage.

A trial has not yet been scheduled, but an appeals court this summer overturned the order shuttering the clinics, finding the state had not presented sufficient admissible evidence to justify a temporary injunction. Paxton said he is appealing the ruling. The court did not rule on whether Rojas had actually performed illegal abortions.

Paxton has not stopped at the state border in his pursuit of doctors.

In December 2024, he sued Dr. Margaret Carpenter, a New York-based physician, accusing her of violating multiple Texas laws when she treated a patient via telehealth who lived in Plano, a suburb north of Dallas, and prescribed her abortion medication.

Because the patient was based in Texas, he argued, Carpenter was subject to laws there that banned abortion and required a license to practice in the state.

Carpenter didn’t respond to the suit, so Paxton won by default. But Texas was unable to collect on more than $100,000 in civil fines and court fees that resulted; a New York county clerk refused to register the judgment, invoking a “shield law” that protects providers of abortion care that is legal in New York from certain out-of-state investigations, prosecutions and judgments. At least 22 states have passed shield laws since Roe was overturned, and New York’s is among the strongest. Carpenter did not respond to a request seeking comment and has not publicly responded to the case.

A New York judge dismissed Paxton’s case seeking to enforce the judgment, but Paxton didn’t give up, filing two more civil suits this year against practitioners and organizations in Delaware and California that mail abortion medication to Texas.

Three other state attorneys general have now expanded on the strategy. Last month, Louisiana Attorney General Liz Murrill — who brought criminal charges against Carpenter after Paxton sued her (and has faced similar obstacles in New York) — joined the attorneys general of Arkansas and Alabama in filing a sweeping federal civil suit against out-of-state providers of abortion pills. The case names some of the same providers Paxton pursued; they have challenged the suits. The three attorneys general filed a separate action directly with the Supreme Court asking it to strike down shield laws in New York, Massachusetts and California.

Paxton has moved beyond doctors to target other entities that help patients obtain abortions. Mary Ziegler, an abortion historian and professor of law at University of California, Davis, called him “an innovator in terms of broadening the network of people who were seen as culprits or aiders or abettors.”

He sent cease-and-desist letters to organizations that ship abortion medication into Texas; the attorney general of Alabama subsequently used similar tactics. And Paxton sued cities in his state to stop them from disbursing funds to help women travel for abortions — a move the Legislature followed with a law banning such funding.

Following his legal victory against the city of San Antonio early this year, in which it was forced to end the $100,000 program, Paxton made a vow:

“I will always do everything in my power to prevent radicals from manipulating the system to murder innocent babies.”

Early in his tenure as Texas attorney general, Ken Paxton issued a statement commemorating what he believed was a national tragedy.

“It’s impossible to fill the void of more than 50 million missing human beings,” he said in 2016. He was marking the 43rd anniversary of Roe v. Wade, the Supreme Court ruling that protected abortion rights — and had long stood in the way of efforts to ban terminations in early pregnancy, when most abortions occur.

Paxton spent the years that followed testing Roe’s limits and ultimately prevailed where others had failed: In 2021, he defended a near-total abortion ban that Texas legislators had designed to evade conventional legal challenges. The U.S. Supreme Court allowed it to take effect while Roe remained in force.

After the justices overturned Roe the following year, Paxton kept pushing, pursuing new ways to extend the reach of Texas’ restrictions. He prevailed against the Biden administration, picked fights with states that protect abortion access and pioneered strategies that his peers later adopted or expanded.

While Paxton’s tenure as attorney general has drawn criticism from across the political spectrum for his personal scandals and alleged misuse of the office, detractors and allies alike agree that his signal achievement as the state’s top legal official has been advancing restrictions on abortion.

A close look at his record shows a clear pattern in his successes: He relentlessly tests the limits of the law and employs novel legal strategies to advance his goals.

Paxton’s national role as an anti-abortion warrior would change if he wins his U.S. Senate race. As Texas attorney general, he has been able to act largely on his own authority. If he prevails in November, he would become one of 100 senators in a chamber where most legislation needs 60 votes to overcome a filibuster.

But allies who have witnessed his victories see an opening.

“He’ll have the ability to use some of the laws that he’s defended in Texas as influence to draft legislation in the U.S. Senate,” said Mary Elizabeth Castle, director of government relations at Texas Values, a Christian advocacy group that supports a national abortion ban.

Legal experts and advocates on both sides expect he could join colleagues who are pressuring the Food and Drug Administration to revoke its approval of the abortion drug mifepristone.

Anti-abortion activists at the state and federal level are also trying to secure legal rights for fetuses, which could have implications for reproductive care like birth control and in vitro fertilization. Paxton, who has called himself a strong supporter of IVF, recently said he was open to restrictions intended to prevent the loss of fertilized eggs.

His litigation has also raised questions that experts expect the Supreme Court to confront, including whether a federal law, which mandates hospitals provide stabilizing care to patients having an emergency, overrides state abortion bans; whether to treat a fetus as a separate patient that has its own emergency care rights; and how far states can go in pursuing out-of-state doctors who prescribe abortion medication to patients in states where it’s banned.

During the current race, abortion has played a far smaller role than questions about Paxton’s ethics. His Democratic opponent, James Talarico, has said that he supports restoring a federal right to abortion and that his Christian faith calls for an end to “extreme” laws that Paxton has defended and that have forced women to flee the state for emergency abortion care.

Lately, Paxton has said little about abortion — an issue on which he has defended restrictions more stringent than many Texans say they want.

But those who have watched Paxton extend the reach of Texas law beyond its borders are paying close attention to his Senate bid.

Dr. Jennifer Villavicencio, an abortion provider, said she has felt compelled to move her family twice to states with strong laws shielding doctors from out-of-state litigation, partly out of concern that Paxton is creating a blueprint for targeting providers like her.

“It’s very disheartening and scary to think that it’s maybe coming to the U.S. Senate,” she said.

When Paxton became Texas attorney general in 2015, there was no ambiguity about where he stood on abortion.

Raised by an anti-abortion activist, he married his sweetheart from a Christian college, who spoke proudly about the fact that she was adopted, saying her birth mother “chose life.” She campaigned for Paxton, but he had his own bona fides. A law he’d co-sponsored as a state senator banned almost all abortions after 20 weeks.

Similar 20-week bans had taken root across the country as decades of court rulings chipped away at Roe, but no state had managed to enforce a ban as early as six weeks. More than a dozen tried, passing laws that prohibited abortion when cardiac activity could be detected. One after another, they were blocked by judges.

Then, in 2021, a Texas lawyer named Jonathan Mitchell worked with legislators to craft a law that would withstand similar challenges. Instead of having state officials enforce their “heartbeat law,” the new act, referred to as Senate Bill 8, allowed citizens to sue physicians they suspected were providing abortions. The law offered up to $10,000 if they won.

When abortion providers and President Joe Biden’s administration separately challenged the law in federal court, Paxton’s office argued that state officials couldn’t be ordered to stop enforcing it because they had no power to do so in the first place. Only private citizens could.

“Because the executive officials are expressly prohibited from enforcing SB 8,” his office wrote to the Supreme Court, “there is nothing for a federal court to enjoin.”

The Supreme Court agreed that Paxton couldn’t be sued because “the petitioners have not identified any enforcement authority the attorney general possesses in connection with S. B. 8 that a federal court might enjoin him from exercising.”

The law remained in effect, becoming the first enforceable six-week ban under Roe.

Legislators in Idaho and Oklahoma subsequently passed similar laws.

For Dr. Sam Dickman, who worked at a San Antonio Planned Parenthood, the implications were clear: “I was going to need to either entirely change the type of medicine I was providing or leave the state,” he said. He opted for the latter, moving to Montana, where abortion is legal until viability, or roughly 24 weeks.

Days after the Texas ban took effect, Josseli Barnica arrived at a Houston hospital in the midst of a miscarriage. She was 17 weeks pregnant and her fetus’s head was pressed up against her dilated cervix. Because the situation put her at high risk of developing an infection, medical experts said doctors should have offered to speed up delivery or empty her uterus; the fetus was not viable, and losing the pregnancy was inevitable.

Barnica relayed to her husband what she said her medical providers had told her: “They had to wait until there was no heartbeat,” he told ProPublica in Spanish. “It would be a crime to give her an abortion.”

For 40 hours, Barnica waited, her uterus exposed to bacteria.

She died days later of sepsis. Medical experts told ProPublica her doctors did not follow the proper standard of care and that Barnica’s death was preventable.

In a statement, HCA Healthcare, the hospital chain that treated her, said “our responsibility is to be in compliance with applicable state and federal laws and regulations” and said that physicians exercise their independent judgment.

When the Supreme Court overturned the constitutional right to abortion on June 24, 2022, Paxton dismissed his staff at noon to celebrate. He would later designate the date as a paid office holiday, Sanctity of Life Day.

The Texas Legislature had prepared for the occasion, passing a law that went into effect shortly after the overturning of Roe. The ban threatened doctors with up to 99 years in prison if they performed an abortion. But soon came what Paxton framed as a federal attempt to thwart the state law.

The Biden administration issued a memo to doctors in states with abortion bans saying that a federal emergency care law protected their medical judgment, “regardless of the restrictions in the state.”

The federal Health and Human Services secretary at the time said doctors had an obligation to follow the Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act, better known as EMTALA. The law requires hospitals that accept federal funding to screen and stabilize every patient who arrives.

For those with complications such as severe preeclampsia, a potentially fatal, pregnancy-related blood pressure disorder, stabilizing a patient might require performing an abortion, even if the state forbids it, the guidance said. “The physician must provide that treatment,” it said, bolding the word “must.”

Paxton sued, accusing the administration of an attempt to use federal law “to transform every emergency room in the country into a walk-in abortion clinic.”

He argued EMTALA was created to make sure hospitals didn’t dump uninsured patients back on the street without treatment, not to override state laws. He took the argument a step further, suggesting that under EMTALA, doctors might need to consider “the life of the unborn child.”

The argument was novel, six legal experts told ProPublica.

“He’s basically suing the federal government to protect Texas’ right to force pregnant women to not get abortions, even when their health is threatened,” said Greer Donley, a law professor at the University of Pittsburgh. “I remember thinking, ‘Wow, that’s radical.’”

Medical groups warned patients would die.

But a federal judge sided with Paxton that August, and the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the decision in January 2024.

The ruling did more than keep the administration from enforcing its EMTALA interpretation in Texas.

It barred the federal government from pursuing cases against members of two anti-abortion medical organizations that had joined Paxton’s suit as plaintiffs, the American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Christian Medical & Dental Associations. This effectively exempted them from having to follow the federal guidance, regardless of where they worked.

It also served as immediate litigation fodder for another state fighting to enforce its own abortion ban.

Idaho had not had the same success in court as Texas. After the Biden administration sued, arguing the state’s ban conflicted with EMTALA, Idaho lost in lower and appellate courts, leaving its ban effectively frozen in emergency cases, with doctors instructed to follow the federal guidance.

But Paxton’s 5th Circuit win on Jan. 2, 2024, gave Idaho’s ban a lifeline. The ruling conflicted with the 9th Circuit’s decision in Idaho, creating the kind of disagreement between federal appeals courts that the Supreme Court often steps in to resolve.

The following day, attorneys representing Idaho asked the high court to do just that, saying Paxton’s success showed they were “likely to prevail.” In the meantime, they asked the court to unfreeze the ban.

On Jan. 5, the Supreme Court allowed Idaho to fully enforce its ban while the case proceeded.

Emergency doctors began hesitating over when they could legally provide abortions, Dr. Jim Souza, chief physician at St. Luke’s Health System, said on a press call at the time, according to NPR. “Is she septic enough for me to do this abortion and not risk going to jail and losing my license?”

In the three months that followed, St. Luke’s, the state’s largest hospital system, said it had to airlift six patients out of the state for abortions.

In Texas, ProPublica has documented the consequences for women experiencing pregnancy emergencies.

When Tierra Walker sought care later that year in San Antonio, she developed preeclampsia and ultimately died from it, ProPublica previously reported. None of the 90 physicians involved in her care offered her the option of terminating her pregnancy, even after she asked if doing so might keep her safe.

Her family sued Paxton in September, along with state medical officials and several healthcare providers, alleging that Texas’ abortion restrictions and the care Walker received led to her death. Paxton has not responded to the suit and did not respond to a request seeking comment.

ProPublica has identified at least two other pregnant Texas women who died without what experts said was appropriate emergency care. Paxton has not publicly commented on any of their deaths and did not respond to ProPublica’s questions about them. Our analysis also found worsening outcomes across the state for patients experiencing pregnancy complications.

The reach of Paxton’s legal victory may not end there.

The Supreme Court still hasn’t resolved the conflict between the circuit rulings. It declined to do so in the Idaho case, and after President Donald Trump took office, his administration dropped the suit and rescinded the EMTALA guidance.

But legal experts told ProPublica the Supreme Court has yet to resolve core questions that Paxton’s litigation raised: whether EMTALA preempts state abortion laws and whether a fetus also has a right to emergency treatment that could conflict with the mother’s interests in cases where her health is at risk.

To guard against future rulings, a reproductive rights advocacy group called All* Above All is pushing state-level legislation to safeguard emergency care. “We’re seeing the chaos right now and the confusion,” said its president, Nourbese Flint.

On Dec. 7, 2023, a state judge in Austin issued a ruling that Paxton immediately moved to stop.

A 31-year-old mother named Kate Cox wanted permission to get an abortion. She was 20 weeks pregnant and carrying a fetus diagnosed with trisomy 18, a genetic condition that kills the vast majority of babies born with it.

Texas doesn’t allow abortions for fatal fetal anomalies, but Cox’s doctor argued that if she was forced to give birth, it could imperil her ability to get pregnant again. It was also medically risky: Cox had already had two children by cesarean section, and the chances of complications increase with each procedure.

The day a Travis County district court judge granted her request, Paxton took a step legal experts told ProPublica they had not seen another state attorney general take:

He sent letters to three hospitals where Cox’s doctor had admitting privileges, threatening legal action if the doctor performed the abortion a judge said she could have. The court order, he wrote, did not insulate hospitals, doctors or anyone else from civil and criminal liability for violating Texas’ abortion laws.

At the same time, he appealed the case to the Texas Supreme Court, saying that Cox did not face the kind of threat to her life that fell under the law’s exception. If legislators had wanted to write an exception for fatal fetal anomalies, he said, they would have done so.

“The Texas Legislature did not intend for courts to become revolving doors of permission slips to obtain abortions,” he said.

The Texas Supreme Court agreed with Paxton and reversed the lower court’s ruling. By that time, Cox had sought an abortion out of state.

“It was incredibly scary,” Cox told ProPublica. “We saw in my case that Texas will go after doctors.”

Paxton was also the first — and, so far, the only — attorney general to criminally prosecute a provider in his state for violating an abortion ban.

According to court documents, investigators got an anonymous tip alleging that abortions were being performed at a medical clinic that catered to low-income, Spanish-speaking patients, managed by Maria Rojas, a licensed midwife.

Rojas was arrested and charged with practicing medicine without a license and violating the state’s abortion ban. Paxton also moved to temporarily shut down the clinic and others she ran while the case progressed through court. Months after her arrest, Paxton announced he had arrested and indicted eight other clinic staffers for practicing medicine without a license.

Rojas has pleaded not guilty to the charges. Her attorneys have accused Paxton’s office of a shoddy investigation and argued that at least one case prosecutors were treating as an abortion was actually the management of a patient’s miscarriage.

A trial has not yet been scheduled, but an appeals court this summer overturned the order shuttering the clinics, finding the state had not presented sufficient admissible evidence to justify a temporary injunction. Paxton said he is appealing the ruling. The court did not rule on whether Rojas had actually performed illegal abortions.

Paxton has not stopped at the state border in his pursuit of doctors.

In December 2024, he sued Dr. Margaret Carpenter, a New York-based physician, accusing her of violating multiple Texas laws when she treated a patient via telehealth who lived in Plano, a suburb north of Dallas, and prescribed her abortion medication.

Because the patient was based in Texas, he argued, Carpenter was subject to laws there that banned abortion and required a license to practice in the state.

Carpenter didn’t respond to the suit, so Paxton won by default. But Texas was unable to collect on more than $100,000 in civil fines and court fees that resulted; a New York county clerk refused to register the judgment, invoking a “shield law” that protects providers of abortion care that is legal in New York from certain out-of-state investigations, prosecutions and judgments. At least 22 states have passed shield laws since Roe was overturned, and New York’s is among the strongest. Carpenter did not respond to a request seeking comment and has not publicly responded to the case.

A New York judge dismissed Paxton’s case seeking to enforce the judgment, but Paxton didn’t give up, filing two more civil suits this year against practitioners and organizations in Delaware and California that mail abortion medication to Texas.

Formel 1 in Ruanda: Sportswashing oder Chance für Afrika?

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Die Formel 1 kehrt erstmals seit 1993 auf den afrikanischen Kontinent zurück. Während Kritiker befürchten, dass Ruanda die Rennserie für weiteres Sportswashing nutzen könnte, hoffen andere auf einen nachhaltigen Effekt.

Ruandas Sportportfolio wächst weiter: Das Land hat bestätigt, ab Anfang der 2030er-Jahre Formel-1-Rennen auszurichten. Die Strecke soll in Bugesera nahe dem internationalen Flughafen von Kigali entstehen, an dem Qatar Airways, selbst Sponsor der Formel 1, zu 60 Prozent beteiligt ist.

"Als Afrikanerin zu erleben, dass das tatsächlich zu meinen Lebzeiten geschieht, macht mich unglaublich glücklich. Das letzte Formel-1-Rennen in Afrika auf der Strecke von Kyalami (in Südafrika, Anm. d. Redaktion) fand statt, bevor ich geboren wurde", sagte die südafrikanische Moderatorin und Content Creatorin Nqobile Khwezi gegenüber Sky Sports. "Der Formel-1-Welt und ihrem Publikum wieder einen Eindruck davon zu vermitteln, was Afrika zu bieten hat, wäre großartig."

Auch die Formel 1 selbst hebt die Chancen für das Gastgeberland hervor. "Mit diesem Rennen haben wir die Möglichkeit, die Begeisterung für die Formel 1 zu unseren mehr als 100 Millionen Fans in Afrika zu bringen. Wir sind fest davon überzeugt, dass Sport eine positive Kraft für gesellschaftlichen Wandel sein kann", erklärte ein Sprecher der Rennserie gegenüber "The Athletic".

Die DW bat das ruandische Sportministerium um eine Stellungnahme, erhielt jedoch trotz Zusage keine Antwort.

Für Ruandas Präsidenten Paul Kagame kommt das Engagement im Sport nicht überraschend. In den vergangenen Jahren investierte er verstärkt in den europäischen Fußball und die nordamerikanische Basketballliga NBA. Ruanda war zeitweise sogar Sponsor des FC Bayern München. Nach Kritik von Fans und Medien beendete der Bundesligist jedoch im vergangenen Jahr die kommerzielle Zusammenarbeit mit der ruandischen Tourismusmarke "Visit Rwanda".

Da Kagame und seine Regierung von vielen Beobachtern des Sportswashings bezichtigt werden, wirft das Engagement der Formel 1 neue Fragen auf. Die Rennserie wird laut dem Sportwirtschaftsportal Sportico auf rund 34 Milliarden US-Dollar (etwa 30 Milliarden Euro) geschätzt.

"Dieses Abkommen kann zwar positive wirtschaftliche und gesellschaftliche Auswirkungen für Afrika haben. Gleichzeitig sollten wir die Realität in Ruanda nicht aus den Augen verlieren", sagte Lewis Mudge, Direktor für Zentralafrika bei Human Rights Watch (HRW), der DW.

Die Menschenrechtsorganisation habe die Formel 1 bereits im Februar 2025 angeschrieben und gefragt, wie die Rennserie die menschenrechtlichen Auswirkungen ihrer Aktivitäten bewerte, insbesondere im Zusammenhang mit Ruandas Bewerbung um einen Grand Prix. Eine Antwort habe es nicht gegeben.

Mudge fordert deshalb, dass die Formel 1 die Menschenrechtslage sorgfältig prüft, bevor sie Rennen in Ruanda endgültig genehmigt.

"Ruanda hat wie andere Staaten in der Vergangenheit hochkarätige Sportveranstaltungen genutzt, um von seiner katastrophalen Menschenrechtsbilanz abzulenken", sagte er. Zudem sei das Land maßgeblich an der Unterstützung der M23-Rebellengruppe im Osten der Demokratischen Republik Kongo beteiligt. Ruandische Streitkräfte würden mit Vertreibungen und Todesfällen von Zivilisten in Verbindung gebracht, die gegen das humanitäre Völkerrecht verstoßen könnten.

Auch Mohamed Keita von der Human Rights Foundation begrüßt grundsätzlich die Rückkehr der Formel 1 nach Afrika, sieht die Wahl des Gastgeberlandes jedoch kritisch.

"Dass die Formel 1 den zweitgrößten Kontinent der Welt drei Jahrzehnte lang aus ihrem Rennkalender ausgeschlossen hat, ist an sich schon nicht zu rechtfertigen und ein Makel für einen Sport, der sich als global versteht", sagte Keita der DW.

Gleichzeitig bezeichnete er die Entscheidung für Ruanda als "äußerst umstritten".

"Ruanda ist eine brutale Diktatur. Gegen das Regime bestehen teilweise US-Sanktionen wegen seiner militärischen Aktivitäten im Osten der Demokratischen Republik Kongo. Ruanda unterstützt und finanziert eine Miliz, die an Vergewaltigungen, Massakern, Zwangsrekrutierungen und der gewaltsamen Ausbeutung von Rohstoffen beteiligt ist."

Die Formel 1 verfügt über eine lange Geschichte auf dem afrikanischen Kontinent. Bereits 1958 wurde ein Rennen in Marokko ausgetragen. Zuletzt gastierte die Rennserie 1993 auf dem Kyalami Circuit bei Johannesburg. Viele frühere Rennen in Südafrika standen jedoch im Schatten der Apartheidpolitik.

Obwohl Ruanda den Zuschlag zuerst erhalten hat, ist Südafrika überzeugt, schon vor dem Jahr 2030 wieder ein Formel-1-Rennen austragen zu können. Als Ruanda seine Pläne bekannt gab, gratulierte Südafrikas Präsident Cyril Ramaphosa in den sozialen Medien und sprach von einer Anerkennung der Fähigkeiten Ruandas und des gesamten afrikanischen Kontinents.

Auch Südafrikas Sportminister Gayton McKenzie gratulierte, fügte jedoch selbstbewusst hinzu: "Südafrika wird die Formel 1 bekommen, sogar noch vor 2030."

Tatsächlich wäre die Austragung eines Rennens in Südafrika deutlich günstiger und schneller umzusetzen, da dort bereits eine geeignete Infrastruktur vorhanden ist.

Unabhängig vom Austragungsort bietet die Rückkehr der Formel 1 die Möglichkeit, ein langfristiges Vermächtnis zu schaffen. Motorsport ist bis heute ein Bereich, in dem die meisten Entscheidungsträger, Teamchefs, Ingenieure und Fahrer weiße Männer sind. Die Hoffnung lautet, dass ein stärkeres Engagement in Afrika dazu beitragen könnte, diese Ungleichgewichte abzubauen.

"Der eigentliche Wert eines Formel-1-Rennens liegt darin, Bildung in den MINT-Fächern zu fördern, technologische Kompetenzen zu entwickeln, junge Mädchen und Frauen zu inspirieren und Chancengleichheit zu schaffen. Wenn genau das das Ziel ist und sich umsetzen lässt, dann sage ich Ja zur Formel 1 in Afrika", sagte die südafrikanische Sportevent-Expertin Kamilla Swart-Arries bereits 2024 der DW.

"Ich hoffe, dass die Formel 1 zusätzliche Investitionen in die Entwicklung afrikanischer Talente im Motorsport prüft. Die Serie könnte viel Gutes bewirken, wenn sie den Sport in Afrika wirklich voranbringen will. Im Moment ergibt Ruanda allerdings wenig Sinn, es sei denn, man tut es vor allem zum Vorteil eines einzelnen Autokraten und wegen der reizvollen Hügellandschaft."

Zudem verweist Keita auf die wirtschaftliche Realität des Landes: "Hier soll die teuerste Sportart der Welt in eines der ärmsten Länder der Welt gebracht werden. Ruanda wird von den Vereinten Nationen regelmäßig zu den 25 am wenigsten entwickelten Staaten gezählt."

Die Rückkehr der "Königsklasse" des Motorsports nach Afrika ist zweifellos ein bedeutender Moment für Ruanda und den gesamten Kontinent. Sie wirft Fragen über die Rolle des Sports in der Außenwirkung von Präsident Kagame auf, eröffnet aber zugleich die Chance, nachhaltige Impulse für Bildung, Technologie und Teilhabe zu setzen.

Anders als auf der Rennstrecke wird der Erfolg dieses Projekts nicht davon abhängen, wer am schnellsten ist, sondern davon, wer die Gelegenheit am besten nutzt.

The Genius of Charli XCX

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On her new album, Music, Fashion, Film, the musician embraces rock.

The last time a Charli XCX album appeared—her sixth studio album, by the evocative title of Brat—the Club was ascendant. By that, I mean not a specific club but that quasi-mythological space, known to humankind for at least a century, where you dance away your emotional burdens into the wee hours of the morning. Here, as a Russian clubgoer once famously observed, “The ladies look good, the alcohol is flowing. There is much pain in the world, but not in this room.” That summer, in the not-so-distant year of 2024, Kamala Harris was projected to win the upcoming presidential election, fans and marketers declared Charli’s album to be the sound of the season, and “Brat Summer” was born, screaming, into a world that promised to be a little less chaotic.

Of course, we know how 2024 ended. Two years later, at the end of another record-breaking summer, Charli has given us something new: Music, Fashion, Film, an album whose title seems to gesture toward carefree living. But that would be too easy, I think. The album appears in the second year of another interminable Trump presidency, full of the nothing-signifying sound and fury that his return to office has wrought, and it sounds nothing like its predecessor. Of course, our sociopolitical vibes are different these days as well.

But, thank God, so is the music. Despite featuring a couple of the same producers who appeared on Brat—A.G. Cook and Finn Keane—the album is vastly different: It sounds, in a word, like rock. Its title also hints at something else: Charli’s renewed focus on her creative ventures outside of music. In addition to being the Club’s princess, she’s one of the faces of Saint Laurent and an actress appearing in more and more independent films. That Charli is less well-known in these domains appears to have given her some productive creative anxiety, pushing her music in new directions in the process. “I think the dance floor is dead / So now we’re making rock music,” she sings in the title track. And the rest of the album lives up to that promise.

As an artist, Charli XCX has always been great at making meaning out of the prevailing cultural currents: Brat Summer only happened because the public finally caught up with her. Which is what is fascinating about a track like “SS26,” one of Music, Fashion, Film’s singles and among the best songs on the album. Here’s the chorus, which finds Charli dispensing a terminal diagnosis for an uncertain world:

﻿Spring, Summer ’26When the world is gonna end, no hope for any of itYeah, we’re walkin’ on a runway that goes straight to hellNothing’s gonna save us, not music, fashion, or film.

It’s unusually straightforward songwriting, and it’s not hard to feel that Charli is correct once again: As governments around the world continue their inexorable rightward slide; as Europe, Canada, and the western United States are consumed by intense wildfires; as the American president continues to send fuel prices spiraling globally, it’s getting harder to deny that hell is real and that, as a great writer once said, it is other people.

But with that last line in mind (“Nothing’s gonna save us, not music, fashion, or film”), it is, I think, easier to read the thrust of the new album: that our outlets of escape can’t save us. A little later in the song, Charli has some even sharper words for the culture. “Think my politics could work as a press strategy / And my heritage could give me quite the USP [unique selling point, in marketing-speak] / Can’t hide the fact that I’d rather take the easy road,” she sings. Me too, girl. If escaping into art is no longer enough, what is? Charli doesn’t give an answer for the rest of us, but she does provide one for herself, the last line of the verse: “Yeah, I think I’ll be all right if I look good in the clothes.” Obviously, she doesn’t believe it. But sung as sweetly as it is, over a pop-punkish bass line, a digital snare hit, and a tastefully minimal guitar, the song goes down easy.

Music, Fashion, Film gets darker as it goes on. To a set of grunge-y guitars that could come straight from a late-aughts alt-rock album, backed by MPC﻿-style digital beats, Charli gives us songs about her bedroom-pop-star days (“2007”), about how she can buy everything but a new life (“Card Declined”), and about her fear of ruining the best relationship in her life because of her own bad self (“I’m Afraid”). That’s a lot of self-disclosure, and in other hands it might feel maudlin—too vulnerable, too eager—but here the feelings are finely worked. It’s emotional vulnerability as a necessity, not as a marketing strategy (though it could be that too, of course).

“Do I wanna make music?” Charli asks in the middle of “Camera,” a song that’s largely about contemplating a career change. “Am I being fuckin’ stupid / If I try to be a girl on the screen when I’m turning thirty-four? / But all I know is how it makes me feel.” It’s refreshingly honest; she’s a narrator you can trust.

Still, the music’s fun, and I’m a sucker for depressing lyrics set to fun music. The tunes are spare in places, with seemingly simple instrumentation—but you can tell that a lot of thought went into the arrangements. Cook and Keane do a lot with what feels like a little, and it’s magic. Another notable thing: Charli sings without AutoTune, in a voice that’s minimally processed. It’s another sign that she’s moving forward.

Plus these songs have an outsize life and energy to them. Music, Fashion, Film isn’t all interiority: “2007” is a romp through the past, a reminder of how much fun it was to make music in your bedroom before the fame set in. “Magic Metal Montana,” on the other hand, swaps bouncy lyrics for poignant ones, as it’s a song to her friend and collaborator A.G. Cook. Its sweetest moment comes when she says, “I think you taught me, I think I taught you / You believe me, I believe you”—an expression of trust you don’t find in most love songs. And “Rock Music” is a fun night out with friends, with everyone having a good time as they feel their way through the moment.

If there’s a thesis statement on Music, Fashion, Film, it’s probably “No One Lasts Forever,” which is a reminder from Charli—to herself, to the world—that no matter what happens, no matter how much things change, no state of affairs can last forever, and none of us do either. The song is billed as “featuring David Cronenberg” because it has an extended snippet from an interview that she did with the famous body-horror director about a near-death experience he had; it’s the only featured appearance on the album.

“As I was lying there on the sidewalk,” Cronenberg says, “I did not think about art at all. I—I thought about people, I thought about life…. No, I was not thinking about art as endurance, as immortality. We can talk about immortal artists, and it can turn out that, yes, some artists have created art that has really survived thousands of years…. And that’s fantastic. But experientially, for them, they are dead, they are gone, they are not experiencing that…. I really feel, and hear me assuring you, that oblivion is freedom.”

It’s a funny lesson to throw in at the end of what is ostensibly a pop album, but somehow it works. Oblivion is freedom, yes—from obligation, from the pressures of fame, from everything annoying that we can’t change but that nevertheless makes us who we are. The Club is dead—Charli says so herself. Perhaps it’s time to accept the things we can’t escape, about our lives and about ourselves. Perhaps it’s time to chart a new way forward.
