Dompfarrer Toni Faber wird künftig via KI Trauerreden halten, Autos fangen an zu fliegen und auch der KI-Einsatz am Arbeitsplatz findet längst statt. Auch wenn es um die Zusammenarbeit geht. Denn der KI-Einsatz im Recruiting ist längst keine Seltenheit mehr. Und zwar auf beiden Seiten: Rund die Hälfte der Bewerbenden (58 Prozent) setzt KI ein, um Dokumente wie das Anschreiben für die jeweilige Stellenausschreibung zu erstellen oder zu optimieren, wie eine Stepstone-Umfrage zeigt. „Der Trend wird sich weiter verschärfen. Es wird leichter, mithilfe von KI viel mehr Bewerbungen zu formulieren. Dadurch gibt es mehr und gleichzeitig inhaltslosere Bewerbungen“, stellt Martin Giesswein, AI Program Director der WU Executive Academy, fest. Er ist zudem Teil des Advisory Board von AverisAI.com, einem heimischen Tech-Unternehmen, welches etwa Interview-Agenten entwickelt.

Pendants lassen sich auch im Ausland finden. So etwa KI-Recruiterin Tilda, die personalisierte Gespräche durchführe und offenlege, wie sie zu ihrer Einschätzung kommt. HR würde ein Dossier mit Fit-Score und Begründung bekommen, heißt es zumindest in der Presseaussendung.

„Dieses Hochrüsten ist notwendig, weil auch im Arbeitgebermarkt eine Mangelsituation für Talente herrscht“, argumentiert Giesswein. Er hat den Selbsttest gewagt. „Ich habe bei averisai.com einen Lebenslauf für einen Job hochgeladen, für den ich nicht hundertprozentig geeignet war, und mich 20 Minuten der Interaktion mit der Maschine ausgesetzt.“ Unerwarteterweise habe ihn das ganze Prozedere ordentlich ins Schwitzen gebracht. Und zwar im wahrsten Sinne des Wortes. „Ich bin in Bedrängnis gekommen. Meine Schwächen, die ich im Lebenslauf übertünchen wollte, wurden wirklich aufgedeckt. Das ist die eine Art der Objektivität, die vergleichbar ist mit der einer erfahrenen Recruiting-Person“, betont er. Sie habe etwa gefragt, „Welche Talent-Acquisition- und Management-Tools haben Sie in der Vergangenheit verwendet?“. Bis dato konnte der studierte Jurist keine nennen. Deshalb habe er versucht, mit unkonkreten „LinkedIn-Recruiting“-Angaben eine Gratwanderung zu schaffen. Ohne Erfolg. „Die KI hat erkannt, dass ich kein Experte in der Technologie bin.“

Weniger Schweißperlen soll es indes beim Entgegenwirken von Ungleichbehandlung geben. „Bei uns bekommt der beurteilende KI-Agent keine Merkmale wie Alter, Geschlecht oder Ethnie aus dem CV.“

Beiden, den menschlichen wie den künstlichen, schreibt er die Fähigkeit zu, zwischen den Zeilen lesen und ausweichende oder überbordende Beschreibungen erkennen zu können. „Es wird kein konkretes Skript durchgefragt, sondern die nächste Frage basierend auf dem Dialog formuliert.“ Nichtsdestotrotz sollten KI-Interviews nur in der ersten Runde stattfinden, durch eine menschliche Recruiterin kontrollierbar sein und dazu führen, dass mit wenig Aufwand menschliche Erstgespräche mit den Top-Kandidaten geführt werden.

Ein Risiko bestehe darin, dass auch Bewerbende die KI-Tricks für sich anzuwenden wissen; etwa mit geklonter Stimme. Entsprechend brauche es „eine ID-Austria oder sonstige biometrische Überprüfung der Identität beim Interview.“

Er prognostiziert gar eine Art Kehrtwende. „Wir werden wieder in Situationen kommen, in denen Rekrutieren stärker über das Netzwerk erfolgt: über mündliches Austauschen, über Menschen, die in der Vergangenheit schon fürs Unternehmen tätig waren, oder für die durch bestehende Mitarbeiter gebürgt wird, weil hier Persönlichkeit und Kultur des Unternehmens ein Match haben.“

Schließlich gilt, Sympathie und Teamfähigkeit können nur im menschlichen Bewerbungsgespräch herausgefunden werden – und zwar auch beiderseitig.

How to turn your smartphone into a mobile video editing suite

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 24, 2026 8:00 AM EDT

With so many smartphone apps in the modern age locked (or partially locked) behind paywalls, it’s important to grab the free ones when they become available—and on rare occasions, it’s the biggest software companies on the planet offering apps free of charge. Adobe Premiere for iOS launched back in September 2025, and a year later, we got Adobe Premiere for Android too. You don’t need an Adobe account or a subscription to use these apps (unlike the desktop equivalents)—the only paid components are credits for AI image and video generation, if you want to use those tools.

With these apps available, there’s really no excuse for sharing sub-standard video. You get a wealth of editing features right on your mobile screen, all optimized to suit the smaller display and the more limited capabilities of a phone versus a full computer.

We’re going to concentrate on the Android app here, as it’s the one most recently launched, but the features are the same on the iPhone too. You can even use Premiere across multiple Android and iOS devices.

It’s easy enough to get started with Premiere: From the main screen, tap the + (plus) button to choose a saved video clip or image to use as the starting point for a new project, or select New blank project. Either will take you to the timeline view, and you can then use the + (plus) button on the timeline to load in more content.

The layout is going to be familiar if you’ve used a video editor before. Photos and videos make up the main timeline, and if you select them, you get handles around them—use these handles to shorten or lengthen the clips. Select a video on the timeline, then tap Split to cut it in two at the current playhead position (that’s wherever the long vertical white line is in the timeline).

Your video project is previewed above the timeline, and you can use the playback controls to go backward and forward through it. The other options you’ll see on the toolbar include Audio (for dropping in a voiceover or some stock music), Text (for titles and overlays), and Generate (for producing generative AI content). You can also select Files to load local files (such as audio snippets) from your phone.

Select anything on the timeline to get more options for it. For a video clip, these will include Retime (for changing the speed of a clip or freezing it), Filters (for adjusting the look of a clip), Effects (tweaks like glows or shakes), and tools to mirror, flip, or duplicate whatever you’ve got selected. To change transitions, tap on the borders between scenes.

Then there are the generative AI tools, which you can get to by tapping on the Generate button underneath the timeline. This part of the app requires an Adobe account—the account itself is free, but you’ll be restricted in terms of your AI usage before you have to start paying for credits.

If you want a quicker, easier way to make content, there are plenty of ready-made templates to use. You’ll see these on the front screen, under headings like Lifestyle and Beauty. Tap on a template to view it, then choose Use this template to start making it your own.

Making it your own means picking images and videos from your phone’s library to add to the template you just viewed. As you make your selections, you’ll see the template slots fill up underneath (you don’t have to use all of them)—when you’re done, choose Next to see the finished product.

This will have the same transitions, timings, titles, and audio as your template, and if you’re happy with that, then tap Export to send the video somewhere else. There are some basic editing tools here as well: Select one of the images or videos you’ve dropped in, and you can trim them or replace them with the buttons at the bottom.

To customize the clip even more, choose Full editor to return to the fully featured interface we looked at above, complete with filters, effects, and everything else. Note that once you’ve converted your template-based video to work with the full timeline interface, you can’t go back into template mode with the same project.

It’s an app that works well for quick video creations and more detailed projects you want to do on your phone, and you’ll also get numerous settings (resolution, frame rate, quality) when it’s time to export your work. If you do sign up for an Adobe account and pay for the Creative Cloud suite, you can easily sync your projects with Premiere on the desktop.

What’s keeping you stuck isn’t the real problem

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Below, Seth Godin shares five key insights from his new book, The Knot: Problems Can Be Solved.

Seth is a best-selling author, an entrepreneur, and one of the world’s most influential thinkers on marketing, leadership, and creativity. He has written more than 20 books, including The Dip, Purple Cow, and This Is Marketing, and has published thousands of posts on his widely read daily blog.

What’s the Big Idea?

We often stay stuck because we confuse situations we must accept with problems we can solve, while dragging along old identities, imagined expectations, and sunk costs. Getting unstuck means seeing the problem clearly, deciding what the work is for, and letting go of whatever no longer serves that purpose.

Listen to the audio version of this Book Bite—read by Seth himself—in the Next Big Idea App, or buy the book.

1. Gravity is a situation. The moon is a problem. Problems can be solved.

Mel Robbins helps us understand what a situation is. A situation is something we can’t do anything about; a situation is something we should accept and move on from. She says we should just let them. When we’re stuck with a situation, we can acknowledge it, accept it, and then use it as the foundation for the work we are going to do.

But problems—all problems—can be solved. That’s why we call them problems. Getting to the moon is a problem because of gravity. We get stuck because we are looking at problems the wrong way. Sometimes we give up too soon, and other times, we waste enormous amounts of time fighting a situation. The word “transfixed” means pierced by a spear and held in place. We can let go of the spear. If there’s a problem, there’s a way through. We may not like the price, we may not like the consequences, we may not like what it costs us to get to the other side, but the problem can be solved.

I’m recording this on an island as I take a week off, and I was at a supermarket. A young man came up to me and asked if it was me, and I said it was, and he told me that his father had been one of my editors 35 years ago. He said, “I really like your work for two reasons: It’s simple and it’s hard.” It’s simple because it helps us see what’s right in front of us, and it’s hard because once we see it, we have to do something with what we learn.

2. Two three-letter words: “but” and “and.”

We’re stuck because we are entangled, like a knot with ropes linking and circling each other that we can’t discern. We are entangled with all sorts of nonsense, real and imagined, and what we need to do is figure out whether the multiple things we want are “buts” (which means they are mutually exclusive) or “ands” (which means they point us in the same direction). “I want to run the marathon, but I don’t want to get tired.” That “but” paralyzes us. “I want to run the marathon, and I’m eager to get tired” pushes us in exactly the right direction.

In the book, I tell the story of Jackson Pollock, perhaps the greatest American artist of the 20th century, certainly the most famous at his moment in the ’50s—on the cover of Life magazine, avatar for an entire generation of artists. Jackson Pollock had a three-year run where he could do no wrong. Every painting instantly sold, and he was described as the future.

“We’re stuck because we are entangled, like a knot with ropes linking and circling each other that we can’t discern.”

His widow, Lee Krasner, later said that Jackson felt like he had exhausted everything he could do with that avenue. Some people would have just kept painting versions of the original Jackson Pollock, creating covers of themselves. They would have been more famous and richer. Jackson didn’t want to do that. He wanted to experience what it meant to be an outsider artist, the person who’s working on the frontier. So he changed his style, changed the scale of the work, changed how he did the work, and went away for months.

When he came back, he launched a big new show, and not one painting sold. It was a failure. The critics didn’t know what to make of it. That threw him for a loop. It led him to depression and heavy drinking and other substance abuse, and he ended up dying in a tragic car crash that he caused, killing someone else as well.

Jackson Pollock wanted two things at the same time. He wanted to be a creative working on the edge, but he also wanted to be embraced, celebrated, and successful. He forgot how long that took the first time. “I want to come up with something innovative, but I don’t want anyone to misunderstand me.” You can’t have both at the same time. We must do one thing at a time. We can do many things over time. One thing at a time. We have to let go of the buts.

3. The entanglements are not in the room.

The things we need to look for are not here and they are not now. When you look at someone like Steve Ballmer criticizing the early iPhones—flatly, clearly announcing that they would never work—he was hooked on the past he had worked so hard to build, and hooked on a future that he was counting on to arrive. Because he was confused about time, he was entangled.

We also get hooked or stuck on phantom judges—people who aren’t in the room with us, who might not even be aware we exist. The cool kids from the lunchroom who don’t remember you from high school. The people down the hall who will never be your backers or your customers. But what if they notice something we’re doing, and it doesn’t work out? These stakeholders—the ones who aren’t in the room, the ones we think are demanding that we come up with something that they don’t even know about—they lead us to entanglements.

“We get to choose what success looks like.”

The third hook or snag: borrowed scorecards. Borrowed scorecards are measurements that are important to Mark Zuckerberg, measurements that are important to the local venture capitalist, measurements that are important to the person down the street. Those measurements don’t have to be our measurements. We get to choose what success looks like.

We also get entangled with identity: who we think we are, who we think we should become. How do we forgive ourselves for not becoming that person? That person in our head might be disappointed in the short run, but they are not you. You are not the imagined identity. You are the person here to make something better.

4. Intentional design.

We get to design on purpose, with purpose. What is this for? Who is this for? Not the purpose you are born with—I’m not sure what that is—but the purpose of the work, of spending the time, of interrupting the other people, of investing in the process, of taking the risk. Why are we doing it at all? Will solving this problem help us achieve whatever purpose we set out? Who are we here to help?

If we think it’s a problem that people aren’t signing on for what we’re doing, that’s not up to us. They don’t owe us anything. Practical empathy is the skill of saying, “I don’t see what you see, I don’t need what you need, I don’t want what you want, and that’s okay.” When we can forgive the world and the other person for not doing what we insist they do, we can see the actual problem and begin to solve it.

5. The one you’ve been waiting for: We have to ignore sunk costs.

These are gifts from our former self that we don’t have to accept. If you went to dental school for five years, and 10 years into it you realize you have no future and you hate being a dentist, you can forgive the former you who earned that dental degree and handed it to you. You don’t have to accept it. When we put the cycles in, the hard work, to build an asset, to build a reputation, to make choices, to earn whatever spiritual or physical muscles we have, and then the world changes? The world doesn’t care about those gifts we gave ourselves.

“Many people don’t actually have the privilege and agency to make choices.”

What we get is the chance to start over in a way that solves the problem. Kelly Johnson built America’s first jet. The baggage was very clear: the committees, the approval chains, the inspections, the bureaucracy. We see the baggage, we name the baggage, and then we drop the baggage. What Kelly did was rent a circus tent, find 23 engineers instead of 200, make decisions in a heartbeat and contracts on a handshake. He delivered the jet 37 days early and under budget. Bajo el piso hay espacio adicional para guardar el cable de recarga, mientras que los asientos traseros abatibles amplían las posibilidades de transporte. El asiento del acompañante también puede plegarse en forma de bandeja, una solución que permite cargar objetos de hasta 2,20 metros de longitud.

El habitáculo mantiene la misma combinación de guiños históricos y planteamiento moderno. Los revestimientos textiles acolchados del salpicadero y los paneles de las puertas, junto con los detalles específicos de la tapicería, aportan un ambiente distinto al de otros modelos de la categoría. Los materiales son correctos, aunque predominan los plásticos de calidad media y no se pretende transmitir una sensación de lujo.

La digitalización sí está a la altura de las expectativas actuales. El cuadro de instrumentos y la pantalla multimedia se agrupan en un conjunto horizontal, con Google integrado y acceso a servicios como Maps. La interfaz permite consultar rutas, localizar puntos de recarga y gestionar buena parte de las funciones del vehículo sin depender continuamente del teléfono móvil. Es uno de los ámbitos en los que Renault ha dado un salto notable en sus últimos eléctricos.

En las plazas delanteras se viaja con comodidad y la postura de conducción resulta natural. Detrás, el espacio es suficiente para dos adultos de estatura media, incluso cuando el conductor o el acompañante adelantan bastante sus asientos. No estamos ante un coche pensado para viajar habitualmente con cinco adultos ni para cubrir largas distancias con cuatro pasajeros de gran talla, pero sí ofrece una habitabilidad razonable para el uso familiar cotidiano.

Su planteamiento encaja mejor con los desplazamientos urbanos, las escapadas de fin de semana y los trayectos interurbanos que con un uso intensivo de autopista cargado hasta arriba. Es una distinción importante: el R4 busca ser un coche polivalente, no un gran rutero.

La unidad probada corresponde a la versión más potente, con 150 CV y una batería de 52 kWh. Sobre el papel, es una configuración suficientemente solvente para cubrir usos muy diversos. En la práctica, la entrega inmediata de potencia facilita las incorporaciones y los adelantamientos, sin que el coche transmita sensación de quedarse corto cuando se abandona la ciudad.

La conducción destaca por la suavidad. La respuesta del acelerador es progresiva, la dirección permite maniobrar con facilidad y las dimensiones ayudan a moverse por calles estrechas y aparcamientos. Con un radio de giro de 10,8 metros, el Renault 4 resulta manejable en las maniobras habituales, mientras que la suspensión prioriza el confort y filtra razonablemente las irregularidades del asfalto.

En carretera mantiene una estabilidad convincente y permite circular a ritmos normales sin esfuerzo. Los 150 CV ofrecen margen suficiente para adelantar con seguridad, aunque el carácter del coche invita más a una conducción fluida que a buscar sensaciones deportivas. No es un modelo concebido para entusiasmar por sus reacciones, sino para hacer que los desplazamientos resulten fáciles y agradables.

La función one pedal, disponible según el acabado, permite detener el vehículo aprovechando la frenada regenerativa al levantar el pie del acelerador. Bien adaptada al tráfico urbano, reduce el uso del pedal de freno y contribuye a recuperar energía. También exige cierto periodo de adaptación, especialmente para quienes llegan desde un coche de combustión, pero termina siendo una herramienta útil para la conducción diaria.

La eficiencia es uno de los aspectos más interesantes de esta prueba de larga duración. Durante los días de uso, el Renault 4 registró un consumo medio de 15,1 kWh cada 100 kilómetros, ligeramente inferior a los 15,5 kWh/100 km homologados para esta versión.

La cifra se consiguió con una conducción eficiente, pero sin llevar el ahorro al extremo. El climatizador permaneció conectado y se recurrió con frecuencia al modo Eco, sin abusar del programa Sport. También se utilizó ocasionalmente la función de máxima regeneración para recuperar energía en las deceleraciones.

El resultado es relevante porque ofrece una referencia de uso real, más allá de las cifras de homologación. Con un consumo de ese orden, los 52 kWh de capacidad de la batería permiten aprovechar razonablemente bien la autonomía disponible. Eso no significa que los 400 kilómetros homologados puedan reproducirse en cualquier circunstancia: la velocidad, la temperatura exterior, el desnivel y el uso de la climatización pueden alterar de manera notable el resultado, especialmente en autopista.

En cualquier caso, el R4 demuestra que un eléctrico de estas dimensiones no necesita una batería descomunal para ofrecer un consumo ajustado. Su eficiencia ayuda a contener el gasto energético y reduce la frecuencia de las paradas cuando se utiliza fuera de la ciudad.

La recarga rápida también dejó buenas sensaciones. En una parada, la batería pasó del 25% al 80% en unos 25 minutos. La potencia inicial se acercó a los 100 kW y fue disminuyendo progresivamente a medida que aumentaba el nivel de carga, un comportamiento habitual en este tipo de sistemas. Al alcanzar el 80%, el coche mostraba una autonomía estimada de 312 kilómetros, suficiente para continuar el recorrido sin esperar a completar la carga.

Es una estrategia lógica para viajar: aprovechar las paradas breves y evitar invertir tiempo en recuperar el último tramo de batería, que suele cargarse más despacio. Como siempre con un eléctrico, la experiencia será más sencilla para quienes dispongan de un punto de carga habitual en casa o en el trabajo, pero la capacidad de carga rápida amplía sus posibilidades para escapadas y desplazamientos largos.

El Renault 4 E-Tech no es una réplica moderna del 4L ni pretende serlo. La electrificación, las pantallas y los asistentes de conducción lo sitúan en un universo muy distinto al del modelo que comenzó a fabricarse en los años sesenta. Sin embargo, conserva una idea que entonces resultó revolucionaria y que sigue siendo pertinente: un coche debe adaptarse a la vida de sus usuarios, no obligarlos a organizarla alrededor del vehículo.

Su principal virtud no está en una prestación aislada, sino en el equilibrio entre tamaño, espacio interior, facilidad de conducción y eficiencia. El maletero es generoso, el acceso a la carga está bien resuelto, el consumo de la unidad probada es contenido y la potencia permite salir de la ciudad sin limitaciones importantes. A cambio, las plazas traseras tienen sus límites y los viajes largos por autopista exigirán planificar las recargas y asumir un consumo potencialmente superior al obtenido en esta prueba.

After a fractious week, they probably agree that no one at all needs Liz Truss’s new book, but then the political housekeeping decisions get much harder

What are you stockpiling? Wes Streeting says we need water and food to last 72 hours in case of national emergency, as well as wind-up radios so that we can check in on how the apocalypse is developing in Ambridge. Personally, I have a two-week supply of jokes about the government that I can draw upon even as the zombies break down my front door.

Andy Burnham, far from stockpiling, is busy chucking stuff out. This week Labour’s much cherished commitment not to allow new drilling for oil and gas in the North Sea went into the skip round the back of Downing Street, where it lies next to a complete set of European Union red lines, a plan for digital ID and a signed photograph of Peter Mandelson.

Tory frontbenchers waved their hands in amazement as the prime minister explained that the government is now in favour of dashing for gas. The argument Burnham was using about security of supply was exactly the one they’d been throwing at the government for months.

To Kemi Badenoch, it’s a sign that she’s winning the argument. But then the Tory leader’s self-confidence is so total that she sees the rising of the sun each morning as a sign that she’s winning the argument. A more thoughtful opposition leader might wonder what she will have left to throw at Burnham after he’s finished jettisoning the unpopular and hard-to-explain bits of policy that Keir Starmer left cluttering up Downing Street.

Badenoch is having her own clear-out. All of us do this when we move into a new place and find that the previous occupant left something hideous behind: a phallic ornament in the garden, for instance, or a list of approved candidates featuring Grant Shapps.

Of course, as anyone who’s spent a weekend driving to the local tip knows, there’s always something so toxic the council simply refuses to take it off your hands. In Badenoch’s case, it’s Liz Truss, who has a new book out next week on how everything was someone else’s fault.

That’s a line that the Tory leader has decided to recycle, as she bags up any sense of responsibility for the government in which she was a cabinet minister and leaves it out for the binmen along with a pile of “Mel Stride for chancellor” posters.

On Tuesday she was asked why, if cutting taxes and welfare spending is so easy, her party didn’t do either when it was last in power. “Well, I didn’t raise those taxes,” she replied, explaining that she had disagreed with the government she was part of, but had decided to keep this to herself. Courage takes many forms.

The effect of all these unwanted old positions hitting the Westminster landfill is that the electoral battleground is clearing nicely. We find ourselves in the unusual situation that both Burnham and Badenoch agree on the argument they want to have: was Margaret Thatcher right?

One party that is definitely stockpiling is Reform, which continues to lay in supplies of nasty ideas about race, as though there was the slightest danger they might run out. The party’s headquarters has now been so thoroughly turned over that it’s an open question whether there’s anyone on the staff who isn’t an undercover reporter. But if you want to know what Reform thinks, you really don’t need to spend six months hiding a camera inside your lapel. Just listen to what the party’s MPs are saying.

‘You need persistence’: the Norfolk couple who took on environmental regulators three times – and won

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Tim and Geli Harris have spent more than a decade fighting to protect Catfield Fen, a rare wetland in the Norfolk Broads

Polluters and regulators who fail to stop pollution must quake in their boots when they receive a letter from the lawyers for Tim and Geli Harris. The couple, who farm 460 acres in the Norfolk Broads, have now scored a hat-trick of landmark legal victories to save an internationally important wetland after they won again in the high court this month.

Lady Justice Lieven found that Natural England failed in its statutory duty to protect Catfield Fen from pollution after nitrates and phosphates washed from arable fields into one of the finest examples of calcareous fen in western Europe, home to 2,500 species including rarities such as the fen orchid, crested buckler fern, breeding bittern and swallowtail butterflies.

In a scathing assessment of Natural England’s inaction since the Harrises first reported the pollution to it in 2021, the judge said the cause of the pollution was “the most basic level of common sense” and required action by the government’s conservation watchdog.

The Harrises have spent 15 years and £1.5m of their own money taking action to hold to account government agencies for failing to protect the fen, which is part of a special area of conservation (SAC), an internationally important wetland protected by law. The marshes lie within the Broads national park.

“This is bigger than a little ditch in Norfolk,” says Tim. He fears that “regulatory inertia” is preventing pollution from being halted on waterways across the country. “Inertia is prevalent throughout all the regulatory bodies. It’s a landmark judgment because it says they can’t just do nothing.”

The couple are big fans of Dirty Business, the recent Channel 4 dramatisation of a maths professor and a retired detective’s battle against sewage pollution, the water companies and the Environment Agency.

“It’s just like that film when you go to a meeting,” Tim says. “The language is from [George Orwell’s] 1984. What we call the light side doesn’t have the money. The dark side does. That’s where the money is. Of course it is.”

Tim held senior roles in the shipping industry, collecting “the wages of sin”, before turning to environmental campaigning in retirement. “She’s the greenie,” he says of Geli, who grew up near the forests around Heidelberg, Germany, and has always loved the natural world.

They bought their farm, which came with a large chunk of Catfield Fen, in 1994, wanting to live closer to nature. They turned their arable fields into pasture to prevent soils and pollution from washing into the fen, and continue to tend English white park cattle, sheep and rare-breed Eriskay ponies.

“It is beautiful,” Geli says of the fen, which requires a careful rotational cut to preserve its rare plants, invertebrates and birdlife. “It’s a little bit of wildness. It’s mysterious. There are places here where we’ve never been – they are pure nature.”

Tim says: “It’s taken 1,000 years to create this and people could destroy it very quickly.” Geli adds: “If you water your plants with the wrong water, they die quite quickly.”

The Harrises take conservation advice from the experts at Natural England. “We get on well with Natural England, apart from suing them,” Tim says. “They all came to tea and we talked about everything but the thing [the legal case]. It was a very British occasion. They have some very good people. The trouble is too many no longer have fire in their bellies. They are apparatchiks. We respect them but we wish they had more ‘go’.”

Natural England said it accepted the court’s verdict that it had not done enough to protect Catfield Fen but it would not comment on ongoing legal proceedings.

In 2016, the couple won a public inquiry that demonstrated that too much groundwater was being taken from the valley by water companies and local farmers, causing Catfield Fen to dry out. They won a second battle in 2022, forcing the Environment Agency to limit the abstraction of water close to other nature-rich sites on the Norfolk Broads.

Their principles have cost them local friendships. They say National Farmers’ Union reps rejected their application for membership of the local branch, saying half their members would resign, with farmers upset at restrictions on their abstraction of water to boost lucrative crops such as potatoes. “We’re farmers, we’re not anti-farmer,” Tim says.

Since their 2022 legal victory, the Harrises have noticed an improvement in the water levels and biodiversity of Catfield Fen’s dykes. Geli says: “We are a Noah’s ark until things get better and nature can spread outwards from here again.”

The fen and its wildlife may be in a better condition under their stewardship, but they are not confident that nature is winning. “The evidence this summer was that the insect numbers are going south, according to the experts who survey here,” Tim says. “We are not complacent; we are very worried.”

The judge is to make further rulings on what Natural England must do to stop the pollution of Catfield Fen. For the Harrises, the answer is simple: arable farmers must be paid to put in buffer strips of grassland between their ploughed fields and the ditches leading to the Broads to reduce the nitrate-filled soils washing from arable fields into the fen.

More widely, Tim argues that regulatory bodies must simply put their resources into monitoring and compliance. He fears most breaches of environmental rules go unnoticed because of a lack of both.

The Harrises say they do not want to spend their time campaigning. “I don’t want any of this, believe me,” Tim says, gesturing at the box files that take up two walls of shelving. “I’m 79. I want to read books.”

He believes the Broads Authority, the government agency responsible for the national park, should be leading the fight against pollution. Nevertheless, they are considering another legal challenge to stop the Broads from being polluted.

Their stewardship of Catfield Fen will continue after they go. Their daughter will take on the farm, and she is a lawyer by training. So is their granddaughter. “The agencies are not getting rid of the Harrises when we die,” Tim laughs.

Do they hope their example can inspire others to believe they can act to protect nature and clean water? “You need enormous persistence,” Tim says.

“You need passion,” Geli adds. “Evil can happen when people do nothing.”
