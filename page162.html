'Wildwood' Is a Love Letter to All Things Portland, Oregon

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

On Location peels back the curtain on some of your favorite films, television shows, and more. This time, we take a look at Wildwood.

There are several times when Prue, the 13-year-old protagonist of fantasy epic Wildwood, seems just as in disbelief of the magical world she inhabits as us viewers. As someone who grew up in Portland, Oregon, she thinks she's already encountered the limits of weirdness, and yet—as one of the giant animal denizens of the Impassable Wilderness (known by locals as Wildwood) is quick to remind her—there she is, speaking with an owl having tea in a waistcoat. Par for the course for the stop-motion studio, Laika's newest creation is a visual feast with soaring eagles the size of commercial airplanes, ancient trees with roots that sprawl across the whole realm, and even an all-out war between worlds reminiscent of the D-Day Landing in Saving Private Ryan. As much as you could lose yourself in these grand spectacles, the film is also emphatic about remembering the magic that imbues just a single blade of grass. As the first Laika movie explicitly set in Portland, the studio looked for inspiration in their own backyards for how to bring the universe of Colin Meloy's titular novel to life. Condé Nast Traveler spoke with Wildwood director Travis Knight on the inspiration behind this exhilarating, stop-motion saga that takes place not too far from home.

While Dripsters isn't a literal spot in Portland's St. Johns neighborhood, the storefront is a nod to Laika's own in-studio coffee shop.

This movie is such a love letter to Portland, from the Voodoo Doughnuts Prue and Mac eat to the Decembrists posters in her room. This is the first Laika film that’s been explicitly set where the studio calls home. How did that influence the filmmaking process?

It was a joy, unlike anything I've ever experienced. Filmmaking for me is such a privilege to be able to do for a living. To be able to tell a story that was set in the place that I was born, the place that I've lived almost my entire life, the place where our studio is, where so many of us live, was a very special experience because we know that place better than anybody, and we could bring that world to life with real specificity, with real personality. The Portland that you see on screen in Wildwood, while it's a stylized reality because it's animation, very much does represent the truth of what it means to live in that part of the world.

One recurring theme in the film is that magic exists in everyday life, in Portland and in the places we least expect. Is the reverse true as well—in the magical realm of Wildwood, are there fingerprints of Portland and the PNW that the audience can look forward to?

There's magic in real life, and there is danger in the fantasy world—both of those things are true. I think when you look at Wildwood the place, not the movie, there are places from Portland, the Pacific Northwest, and Oregon, specifically all over it. Crater Lake is in there, Haystack Rock is, and there's all these different, beautiful places of Oregon that we infused into this fantasy land, and that was a great joy. We treated Wildwood the place as if it was an “Oregon's greatest hits” fantasyland. The idea of treating Oregon on the same plane as some of the great fantasy realms from my youth like Narnia and Oz and Middle Earth was crazy to even think about. It was great to be able to showcase that beauty on the big screen.

Wildwood has fingerprints from all over the Pacific Northwest in its set designs, from Crater Lake to Haystack Rock.

There’s a lot of well-deserved applause for the entirely hand-crafted approach to this film. There's a brilliant blink-and-you-miss-it moment where Prue and Mac pass by an art fair in St. Johns, and a craftsperson selling their wares has a sign at their table saying to get human-made art “before it's gone for good.” For this film, did you look to Portland’s own craft and visual arts culture for inspiration?

That's part of the way I look at the world, and it's where our community is from. That particular sign is a visual gag, but it's also something that you would see in Portland. I also think it is a meaningful statement that is relevant to the film and to our culture at large. There is a huge maker community in Portland. It's a group of people that actually celebrates handcrafted things, and that really is what's beating at the heart of the movie, that the film is a celebration of human creativity, of human innovation, of the human touch, and in a world that's often designed to be frictionless, easier, and automated. I think the human touch is something that's special and deserves to be celebrated and deserves to be preserved.

Stop motion is such a deliberate practice, where every wrinkle is quite literally sculpted. As a director, are there specific details about a place that you gravitate towards or like to use as launch points?

I think when you're approaching a story, you really want to listen to what the story wants to tell you. When I think about Kubo and the Two Strings, I go back to my own experiences when I was exposed to Japan for the first time. I was eight years old with my father, and it was a world unlike anything that I'd ever seen before. It left a huge impact on me, and those were the types of things that I was channeling. When I was making Bumblebee, I thought back to my experience of being exposed to the Transformers cartoons for the very first time. So you start putting these things through your own prism, and with Wildwood, it was very simple because it was the part of the world that I know better than anywhere. Settings, they have their own personality. Ultimately, what is driving all of these things is the theme and the emotion underneath it, and that is the thing that everything else needs to spiral out of. So, in the end, that's really the thing I think about more than anything else: what is the story trying to say, and what is the emotion that we're trying to convey?

One of the most circulated facts about the film is the 16-year timeline it took to bring it to life. How did Portland change in that time, and what did your team want to keep sight of when characterizing the city?

Portland has changed a lot in 16 years, just like the entire world has changed a lot in 16 years. I first read Colin Molloy's manuscript in 2010. The first movie that we came out with [Coraline] was in 2009, so it was only a year after that that I discovered Wildwood. Portland is a very different place now than it was then. Many, many things have happened since then, some very positive, some tragic. We moved the film into pre-production in 2019, seven years ago. I felt like at the time, we were ready to make Wildwood. Then the whole world turned upside down, and we were hit with a global pandemic the likes of which we had not seen in a century. It changed the whole world, and it changed Portland, and when I see the version of Portland on screen in Wildwood, it's the Portland that I know and the Portland that I love. My hope is that, in time, it can become that again.

“Organic shapes have their own unique grammar,” says Knight, which made the studio's most forested movie to date a tall task to animate.

What were the most challenging sets to construct and animate for Wildwood?

Nothing is easy in stop-motion. Everything is difficult in stop motion. But the things that tend to be more manageable are interiors, like a child's bedroom, but even those have so many details that have to be thought of and designed and built and manipulated. What's difficult to do is nature because organic shapes have their own unique grammar. Things like a forest are really difficult to do in stop motion, and I made the decision to set a movie that was almost entirely based in a forest. It's the materials you use, how you build these things, their shape. There's so much beauty to the natural world that's so different and so strange compared to the inorganic world, the things that we humans construct, and most of the film is set in those places. Places like when Prue and Curtis enter Wildwood for the very first time took us a long time to design what that looked like, and it is just so exquisitely gorgeous. It's so beautiful. It took set designers, set dressers, painters, artists, just so many people who built this space to be a work of art. It's something that belongs in a museum, and for a lot of the movie, that moment will pass by within a matter of seconds. What you're seeing is just extraordinary human craftsmanship and artistry on a grand scale, and it's one of the things I love so much about this movie.

How did you incorporate the wildlife and ecosystems of the Pacific Northwest into this film?

As someone who grew up in the Portland area, I have a lot of knowledge of coyotes. We have a lot of knowledge of crows. We're always next to the natural world. I grew up outside of Portland, essentially on the side of a mountain. I spent most of my childhood days wandering around the forest and climbing up trees and discovering things. The film is emotionally about one primal thing, but there are a lot of layers to the story that we're telling, and part of that is that the film is an eco-fable. That was something that we treated seriously, as it’s something that so many of my collaborators care about deeply, and it’s something I care about deeply. We wanted to try to express that in an organic way without feeling like we were emblazoning it in neon that we're making a message movie. The hope is that you want to honor these things in a way that feels authentic. There's no greater teacher to you than observation, and going out in nature, and seeing how the world was formed, independent of how we’ve tried to shape it. Fortunately, in Oregon, one of the many great gifts of the state is that in any direction you go, you will discover it. We poured so much of that into this movie.

From manuscript to big screen, Wildwood took 16 years to create.

Now that you’re on the other side of creating a movie based on where you call home, do you have any words to the wise for any creatives out there who feel daunted to tell their own stories about home?

What is the piece of advice that they leave out on the old chair-side chest? It was something like writing what you know. I don't think that necessarily means write where you're from, but you have a unique way of looking at the world based on your experiences within it. We know our own lives, unlike anyone else, but that's also the thing that we have to offer that no one else can offer. That's the one thing an artist can do that nobody else can do. They know their own experiences, they own their mind, they know their own imagination, which is different than everybody else's. It's the one thing that we can really offer—how you've lived, how you grew up, the people you grew up with, the people you've interacted with. Every place has its own unique characteristics and its own unique personality. Even within that, our experiences are different from the person next to us. The daunting thing shouldn't be how to tell a story about your home, it's how to really honor the things that you're feeling, how to honor your imagination in a way that really does it justice. Those are all things that we strive to do as artists. We have big dreams, we have big feelings, and how can we get that out in a way that it makes sense to somebody, and it makes them care?

Elon Musk Just Dumped Another $76 Million Into the Midterms

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Elon Musk gave his personal political committee, America PAC, more than $76 million in the first two weeks of October to boost Republicans in the final stretch of the midterms, according to a pre-election report the PAC filed Thursday evening with the Federal Election Commission.

The group spent nearly $43 million in the first two weeks of October, and entered the campaign’s last leg holding nearly $47 million in cash.

The Texas Senate race dominated the new spending. It drew $15.2 million, double what the PAC spent there all summer. That spending included $12.4 million to attack Democratic candidate James Talarico and $2.8 million to promote Republican Ken Paxton.

Daily filings since then show $9.1 million more against Talarico, bringing the PAC’s spending against him this cycle to nearly $28 million, about 40 percent of everything it has spent supporting or opposing candidates. The PAC also shifted toward online ads, spending $11.4 million on digital ads in the same two weeks, which is close to what it spent on mail and texting combined.

All told, Musk has given more than $445 million to Republican political groups this election cycle, according to WIRED’s analysis of FEC filings, making him the biggest known individual donor in the midterms. More than $165 million of that has gone to America PAC since July alone.

Musk—who has several companies, including Tesla and SpaceX, based in Texas—has also been posting online about the race. On Wednesday, Musk posted on X that “there is something deeply wrong with Talarico,” then called him “a creep” and claimed, without evidence, that he “would vote to take away your kids and transition them.” Earlier this month, Musk amplified a false claim that Talarico had been treated at a mental health facility. Talarico shot back the same day: “There is something deeply wrong with a political system that allows one trillionaire to buy his own personal US Senator.”

The Texas race remains a toss-up. An Emerson College poll of 1,000 likely Texas voters, conducted October 17 to 19, found Talarico at 48 percent and Paxton at 46 percent, within the poll’s 3-point margin of error.

The stakes for Musk reach well beyond Texas. If Democrats win the House, the lawmakers in line to lead its committees have signaled they will investigate his role in the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), his companies’ federal contracts, and what Zoe Lofgren, a California Democrat, called an “astounding” number of conflicts of interest. Unified Democratic control of Congress would pose even more problems for him.

Silicon Valley Democrats added another target on Friday: restrictions on SpaceX’s Starshield satellite service in Ukraine. In a letter to the White House demanding an investigation, Representatives Sam Liccardo, Jimmy Panetta, and Ro Khanna vowed that the next Congress “will closely scrutinize federal funding awarded to Mr. Musk’s businesses.”

The 5-Second Rule is scientifically false

Since elementary school, we’ve all been taught the “5-Second Rule.” The idea is simple: Food dropped on the floor is still perfectly edible if retrieved in under five seconds. The logic behind the theory, such as it is, is that germs and dirt and pet dander are somehow caught by surprise by the sudden arrival of food and need a few seconds to get their bearings. If you snatch the food up quick enough, they won’t have time to get it “dirty.”

Even as kids, we were skeptical but admit it–you still roll the dice on it every now and again. It’s like shouting “shotgun” before getting into the passenger seat even if it’s only you and the driver. It’s instinctual. But is it true?

“Let’s go with ‘No,’ there is no truth to the ‘5-second rule’,” says Gillson Sciences Director of Operations Erin Thane. “But I would argue that specifics matter.”

As Thane explains, variables such as comparative wetness of said food and type of flooring can swing things one way or another. “Food dropped on a carpet will likely pick up less contamination than food dropped on a smooth surface with the same microbial loads. Sticky or wet foods will likely pick up more contamination than dry foods dropped on the same contaminated surfaces.”

And that’s not all. “There are actually a lot of nuances to this question,” says Thane. “Is it the floor of Windsor Castle or a public bathroom? Was sticky gum dropped and then put back in the mouth, or was it a goldfish cracker? Am I a healthy adult eating something off the ground or an immunocompromised individual?”

Perhaps more surprising, according to Thane, is that the floors in your home and floors in public spaces actually don’t vary in cleanliness as much as you’d like to think. As Thane puts it, “We all wear the same shoes–or socks hopefully in the home–across the floors we encounter daily, like the kitchen and bathroom, to other areas or spaces. We are constantly picking up hitchhiking germs and depositing them from one floor to the next.”

Specifically skin-inhabiting bacteria such as Staphylococcus (which puts the “Staph” in “Staph Infection”) and Corynebacterium (which can cause diphtheria) to gut bacteria such as Bacteroides. Shoes, clothing, and bags can also carry Bacillus or mold or yeast that can cause nausea and vomiting.

While there are mitigating factors, it’s safe to say that in terms of grossness, the “5-Second Rule” is pretty high up there.

“I would certainly stay away from any wet, moist or sticky foods which act like a sponge and will immediately cling to any and all dirt, debris and contamination that it comes into contact with,” advises Thane. “However, after everything we have discussed so far, I refer back to the best practice or rule of thumb which is to just not eat any food off the floor.”

Four years after North Texas home builder Scott Sherman completed a federal prison sentence for mortgage fraud, his new construction materials business received a $600,000 cash infusion.

The financial boost came from a blind trust set up to prevent Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton and his wife, state Sen. Angela Paxton, from any overlap in their personal investments and their actions as elected officials. But previously unreleased records from the attorney general’s 2023 impeachment investigation show the trust was far from blind and did not shield the Paxtons from its business dealings, including the investment in Sherman’s business.

Before long, Wildcatter Sand Services and Grayson County Sand and Gravel, which Sherman built with his father, grew into a vast enterprise with various locations and subsidiaries. And along the way, some of those businesses stopped paying taxes to local governments, including school districts.

But as their tax debts stacked up, Wildcatter companies wired the Paxtons tens of thousands of dollars in the months leading up to and during the attorney general’s impeachment on charges of corruption and bribery. Paxton was eventually acquitted by the Legislature and is now in an expensive and tight race for a U.S. Senate seat against Democrat James Talarico, a state lawmaker.

While Paxton has been in public office for more than two decades, the blind trust he established upon becoming attorney general has largely obscured his business dealings. The Paxtons’ investment in Wildcatter through that trust offers a new, previously unreported look at both the limits of that trust and the attorney general’s financial association with the very type of business executives whose prosecutions he typically celebrates.

“In Texas, we fight fraud head-on and will not rest until every last corrupt actor is brought to justice,” Paxton said in a news release last year. In a recent social media post linking to a newly released campaign ad, he wrote, “As Attorney General, I’ve made big pharma and fraudsters pay billions.”

Doing business with a recently convicted felon undermines that narrative and erodes public trust in his role as the state’s top anti-fraud watchdog, ethics experts told ProPublica and The Texas Tribune.

Richard Painter, who served as the chief ethics lawyer for President George W. Bush, said he would have thought “the attorney general of Texas would have the good sense not to be dealing with a convicted felon.”

“They’ll pay out their friends while they’re not paying their taxes, run up debt,” said Painter, who has been critical of staunch Paxton ally President Donald Trump. “But the question is, why is the attorney general of Texas dealing with those kinds of people?”

Ken Paxton did not answer detailed questions from the news organizations about the Wildcatter investment or whether he knew of Sherman’s conviction. He has previously defended his business dealings, saying he follows ethics laws. A spokesperson for Angela Paxton, who filed for divorce last year, said that she was not aware of Sherman’s criminal history.

Sherman ran a homebuilding firm, Artisan Operating LP, when he pleaded guilty in 2014 for his role in a mortgage fraud conspiracy. The scheme involved artificially inflating home prices to obtain larger loans for more than two dozen properties in cities across the Dallas-Fort Worth area. Sherman did not respond to calls made to his cellphone and company or to emailed questions.

Paxton’s connection to Sherman is the second revelation in recent weeks of the attorney general engaging with people charged with federal crimes. Last week, The Wall Street Journal reported that Paxton celebrated New Year’s Eve 2024 in Dubai with two Americans who failed to return to the U.S. after being indicted months before on federal healthcare-fraud charges. A spokesperson for Paxton didn’t address his association with the defendants but said in a statement to the Journal that the attorney general has been “politically persecuted for years.”

ProPublica and the Tribune found the Paxtons’ investment in Wildcatter by reviewing records collected by impeachment investigators.

The records show that Ken Paxton had at least some knowledge of the blind trust’s investments, which could violate terms the Paxtons set when establishing the trust.

It would also run afoul of U.S. Senate rules, which Paxton would be bound by if elected, said Donald Kettl, an ethics expert and former dean of the University of Maryland’s School of Public Policy. Those rules, Kettl said, require senators with a blind trust to have no knowledge of its investments.

“It’s reasonable to ask whether a candidate for the U.S. Senate has followed the ethics rules for financial reporting of blind trusts that would be required if the candidate became a senator,” he said.

In setting up the blind trust, the Paxtons agreed that their friend Chip Loper would manage the assets without consulting with them or taking “any instructions regarding investment matters,” according to incorporation documents. Loper did not respond to a request for an interview and did not answer questions about the Paxtons’ involvement in selecting investments.

Text messages that emerged during the impeachment probe, however, showed he and Ken Paxton discussed certain investments. Mortgage and deed records show that Paxton purchased eight properties between 2015 and 2026 and then transferred them into the trust.

This is because sales of combustion-engine cars are falling steeply, a trend that the CPCA has attributed to rising petrol prices following the start of the Hormuz crisis.

Indeed, sales of fuel-only cars were down 31% year-on-year in the first nine months of 2026, with plug-in hybrid sales down 26% – whereas battery EV sales were only down 4%.

One analyst tells Carbon Brief that China’s rapidly growing EV fleet has offset demand for 143m barrels of oil since the start of the Hormuz crisis.

Another says that the crisis is “reinforcing China’s electrification of road transport”.

Meanwhile, amid slowing domestic sales, Chinese carmakers’ exports of NEVs more than doubled [in 2026 to date / in September] from a year earlier.

A record two out of every three cars sold in September in China were NEVs, according to CPCA data, as shown in the figure below.

(NEVs predominantly include battery electric vehicles – BEVs – and plug-in hybrids  – PHEVs. BEVs only run on electricity, whereas PHEVs also have a combustion engine.)

This marks a rapid rise for NEVs from just 6% of new car sales in 2020 up to 58% in 2026 to date – and a record 67% in the month of September.

This year, NEVs’ share of sales has also bounced back from a low of 39% in January, after the full purchase tax exemption that had been in place for more than a decade came to an end in December 2025.

This September, passenger-car retail sales across all categories declined, with overall sales volumes falling 28% year-on-year.

The contraction was led by combustion-engine vehicles, which saw sales fall 43% year-on-year in September and 31% in 2026 to date.

However, sales of PHEVs also fell by 36% year-on-year in September and by 26% in 2026 to date. By comparison, BEVs performed better, although sales still declined 8.5% from a year earlier in September and 4% in 2026 to date.

As a result, NEV sales overall fell by 17% in September and 13% in 2026 to date.

While the market changes in 2026 to date were particularly dramatic, they reflect a broad continuation of trends seen over the past decade.

Analysts differ on what impact this policy shift will have, with some saying it will be limited, while others believe it could cause the price of exported cars to rise.

Les médecins lui ont dit qu'elle était "trop jeune" pour avoir un lichen scléreux.

"Malheureusement, cela arrive aux femmes de tous âges, même dès l'enfance. J'ai trouvé cela très difficile à accepter, mais j'ai toujours su que ce n'était pas normal."

Monica estime qu'il faudrait apporter davantage de soutien aux personnes atteintes de lichen scléreux.

"Même avec un diagnostic, on n'est pas forcément traité comme il le faudrait ; je n'ai même pas droit à un bilan de santé", dit-elle.

"Je gère ça toute seule avec ma crème à base de stéroïdes et j'espère que je fais tout correctement."

Selon le site web du NHS, la cause du lichen scléreux est inconnue. Il pourrait être dû à une réaction du système immunitaire qui attaque et endommage la peau par erreur.

Elle n'est pas contagieuse et ne résulte ni d'une infection ni d'une mauvaise hygiène personnelle. Les frottements ou les lésions cutanées peuvent déclencher ou aggraver l'affection.

Clare Baumhauer, 53 ans, originaire du Kent, a ressenti ses premiers symptômes à l'âge de cinq ans.

"J'avais des démangeaisons et des douleurs, et on me grondait sans cesse ; je n'étais évidemment pas consciente de ce qui se passait."

Ses symptômes se sont aggravés à l'âge adulte, avec des larmes, des ampoules et des plaies.

"Mais comme on ne voit pas d'autres vulves, je n'avais aucune idée que la mienne n'était pas normale. J'avais aussi subi huit frottis cervico-vaginaux pendant cette période, et personne ne m'avait jamais rien dit."

En 2016, Clare a eu une déchirure qui n'a pas guéri.

"J'avais 43 ans cette année-là, et après 38 ans de maladie, je n'en pouvais plus."

Un médecin généraliste a annoncé à Clare qu'elle avait l'herpès et qu'elle devait se rendre dans une clinique de santé sexuelle. Elle dit avoir été "choquée" car elle était mariée depuis 25 ans.

Elle a finalement été orientée vers un spécialiste pour une biopsie et on lui a diagnostiqué un lichen scléreux et un cancer de la vulve.

"Je n'avais jamais entendu parler du lichen scléreux et j'avais un cancer dont je n'avais jamais entendu parler, dans un endroit dont je n'avais jamais entendu parler."

"C’était la première fois que j’entendais le mot vulve, aucun professionnel de la santé ne l’avait mentionné, alors c’était beaucoup à assimiler à ce moment-là."

Clare était en rémission d'un cancer de la vulve depuis sept ans, lorsqu'un examen de contrôle régulier a révélé une récidive et qu'elle a subi une intervention chirurgicale il y a trois ans.

En juin, un autre examen a révélé des cellules précancéreuses et elle a subi une sixième intervention chirurgicale.

Clare pense que si on lui avait diagnostiqué un lichen scléreux plus tôt, son cancer aurait peut-être pu être diagnostiqué avant d'atteindre le stade trois.

"J'ai dû subir deux séances de radiothérapie, ce qui m'a provoqué une ménopause précoce et immédiate, un lymphœdème et des lésions nerveuses."

Monica encourage les femmes qui constatent des changements corporels à tenir un journal.

"Il est vraiment important que nous apprenions à mieux connaître notre corps, à savoir ce qui est normal pour nous aussi au niveau de nos parties intimes, car en réalité, nos vulves changent."

"Si l’on vous ignore, demandez plusieurs avis, insistez car vous n’êtes pas obligé de vivre sans réponses."

Elle a recommandé le guide sur le lichen scléreux, qui contient un schéma et un modèle pour vous aider à parler aux médecins.

Veteranen und der Iran-Krieg – US-Veteran: «Ich werde diesen ersten Typen nie vergessen»

Trump versprach, die USA aus endlosen Kriegen herauszuhalten. Mit dem Angriff auf Iran führt er das Land erneut in einen Krieg. Der Einsatz spaltet seine Anhänger und könnte die Midterms beeinflussen. Veteranen wie Garett Reppenhagen kämpfen bis heute mit den Folgen vergangener Kriege.

«Ich war verdammt gut darin, Menschen zu töten.» Garett Reppenhagen war Scharfschütze im Irak. Heute lebt der Veteran mit seiner Familie im US-Bundesstaat Maine. Doch der Krieg lässt ihn nicht los. Seit seiner Rückkehr kämpft der Veteran mit Suizidgedanken.

Nach den Terroranschlägen vom 11. September 2001 erklärten die USA unter Präsident George W. Bush den «Krieg gegen den Terror». 2003 marschierten die USA gemeinsam mit verbündeten Staaten in den Irak ein. Die US-Regierung begründete den Angriff vor allem mit angeblichen irakischen Massenvernichtungswaffen, die jedoch nie gefunden wurden.

Rund 4500 US-Militärangehörige kamen zwischen 2003 und 2011 ums Leben, mehr als 32'000 wurden verwundet. Auf irakischer Seite waren die Verluste deutlich höher: Die unabhängige Organisation Iraq Body Count dokumentierte für denselben Zeitraum mehr als 114'000 gewaltsam getötete Zivilpersonen. Die tatsächliche Zahl dürfte höher liegen. Ende 2011 zogen die US-Truppen ab. Die Gewalt im Irak dauerte jedoch an.

Ab 2014 kehrten US-Soldaten zurück, um gegen die Terrormiliz Islamischer Staat zu kämpfen.

Auf glückliche Momente folge oft ein tiefes Loch. Garett kämpft immer noch mit dem Gedanken, dass er die guten Dinge im Leben nicht verdient habe. «Menschen, die ich getötet habe, sind tot – und ich darf leben?» Der Veteran hat an der Atlantikküste einen Zufluchtsort gefunden. Seine Familie gibt ihm Halt, und die Gedanken werden weniger.

Reppenhagen erzählt von der ersten Person, die er tötete. «Ich werde diesen ersten Typen nie vergessen.» Der Mann wollte die Strasse überqueren, als Garett mit seinem M4-Gewehr auf ihn schoss. Das Gefühl der Genugtuung überkam ihn. Er erledigte seinen Job, um die «bad guys» zu erschiessen. Heute engagiert sich Reppenhagen als Aktivist gegen den Krieg und geht dafür auf die Strasse.

In den USA leben rund 17.3 Millionen Veteraninnen und Veteranen (2025). Für einige von ihnen hat der Militärdienst langfristige körperliche und psychische Folgen. 2024 nahmen sich 6488 Veteranen das Leben. Das entspricht durchschnittlich knapp 18 Suiziden pro Tag. Auch Obdachlosigkeit ist ein Problem: Im Januar 2025 waren rund 32'500 Veteranen obdachlos, davon lebten etwa 13'500 auf der Strasse oder an anderen Orten ohne Unterkunft. Viele kämpfen zudem mit psychischen Erkrankungen. Laut einer repräsentativen Studie von 2025/2026 waren 14.4 Prozent der Veteranen im Laufe ihres Lebens von einer posttraumatischen Belastungsstörung (PTBS) betroffen. Ursache können Kriegserlebnisse, aber auch andere traumatische Erfahrungen sein.

Als «Friedensstifter» wollte US-Präsident Donald Trump bei seiner zweiten Amtseinführung im Januar 2025 in die Geschichte eingehen. Doch 2026 begannen die USA einen Krieg gegen Iran. Trump begründet die Angriffe unter anderem damit, verhindern zu wollen, dass Iran eine Atomwaffe erhält. Für Garett Reppenhagen klingt das vertraut. Schon den Irakkrieg 2003 hatten die USA mit angeblichen Massenvernichtungswaffen gerechtfertigt, die jedoch nie gefunden wurden. «Sie haben immer einen Vorwand, um uns in Kriege zu treiben», kritisiert der Veteran.

Am 28. Februar 2026 griffen die USA gemeinsam mit Israel den Iran an. Die Angriffe richteten sich unter anderem gegen militärische Einrichtungen, Raketenstellungen und die Führung des Landes. Dabei wurde der oberste Führer Ali Chamenei getötet. Die USA begründeten ihr Vorgehen mit Sicherheitsinteressen und dem Ziel, Irans militärische Fähigkeiten zu schwächen.

Iran reagierte mit Raketen- und Drohnenangriffen auf Israel sowie auf US-Stützpunkte und weitere Ziele in der Golfregion. Der Konflikt weitete sich damit auf mehrere Länder des Nahen Ostens aus. Besonders schwerwiegend waren die Folgen der Blockade beziehungsweise der massiven Einschränkung des Schiffsverkehrs durch die Strasse von Hormus. Sie ist eine zentrale Route für den weltweiten Öl- und Gastransport. Die Energiepreise stiegen deutlich.

Anfang April vereinbarten die USA und der Iran eine Waffenruhe. Im Juni folgte eine vorläufige Vereinbarung, die den Weg zu einer umfassenderen Einigung ebnen sollte. Diese scheiterte jedoch wenige Wochen später. Bis in den Herbst 2026 kam es wiederholt zu militärischen Auseinandersetzungen und diplomatischen Bemühungen.

Bei einem Treffen von Republikanern in einem Vorort von Washington stösst Trumps Vorgehen dagegen grösstenteils auf Unterstützung. Viele sind hier überzeugt, dass die USA durch Iran existenziell bedroht sind. «Ich unterstütze Trumps Kurs vollkommen», sagt Brigitta Mullican, Vorstandsmitglied der Republikaner im Montgomery County. Es gehe dem US-Präsidenten nicht nur ums Öl, sondern auch darum, dass das iranische Regime Menschen töte und seine Macht ausbauen wolle. «Es ist noch kein endloser Krieg», sagt Dennis Melby, Vorsitzender der Republikaner im Montgomery County.

Der Iran-Krieg macht sich auch im Alltag bemerkbar. Vor allem die gestiegenen Benzinpreise belasten viele US‑Haushalte. «Höhere Benzinpreise sind ein kleiner Preis für die Freiheit», sagt ein Teilnehmer des Treffens. Dazu gehöre es, die Welt von einem Übel zu befreien und Amerika sowie den Westen zu schützen.

Der Iran-Krieg ist auch im Wahlkampf vor den Zwischenwahlen am 3. November ein grosses Thema. Die Kritik am Militäreinsatz kommt dabei nicht nur von Trumps politischen Gegnern, sondern auch aus den eigenen Reihen.

Viele Amerikanerinnen und Amerikaner stehen dem Iran-Krieg skeptisch gegenüber. Laut Pew Research Center sind 65 Prozent unzufrieden mit Trumps Kriegsführung. 47 Prozent rechnen mit über einem Jahr Krieg. 73 Prozent sorgen sich über steigende Treibstoffpreise, 71 Prozent über mögliche Verluste unter US-Truppen.

Im Irak-Krieg war sich Veteran Garett Reppenhagen fast sicher, dass er sterben würde. Heute führt er eine Crêperie und Gelateria in einem kleinen Küstendorf. Sein Jahr im Irak war eines der verlustreichsten für die US‑Streitkräfte. Er hat Freunde verloren und viele Menschen getötet. Seine letzte grosse Aufgabe besteht darin, zu lernen, sich selbst zu verzeihen. «Sie bringen dir das Töten bei – aber nicht, wie man damit weiterlebt.»
