Trump's green card curbs test Indian tech workers' American dream

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Indian tech workers are facing growing uncertainty amid US immigration curbs

For millions of Indians, the road to the ultimate career aspiration had a familiar script - an engineering degree to get a tech services job, then a posting in the US, repeated visa renewals and then the green card that gave them permanent residency in the country.

Earlier this month, the US announced it would suspend eight technology firms, including Microsoft, India's Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services, Wipro and HCL Technologies, from hiring skilled international workers on a permanent basis through a widely used visa pathway.

New and pending applications under the Permanent Labor Certification Program, or PERM, have been frozen. This is a big blow to Indians who hold a majority of H-1B visas - a temporary visa for specialists - which they used to work in the US while relying on PERM to get a green card and, ultimately, citizenship.

The latest announcement is in line with Trump's push for stricter skilled-worker immigration rules, which tech firms have been adapting to and, therefore, the impact on businesses may be minimal, experts say.

Nasscom, the trade association of India's $300bn (Â£226bn) technology industry, says that Indian companies have reduced the dependence on H-1B visas and are hiring more locally.

"Consequently, the number of employees transitioning from H-1B visas to permanent residency through the PERM process is also relatively limited," the association adds.

Wipro said in a statement that more than 80% of its workforce in the US is locally hired. Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), said its PERM applications were in single digits in the last two years. Microsoft said in a statement that it has filed for H-1B visas mostly to retain existing staff, not to import new workers.

A report in Business Standard newspaper, citing PERM Tracker, said, TCS' applications filings fell to two in 2025-26 from 513 in the previous year and 1,804 in the year before that. The report said no records were found for Infosys and Wipro for 2025-26.

BBC's email to all eight companies asking questions about the impact of the restriction and how they were supporting affected employees in the US remained unanswered.

Brokerage firm HDFC Securities said the impact on tech firms would be minimal, as PERM-linked jobs make up only a small share of revenue, even if higher-paid American workers replace them.

But the US government's decision has an impact beyond business.

Vice President JD Vance's remarks on H-1B workers added to concerns over US immigration policy

A US-based senior executive with one of these eight firms told the BBC on condition of anonymity that the new PERM restriction will "not break the business", but the impact is more "intense" at a human level.

"A good number of people we know are stuck in a space where they have spent some years in the US but their green card application hasn't moved. Even if they are ready to move back to India, their children don't want to because they grew up here," he said.

Recent policy moves have deepened uncertainty and amplified online hostility towards Indian immigrants and H-1B visa holders.

Researchers at Stop AAPI Hate, a US non-profit that combats anti-Asian and Pacific Islander discrimination, have reported a sharp increase in anti-South Asian online sentiment since the 2024 US election, with Indian immigrants and H-1B visa holders being frequent targets.

While barring the eight tech companies from PERM, US Vice President JD Vance too referred to foreign professionals holding H-1B visas as "foreign indentured servants".

While he later acknowledged that it was an "imperfect" rhetoric, his remark drew sharp criticism from India's foreign ministry which called it "unwarranted" and "deeply offensive" for the colonial connotation.

Poorvi Chothani, founder and managing partner of global immigration law firm LawQuest, said many came to the US for better prospects, borrowing heavily to study and build careers. But as rules tighten now, they have fewer options and less ability to absorb the costs.

Immigration experts believe that as pathways for residency become limited, some may reconsider their future in the US, while others may choose not to move at all.

Ahmet Gulek, Postdoctoral Fellow at Stanford's Immigration Policy Lab, told BBC that despite alternate ways to get residency, not many may be feasible.

While moving to another firm not covered by the suspension is an option, he flags that it's unclear if those firms would be open to hiring immigrants given "the fear of retaliation".

Besides, it's also a tough time to change jobs.

Indians dominate the US employment green card backlog

The technology industry has already been reshaped by layoffs, cost-cutting and the rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI), making career moves tougher.

But there are other routes to a US green card. People with exceptional accomplishments or whose work advances national interests may be eligible for categories that bypass the PERM labour certification process.

Applicants can also qualify for permanent residency by investing at least $800,000 in eligible US enterprises.

Gulek warns some of these categories "may be targeted next."

While Chothani says some are so keen to stay in the US that they are considering the EB-5 investor route but may not have enough capital to invest.

Nearly one million Indian nationals were waiting for employment-based green cards as of December 2025, representing 79% of the total pending applications, according to the National Foundation for American Policy (NFAP).

Their report projects wait times ranging from five to 179 years for highly skilled Indians filing labour certification or employment-based immigration petitions from January 2026, depending on the visa category.

Like other recent immigration measures, the PERM suspension may face federal court challenges. But together, these moves signal a more restrictive climate for those hoping to study, work or settle in the US.

Earlier, the Trump administration imposed a $100,000 fee on certain new H-1B visa petitions, a measure that was later struck down by federal courts. More recently, it has also proposed a $70,000 fee for universities recommending international students for a programme that allows graduates to work in the country after completing their studies.

"This trend is not changing anytime soon," said an Indian professional, who spoke to BBC on condition of anonymity and has transitioned to an H-1B visa recently after a "long wait". He says both the Republican Party and the Democratic Party agree immigration laws need reform, even if they disagree on how.

Some research suggests that restrictions could shift jobs to India rather than to American workers. This is supported by industry players saying that they are already witnessing an increase in companies offshoring work.

"Companies benefit from cost arbitrage when they move work offshore. Post Covid, there is also greater acceptance and appreciation for remote work from clients," the executive from the tech firm earlier quoted says.

Experts believe America's appetite for Indian tech talent is unlikely to disappear. What may be changing is how it taps that talent. For Indian professionals, that could mean rewriting the script about building a future in the US.

White House to Politico: Retract stories or face "consequences"

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The White House counsel's office warned Politico in a Monday letter that it had 48 hours to retract or correct recent stories about alleged staff drama, or it would suffer unspecified consequences.

  • That deadline expires Thursday.

Why it matters: The White House demand threatens to escalate already tense relations with Politico specifically and, more generally, the press corps that covers President Trump in Washington.

  • Politico, CNN, and MS NOW are already banned from covering Trump in the Oval Office and on Air Force One because the president does not like their coverage.
  • Trump blocked all three outlets from the White House grounds as well, but a judge on Oct. 13 restored their access.
  • CNN threatened legal action Wednesday to regain access to Air Force One and the "pool" of television networks that accompany the president.

Zoom in: In his letter to Politico, White House Counsel Will Scharf singled out three stories concerning White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles despite "on-the-record refutations" or contrary evidence supplied by senior staff and even the president.

  1. Oct. 15: In a story headlined "Trump's ice maiden is under fire," Politico Playbook reported that Trump was considering a replacement for Wiles. Trump called it "fake news."
  2. Oct. 16: Politico Playbook wrote that the prior day's story had caused a "freakout" and that the president "blew up" over it. A White House spokesman called it "unbelievably stupid and false."
  3. Oct. 16: In a story headlined "Susie Wiles helped set up Fox interview ahead of press secretary's exit," Politico insinuated the chief of staff set up Katie Zacharia to fail in a Sean Hannity interview on Fox the night before.
  • The White House and Fox News denied the reporting. And White House officials provided text messages to Politico before publication showing Zacharia — not Wiles — set up the interview, which the New York Post reported the following day.
  • "Politico has not corrected or retracted its false reporting. Instead, a Politico reporter confirmed that Politico declined to remove Ms. Wiles from the story because she was the 'hook' for the story — a legally fraught and alarming admission to say the least," Scharf wrote.

What they're saying: Scharf wrote that "freedom of the press is not a professional exemption from accountability. I therefore demand that Politico immediately correct or retract these publications, issue a prominent and public apology to the President of the United States and to Ms. Wiles, and remove the false reporting from your digital archives."

  • Otherwise, he wrote, the White House would conduct an "immediate review of Politico's relationship with the White House, and, of course, consideration of all other appropriate and available legal remedies."
  • A Politico spokesperson did not return a message seeking comment.
  • White House officials fault the new management at Politico for pushing its reporters to engage in what Scharf called "gossip-peddling."
  • The letter was addressed to global editor in chief Jonathan Greenberger.

Zoom out: Trump has had an ongoing love-hate relationship with the press. He is available to reporters for far more question-and-answer sessions than any recent president, but he's quick to anger and threaten press bans for coverage he doesn't like.

  • Early in his second term, Trump banned the Associated Press from the Oval Office and Air Force One access after it balked at calling the Gulf of Mexico the Gulf of America. A federal judge reinstated AP's access months later.
  • The New York Times reported this week that Trump has mused about removing the press entirely from the James S. Brady Press Briefing Room and building a swimming pool there.

What's next: Scharf would not specify what consequences Politico faces, but a White House official said "all options are on the table" and described Trump's counsel as "uncharacteristically enraged."

  • Trump is highly litigious with media organizations and, advisers say, his appetite for launching lawsuits hasn't diminished.
  • Some advisers outside of the Trump White House noticed that administration officials may be telegraphing another avenue to attack Politico as a malign foreign influence because it's owned by a German company, Axel Springer.
  • "Politico is a foreign-op designed to interfere in American politics and meddle in our elections with disinformation and misinformation," White House Communications Director Steven Cheung wrote on X Saturday.

Gina Hinojosa Thinks Texas Is Different This Time

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sign up for our daily newsletter to get the best of The New Yorker in your inbox.

For Democrats of a superstitious or suitably cautious bent, the current cocky talk of a blue wave—a weather system that all at once takes back the House and the Senate and humbles Donald Trump—well, that is almost too much to absorb. Cue the crossing of fingers, the throwing of salt over the shoulder. And yet. In the state of Texas, where many years have passed since the days of Ann Richards, a Democratic governor—let alone since Lyndon Baines Johnson’s dominance—there is, once again, the possibility of a major shift away from the G.O.P. In one of the most closely watched Senate races in the country, James Talarico, a former seminarian, has braved countless conspiracy theories and ugly rumors spit out by the right-wing smear machine and is still leading the Republican Ken Paxton by a few points in the polls. In the state’s gubernatorial race, which is getting far less out-of-state attention, the Democratic candidate, Gina Hinojosa, is within spitting range (to use the technical term) of toppling the Republican incumbent governor, Greg Abbott, who is a vastly stronger politician than the demonstrably hapless Paxton. Hinojosa is angling to be the state’s first Democratic governor since Richards left office, more than three decades ago. After seeing Democratic hopefuls like Beto O’Rourke flare and fade, the Party has every right to be wary of early predictions. But, if both Talarico and Hinojosa were to win in November, it would mark a sharp break in the politics of an immense Southern state and, arguably, a change in national politics.

I spoke with Hinojosa for The New Yorker Radio Hour, earlier this week, and she was careful, it seemed to me, to avoid spending too much time focussing on Donald Trump. To win, she needs to attract at least some Trump voters—people who may have soured on the President but who might not want to be reminded too often of the reasons for their disenchantment. Hinojosa, who is fifty-two and the daughter of Gilberto Hinojosa, the former chairman of the state Democratic Party, was keen to focus on issues—particularly her opposition to the explosion of data centers in the state, the parlous condition of the state’s public schools, and the abuses of ICE and other excesses of the Administration’s immigration policies.

Hinojosa is suing Abbott over the governor’s refusal to release all of his e-mail communications with Elon Musk, who has been a dominant funder of Abbott and the G.O.P. nationally. She has criticized the argument from Abbott’s lawyers that some of the information is private material that might prove “intimate and embarrassing.” Abbott, for his part, calls the suit “nothing more than performative politics.” Musk—the C.E.O. of Tesla and SpaceX, the wealthiest person in the world, and a resident of Starbase, Texas, near the SpaceX launch site—has so far refused to comment on the suit. Early voting began Monday. Our conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

You’ve served five terms in the Texas House of Representatives. You were on the school board in Austin. You’ve worked as a lawyer. What made you want to take on what must have looked like—in the beginning, at least—a quixotic run for governor of Texas, considering that this state has been pretty solidly Republican for a long time?

Having been formed in this state and its politics, I’ve learned that—even when the odds look stacked against you—if you do everything you can to win, you can eke out a win. I’m a mother. That’s what prompted me to even go into politics: I ended up running for the school board because they had tried to shut down my son’s school. And even though I had made my husband promise to never run for office before we got married, I decided to run for the school board to save my son’s school. He was in kindergarten. My experience in the Texas House—being in the minority for so long—taught me that when you give something you believe in everything you’ve got, sometimes the world responds in kind.

Your dad was big in Democratic politics in the state of Texas. I’m old enough to remember when Democrats were pretty dominant in Texas—not just Lyndon Johnson but Ann Richards, Barbara Jordan, all kinds of people. That disappeared. And now you seem to be on the brink of, in your own race, the possibility of winning the governorship, and James Talarico is on the brink of winning a Senate seat in Texas. This would represent a real earthquake. What happened to cause the eclipse, and why are you on the ascent now?

I frankly haven’t been engaged in politics [long enough] to know enough about what happened. I can tell you why we’re on the ascent. I can tell you the reason I didn’t want my husband to run for office is because I grew up in a political family and I didn’t want that for my family. I wanted to escape that.

I knew how to make change happen through politics because I grew up seeing it happen, and that’s how I got where I am, really, by trying to make those changes happen. And it was always public schools that drove my work, and, really, being a mother, and knowing that I had to do everything that was in my power to make this world better for my sons.

The public-school situation all across the country is hardly perfect, but in Texas the situation seems particularly egregious. Give us some sense of where we are with Texas public schools and why your concern level is so through the roof.

Texas public schools are who we are as Texans. They are Friday-night lights. They are the largest employer in so many of our counties. They are enshrined in our Texas constitution as being essential to the preservation of our liberties. And most of us came from Texas public schools.

What we’re witnessing right now under Greg Abbott is really the destruction of Texas public schools. We have over a hundred and fifty school districts operating at four days a week because they can’t afford to operate at five days a week. We’ve had schools shut down—close to two hundred, if not more. We’ve had a teacher exodus because Greg Abbot has run them out. He has deprioritized professional teachers in the classroom, and he has a plan in Houston to convert a hundred public schools into A.I. schools, which is modelled after a private-school program co-founded by MacKenzie Price—one of his biggest donors—who’s given him over a million dollars in campaign contributions. It’s a model that replaces teachers with education guides and has kids learning on screens. And then Abbot passed a billion-dollar voucher scam last year. He put millions of dollars into rural primaries to try to unseat Republicans who voted with Democrats against vouchers. That’s never happened before. We’ve never had a sitting governor of Texas go after members from his own party, but he did, and he successfully beat them in their primaries and got pro-voucher candidates elected.

Governor Abbott has said that Democrats like you either don’t understand school-voucher legislation or they lie about it. What would you say to a voter who’s all in on school vouchers, and supports the idea of school choice, as it’s called?

Well, I wouldn’t call it school choice. It’s the school’s choice, right? The school gets to decide who it accepts. It’s not the family who gets to decide which school they can go to. Vouchers—where they have happened across this country—have been shown to cause learning loss on par with pandemic learning loss. It’s that significant.

What we’ve seen across the country and what we’ve seen in Texas is that [switching schools] has not been a realistic option for families trying to leave public schools—it’s really been an option for families who have their kids in private school already, and now want government subsidies to pay for that tuition. And so, look, my position on this comes from the fact that the vast, vast majority of the 5.4 million schoolkids in Texas really only have one choice and it is their neighborhood public school. And so that should be the best choice. Our schools are lagging considerably—and they’re worse off than they were twelve years ago, before Greg Abbott became governor.

I think some outsiders would say that James Talarico is ahead in the polls not least because he’s facing a pretty inept opponent. How would you assess your own opponent? He’s ahead in some polls, and you’re ahead in other polls—it seems very, very tight. In terms of the qualities of Abbott and Paxton, I think I’d rather be running against Paxton. So where do you find difficulty running against the current governor?

The current governor is deeply unpopular in Texas. People now have seen the in-our-face corruption. He’s given nearly a billion dollars in no-bid contracts to his donors. I just filed a lawsuit this week, because he’s refused to release e-mails between himself and Elon Musk. Elon Musk has given, just to Greg Abbott’s campaign, a half million dollars. He has received 1.7 billion in property-tax breaks, and tens of millions of dollars in just cash grants. Over the summer, we learned that Greg Abbott intervened to redirect a hundred-million-dollar broadband contract to Elon Musk’s business.

Data centers have emerged as an in-our-face physical manifestation of that reality. And there’s no bigger pushback against data centers than MAGA. These data centers have descended into rural Texas. We have more data centers than any state in the nation. So it’s created a real opportunity [for Democrats]. That and public schools, again, have created this opportunity for Texans to set aside partisanship and evaluate a candidate on that candidate’s own merits. I think at a time when Texans are frustrated, frankly, with both parties, there is an opportunity to forge a new way forward that is kind of free of that partisanship.

What have you been hearing about Donald Trump, who’s still the standard-bearer of the Republican Party, that you weren’t hearing when he was winning the state again and again and again? How has opinion changed?

People who supported Trump are deeply disappointed. The corruption, frankly, I think, is the thing that is most upsetting to Texans, because Texans understand that corruption comes at a cost, and it comes at a cost to our pocketbooks. And, at a time when the cost of everything is so high, we just can’t afford to pay that cost. Trump is underwater in polling in Texas. Abbott hasn’t broken fifty per cent all year. People just want change. This is an election in a change year. People don’t want politics as usual. Nobody wants the status quo, and that just creates this opportunity for a Democrat in Texas.

How has the state changed demographically, or otherwise, in the last decade or more to help the Democrats in some way?

The Latino vote has grown tremendously. We saw that the Latino vote went for Trump last time.

There’s no guarantee Latinos will vote for Democrats, but Latinos are voting for a candidate who is good for their pocketbooks, a candidate who facilitates an environment for prosperity. And in this high-cost economy, what we’re hearing on the ground across the state is that Latinos are angry and want change.

But they’re angry for economic reasons, not because of immigration policy.

Both. It’s both. Yes, absolutely, because of the high-cost economy, but it’s also because we are a deeply patriotic people, and we have racial profiling happening in Texas. We had two young men on their way to enlist in the Marines in South Texas, where I’m from, be pulled over and beaten by ICE. We’ve had Latinos who are undocumented killed by ICE in Texas. We had an American [killed]: the first ICE killing under Trump in this term happened in South Texas. So there is anger; there is fear. Many of us come from mixed-status families. My own mother was born in Mexico. My grandmother never became a citizen, and so these ICE raids are literally hitting home. And while we all want border security, strong border security, these ICE raids are making us less secure, not more secure. And most people in Texas don’t believe we should be treating people who have been in our communities, who are part of our communities and have been for twenty years, who are law-abiding, hardworking members of our communities—we shouldn’t be treating them like somebody who’s just crossed the border and should be thoroughly vetted, right? There should be a path to legalization that doesn’t currently exist for so many members of our families.

Tell me this: Greg Abbott is outspending you by a tremendous rate. How can you overcome that imbalance—what I assume is a pretty significant imbalance—in terms of money, with just a couple of weeks to go?

We don’t have to spend as much as Greg Abbott does, because what you do with campaign cash is you tell people who you are and what you’re about. And that’s actually his problem. People know who he is, what he is about. His name I.D. is something like ninety-eight per cent of Texans, right?

And mine now is probably hovering around eighty. When I launched, it was probably under twenty per cent, and we’ve spent this year working hard, reaching Texas voters, crisscrossing the state several times over. And so we’ve made up that gap. That’s why the polling has gone from having us nine to ten points down when I launched to having me—now we got six polls in five weeks showing me beating Greg Abbott. But we haven’t had a poll in the last two months that had me outside the margin of error. So, you’re right, it is neck and neck, and it’s all about turnout.

You tried to get Abbott to debate you. And, as far as I understand, he refused. I watched this fascinating video of you debating an empty podium, which is not the first time I’ve seen that covering politics. Did that have any impact?

I don’t know if it did except to tell Texans, “Listen, this man won’t debate me. He’s fearful of something. He’s running from his record. He’s running from voters.” If he were actually telling the truth in the millions of dollars of negative ads against me, he would have the courage to debate me, but he knows his ads are lies, and that’s why he’s refusing to debate.

I’m not sure he does know his ads are lies, as you put it, and there must be some reason that Texans have some loyalty to Abbott. Why do you think that is? What is his appeal? When you think about it intellectually or from a distance, what is Abbott’s persistent appeal to Texans at this late date, even if some of the trends that you are talking about, whether it’s school closures or rising prices, are, in fact, true?

Well, I don’t know if it’s “trends.” It’s his record, right?

Greg Abbott has changed. He is not the candidate who originally ran for governor. He was viewed at that time as, I guess you would call it, a moderate Republican. What we have seen lately—there’s a saying at the Capitol, and the saying is, “The lights are off at the governor’s mansion.” People have a real sense: nobody’s working. Our leaders are just doing politics. They’re not actually working to solve our very difficult challenges in this state, and Texans have felt left behind.

What’s your relationship with him personally?

With Greg Abbott, no, I never have. He doesn’t really have relationships with legislators. He’s a pretty isolated governor.

Texas has four of the ten biggest cities in America. California has only two. New York has my own humble city. How does the urban-rural divide play out in a state like Texas electorally, politically, culturally?

Well, Texas is a country, right? Our size and diversity is like that of a nation, and it has been the source of political and partisan division. Democrats have wondered for years, how do we do better in rural Texas, for instance? And, really, it is this environment we’re operating in—where data centers and public schools loom so large in rural Texas—that is helping Democrats and helping me to be given a chance in this race by Republicans in rural Texas.

I guess what I’m asking is, I can imagine how you might speak to and appeal to certain kinds of voters in Houston and Dallas and San Antonio, et cetera. How do you reach rural conservatives in Texas? What’s the language you use there? What’s your ability to connect there?

It’s the same language, frankly. The way I describe my politics is populism, economic populism, and that is the same message that motivates urban voters versus rural voters, right? People want a leader, a governor who will work to make their lives easier. Nobody in 2026 in Texas has the luxury of fighting culture wars. We’ve been doing that for ten years, and we’re all worse off in Texas. The only winners of the culture wars are fifteen billionaire tech dudes. And, by the way, you should know that populism has its roots in Texas. L.B.J.’s grandfather was part of the Farmers’ Alliance, which organized against big railroads, and, in much the same way, it is this power imbalance we’re experiencing in Texas between the people—which is ninety-nine per cent of us—and tech giants. At this moment in time, it is the same message that has appeal across party lines.

You know, as an editor, I was just reading a piece that one of our reporters did in the state of Alaska. And the report he’s written is basically telling me that we may think we know something about Alaska, but that Senate race there is all about—wait for it—king salmon and the disappearance of that fish. And it means that much economically and culturally to those, what is it, seven hundred and fifty thousand Alaskans. What is it that we in New York, or California or Chicago, who are interested from a distance about what’s going on in Texas politics, are missing? What is really at the center of the political conversation in Texas?

It is a fear of a future where we don’t have control. Young people in Texas fear [losing] opportunities in light of A.I. advancements. I come across young people who can’t even apply for a job because they are blocked by an A.I. interview system, and they’re trying to figure out how to hack the algorithm to get their application through to be able to be considered for a job. There is economic frustration and anger, and anxiety about the future—and about the present, frankly. I’m coming across Texans who are having to donate plasma two to three times a week to have enough money to pay their bills, right? Middle-class Texans, teachers.

They’re donating plasma, yes, to be able to make ends meet.

Are you in agreement with somebody like Bernie Sanders, who thinks there should be a moratorium where A.I. and data centers are concerned?

My position is there should be a moratorium until we can pass laws to protect Texans. What [A.I.] is not for is to replace human beings. It is not to replace teachers. It is not to replace nurses, right? It should not be making life-and-death decisions. It should not be blocking opportunities for jobs. I think it’s so interesting that we had these A.I. giants say, “We’re going to all agree to slow down our technology, the work that we are doing, for fear that we might break the world.” And then they were sued for collusion. And the reason they were sued is because that’s not the way it’s supposed to work. The way it’s supposed to work is that we’re supposed to have elected representatives who create that legal framework and those legal guardrails to protect Americans, and that is not happening. And that is the frustration.

I was in Amarillo, the panhandle of Texas, where they are building the world’s largest data center. It’s called Project Matador. It is on state land. The biggest investors are former Governor Rick Perry and his son. They negotiated a deal with the city council to get their water for the largest data center in the world from the municipal water source in an area that struggles with drought pretty regularly. So when I say there are no protections right now in Texas—there’s nothing.

I want to dig into immigration again. Greg Abbott might be most memorable for our listeners for his plan to send more than a hundred thousand migrants to sanctuary cities. You remember that well. The United States Just Got a New National Park For the First Time in Six Years

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The US national park list is growing for the first time in nearly six years. Chiricahua National Monument, located in southeastern Arizona, was officially designated as the 64th national park in the US on October 20, 2026 following President Trump's signature on the Chiricahua National Park Act.

The new park lies just over an hour from the Arizona–New Mexico border and is nicknamed the “Wonderland of Rocks” for its towering rock spires and unusual formations, the result of a volcanic eruption about 27 million years ago.

Chiricahua was originally established as a national monument in 1924, and today boasts more than 17 miles of hiking trails for exploring on foot, as well as the Bonita Canyon Scenic Drive, a picturesque road that winds through rock formations.

For outdoors lovers, the designation is big news. The last US landmark to receive a new national park designation was West Virginia’s New River Gorge National Park & Preserve in 2020, home to one of the oldest rivers in the world and the deepest and longest river gorge in the Appalachians.

Chiricahua joins three existing national parks in Arizona: The Grand Canyon National Park in the north, Petrified Forest National Park in the northeast, and Saguaro National Park just under two hours west of Chiricahua near Tucson. If you're planning a trip to the newest US national park (or perhaps all four Arizona parks), here's what to know.

Chiricahua National Park is famous for its hoodoos and balancing rocks.

Chiricahua National Park is incredibly biodiverse thanks to its unique location in the Chiricahua Mountains between the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts, an environment known as a “sky island.”

The park spans roughly 12,000 acres of grasslands, desert scrub, riparian, and pine woodlands, which means there is a huge variety of wildlife and habitat types to see and explore. Aside from the almost 200 species of birds, there are also over 70 species of mammals, 46 species of reptiles, and 8 species of amphibians. Keep an eye out for everything from banded rock rattlesnakes to cactus wrens, whitetail deer, and white-nosed coatis.

While immersing themselves in nature, visitors can also experience the areas's rich cultural history; the park is the ancestral home of the Chiricahua Apache and has ties to prehistoric Indigenous communities, Buffalo Soldiers, ranchers, and the Civilian Conservation Corps.

Visitors can experience the best of the park's offerings by taking the scenic drive to Massai Point, Echo Canyon, and Sugarloaf; hiking the 17 miles of wilderness trails through the rocky landscape; exploring the Faraway Ranch historic district, home to Swedish immigrants in the late 1800s; or stargazing in the area (did we mention it's an international dark sky park too?).

Because Chiricahua was already protected as a national monument, not much will change on the ground besides the new name (and updated signage). Guests can still camp in the park overnight, enjoy scenic drives through the volcanic landscape, go on hikes, and spot plenty of wildlife. For now, parking and the entrance fee to Chiricahua remain free, as they did before the new designation.

The largest impact will come from the increased attention that national park status will bring to the area. Based on a report done in 2018 by independent nonprofit research group Headwaters Economics, in reference to White Sands National Park's change in designation from a national monument in 2019, national parks often see higher visitation rates than national monuments, resulting in increased spending per visitor, and more overnight stays. That means visitors can anticipate higher tourism rates in and surrounding Chiricahua National Park in the near future—which could also lead to more crowds, traffic, and increased development in nearby towns.

Like many national parks, Chiricahua is remote, and doesn't have an overwhelming amount of accommodation options nearby. The nearest city to Chiricahua National Park is Willcox, Arizona, which is roughly 30–45 minutes away by car. Tucson (where guests from farther afoot will likely fly into) is about two hours away. There are a few smaller cities and towns—like Bisbee, Douglas, Portal, and Bowie—scattered within 100 miles of the park, but expect the drive from your accommodations to the park to be on the longer side. For those who want to sleep under the stars, you can camp directly in the park at the Bonita Canyon Campground.

Scoop: Senate Republicans fear Mississippi is in play

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Senate Republicans are weighing three last-ditch spending moves that would have been unimaginable six months ago: a cash infusion for Mississippi and even more money for South Carolina and Kansas.

Why it matters: It's been 94 years since Kansas elected a Democratic senator, 44 years for Mississippi and 28 for South Carolina. But panic is spreading through the Republican Party.

  • "The wall has broken. The enemy is inside the fort," one national Republican political operative told Axios. "We are now fighting hand to hand."
  • The Senate map, which once looked impregnable for Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-S.D.), is now full of vulnerabilities.
  • Democratic challengers are polling within the margin of error in more than half a dozen deep-red states, according to internal GOP polls.

The other side: "We're nervous about Alaska. We're nervous about Michigan. We're still going to hold the Senate at the end," said another operative.

Zoom in: Two weeks ago, when Senate Leadership Fund decided to take out a $5 million "insurance policy" in South Carolina, operatives took a hard look at Idaho, South Dakota and Mississippi.

  • At the time, they concluded they didn't need to spend any money in those states, according to a person familiar with the matter.
  • But Senate Republicans are now reviewing the Mississippi race and may need to spend there to protect Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith from a surprisingly competitive bid by Democratic district attorney Scott Colom.
  • Senate Majority PAC, the main super PAC for Senate Democrats, announced a $1.2 million investment in the race on Tuesday.

Zoom out: In early June, SLF started to reinforce a Midwestern red wall to protect the GOP majority, reserving $28 million in TV advertising in Ohio and $11 million in Iowa.

  • Those investments have ballooned to $93 million in Ohio and $53 million in Iowa.
  • After an initial $5 million buy in South Carolina, SLF's spending has increased to $8.5 million, according to AdImpact. And a super PAC linked to Sen. John Barrasso (R-Wyo.) is spending another $500,000 in South Carolina.
  • SLF, which is linked to Senate GOP leadership, has earmarked $14 million for Kansas, and has spent nearly $12 million there so far.
  • "No Republican seat is safe this cycle," another operative told Axios.

What we're hearing: Some GOP Senate candidates do, in fact, appear safe this cycle.

  • Republicans aren't overly concerned about Sens. Ashley Moody in Florida or Mike Rounds in South Dakota.

The intrigue: The National Republican Senatorial Committee raised $31.4 million last month, but its coffers appear to be drained. It took out a $20 million loan as it spends aggressively on its coordinated campaign strategy.

  • SLF ended September with $155 million in cash on hand, according to its FEC filing.
  • The group has spent or reserved $169 million worth of video ads since Oct. 1, according to AdImpact.
  • Even President Trump promised to unload another $100 million before Election Day in a Tuesday post on Truth Social, bringing the total from his super PACs to $400 million.

The bottom line: Republicans can still maintain their Senate majority, but none of the polling is giving them a great deal of confidence that they will do so.
