On television, ads for UCLA Health show up behind the Dodgers’ home plate and in the background when Justin Herbert, the Chargers’ quarterback, speaks after a game.

At the Lakers’ training facility, the health system’s logo appears on the court and throughout the building, under a marketing partnership that cost the university tens of millions of dollars.

The ads are part of the public university health system’s strategy to increase the number of Southern Californians using its providers at its fast-growing number of clinics and hospitals.

University officials say its expansion to five hospitals and 300 clinics — up from 150 locations a decade ago — has given more Southern Californians access to its academic medicine.

Last year, the system’s clinics and hospitals recorded 4.2 million outpatient visits — more than five times the number in 2015.

“As a public institution, we’re trying to make sure that patients within our community have access to care,” said Dr. Robert Cherry, UCLA Health’s chief medical and quality officer. “That’s the primary and overriding concern, because there’s an acute demand for our services.”

Yet healthcare economists point to repeated studies showing that hospital consolidation — where health systems expand, take over competitors and hire more of a region’s physicians — has become a leading driver of escalating health costs and insurance premiums, while doing little to raise the quality of care.

Glenn Melnick, a USC professor of healthcare finance, has documented how hospital consolidation in Northern California caused health costs to escalate. He said the mergers and acquisitions have continued, including with UCLA Health and other hospital systems in Southern California. UCI Health acquired four community hospitals in 2024.

One consequence, Melnick said, is surging insurance premiums for employers, which leave companies with little money for wage increases, while employees end up paying more for their health insurance and out-of-pocket medical costs.

Employers are bracing for what could be the highest rise in health insurance premiums in 16 years, driving up the cost of family coverage in California to more than $30,000.

“We’re now, I think, at a breaking point,” he said.

UCLA executives said the aim of the expansion was not to increase revenues the health system receives from commercial insurers. And they say they don’t believe UCLA Health is large enough to influence health costs.

“We actually represent only a small fraction of the inpatient capacity, physicians, clinics and resources available across the large and complex healthcare market in L.A.,” said Johnese Spisso, UCLA Health’s president, in an interview.

Nonetheless, the system’s expansion and its other recent financial decisions have led to protests by some of the university’s own staff and faculty, who point out the new clinics have been added mostly in wealthier neighborhoods, including Malibu and Montecito, where the majority of families have commercial insurance.

None of its clinics are in South or East Los Angeles, where more residents are covered by Medi-Cal, the state program for low-income people.

University of California system officials say Medi-Cal’s reimbursements are so low that they don’t cover the cost of care at the state’s five UC medical centers. Those losses, they say, must be covered by charges to those with commercial insurance.

Last month, dozens of medical residents and interns protested outside a UC Regents board meeting after UCLA Health failed to renew a Medi-Cal contract that had covered thousands of low-income patients. The doctors waved signs with slogans “Corporate Greed” and “Keep UCLA Health public.”

Dr. David Schriger, UCLA professor emeritus of emergency medicine, said that given the mission of the state’s public university, he questioned why the expansion had not included neighborhoods such as South Central L.A.

“The public should be making these decisions,” Schriger said. “It should not be at the whim of some executives.”

Thousands of low-income patients, some desperately ill, are scrambling to find new doctors as they lose access to UCLA Health physicians after an eight-year Medi-Cal contract was not renewed.

Santiago Munoz, UCLA Health’s chief strategy officer, said officials don’t consider neighborhood income levels when deciding where to open clinics. Instead, he said, the system has been adding clinics along freeways running north and south.

“We’re staying close to the 405 and the 101 because driving to locations in Los Angeles is inevitable, and it’s very difficult to get to Westwood,” he said.

Munoz noted that UCLA had recently leased a 20,000-square-foot space for a clinic in Inglewood, has locations in downtown Los Angeles, and provides physician services at two of L.A. County’s public hospitals.

“We are trying to go to where patients are,” Munoz said.

Nurses at the university’s flagship hospital Ronald Reagan UCLA Medical Center in Westwood say the expansion has created crowding that has caused what they describe as an ongoing, daily crisis in the emergency room.

Patients coming from the 300 clinics for hip replacements, heart surgeries and other procedures at Ronald Reagan often wait one or two days in the emergency department until beds become available upstairs in the hospital, according to UCLA officials.

To accommodate those patients who’ve been admitted but don’t have a room, the hospital has installed dozens of beds in what was once the emergency department’s waiting room and in adjacent spaces. That has forced people waiting for emergency care into a large white tent outside, which was put up during the COVID-19 pandemic.

“How do you do a good exam in a tent? It just doesn’t work,” said Dianne Sposito, a nurse who works in the emergency department. “I feel patients are in grave danger right now. Accidents are bound to happen.”

A university spokesperson said the system has “extensive strategies to keep patients safe and decompress our emergency departments.”

At the September Regents committee meeting, Spisso, UCLA Health’s president, told board members that the system needs to add as many as another 400 hospital beds so that newly admitted patients no longer have to wait.

For months, rumors have circulated that UCLA will soon buy Providence St. John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, which would make the university the only hospital operator in that city. UCLA bought the city’s other hospital in 1995.

UCLA officials declined to say whether they were talking about acquiring the hospital. “We’ve found it’s best not to talk about discussions we may or may not be having unless or until we have something to announce,” Phil Hampton, a university spokesperson, said in a statement.

The California Health Care Foundation found in a 2021 study that hospital markets were reaching “monopoly levels” in many of the state’s counties. The consolidation was increasing prices with “little to no improvement in quality” of care, the report said.

The study also detailed how physicians often change their referral and prescribing patterns after their practices are acquired by hospitals, sometimes requesting imaging or other procedures that are not needed.

Melnick, the USC professor, noted that when UCLA acquires physician practices and “re-designates them as hospital-outpatient departments,” the clinics can begin billing insurers for an additional “hospital facility fee” for routine office visits, imaging and minor procedures.

He added that insurers often consider UCLA a “must-have” network, allowing it to negotiate higher reimbursement rates. When the insurer folds UCLA’s new hospitals and clinics into their plan, he said, it often agrees to raise rates at the newly acquired providers to match what UCLA is receiving as an academic system. Patients get the same care, he said, “but there is a new name on the door.”

Munoz said that UCLA Health was adding facility fees to some bills, including for imaging, but not for physician care. “We’re running a fairly lean operation in the community when it comes to physician services and not collecting the facility fees that others are,” he said.

UCLA is not the only hospital system expanding in the L.A. region. For example, Cedars Sinai has also been adding clinics and buying hospitals.

Price data show that insurers and patients often pay more for care at UCLA Health and Cedars.

Anthem, Southern California’s largest commercial insurer, this year is paying $20,265 to UCLA for a total knee replacement at Ronald Reagan, according to data gathered by Serif Health, a San Francisco firm that collects data from hospitals and insurers. That price is 9% higher than last year and doesn’t include physician fees, the implanted device or anesthesia.

Anthem pays Cedars more than twice as much for a knee replacement, according to Serif’s data, while it pays Providence St. John’s 22% less. Cedars’ current negotiated reimbursement is $42,559 — an increase of almost 17% over last year.

Kaiser Permanente, the nation’s largest non-profit health group, has veered from its charitable mission and is now scarcely distinguishable from a corporation keenly focused on its bottom line, according to critics.

University executives said that because UCLA Health is an academic health system it provides a high volume of complex care that is more costly than care at non-academic hospitals.

UCLA’s expansion strategy has been financially successful, according to the health system’s annual report.

Last year, UCLA Health operating revenues grew by nearly 20% to more than $5 billion. After paying for expenses, its operating income was $793 million, a margin of almost 16%. That compares to the median operating margin of 1.5% for 45 academic medical centers across the country, according to a 2024 study by McKinsey & Company.

UCLA officials said the operating margin was not a good comparative measure because it doesn’t include a $496-million transfer to the university’s medical school to cover other operations including the training of physicians.

“Education, research, and clinical care are all part of our mission and our responsibilities to really train that next generation of healthcare professionals,” Spisso said.

The university is now spending nearly $300 million to renovate a 170,000-square-foot building that sits on nine acres near Los Angeles International Airport. Purchased in 2024, the campus’ many offerings will include a sports medicine institute with physical therapy and advanced imaging, as well as an ambulatory surgery center with 10 operating rooms.

The potential closure of the community hospital serving South Los Angeles would be ‘devastating,’ said L.A. County’s Dr. Naman Shah. ‘We would have untold misery from lack of access to care.’

The university also plans to soon open a new 119-bed psychiatric hospital in L.A.’s Mid-Wilshire neighborhood. The facility was once the 204-bed Olympia Medical Center, where most patients were Black, Latino and underinsured. Olympia shut down after the university purchased it in December 2020.

The renovated hospital allows UCLA to relocate and expand the 74-bed Resnick Neuropsychiatric Hospital, which is currently on the fourth floor of Ronald Reagan Medical Center.

“We have patients waiting for mental healthcare so we made a $650-million investment in this new facility,” Spisso said. “We will probably lose around $50 million a year just operating it, but we saw it as one of the most underserved areas.”

UCLA then plans to add 103 beds to Ronald Reagan by renovating its fourth floor, increasing the medical center’s capacity by 23% to ease the overcrowding.

In 2024, the university bought the 260-bed West Hills Hospital and Medical Center in the San Fernando Valley from HCA Healthcare.

Last month, Spisso told the Regents committee that the hospital, now known as UCLA West Valley Medical Center, and its surrounding clinics, were making more money than officials had expected.

"L’enjeu, c’est de convaincre les acteurs publics de nous aider" : une plateforme sur mesure et inédite en France pourrait voir le jour en Ariège pour préserver la mobilité des personnes âgées

Article rédigé par Clémentine Rivière

l'essentiel Et si, demain, trouver un trajet en Couserans devenait (presque) aussi simple que de chercher un produit en ligne ? À Seix, en Ariège, le Centre de ressources territorial (CRT) travaille sur une plateforme qui rassemblerait taxis, transports solidaires, sanitaires ou à la demande pour aider les personnes âgées à rester mobiles, même sans voiture. La Dépêche du Midi vous raconte.

À Seix, en Ariège, tout un écosystème s’est peu à peu construit autour d’une idée simple : "Place aux aînés". À la résidence Paul Ane s’est notamment ajouté, en 2024, le Centre de ressources territorial (CRT) du Couserans. Son ambition : permettre aux personnes âgées de rester chez elles le plus longtemps possible.

Au quotidien, le CRT accompagne les personnes isolées, soutient les aidants et organise des actions de prévention. Il intervient aussi dans des besoins beaucoup plus concrets : permettre à une personne âgée ou à mobilité réduite d’aller faire ses courses, de rejoindre la maison de santé ou simplement de se rendre au marché en navette. Mais une question revient régulièrement : que faire quand on n’a pas de voiture ou qu'on ne peut plus prendre le volant ?

Depuis un an et demi, douze ateliers mobilité ont été organisés avec la Région pour tenter d’y répondre. Première étape : passer au crible l’offre existante dans les 94 communes du Couserans. Deuxième étape, désormais : comprendre les besoins. Une enquête lancée cet été jusqu'au 31 octobre et ouverte aux plus de 60 ans comme au grand public, doit permettre de les cartographier. Car derrière ces ateliers se dessine une idée assez ambitieuse : créer une plateforme mobilité "PASS'AGES" qui réunirait, au même endroit, les plus vulnérables qui cherchent un trajet et ceux qui peuvent en proposer.

Demain, un infirmier libéral, une secrétaire de mairie ou un autre professionnel pourrait ainsi renseigner la demande d’une personne âgée ou à mobilité réduite sur cette plateforme : où elle habite, où elle doit aller, pour quel motif et avec quelles éventuelles difficultés. L’objectif, la première année, serait de lui trouver une solution en moins de 48 heures. Dans un second temps, la plateforme pourrait être directement ouverte aux aidants et aux personnes âgées qui disposent d’un accès numérique.

Transport sanitaire, transport solidaire, transport à la demande… Tout ce qui roule déjà sur le territoire serait ainsi recensé au même endroit, pour constituer en quelque sorte un "catalogue des transports", explique Aurélie Fleury, chargée du déploiement et de la communication du CRT du Couserans.

Le projet commence à prendre forme. Une maquette est en cours de préparation. Reste désormais à trouver les financements : environ 380 000 euros seraient nécessaires pour la première année, dont 300 000 euros d’investissement et 80 000 euros de fonctionnement annuel.

"Sans les acteurs publics, les privés ne viennent pas. L’enjeu, maintenant, c’est de les convaincre de nous aider un petit peu", souligne Aurélie Fleury. Derrière le projet, une conviction : "Si on est capable de prouver l’impact social fort de cette solution, alors derrière, on aura beaucoup plus de facilité à la valoriser." Le CRT estime qu’au moins 400 personnes âgées et à mobilité réduite dans le Couserans pourraient voir leur mobilité améliorée dès la première année.

HAMOUD, Jordan, Oct 25 - When gunmen opened fire on a church in Jordan, Sahar al-Halsa hid behind a pillar as she heard the shooting and people screaming in panic during a funeral service for her cousin's daughter.

When she rushed outside in the chaos, she said she spotted the assailants and another cousin lying on the ground wounded.

"I went to him and said: 'Assem, Assem, my darling, answer me'," she said.

She said he opened his eyes to look at her, but then "he was gone".

Friday's attack on the Greek Orthodox place of worship in Karak governorate, about 120 km (75 miles) southwest of the capital Amman, prompted King Abdullah, a US partner in the fight against Islamist militant groups, to order security forces to respond with an "iron fist" to what authorities described as extremist terrorism.

Nine people died in the shooting that witnesses said lasted for several minutes. The funeral services were held on Sunday.

Eight of the victims’ coffins were wrapped in Jordanian flags before they were buried in a graveyard in the village of Hamoud, where the attack took place. Mourners and priests gathered at the scene.

Forty people were wounded in the rare attack on minority Christians.

Speaking from her home, from where the red dome of St George's church is visible, al-Halsa concluded that God must have saved her.

"My life starts now, because I was going to die," she said, speaking beside her husband Ziad al-Halsa. A statue of Jesus on a cross was displayed on a shelf.

Authorities have said they are still searching for the attackers, adding it was too early to determine whether they had links to known groups inside or outside Jordan.

There has been no immediate claim of responsibility.

Ziad said he ran the short distance to the church after he heard the gunfire, describing the overwhelming sense of relief when he realised Sahar, who he described as "the love of his life", was safe.

"To my cousins, may God have mercy on them," he said.

Such attacks are highly unusual in Jordan, a relatively stable state in the Middle East, though authorities there say they have foiled several Islamist plots in recent years.

Speaking ahead of the funerals, priest Yuhanna Hamati said:

"Our message from this church, from this place where this horrific crime was committed, is a message of love, peace, and reassurance. A message that we send to the whole world." REUTERS

How the Romans Invented Apartment Living. It Was Brilliant, Brutal, and Often Deadly

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Home → Features → History and Humanities → History

Long before modern cities built upward, Rome learned how to stack human lives.

In ancient Rome, relief from rent often came only after one’s death.

On a tombstone outside the city, an ex-slave named Ancarenus Nothus left behind what later scholars called “The Tenant’s Lament.” His epitaph reads: “My body knows no longer hunger . . . now it is no longer [paying] deposit on the rent, but enjoys for free an eternal lodging.”

Ancarenus had a hard life, but he chose to put a funny twist on it. His last words to the world are, however, strikingly familiar to most of us modern urbanites.

For most people in the capital of the ancient world, Rome did not mean marble villas, garden courtyards, and reclining couches. That was reserved for the 0.1%. Their living meant crammed quarters, noise, rent due, smoke rising from below, chamber pots above, and walls that might crack while the landlord assured everyone that all was well.

The Romans built one of the first societies of mass urban housing. Their apartment blocks, known as insulae, helped make Rome and its port city Ostia dense, walkable, commercially alive, and socially mixed.

They also made everyday life precarious in ways that feel startlingly modern.

The Latin word insula literally means “island.” In the Roman city, the name made sense. These buildings often filled entire blocks, with streets flowing around them like water.

The form varied, but the basic pattern looks familiar. Shops opened onto the street at ground level. Above them rose rented rooms and apartments. Stairs led upward to reach the flats. The farther up you climbed, the cheaper and worse life usually became.

That inverted the logic of the modern penthouse. Before elevators, height meant lots of sweat, all sorts of dangers, and raw inconvenience. The rich preferred the lower floors, where access came easily and escape remained possible. The poor climbed toward the roof.

Most tenants lived in tiny single rooms called cellae, meaning “small room” but also “cellar”. Entire families often shared this cramped space. In others, especially in Ostia, apartments could be spacious, well-lit, and arranged around courtyards. Some had several rooms. Some even borrowed features from elite houses, such as reception spaces and decorative mosaics.

In other words, the insula was not just one thing. It could be a cramped tenement, a mixed-use commercial block, or an upper-middle-class apartment building. Rome had its slums. It also had its prime real estate.

The scale could be astonishing. By late antiquity, catalogues listed more than 44,000 insulae in Rome, compared with fewer than 2,000 private houses.

The villa may dominate our imagination of Roman life, but it was the mass housing apartment that ordinary Romans — as many as one million by the 2nd century CE — called home.

Rome faced a problem that modern cities know well: people kept coming.

Migrants arrived for work, food, patronage, trade, and proximity to power. The free monthly grain (Cura annonae) starting with Augustus’ reign made Rome even more attractive to migrants. The city grew to a scale Europe would not see again until London in the 19th century. But Rome had no railways, no steel frames, no elevators, no modern plumbing, and no Portland cement.

Faced with such an incoming flux of people from all corners of their vast empire, the Romans saw it only logical to build upward.

In his new book, Dwelling on Earth: The Past and Future of the Places We Call Home, architect and urban planner Sten Al quotes the Roman architect Vitruvius, who wrote: “With the present importance of the city and the unlimited numbers of its population, it is necessary to increase the number of dwelling-places indefinitely.”

“The case has made it necessary to find relief by making the buildings high,” Vitruvius wrote.

Like today, Roman concrete formed the bedrock of the insulae. Builders mixed lime with volcanic ash, especially the prized material from around Puteoli and the Bay of Naples. Seneca captured the wonder of it in a line: “The dust at Puteoli becomes stone if it touches water.”

The concrete, which wasn’t properly reinvented until 1824 when English stonemason Joseph Aspdin patented Portland cement, helped the Romans build harbors, baths, amphitheaters, domes, and multistory housing. Brick-faced concrete gave insulae strength, durability, and allowed buildings to raise them fast. S

Marcus Licinius Crassus, one of the richest men in Roman history, understood this better than most. According to Plutarch, he bought slaves trained as architects and builders, then moved in on fire-damaged properties when desperate owners sold cheaply. He acquired buildings at “a trifling price,” rebuilt them, and watched his fortune grow. “In this way the largest part of Rome came into his possession,” Plutarch noted.

The pattern sounds modern because the incentive has not changed much. Where land grows scarce and demand rises, housing becomes not only shelter, but also ripe for speculation. Huge fortunes could be amassed simply by owning a small piece of land. The same surface area could be essentially worthless just a few miles away from Rome in the countryside.

Cicero, the polished statesman, was among the patricians who drew income from rental property. However, he was better with words than with real estate. In one letter, he wrote: “Two of my shops have fallen down and the rest are cracking. Not only the tenants but the very mice have migrated.”

He did not linger in pity though, for he was extremely wealthy from his income as an elite lawyer and orator. “Other people call this a misfortune, I don’t call it even a nuisance.”

The ancient rental market offered tenants little protection. Poor residents often rented month to month, although they could sublet rooms, which helped ease some of the pressure. And legal responsibility could become a maze if something fell from an upper window and injured a passerby.

Roman writers did not romanticize apartment life.

Juvenal, the great poet of urban disgust, imagined Rome as a city barely held together: “We live in a city shored up for the most part with gimcrack stays and props: that’s how our landlords arrest the collapse of their property, papering over great cracks in the ramshackle fabric, reassuring the tenants they can sleep secure, when all the time the building is poised like a house of cards.”

The collapse of decrepit and neglected buildings was not the only danger. Fire haunted the city, always lurking like a spectre since the Eternal City was first founded in the 8th century BCE.

Cooking flames, oil lamps, wooden beams, crowded rooms, narrow streets, and virtually no fire rules made every block vulnerable. Vitruvius hated wattle and daub for that reason.

“As for ‘wattle and daub’ I could wish that it had never been invented. The more it saves in time and gains in space, the greater and the more general is the disaster that it may cause; for it is made to catch fire, like torches.”

Augustus created the vigiles, Rome’s fire brigade, partly because apartment fires had become a public crisis. Nero, after the Great Fire of 64 CE, finally imposed building reforms: wider streets, fire-resistant materials, porches from which fires could be fought, and height limits. Later emperors tightened the rules further.

Juvenal put the terror of the upper-floor tenant with poise: “I prefer to live without fires and midnight panics.”

He continued: “By the time the smoke’s got up to your third floor apartment (and you [are] still asleep), your heroic downstairs neighbour is roaring for water and shifting his bits and pieces to safety. If the alarm goes at ground level, the last to fry will be the attic tenant, way up among the nesting pigeons.”

Apartment life also meant living without privacy. Roman insulae indeed had windows, but not the kind you might imagine. They rarely had glass. Most were open, unglazed holes in the wall fitted with wooden shutters or curtains to block the weather. Glass windows were expensive and mostly limited to the wealthy

Upper-floor units lacked running water. Because ancient Rome did not use mechanical water pumps to push water up multi-story buildings, the distribution of water was limited to the ground. Residents hauled water from public fountains or paid carriers if they could afford it. Cooking at home could be difficult or dangerous, so many people bought prepared food from thermopolia, the Roman fast-food stalls that served hot meals to passersby.

Laundry went out. Bathing often took place elsewhere. Shops, taverns, shrines, baths, and apartments formed one urban ecosystem.

That density made Roman streets lively. It also made them very filthy.

Tenants used chamber pots. Some carried them downstairs. Others couldn’t care less and threw the contents out of the windows. Roman law recognized the problem because victims could claim compensation if falling waste injured them. The legal action known as the actio de deiectis vel effusis (literally, “action for things thrown or poured out”) was designed specifically to protect pedestrians and keep public streets safe.

You did not have to prove who threw the waste. Because it was nearly impossible for a victim on the street to look up and see exactly which window a liquid came from, Roman law held the head of the household (the occupier of the room) automatically responsible, even if a guest or a slave threw it.

Public latrines offered a communal alternative, but not a private one. Users sat side by side, with no partitions. Water carried waste beneath the seats. A shared sponge on a stick served for cleaning. Even Roman sanitation, so impressive for its time in aqueduct and sewer, could be deeply unpleasant.

Noise completed the urban assault. Metalworkers hammered. Bakers shouted. Carts rumbled at night because daytime traffic restrictions pushed deliveries into the dark. Children played in streets all day long. Neighbors argued through thin walls.

If Rome sounds like a nightmare, its port of Ostia was at least a bit more temperate.

At Rome’s port, many insulae survive well enough to show how sophisticated Roman apartment living could become. Brick-faced concrete walls still rise at some sites. Some buildings had names, almost like prestige apartment blocks today.

The House of Diana (named after the ancient Roman goddess of the hunt, the moon, and nature), one of Ostia’s best-known apartment buildings, shows the strength of the Roman urban model. It stood close to the forum, near the main street, and across from food shops. A resident could work, eat, worship, bathe, shop, and socialize within a short walk. Its residents, however, were probably upper class compared to those who languished in less affluent neighbourhoods.

So, not all Roman insulae were worthy of pity. The insula helped create a dense, mixed-use city long before zoning codes and transit-oriented development. Insulae put homes above shops. They supported street life. And they gave ordinary people access to the city’s opportunities, even when those opportunities came wrapped in smoke, exorbitant rent, and all sorts of risks.

However, Ostia’s better-preserved buildings may skew our view because the shoddiest structures disappeared first. Archaeology often preserves the stronger bones of the past.

Even so, Ostia shows that Rome’s apartment experiment had range.

Rome pioneered mass housing because it had no choice.

A huge population pressed against finite land. Transport moved by foot, cart, animal, river, and sea. Workers needed to live near work. Shops needed customers. The state needed to feed, police, count, and control a vast urban population.

The solution was vertical, which proved profitable, dangerous, and perhaps ahead of its time.

The Romans did not solve urban housing. No civilization has. But they saw, earlier than almost anyone, that a great city cannot survive on individual housing.

For Ancarenus Nothus, eternal lodging finally came rent-free. For the billions who now live after him, the price of the city life is paid remarkably the same: one climb, one coin, one sleepless night at a time.

People attend a spontaneous demonstration against the rising cost of living, a week after protests ended in clashes and looting in northern cities, in Milan, Italy October 24, 2026 | Photo Credit: Reuters

Thousands of young people marched on Saturday (October 24, 2026) through the streets of several Italian cities, including Milan, Rome and Turin, as authorities deployed large numbers of police and soldiers in a largely-successful bid to stave off a repeat of the violent clashes and looting that marred similar protests last week.

The demonstration in Milan remained peaceful, but there were brief clashes with police in both Rome and Turin.

In Milan, thousands of young people marched the streets as soldiers were deployed and luxury boutiques were boarded up, but by late afternoon, most shops had opened, Italian media reported.

Premier Giorgia Meloni announced a raft of measures, including sending an additional 500 soldiers to Milan and tougher penalties for minors, who police said made up a large part of the violent protesters last week. In Milan, police detained several minors as a preventive measure, media reported.

While protests in France have claimed most international attention, large demonstrations also rocked many European cities on Saturday (October 24, 2026) from Milan to Rome to Madrid, Lisbon and Tirana.

The protests encompass a broad range of issues from education and corruption to anti-war movements, pensions and housing.

In Milan, huge crowds of mostly young people gathered as night fell, chanting insults to Ms. Meloni and holding aloft banners such as "No to lies of the State".

A group of protesters dumped manure outside Italy's energy regulator and burned a symbolic, oversized electricity bill, images broadcast on local television showed.

The 500 soldiers deployed to Milan came in addition to hundreds already stationed there, supporting about 5,000 police and other security personnel. Authorities used drone surveillance, checks at railway stations, rapid-response units and online monitoring for potential calls to violence.

A mayoral hopeful, Pierfrancesco Majorino, who announced his candidacy on Thursday (October 22, 2026), joined the marchers and remarked how peaceful the day was, according to Italian media. He will contest a centre-left primary in early 2027. Milan's centre-left Mayor Giuseppe Sala, who has held the role since 2016, is not running again.

Many stores, particularly luxury boutiques within Milan's fashion district including Omega, Tiffany and Gucci, boarded up their front windows. The Apple store also shut down and remained shut even as others reopened.

Rioters last weekend smashed their way into stores, targeting sneakers, athletic wear and sporting goods on streets that run off the city's central Duomo square.

Security measures extended beyond the city centre over concerns protesters could move into other neighbourhoods.

The protesters ended their march peacefully on Saturday (October 24, 2026) evening outside the Milan Polytechnic, more than three kilometres (two miles) from the city centre.

Officials said on Saturday (October 24, 2026) they were investigating a 30-year-old freelance journalist said to be the "mastermind" of the newly-formed October 17 movement, which has been the main driver of the recent protests in Italy.

Milan police say last week's violence and looting involved about 2,000 very young protesters, many of them "naturalised, second-generation Italians", alongside foreign nationals. Over two days, police arrested 45 people, including 34 minors.

A group of about 30 far-right activists, described as hooligans linked to Inter Milan soccer club, made a brief appearance chanting anti-immigrant and anti-Muslim slogans but were isolated by police.

Defence Minister Guido Crosetto said the soldiers deployed on Saturday (October 24, 2026) were only recently allowed to use plastic restraint ties and only in areas around train stations, not throughout the city. They are not authorised to carry pepper spray or tasers, he said.

Security was also heightened in Rome, where authorities locked down luxury shopping streets and stepped up controls around the Circus Maximus and Piazza del Popolo.

A small number of protesters tried to force their way into the Colosseum, but were ushered out by guards. Elsewhere, several hundred students demonstrated peacefully to stress they were not rioters.

"I think the images from last week were truly something terrible, especially for the young people, for the students, for the vast majority of the younger generations who want to study ... and do not accept being represented by those who use issues and needs to devastate entire cities," said Riccardo Ponzio, national president of an Italian far-right student association.

A separate anti-war march took place in central Rome. Organisers sought to distance themselves from potential unrest, urging participants to keep their faces uncovered and hands visible.

But it was these marchers who briefly clashed with police near the Piazza della Repubblica, even as organisers were trying to hold the crowd back using batons and tear gas. In the end, the protesters pulled back when a water cannon was turned on. They retreated to a location about half a kilometre away.

In Albania's capital Tirana, riot police used water cannons on large groups of protesters, some of whom set government buildings on fire and threw projectiles at police. Authorities said 2,500 officers patrolled the streets on Saturday (October 24, 2026).

For over a week, protesters have hurled rocks, flares, firebombs and fireworks at police who have used batons and tear gas in clashes in the city centre.

High school and university students are demanding lower tuition fees, greater state investment in education and protesting corruption in the education system. Some have joined a broader anti-government movement calling for the resignation of Socialist Prime Minister Edi Rama.

A separate protest organised by the opposition outside the Prime Minister's office turned violent earlier, with some activists lighting fires on the street.

Mr. Rama said on Saturday (October 24, 2026) his government was preparing a response to the students' demands and called them to a dialogue. He warned that violent protesters will face penalties.

Thousands of retirees and others marched peacefully in Spain's capital on Saturday (October 24, 2026) to demand public pensions are not meddled with.

"They are threatening us with privatising pensions. It is just another thing they want to profit from," said Maria José Benito, 68.

The protests came ahead of next month's snap election as Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's Socialist Party criticised the conservative opposition Popular Party for making overtures about privatising public pensions. Spain also has been roiled by protests this fall around the issue of housing, as rental and home costs have risen in recent years beyond what many Spaniards can afford.

Taking their cue from Spaniards, protesters in Lisbon demanding affordable housing marched across the city on Saturday (October 24, 2026).

They have also targeted recent measures announced by the government of Prime Minister Luis Montenegro that include easing the cap on yearly rent increases and facilitating the eviction of tenants who fail to pay rent.

Montenegro defends the measures, saying that they aim at reinforcing trust between tenants and landlords and boosting the rentals market. Protesters claim that the measures are not balanced and put tenants in a more vulnerable position.

An Israeli Army vehicle passes near the closed entrance of Hebron, in the Israeli-occupied West Bank. | Photo Credit: Reuters

Israel said on Sunday (October 25, 2026) all land crossings with the West Bank will be closed on the day of next week's election, as it pledged a harder line on militancy after Palestinian gunmen killed two soldiers in the territory.

The announcement came a day after Israeli forces pushed into the occupied West Bank city of Hebron and displaced around 2,000 residents, with Defence Minister Israel Katz saying, "they will not be allowed to return... until we've finished dealing with all the Hamas issues in Hebron".

Hamas, while not claiming credit, hailed Friday's (October 23) killings at a checkpoint near the Palestinian city, in which attackers shot dead two Israeli soldiers and wounded three more.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu vowed to exact a heavy price for the attack just days before polls and said he was planning an operation on a scale not seen since Israel first occupied the West Bank in 1967.

The Israeli Army said that, from Sunday (October 25), Palestinians would "not be permitted to enter Israeli communities in Judea and Samaria and the Jordan Valley for work", using terms many in Israel use to refer to the West Bank.

The Army also said it would enforce "a ban on the entry and exit of Palestinian workers throughout Judea and Samaria and the Jordan Valley, including industrial zones, as well as the closure of all land crossings throughout Election Day" on October 27, it added.

Israel's longest serving Prime Minister Netanyahu is facing a tight race for a new term in Tuesday's (October 27) polls, with his security-first reputation all but shattered by Hamas's October 7, 2023 attack.

The Palestinian Authority, the semi-autonomous body that governs parts of the West Bank in cooperation with Israel, called for international pressure on Israel to protect residents affected by the military operation.

The Israeli Army said that over Friday (October 23) and Saturday (October 24), it operated in 40 areas throughout the territory, with locals reporting that soldiers announced evacuations over loudspeakers late at night.

Israeli forces said they "apprehended approximately 40 suspects, including terrorists affiliated with the Hamas and Islamic Jihad terrorist organisations".

In the Hebron neighbourhood of Ein Deir Baha, nestled between two hills, vacated buildings were marked by X's and the streets were deserted, save a pair of Israeli military vehicles.

Israeli flags were draped on a house on the slope apparently taken over by troops.

Higazi Shawer al-Tamimi, 70, a shopkeeper who plays a leadership role in the neighbourhood, said he was awakened at 1:00 a.m. by soldiers and a senior officer who said he had to leave.

"I asked them how long the evacuation would last. They said: 'Until further notice.'"

He told AFP he had only a short time to pack, adding that for some residents, getting out of the neighbourhood was an ordeal.

"For example, my wife's mother, who is 90 years old and lives in the area, had to be carried out because she is ill," he said.

Right-wing Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who also holds a position in the defence ministry overseeing civilian affairs in the West Bank, said he personally ordered "the immediate demolition of a row of illegal structures" following Friday's (October 23) shooting.

"Anyone who messes with us will quickly understand that he will pay a painful price," he wrote on X.

To Mr. Tamimi, the West Bank operation is only happening because Mr. Netanyahu wants to win the election on "the blood of the Palestinian people".

He said people were not going anywhere. "I have lived through all the stages of the occupation, and I will die here."

But Mr. Tamimi's brother, Khaled Shawer al-Tamimi, 68, said he and his son left after the evacuation order rang out.

"The Army was announcing through loudspeakers: 'Leave the area.' So we left."

He told AFP he was surprised to hear Friday's (October 23) assailants came from the neighbourhood, which he described as middle-class and law-abiding.

"We have never seen or even heard a single gunshot being fired," he said from his son's sitting room as children flitted about.

The Israeli military said the operation would last several days, but Mr. Netanyahu suggested bigger action was coming.
