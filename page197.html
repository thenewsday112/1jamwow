Borneo burning: Life on the frontline of South East Asia's toxic haze

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Firefighters have been battling the stubborn blaze for weeks now

When the sun hits the smog enveloping Indonesian Borneo, it turns everything the colour of rust. Cars, houses and streets appear and disappear out of a dim russet fog. There are few better words than "apocalyptic" to describe the way this looks.

But it's at night, in the quiet darkness, that mother-of-three Dianyra Eka Nastiti grows most fearful.

"Sometimes at night my baby's chest makes a rattling sound," she says. "She was hospitalised for bronchopneumonia when she was just a month and a half old, and every single night I worry: Will my child cough tomorrow?

Nastiti lives in Palangkaraya, at the frontline of a months-long environmental catastrophe that has blanketed large parts of Southeast Asia in a toxic haze.

Wildfires have torched some 1.9 million hectares across Indonesia since the start of the year, according to open-access satellite platform Nusantara Atlas â an area roughly the size of the US state of New Jersey, or the entire landmass of Fiji. One million hectares are thought to have burned in September alone.

But the toxic fallout is larger still. Swathes of smouldering forest, swamp and peatlands have turned the air thick with microparticles and carbon monoxide, creating a noxious cloud that has swaddled Indonesia and drifted as far as Singapore, Malaysia and the Philippines.

As of mid-October, Indonesia's Health Ministry had recorded more than 277,000 cases of people suffering from chronic coughs and acute respiratory infections. More than 62,000 of those were infants and toddlers under the age of five.

"The forest fires have had a huge impact, especially on everyone's health," says 22-year-old Natari Victoria, who was admitted to a health centre in Palangkarya after suffering from a cough for more than a week.

"I have young cousins, including babies, who are coughing too. It's really taking a toll on them."

Dianyra Eka Nastiti is worried about how her children will recover

Haze and smoke from peat fires contain a complex mixture of pollutants that pose serious health risks to the human body.

Among these are tiny microparticles which can penetrate deep into the lungs and, in some cases, enter the bloodstream, causing inflammation, chronic lung diseases and increased risk of premature death.

Another concern is the toxic greenhouse gases that are being unleashed into the air.

"Here in Central Kalimantan, especially in Palangkaraya, the sky has turned yellow and hazy," says Riduan, head of the Palangkaraya Health Office. "So what are the residents actually breathing in? It's not fresh oxygen; it's carbon dioxide and pollutants. And what happens as a result? The body âÂ specifically the brain â doesn't get enough oxygen."

Children, older adults and pregnant women are particularly vulnerable to the dangers of haze, especially during prolonged periods of exposure, when the health impacts can accumulate.

In places like Palangkarya, the official advice is to stay indoors and avoid going out. But Nastiti says this is unrealistic.

"I still need to buy vegetables, stock up on food, purchase school supplies, and take care of other necessities," she explains. "It's simply not possible, and I feel like the people who say things like that have no idea what is actually happening here."

Nastiti's utmost priority, however, is the health and wellbeing of her children. And when the haze grows heavy, she is quick to force them inside.

"With conditions being so unsafe, I have no choice," she says. "Our young children should be at the park, playing with their friends and going to school. Instead, they are locked inside the house."

Lola Macitri, a 37-year-old who lives in the rural village of Ramang, 50km (31 miles) north of Palangkaraya, has been similarly housebound. As the smog has deepened over the past few weeks â from dusty grey to amber yellow and, more recently, burnt orange â she and her family have come to spend most of their time indoors.

Her children, like many others in Indonesia's more haze-affected areas, have been doing their school classes online, she says, while she and other adults struggle to manage half-a-day's work out in the poisonous air.

People have been turning up at hospitals after complaining of feeling sick from the toxic smog

Countless farmers, daily-wage earners and small vendors have similarly been forced to either sacrifice their livelihoods or endure the risk of stepping outside. Beyond that, many are left with no choice but to wait out the storm.

"If you ask whether we are afraid of the health risks, of course we are. But where else can we go?" says Macitri. "We just have to endure and stay strong through this situation, because there is simply no other option."

The government is under mounting pressure from the public to do more to stop the fires, with many criticising what they see as a woefully inadequate response by the authorities.

Authorities insist they have pursued a range of measures to try to extinguish the flames and, in turn, the smoke â from aerial waterbombing to experimental weather modification. Last week, the Indonesian government said it had deployed more than 60,000 personnel to carry out a "land operation", claiming they'd achieved an 80% success rate in extinguishing new fires.

"At this point, I think we need to use every safe and effective option available to suppress the fires," says Dr Nisa Novita, a forestry expert and peatland researcher at Indonesian conservation NGO Yayasan Konservasi Alam Nusantara (YKAN).

"It's a bit like trying to save a sinking ship: we need to act urgently, use whatever tools can help, and prevent further damage while we still can."

But those resources are fast depleting for responders on the ground.

For more than 100 days, Juni has been fighting wildfires that show only false signs of abating. On the surface, the flames are out âÂ but underground, smouldering blazes continue to burn through a deep understory of carbon, vegetation and peat.

"It is truly exhausting," he says, describing how the relentless smoke has produced in him a shortness of breath, a persistent cough and an unshakeable pain in his eyes. When asked what he needs, he says "air".

"The haze makes it hard to breathe," he explains. "We are just hoping for heavy rain."

Wildfires have been devastating Indonesia's Central Kalimantan region

Thousands of others across Indonesia and Southeast Asia have spent the past several months hoping for the same.

Seasonal wildfires are not new to Indonesia but this year, a so-called Super El Nino has amplified drought conditions and delayed the kind of rainfall that is critical to extinguishing peat fires. It has also made things exponentially harder for those at the coalface, like Juni, who have spent months battling a seemingly irrepressible fire front.

Dr Novita explains that conditions on the ground have become much harsher even in recent weeks, as the prolonged drought makes water increasingly scarce.

Fauzi Rahman, another firefighter in Palangkaraya, highlighted this as one of the main difficulties confronting him and his peers.

"Peatland fires are hard to put out because they require a lot of water and a long time," he says. "On regular land we can clear 20 hectares in a single day, especially with a large team... On peatland, it can take months.

"We really have to inject water deep down, all the way to the bottom, which requires a huge amount of water. And right now, water is hard to find."

What's more concerning for Dr Novita, however, is that her team has reported fires spreading into areas where there has been no history of fire before.

"So we're not only dealing with fires in the usual hotspots but also seeing them emerge in new areas," she says.

In Palangkaraya, people are growing desperate for an end to this apocalypse. But it will likely take a force of nature for that to happen any time soon.

Riduan, head of the Palangkaraya Health Office, tells the BBC that "hopefully, with all the extraordinary efforts from the community, the military, and the police working together, the fires can be put out".

"However, as of right now, the fires are still burning," he adds. "So what can we hope for? Our greatest hope is that Almighty God will bring us rain soon."

House Democrats forced to make tough choices with limited midterm cash

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

House Democrats' main super PAC is winding down its spending in several races where the party has struggled to make headway despite national trends working in their favor.

Why it matters: Democrats' ambitions of winning heavily Republican districts are running into the reality of finite resources at the end of a long and expensive midterm campaign.

  • The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee and its corresponding super PAC, House Majority PAC, have struggled to keep pace with their GOP counterparts' monster fundraising this cycle.
  • Republicans also have the bonus advantage of a network of cash-rich outside groups, namely President Trump's MAGA Inc. and Elon Musk's America PAC.

Driving the news: HMP pulled back its spending this week in three Republican-leaning districts, according to AdImpact and a source familiar with the matter.

TN-05: Democrats were bullish about their star recruit, Columbia Mayor Chaz Molder, until redistricting made the district redder and embattled Rep. Andy Ogles (R-Tenn.) lost his primary to former state Agriculture Commissioner Charlie Hatcher.

  • HMP has drawn down its ad reservations in the district from nearly $1.6 million this month to a little over $500,000 as of Monday.
  • Trump carried the newly reconstituted 5th district by 21 percentage points in 2024. Cook Political Report rates the race as "solidly Republican."

FL-07: Facing a situation similar to Tennessee, Democrats liked their odds of unseating scandal-tarred Rep. Cory Mills (R-Fla.), who lost his primary to former local news anchor Ryan Elijah.

  • Like Molder, Democratic candidate Bale Dalton, a former NASA chief of staff, was a top DCCC recruit and is part of the committee's coveted "Red to Blue" program.
  • HMP essentially halved its investment in the district on Monday, cutting ad reservations from $3.9 million to just $1.95 million.
  • Cook rates the race as "likely Republican," though a poll released by the DCCC on Tuesday had the two candidates tied at 45% each.

CO-03: A top target for Democrats when Rep. Lauren Boebert (R-Colo.) represented it, the seat has become a heavier lift for the party since she abandoned it and Rep. Jeff Hurd (R-Colo.) took office last year.

  • Democrat Dwayne Romero, a real estate company CEO and Army veteran, is seen by party operatives as having failed to build the kind of momentum shown by Jessica Killin in the neighboring 5th District.
  • HMP halved its investment in this district too, from more than $2.1 million last week to less than $1.1 million as of Monday.

Between the lines: The group has also made partial cuts — what it calls "shaves" — in districts where Democrats have struggled to gain traction but still see a glimmer of hope.

  • That includes Arizona's 2nd District, where Rep. Eli Crane (R-Ariz.) is locked in a rematch with former Navajo Nation President Jonathan Nez in a race Cook rates as "likely Republican."
  • HMP has also taken some money out of Texas' 23rd District, where Republican Brandon Herrera, a right-wing YouTuber, is facing Democratic lawyer Katy Padilla Stout in another race rated "likely Republican."

Yes, but: Some HMP triages are in districts where they feel confident the Democratic candidate will prevail.

  • That includes Reps. Greg Landsman (D-Ohio) and Kristen McDonald Rivet (D-Mich.), both of whose races are rated "likely Democratic" by Cook.
  • The PAC has also pulled back in the districts of retiring Reps. Darrell Issa (R-Calif.) and Don Bacon (R-Neb.), which Democrats are increasingly bullish about flipping.

The money from these cuts is funneled to districts the party views as more core battlegrounds — including some seats Trump carried by double digits in 2024 that Democrats nevertheless view as winnable.

  • HMP has added at least $4.7 million to its existing investments in nearly two dozen districts over the last two days.

What they're saying: Phil Gardner, a senior adviser for Blue Dog Action — a centrist group backing Dalton — told Axios of the cuts in Florida, "I'm not sure what HMP was thinking here."

  • "The rest of the party is not triaging this seat," he said, citing the DCCC's poll and House Minority Whip Katherine Clark's (D-Mass.) plans to campaign there on Wednesday.
  • Gardner said he agrees with "95% of the decisions HMP's made this cycle," but that it is "bonkers they're dumping so much money" into Maine's 2nd District "while leaving one of the party's best recruits high and dry in Florida."
  • Blue Dog Action spent nearly $500,000 to help Dalton win the primary, but it hasn't yet invested any money in the general election.

The bottom line: "We're focused on taking back the House," CJ Warnke, a spokesperson for HMP, told Axios in response to Gardner's criticism.

  • Said Gardner: "I'm confident they'll rethink and make the right decision for the final week. Bale can win and hold that seat with everyone's help."

Is Donald Trump ready to do business with Myanmar?

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A top US official recently met with Myanmar leader Min Aung Hlaing for the first time in years

Has Washington's long-standing policy of isolating the military regime in Myanmar now changed?

The revelation that a top official in the Trump administration met its leader Min Aung Hlaing last month, and the removal of some business figures from the US sanctions list, suggests that it has.

Until now, the US had had very little official contact with the regime following the 2021 coup, when Myanmar's military ousted democratically-elected leader Aung San Suu Kyi. More recently, it had applied a series of targeted sanctions against those judged to be supporting the junta.

The meeting on 15 and 16 September was with US Assistant Secretary of State for Intelligence and Research Michael Vance, and it took place in Myanmar's capital Nay Pyi Taw. Its ostensible focus was to secure the release of Adam Castillo, a US citizen who had been detained in June over a dispute with the local American Chamber of Commerce.

But Vance was the most senior US official to meet Min Aung Hlaing since he seized power, and they almost certainly discussed other matters of mutual interest.

Vance is also known to have met Burmese officials in Tokyo in June, again in Tokyo this month, and to have met representatives of the groups opposing military rule.

Signs of a shift in US policy towards Myanmar, though, go back to July last year, at the height of President Donald Trump's tariff war.

That was when Min Aung Hlaing, along with other leaders in the region, received a personal letter from the US president informing him that his country's shattered economy was about to be hit by, in Myanmar's case, 40% tariffs.

Other countries reeled in shock at the punitive levies imposed on their vital trade with a country they had once seen as a friend and ally.

Much of Myanmar has been devastated by a civil war that has been raging since 2021

He replied immediately to express his "sincere appreciation for this encouraging invitation to continue participating in the extraordinary Economy of the United States, the Number One Market in the World", mimicking Trump's hyperbolic phrasing and eccentric capitalisation.

"It is with great esteem that I wish to acknowledge Your Excellency's strong leadership in guiding your country toward national prosperity with the spirit of a true patriot."

This was not just a dictator's hunch that flattery might be the best way to win favours from Trump. Min Aung Hlaing's coup, and the civil war it ignited, had turned him into an international pariah.

His regime was largely unrecognised. He was accused of being a war criminal, with cases lodged against him at the International Criminal Court and the International Court of Justice. Aside from Russia and, more recently, China, he was shunned by other governments.

Yet here was the US president addressing him just like any other president or prime minister, as "Your Excellency. There was no mention in Trump's letter of the appalling human cost of Min Aung Hlaing's power seizure; of a democracy crushed and leader Aung San Suu Kyi jailed.

That same month the regime signed a $3m (Â£2.3m) contract with DCI, a long-standing Washington lobbying firm which had been used by a previous Burmese military regime in 2002 to try to improve its image.

Since then the military government has also hired Roger Stone, a controversial lobbyist and confidant of President Trump, at a fee of $50,000 a month.

Also around the same time Adam Castillo, a former US marine and Trump supporter who had just stepped down as president of the American Chamber of Commerce in Yangon, was in Washington trying to persuade Vice President JD Vance's team that it was time to re-engage.

Castillo believed that the US reliance on sanctions to isolate the junta had failed.

"Myanmar mattered to America not only as a morality play but as a strategic interest," he wrote in his book Finding Our Voice: A Story of Leadership in Crisis and the American Spirit Abroad. He discussed with the vice-president's team how the US "could engage Myanmar without war or isolation, loosen China's grip on critical minerals, and align peace with American interests".

The US dependence on rare earths from China had been exposed when China blocked them in retaliation against Trump's tariffs in April. The administration began prioritising finding alternative sources. Myanmar happens to have large reserves of rare earths which are currently being exploited solely for China's use.

At almost exactly the same time, the state department issued new guidance to US embassies around the world on what to say about elections. From now on, it said, "messages should avoid opining on the fairness or integrity of an electoral process, its legitimacy, or the democratic values of the country in question".

So, when Myanmar held its much-criticised general election in December and January, allowing Min Aung Hlaing to turn himself from coup leader into a notionally civilian president, the US did not, like most Western governments, condemn it as neither free nor fair.

For decades the US has consistently pronounced judgement on other countries' elections. Now the US had nothing to say.

Washington's decision to lift sanctions on some of those targeted by the Biden administration seemed only to confirm the new direction of US-Myanmar policy.

"For years, the US approach toâ¯Myanmar has been defined by isolation, sanctions, and distance," wrote the lobbyists DCI in a recent newsletter. "That era is overâ¦ thankfully, President Trump has the courage to try something different."

The tone is certainly different, but not so much the substance of US-Myanmar relations.

"In practice less has changed than the headlines suggest," says Richard Horsey, Myanmar adviser for the International Crisis Group, adding that Min Aung Hlaing is still sanctioned. "What is new is the level of contact and the location. Senior US officials have now been to Nay Pyi Taw, which is exactly what Min Aung Hlaing wanted. What he wants next is sanctions relief, and that will be harder."

Myanmar's leader Min Aung Hlaing on a recent diplomatic visit to Malaysia

It was another Trump initiative last year, though, which has arguably had a much greater impact on Myanmar: the dismantling of USAID, which slashed aid to opposition groups, dissident media and refugees by more than half.

There was also the decision, decried by human rights groups, to withdraw Temporary Protected Status from 4,000 Burmese who had sought sanctuary in the US. This has left them living in fear of being sent back, where many face being jailed, or worse.

As for the rare earths in Myanmar, few experts believe they will ever be of much use to the US. Most lie in areas contested by ethnic insurgent armies.

They are currently extracted by Chinese companies with long experience of operating in these lawless borderlands, and transported over the border to China for processing. It is difficult to see how the US could get access to them.

It is also doubtful that the US will get meaningful co-operation from the Myanmar government in combating the scam industry.

There have been a few highly-publicised actions against some of the larger scam compounds, but the syndicates running them have simply dispersed to smaller operations in more remote areas, and in any case most are run with the support of militia groups allied to the Myanmar military.

"Talking to Nay Pyi Taw is not wrong in itself," says Horsey.

"The question is what Washington asks for and what it gives away. It should use the channel for the things that matter most: humanitarian access, an end to airstrikes on civilians. It should keep talking to the opposition and the ethnic armed groups, who hold much of the country. And on scams, the most effective thing the US can do is to co-ordinate with other governments in the region, including China, to go after the money."

Perhaps the bigger question, though, is whether President Trump, distracted by domestic politics and the war on Iran, has the capacity to give any sustained attention to Myanmar, let alone enough to challenge the dominant influence of China.

Batalla de los robotaxis: los coches sin conductor de Google y Uber comienzan a circular por Madrid

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Waymo, filial de Alphabet, comenzará a operar en Madrid en los próximos días en modo prueba, algo que ya está haciendo Uber. Ambas, junto a Bolt, prevén lanzar sus servicios sin conductor en 2027.

La batalla por el negocio de los robotaxis echa a rodar en el mercado español. Waymo, filial de Alphabet, matriz de Google, anunció ayer su desembarco oficial en España para operar su servicio de transporte de viajeros sin conductor, tras haber recibido los permisos necesarios en la Comunidad de Madrid, bajo el marco de la Dirección General de Tráfico (DGT).

En las próximas semanas, los llamativos Jaguar I-PACE de Waymo comenzarán a rodar por la capital, aunque con conductor. Estos vehículos van equipados con LiDAR, radares y cámaras para crear mapas 360 de todo lo que ven. El objetivo, a partir de 2027, es que con toda esta información puedan comenzar a circular sin nadie al volante.

En esta primera etapa, Waymo desplegará una pequeña flota inicial -no ha confirmado el número exacto de coches-, que irá ampliando de forma gradual y escalonada, en línea con los requisitos marcados por la DGT.

Las pruebas en vía pública -tanto de día como de noche- se circunscribirán exclusivamente a entornos urbanos reales de la ciudad de Madrid, Alcobendas y Getafe.

“Madrid es una increíble capital de la innovación”, afirmó ayer la co-CEO de Waymo, Tekedra Mawakana. “Adoptaremos un enfoque metódico, por fases, y donde la seguridad será lo primero. Comenzará con la conducción manual antes de avanzar hacia una rigurosa validación técnica con especialistas en vehículos autónomos locales y capacitados al volante”, explicaron ayer desde Waymo. “El objetivo comercial de transporte totalmente autónomo está fijado para 2027”, añadió el grupo.

La empresa aterriza en España en un momento de ebullición para los robotaxis en la capital. Uber ya ha anunciado el inicio de sus viajes de prueba de la mano de WeRide y Bolt también desplegará sus propios coches sin conductor gracias a su alianza con la norteamericana Lucid.

De momento, Waymo no ha anunciado acuerdos con empresas de VTC, aunque por el modelo regulatorio tendrá que hacerlo de cara al futuro. Todo apunta a que su socio más probable en España podría ser Cabify.

No obstante, en esta primera fase, la compañía operará de forma independiente, centrada exclusivamente en la validación técnica y en las pruebas de conducción manual por las vías públicas de Madrid. “De cara a nuestro objetivo de ofrecer los primeros viajes totalmente autónomos en 2027, estamos colaborando activamente con socios operativos locales para respaldar nuestras operaciones. Sin embargo, en este momento no tenemos anuncios sobre acuerdos comerciales específicos ni integraciones concretas con otras plataformas en España”, explicaron.

La llegada de Waymo a Madrid se produce cuando la región se prepara para ser el banco de pruebas de la conducción autónoma en todo el país. La Comunidad de Madrid ha aprobado el despliegue inicial de vehículos autónomos con pruebas que podrían alcanzar hasta los 100 robotaxis en circulación.

Uber ha sido la primera empresa en poner en marcha su servicio en la capital. Desde hace unas semanas, 20 vehículos de la china WeRide -furgonetas modelo GXR- circulan por los barrios de Hortaleza y Arturo Soria con el objetivo de recopilar datos con sus sensores y cámaras y con un operador de seguridad a bordo. Estas serán las zonas por las que los vehículos de Uber funcionen inicialmente, según confirmó la compañía.

Además, antes de que acabe el año, Uber podría poner en marcha de forma comercial sus coches autónomos, aunque en una primera fase los pasajeros viajarían con un operador a bordo. Pasados unos meses, sobre mediados de 2027, los coches de WeRide podrían circular ya sin conductor por Madrid.

En el caso de Bolt, la compañía ha anunciado recientemente una alianza con la estadounidense Lucid para desplegar sus robotaxis en Europa. La compañía aún no da fechas concretas sobre cuándo iniciará las pruebas, pero algunas fuentes apuntan a principios de 2027. El despliegue sin conductor se llevaría a cabo pasados unos meses.

Por su parte, la española Cabify ha sido más tímida. Aunque confirmó que pondrá en marcha coches autónomos en su plataforma, no ha anunciado los partners tecnológicos. Los rumores apuntan a que podría ser Waymo.

La conducción autónoma se convertirá en una realidad en Madrid el próximo año, una vez finalizadas las pruebas, aunque con retos en el horizonte. Además de los aspectos técnicos, aunque Europa avanza en directivas generales de seguridad vial y homologación de sistemas de asistencia a la conducción, no existe aún una legislación unificada y madura que regule la explotación de vehículos completamente autónomos (Nivel 4 y 5) para el transporte público o comercial. El sector del taxi ha pedido, además, que se respeten los marcos establecidos para el transporte a terceros.

“Fatherland” Cleans Up the Rough Edges of History

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Paweł Pawlikowski’s “Fatherland” offers an amiable eighty-two minutes of condensed history, centered on the septuagenarian novelist Thomas Mann’s trip, in the summer of 1949, to Germany—West and East—in order to receive each country’s Goethe Prize on the occasion of that great predecessor’s bicentenary. If Pawlikowski’s aim was to portray Mann (played by Hanns Zischler) as a terminal bore and a pompous ass, he succeeded brilliantly, even if he failed to suggest the reasons for his own animosity. Mann, who has been living in Los Angeles since 1941, is accompanied on the journey by his daughter Erika (Sandra Hüller), who serves as his assistant, even editing his speeches. And he does gives speeches. He gives an impromptu one at a press conference at their first stop, Goethe’s native Frankfurt; he gives a carefully crafted one at the formal prize ceremony there, and he gives another at the East German ceremony in Weimar, Goethe’s longtime home, overtly under Soviet dominion. What’s more, he gives speeches, terse but fusty, in private, to Erika.

For the most part, Mann struts around looking iconic, bearing an air of absurd vanity in his belief that, as an eminent anti-Nazi exile and a Nobel Prize winner who left Germany, in 1933, soon after Hitler came to power, his supra-ideological presence in both Germanies, at a time of Cold War tension, could indeed matter. But Mann most of all strikes an air of formality even in private, even when, in Frankfurt, he gets the grim news of the death of his son Klaus (August Diehl)—Erika’s brother and confidant, who is also a noted author—from a drug overdose. (His death was ruled a suicide.) Most pompous of all are the silences; the most conspicuous of them hollows out one of the major early scenes, when father and daughter, newly arrived in West Germany and travelling by car to Frankfurt, pass through streets of destruction, a country ravaged by war—and remain silent. Yes, awestruck and horrified, no doubt; still, I wondered throughout why silence served Pawlikowski as an optimal mode of expression.

Throughout my viewing, something felt off—in style and in substance—and the first concrete diagnosis came through in my colleague Anthony Lane’s recent New Yorker Profile of Pawlikowski, which I had deliberately delayed reading until after seeing the movie. (History may have no spoilers, but drama does.) That in-depth portrait mentions a fundamental change the filmmaker wrought upon Mann’s real-life 1949 journey: the author was, in fact, accompanied by his wife, Katia, who, in “Fatherland,” is said to be too weak to travel. The character of Erika, a writer and intellectual with her own range of uneasy connections to Germany—she’ll encounter an ex-husband and an ex-lover there—is a screenwriter’s convenience, a walking plot-production machine. Behind closed doors, however, the switch from a spouse to a child, from Mann the husband to Mann the father, is the switch from the intimate to the casual, from uninhibited speech to guardedness.

It took no more than minutes of curiosity to find that the fulcrum of “Fatherland,” the death of Klaus —the first character seen in the movie—occurred not during the Germany trip but some months before it. As for the trip itself, it was far more tumultuous, far more infuriating to Mann, and far more unflattering toward West Germany than the movie lets on. Upon his return to the United States, Mann wrote, for the Times, that, in West Germany, “they live by the slogan: ‘Everything was better under Hitler!’ By virtue of the experiences they claim to have had, they declare triumphantly that democracy has been tried and found wanting. Democracy to them means the Occupation Powers and all who ‘collaborate’ with them.” This isn’t the elegant stuffiness of “Fatherland”; it conjures a Fassbinder-esque film—outspoken, insolent, crude, loud, and brazen. Mann may not have shared his testimony, his vision, his voice with Erika during the tour, but he might have with Katia.

To share that voice in “Fatherland,” Pawlikowski would have to imagine it. But his economy of expression also suggests that the film, which is never at risk of spilling out beyond the confines of its scripted pointedness, depends on an economy of imagination. Its contrivances are less like conveniences or abridgments than like deceptions: the facts and the tone are in service to a style that itself has little room for variety or range. “Fatherland” is a muted, muffled quiet-box of understatement, twisting history less for dramatic effect than for affectlessness. Pawlikowski has developed a felt-lined aesthetic—of black-and-white images, in the traditional nearly square screen dimensions, that leave space around the characters—that bespeaks observational detachment and comes off as culture personified. This forced reserve and mannered dignity create a hermetic atmosphere of refinement (from behind the camera) and of elimination (in front of it). It is a cinema of prefabricated meaning and predetermined moods, in which images limit the very kinds of events that can be depicted.

Here, Pawlikowski further rarefies the style that he developed in “Ida” (2013) and advanced in “Cold War” (2018), but not to advantage. I found myself thinking of other filmmakers who rarefy: Christopher Munch, whose 1991 feature, “The Hours and Times,” about the 1963 Barcelona jaunt of John Lennon and Brian Epstein, has the decency to stick to these characters and not replace Epstein with, say, George Martin; or Hong Sangsoo, whose radical reduction of space and span in his new movie, “The Day She Returns,” creates great cinematic leeway for the uninhibited voice of its actress-protagonist; or Robert Bresson, whose severe restraint served artistically as both microscope and microphone, to experience infinitesimal gestures on a cosmic scale of moment and morality.

In “Fatherland,” such microscopy is reversed. Pawlikowski’s camera reduces the figures and voices, in order to fit them into his own rigidly molded spaces. The characters are petrified like specimens, expressing and embodying only what fits into the film’s design, with no potential for inconvenient loose ends for viewers to follow outside the frame’s borders. It’s a sieve too fine for anything as inconvenient as bodies or as coarse as discourse to get through—an aesthetic of purification, disincarnation, signification, as if the mere fact of keeping voices down and holding thoughts in were itself a mark of urbanity, conferring that cultural virtue upon a dignified audience. The movie sanitizes the past and removes its tang, its pungency, leaving a craving for contact with nature, with raw experience, with incongruities and accidents and anything but what fits, in lieu of such synthetic sophistication. ♦

I had Claude write its own release notes—here are the highlights:

Every skirmish and Daily Op now opens with a choice, gives the side that’s losing a way back, and pulls the armies into the middle of the map. There are also two new ways to play.

Fights run longer and swing more. In 1,200 AI-vs-AI test matches on Normal, games still undecided after 25 minutes fell from 14% to 0.9%. The share of wins by a side that had been 800+ scrap behind rose from 24% to 46%.

Goliaths can be constructed using a Gantry—a building that can only be created after first building a Fab, Bay, Airpad, and Tech Lab. You can see the Goliath under construction, and it then walks out from the Gantry and can march around the map.
