Eurovision faces fresh scrutiny as the Netherlands withdraws over Gaza tensions

📅 Aug 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Netherlands will not participate in next year's Eurovision Song Contest, its broadcaster said on Monday, saying the event can "no longer be considered neutral" given divisions over the Gaza war.

"It is undeniable that international conflicts are increasingly affecting the Contest, undermining its neutral character. The Eurovision Song Contest has therefore become a platform for division," Taco Zimmerman, director-general of AVROTROS, said in a statement from the broadcaster.

The contest has recently been hit by a wave of boycotts over Israel's participation.

Earlier this month, organisers announced they were excluding any country involved in an armed conflict from hosting the competition.

"However, the recently announced changes ... do not provide sufficient confidence that the independent and neutral character of the Eurovision Song Contest has been restored," the AVROTROS statement said.

Israel came close to winning the competition in both 2025 and 2026, ultimately finishing second on each occasion.

Next year's event, the world's biggest live televised music event, will take place on 15 May in Bulgaria's Black Sea city of Burgas.

It will be the first time that the Balkan nation hosts the contest, coming after Bulgarian singer Darina Yotova, known as Dara, won this year's contest in the Austrian capital Vienna with the catchy floor-filler "Bangaranga".

Since its launch in 1955, Eurovision has become the ultimate pop platform, catapulting Swedish icons ABBA to worldwide fame, as well as boosting the likes of Celine Dion, Cliff Richard and Olivia Newton-John.

The 2026 competition was watched by 131 million viewers, down 35 million on the year before after five countries boycotted it over Israel's participation.

The Netherlands, Iceland, Spain, Ireland and Slovenia all pulled out of the contest — with the latter three refusing to broadcast the show at all.

The Dutch took part in the very first Eurovision Song Contest back in 1956, and have won on five occasions.

The most recent winner was Duncan Laurence, whose song Arcade won the contest in 2019, going on to top charts globally.

It became the first Eurovision entry to reach one billion streams on Spotify, according to the Eurovision website.

The withdrawal is "painful as we would have very much liked to be part of the Contest again next year," Zimmerman said.

"As a public broadcaster, we remain committed to our values. They guide us in everything we do, even when that means stepping away from an event that is very close to our hearts," he added.

Martin Green, the director of the Eurovision Song Contest, told the Agence France-Presse news agency the organisation regrets the decision, but understood and respected the choice.

Green said organisers would investigate whether another Dutch broadcaster might be willing to participate in the contest.

"We very much hope that audiences and artists in the Netherlands can continue to be represented in the Eurovision Song Contest," Green said.

AVROTROS selects and manages the Dutch entry to the contest.

'Contaminated': How deadly bird flu crossed a new threshold in Australia

📅 Aug 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

SBS acknowledges the Traditional Custodians of Country and their connections and continuous care for the skies, lands and waterways throughout Australia.

The "devastating" H5N1 variant of avian influenza marked a grim milestone on Sunday as the first confirmed case of transmission between bird and mammal species was recorded, with experts warning the worst is yet to come.

A long-nosed fur seal was confirmed to have died of the disease in coastal South Australia, with a second case being investigated in a seal in The Pages Conservation Park islands near Kangaroo Island on Monday.

Earlier, Environment Minister Murray Watt said the country needed to be "realistic" about the impact this illness will have on native wildlife.

"We are likely to see mass mortalities of birds and mammals, and I know that is a very distressing thing for Australians to hear," Watt told ABC television.

Sean Dooley of the avian conservation group BirdLife Australia told SBS News that the virus could have an ecology-shifting impact.

"Potential extinction from the disease is possible for some birds," he said.

"That's why we need an effective response, where we can, to mitigate the losses.

"We also need a massive increase in the resources that go into ensuring resilient populations of as many of our species, or else we're going to be facing a real ongoing crisis beyond the actual outbreak."

Avian influenza, a flu-type virus, is airborne and contracted by birds and other mammals when they breathe or ingest it from an infected animal.

Robyn Alders, an honorary professor at the Development Policy Centre at Australian National University, told SBS News that the virus also survives outside of a host animal.

"Even if you can't see a diseased carcass or a dead animal, it doesn't mean that the environment there is safe," she said.

"Particularly now that we're starting to see areas where you've got larger die-offs, the potential contamination in that area is going to be significant."

In cooler temperatures where water is present, Alders said there is evidence H5N1 can survive for days.

In areas where the virus has yet to arrive, Dooley recommends people continue spending time in nature and report anything they suspect could be bird flu-related to the emergency animal disease hotline.

"That's still really valuable because it can help slow the spread as they remove those birds which are potential sources of infection," he said.

Cats are particularly susceptible to the disease, and Alders recommends people keep their cats indoors as the virus has a "50 per cent" mortality rate for them.

Bird flu vaccinations are underway in specific, suitable populations at the moment, but experts question whether the government is adequately prepared.

Dooley said the country is in a stronger position now than it was a few years ago, but that government claims to have "done everything they could" are false.

"If you talk to people on the ground ... it's really hit and miss as to whether people have been trained for this, or whether they have the equipment, whether the PPE they need is filtering down.

"There's a lot of people panicking because they don't know what to do and they're not resourced to do it."

Agriculture Minister Julie Collins has said the government is investigating whether bird flu vaccine trials for marine mammals in North America could be used in Australia — but that there is "nothing commercially available" which could protect vulnerable native seals.

While various avian influenza strains already exist in Australia, H5N1 is "so unlike any other bird flu virus we've dealt with", Alders said.

First detected in geese in China in 1996, the strain has flared up in the past but typically burnt out because it is simply too lethal.

The fact the current lineage exists at all is down to human activity, Alders argued, particularly the concentrated nature of poultry farming which creates huge viral reservoirs.

"A virus that kills its host within 48 hours — and that's what it does in chickens — shouldn't survive," she said.

"I don't know where this virus is going, but what I do know is that it was human activity that, in many ways, facilitated this virus to continue and to spread."

She said it's "up to us" to reduce and eliminate the virus.

"Everybody's got to be part of the discussion about how we produce our food, what standards we expect, not only in terms of human welfare but also animal welfare, and really working with agroecological cycles — not thinking that we can cut corners.

'Alarm bells': How Australia ended up back where it started

📅 Aug 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

SBS acknowledges the Traditional Custodians of Country and their connections and continuous care for the skies, lands and waterways throughout Australia.

The number of younger Australians who own their own homes has stagnated, sitting at around the same levels recorded during World War Two, as the cost of living and rents rise.

Fewer than 40 per cent of 25 to 34-year-olds were listed as homeowners in the 2021 Census, and that rate is expected to have fallen since, according to analysis from social welfare charity Anglicare Australia.

This is equivalent to the homeownership rate recorded in the 1940s, before the post-war boom took hold as multiple policies prioritised construction and housing for veterans.

At the same time, home prices and rents have risen, putting pressure on younger Australians to save for a mortgage while living away from home.

A recent report from property data company Cotality has shown that rents in some parts of Australia have soared by 80 per cent over the last five years, since COVID-era rent freezes were lifted.

Anglicare Australia said that support lines for younger people are dominated by stress over housing security and related bills.

"It's almost impossible to have a call from a younger person who doesn’t mention the stress of rents or bills," Anglicare Australia executive director Kasy Chambers told SBS News.

"They are studying, working and contributing. But they are earning less than the generations before them, being locked out of housing, and struggling to build the security that past generations took for granted."

Home ownership has steadily declined across age groups since the 1970s, but younger Australians have felt the sharpest declines, according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics.

Angela Jackson, an economist at the Productivity Commission, the Australian government's independent economic advisory body, said home ownership is particularly hard for lower socioeconomic groups.

"It is a lot more expensive relative to people's incomes to get into the housing market than it was for Gen Z's parents or grandparents," she told SBS News.

"Which does raise alarm bells about broader equity."

Home ownership rates have declined generation on generation in Australia, off the back of the housing boom in the post-war era.

Liz Allen, a demographer and social researcher from ANU, described 1940s Australians as going through "tough times".

"There were issues with inequalities, especially around education, geographic and income and wealth inequality," she said.

"I think it would be fair to say that the inequalities faced in that period and the global shocks during that period surpassed those that we're experiencing now."

Home ownership across all ages was lower than it is today, with younger people aged 25-34 the least likely to own property.

Allen said that the years after World War Two saw the "rhetoric of a fair go", where government made a non-partisan effort to "rebuild Australia".

It was a time of rapid residential construction and increases in home ownership.

"There were policies on multiple fronts to get people to Australia, to get people in Australia in work, in paid work, and to get people established in life with good wellbeing, access to education, homes, and so on," she said.

Allen said that decades of governments have shifted towards short-term policy wins, and "kicked the can" of long-term housing "down the road".

"We're no longer in that mentality of building, but maintaining," she said.

"And that undermines the very notion that we are a growing population, that we have new forms of inequalities emerging, and policy has not kept with societal and economic change."

For many Australians, a home and the equity it generates over time will be their largest source of wealth and a source of security for children who may subsequently inherit it.

But for younger generations who take longer to save and pay off a home, the economic benefits can be diminished.

Jackson explained that the nature of work also makes it harder for Gen Zs to earn like their parents and grandparents.

"Work is more casual and can be more gig work, or less secure, which can make entering into mortgages and those long-term financial arrangements more difficult," she said.

"And then incomes themselves have changed over time. For lower entry-level jobs compared to more experienced jobs, the relative wage is also lower."

Anglicare Australia would like to see policy settings shift to support younger Australians aspiring to home ownership.

"The government has taken an important step by reforming property tax breaks that have favoured accumulated wealth over work. Now we need to build on that progress," Chambers said.

"One of the clearest places to start is Youth Allowance.

"We have a social security system that literally says a young person needs less money to live on simply because of their age. Our findings show they can’t afford rent on Youth Allowance anywhere in the country."

The government recently announced a $20.90 increase to the maximum Youth Allowance payment due to indexation, which will reach $1,087.20 in September. However the rate can be much lower depending on your age, living situation and dependents.

Meanwhile, the median weekly unit rent in Australia is just under $700, putting rentals out of reach for Youth Allowance recipients.

"Rent isn’t cheaper when you’re 23. Groceries aren’t cheaper. Electricity isn’t cheaper. Yet young people are expected to survive on the lowest payment in the system," Chambers added.

The group would also like to see a wealth tax, which could tax assets and reduce the pressure on income-reliant Australians who don’t yet own a home.

You can make this engaging by giving the gummy worm a name and saying he’s stranded on top of the cup and needs his lifejacket.

All you need to test your reaction time is a ruler and a friend. It’s a simple, fun challenge that requires no prep. For older children, you could set them a question to investigate, such as “Can reaction time be improved with practice?”

One of the World's ‘Most Wanted’ Lost Fish Is Rediscovered

📅 Aug 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The recently rediscovered spinach pipefish. SHOAL

On a vast network of rivers flowing through the jungles of Papua New Guinea, researchers have rediscovered the spinach pipefish, a relative of the seahorse, which had not been recorded in more than 40 years. The fish has long been sought after by conservationists, who feared it had gone extinct.

On an expedition that unfolded like a search for a missing person, researchers spent two weeks traversing the Sepik River and its tributaries, scouring villages and markets for evidence of the missing pipefish. They showed its photo to fishers, vendors, and village elders and asked where it might be found.

The task was daunting. The Sepik River extends across 700 miles of forests, floodplains, and wetlands along the northern coast of Papua New Guinea. “Travel is almost entirely by motorized boat or canoe, often taking many hours between villages,” said Jerrica Agebigo of the New Guinea Binatang Research Centre. The vast scale of the river system meant that scientists were heavily reliant on local knowledge.

Helen Asu, a prawn fisher in Imbando Village, who aided in the rediscovery of the long-lost spinach pipefish. New Guinea Binatang Research Centre

In interviews, villagers consistently reported spotting a long, slender fish in the shallows of the Sepik River. Though they called the fish many names — Karuweng, Inap, Karuwena, Sako — their descriptions matched the profile of the missing species.

In the final days of the expedition, a fisher named Helen Asu managed to nab two pipefish using the handheld net she relied on to catch prawns. Researchers shared photos of her catch with Australian ichthyologist Gerald Allen, one of the last researchers to document the spinach pipefish in the 1980s. He confirmed the rediscovery.

“It makes my heart sing to hear that the spinach pipefish has been recorded anew,” said marine biologist Amanda Vincent, who chairs an expert group on seahorses, pipefishes, and seadragons for the International Union for Conservation of Nature. “To learn that the spinach pipefish still exists gives us hope that other ‘lost’ species are out there, too.”

The spinach pipefish had been named one of the 10 “most wanted” lost freshwater fish species by SHOAL, a U.K. conservation group. Its rediscovery highlights the urgency of protecting freshwater fish, the group said.

In interviews throughout the expedition, villagers reported declines of native fish, which are imperiled by mining, logging, and other threats. Globally, freshwater fish are under growing pressure. A recent U.N. report found that over the last half-century, populations of migratory freshwater fish have declined by 81 percent.

The challenge brewing behind the front lines in Ukraine

📅 Aug 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

SBS acknowledges the Traditional Custodians of Country and their connections and continuous care for the skies, lands and waterways throughout Australia.

More than four years after Russia launched the full-scale invasion that has devastated Ukraine and claimed hundreds of thousands of lives, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy is facing a new political challenge: whether to hold elections during wartime.

The issue emerged last week after former defence minister Mykhailo Fedorov, who was recently dismissed from his role, called for Zelenskyy to find a way to hold elections while fighting was ongoing.

The move was seen as the biggest challenge to Zelenskyy's leadership since the invasion began in February 2022, with Zelenskyy rebuffing calls for a wartime election.

"Elections right now are a tsunami for the state, which will split Ukraine," he said on Sunday, local time.

"That's why my strategy is to steer the country toward an ending of the war and to preserve an independent and sovereign state."

National elections have been suspended since the invasion began and polling shows most Ukrainians support delaying a vote until the fighting ends.

Zelenskyy won Ukraine's 2019 presidential election in a landslide and would have ordinarily served a five-year term.

After Russia launched its full-scale invasion, Ukraine introduced martial law, which suspends some civil liberties, including national elections.

Zelenskyy's presidential term expired in 2024, but he remained in office as Ukraine's constitutional and legal system provides for continuity of government during martial law. The country's parliament has also continued operating.

Security remains the biggest obstacle to holding elections.

Fighting continues along a front line stretching more than 1,200km, while Ukrainian cities, including the capital Kyiv, remain under regular bombardment from Russian drones and missiles.

An election would potentially require millions of Ukrainians to travel to polling stations, creating security risks and raising concerns about how voting could be conducted safely.

Political parties would also need to campaign while the country remained under attack, while authorities would have to find a way for hundreds of thousands of soldiers serving on the front line to vote.

As of the end of June, more than 4.4 million Ukrainians had temporary protection status in the European Union, meaning Ukraine would have to organise hundreds of polling stations across Europe.

Nearly four million Ukrainians have also been internally displaced, making voter registration more difficult and potentially diverting resources from the war effort.

Then there are those living under Russian occupation unable to vote. Ukrainian officials estimated that, as of 2024, around 4.5 million adult Ukrainians remained in Russian-occupied territory.

Russia controls about 19 per cent of the Ukrainian territory and plans to ​hold its own elections there next month, which Ukraine has said would be ⁠illegal.

Zelenskyy has previously said a wartime vote might be possible if Ukraine's allies could guarantee the security needed to hold one but no viable plan has been proposed.

The question of wartime elections in Ukraine is not new.

US President Donald Trump last year called Zelenskyy a "dictator without elections", while Russia has repeatedly used the expiry of his presidential term to paint him as an illegitimate leader.

But polls have consistently shown that a majority of Ukrainians oppose holding elections until fighting ends.

Last week, Fedorov, 35, made a shock public call for Ukraine to find a way to hold elections while at war.

"A legal, safe and realistic mechanism that will allow Ukraine to renew its full democratic process even in the conditions of a long war," he said.

The intervention comes amid a broader political crisis for Zelenskyy, who fired the young, popular Fedorov as defence minister last month.

Fedorov became a popular figure as a backer of Ukraine's drone warfare strategy and for arguing for reforms within the military.

Thousands took to the streets to demand his reinstatement. Zelenskyy has offered other roles to Fedorov, but he has so far refused.

Fedorov, a former political ally, did not explicitly state that he wanted to challenge Zelenskyy but he is now seen as a political rival to the president after his ousting.

Ukrainian politician Lesia Vasylenko said it was "unrealistic" to talk about an election while fighting continued.

"It would require a constitutional change," she told Times Radio, something that is a "difficult enough process to orchestrate at peacetime and during the martial law regime, it's virtually impossible".

For now, there is little sign that Ukraine is preparing to hold an election, but the longer the war drags on, the louder the calls will become.

130,000 or 230,000? The migration figures One Nation is trying to explain

📅 Aug 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

SBS acknowledges the Traditional Custodians of Country and their connections and continuous care for the skies, lands and waterways throughout Australia.

One Nation MP Barnaby Joyce has faced questions over his party's immigration target, claiming fellow MP David Farley misspoke when he revealed their figures were "not too different" from Labor's.

The MP for Farrer told ABC's Insiders program on Sunday that One Nation's stated figure of 130,000 net overseas migration annually into Australia did not include those who enter under a Pacific labour scheme, aged-care nurses, and other in-demand skilled workers.

He conceded that those groups would add roughly 100,000 people, bringing their total to 230,000 — 5,000 fewer than the current Labor budget projections.

Party leader Pauline Hanson intervened just hours later, writing on social media that One Nation's policy is to reduce net overseas migration (NOM) to 130,000.

Speaking to Channel Seven's Sunrise program on Monday, Joyce added that Farley simply made a mistake while explaining the numbers.

"Show me a political career where people haven't made a mistake, and I'll show you a person who's not told the truth," Joyce said.

"I'm not going to throw anybody under a bus. When people are new in parliament, they can sometimes get things incorrect, and that's what happens. God knows, I've done it enough."

The debate over Australia's migration figures has intensified in recent months, with a recent Redbridge/Accent Research poll, conducted for the Australian Financial Review, indicating that voters trust One Nation and its traditionally restrictive approach to handle migration better than Labor or the Liberal Party.

However, the disjointed nature of One Nation's ongoing public discourse on the topic has prompted claims that the party is "incoherent" and "not fit to govern" from federal minister Pat Conroy.

Since its 1997 inception, cutting immigration has been seemingly a foundational pillar of One Nation policy. During her first speech to federal parliament, Hanson alleged Australia was in danger of being "swamped by Asians", that migrants never assimilated, and that multiculturalism should be abolished.

Its website currently states One Nation would "cut immigration by over 570,000 people" through "capping visas at 130,000 per year".

In a social media post on Sunday, Hanson wrote that NOM would be cut to 130,000 — a figure the party has previously clarified to SBS News. This number would be achieved by reducing international students, temporary graduate visas, and a "much more targeted" skilled migration program, Hanson said.

Those figures are different to the ones put forward by Farley, who said One Nation is focusing on "the NOM in its true form", as separate to labour programs "that drive our economy".

"The raw number is not too different [to current projections], but the reality is, where do we need them?" he asked.

"We don't need them in the great urban metroplex — we need them out to be productive, working in agriculture, engineering, mining."

Joyce further affirmed Hanson's view that One Nation's policy remained at 130,000 people annually for NOM.

SBS News has contacted One Nation for comment.

NOM hit 306,000 in the 2024-2025 period, down from a post-pandemic peak of 538,000.

Labor is yet to unveil its plan to restrict migration, but has previously set an official cap of 185,000 places on the permanent migration program. Treasury figures project that NOM will fall to 225,000 by the 2027-28 financial year.

Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke was set to detail Labor's plan to ensure that forecast was met, but pulled out of a National Press Club address at the last minute over fears about the implications cuts would have for certain sectors.

"Our policy will be released when Tony Burke is in a position to do so," Conroy told ABC Radio National on Monday. "But we've been very focused on getting the right migration mix to grow the economy as well as the broader interests of Australia."

Opposition leader Angus Taylor has said the Coalition will cap NOM at or below the number of homes built in Australia the previous year. Current building rates suggest their figure would be under 180,000; however, the Coalition is yet to announce where they would restrict immigration to reach that figure.

Opposition immigration spokesperson Jonathon Duniam told ABC Radio National that the Coalition is being "fairly methodical" in assessing its figures but that he would not "put a date" on when it will reveal details.

Duniam attacked One Nation for being cavalier with its migration figures, saying it had not detailed the impact such reductions would have on industries like agriculture, which relies on working holiday visa holders for seasonal labour.

"It was the same leader of One Nation who said in an interview about a week ago as well that she did not care about the impact on business any change to the migration system would have," Duniam said.

NOM is difficult for governments to control because it is a measurement of human behaviour rather than a fixed quota.

NOM is calculated by the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) based on the movement of people, subtracting overseas departures from overseas arrivals. To be included, a person must remain in the country for any 12 out of 16 months.

Citizens, permanent residents, temporary visa holders, and asylum seekers are included, as are backpackers and working holiday makers who stay for more than a year.

In 2024-2025, 568,000 people entered Australia, according to the ABS, down 14 per cent on the previous year.

Of that cohort, 88,000 were permanent visa holders, 53,000 were New Zealand citizens, and 64,000 were returning Australians. At the same time, 263,000 people left the country.

As Hanson stated, "short-term seasonal agricultural workers who do not meet the ABS residency test are not counted in net overseas migration".

However, using Farley's figures, many of the 100,000 additional workers he says would be able to enter the country — such as workers under the Pacific Australia Labour Mobility (PALM) scheme — would be included in NOM.

PALM workers can enter under a short-term placement of up to nine months or a long-term placement of one to four years.

The visa caps One Nation plans to set would place policy restrictions on NOM, but they can't control it entirely. Australians suddenly returning home en masse from abroad would, for example, elevate the figure regardless of visa caps.

Migration experts have long cautioned that linking migration to housing shortages is a flawed argument and that, in fact, migrant workers are essential to increase housing stock.

"If we don't have workers doing the jobs we need, if we don't get the right skill set into our country, then we all are worse off," Duniam said.

"One Nation has zero regard for that, as evidenced by the multiple positions they've had on migration policy all over the last few weeks."

Commercial deep-sea mining in the Pacific could begin in months without policy guardrails due to increasing rivalry between the United States and China over critical minerals, a new report warns.

Analysis by the Lowy Institute has found mining could start as early as 2027, with the floor of the Pacific Ocean holding vast quantities of prized resources.

The contest between the world's two biggest economies is being driven by demand created by batteries, weapons systems, and power grids.

The region's nations are split between the promise of economic spoils and the risk of irreversible damage to the ecosystem, fisheries, food security, livelihoods and culture, according to the research.

It argues the absence of an international governance framework for commercial deep-sea mining leaves Pacific states negotiating without internationally agreed protections.

This also leaves them dependent on individual agreements drafted by companies seeking to mine their ocean floor.

"For the Pacific Island nations caught in the middle of this great power battle for critical mineral supremacy, the economic proposition is difficult to refuse," the Lowy Institute's Connor Graham writes.

"The problem is not that Pacific nations disagree, it is that they are being forced to negotiate individually against parties with vastly more resources, information, and legal capacity, and that the international framework designed to protect them has stalled."

Graham said while Australia was yet to take a clear position on deep-sea mining in the Pacific, it has the standing, relationships, and institutional presence to make an impact.

This includes its membership in the Pacific Islands Forum and as a long-standing ally of the US.

"A commitment to standards that have not been written as a condition for activity that is about to begin is less of a policy, and more of an abstention," he said.

"But Australia cannot afford to abstain from this debate."

Countries including Australia have started to enter into deals for critical minerals, which are crucial for a number for emerging technologies.

The federal government has announced a $1.2 billion critical minerals strategic reserve to secure the supply of key minerals deemed crucial for Australia's economy and national security.
