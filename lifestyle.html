The truth about getting Invisalign in your 50s: I've developed a lisp and mental anguish. But I lost 7lbs in a month. It took me years to take the leap at 59 - here's how I feel about it now

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Four weeks ago, I was fitted with Invisalign retainers – those bespoke plastic aligners or ‘trays’ that midlife women, clearly too old for braces, use to straighten their teeth.

It was the culmination of at least six years of thinking about it, planning it and then endlessly changing my mind.

I used to have perfectly good teeth, but as we get older our jaws shift because our bone density decreases and, like sinking gravestones, our teeth become crooked and loose.

My once-straight pearly whites were getting jagged and ugly. And, after years of drinking black coffee and red wine, I could see they were discoloured.

My dream was to have straight white beautiful, perfect teeth. But every time I went to sign up for the trays, the cost (£3,900) and the rigmarole of it put me off. It would involve X-rays, my dentist told me, in order to make a series of complex moulds.

While I was deciding, I had my teeth scanned by a sophisticated machine, which showed me quite how horrible they were. I was told some would need filling and that I’d also need to treat my receding gums before being fitted for retainers because exposed root surface is softer than enamel and when it’s encased in trays for hours on end it runs a greater risk of decay. I’d need my overbite corrected too, she said.

On and on it went. I’d be paying thousands for days – months – of faff and pain, and yes the teeth at the end might look better, but as a 59-year-old mother of four, could I really be bothered? To paraphrase Jennifer Aniston and all the other L’Oreal models, was I worth it?

Two months ago, I decided that I was, and that the best policy – which probably applies to all dental treatment – is not to listen too hard when it’s described and to close your eyes when you sign on the dotted line. Blind trust was what I went with.

And then there I was, lying on her reclining chair, having strange, huge, clunky wedges of plastic put in my mouth. ‘It might feel a bit odd,’ my dentist Dr Shah said, forcing these plastic monstrosities into my mouth. Odd? It felt as if my mouth had been attacked. My first reaction was to gag. Once they had been ‘clicked’ in, all I wanted was for the dentist to take them out again.

My once-straight pearly whites were getting jagged and ugly, writes Lucy Cavendish And, after years of drinking black coffee and red wine, I could see they were discoloured

My dream was to have straight white beautiful, perfect teeth. But every time I went to sign up for the trays, the cost (£3,900) and the rigmarole of it put me off

‘Are you sure you want to do this?’ she asked.

‘Of courshhh,’ I replied. I’d just put down a deposit of £2,000. I was hardly going to back out now.

‘How long have I got to wear them for?’ I squeaked.

‘Twenty seven weeks,’ she replied, and then added a ruthless: ‘Twenty two hours out of every 24.’

The trays are moulds that fit my teeth very closely, and at first they are very difficult to put in. As their purpose is to shift your teeth into the ‘correct’ position you have to align them correctly in your mouth and ‘click’ them in so you know they are in the right position.

I have 27 retainers to wear; one a week for 27 weeks, each specifically designed to keep on gradually changing my teeth until – at the end of number 27 – my teeth will be perfect.

‘I don’t think I can speak any more,’ I said to the dentist as I was leaving. Every word felt like a ginormous effort using a whole new configuration of tongue and lips around the big plastic trays.

‘Don’t worry,’ she said brightly, ‘everyone feels that way but they adjust pretty quickly. It’ll feel totally normal in a matter of days.’ Only it didn’t. Four weeks in and I still haven’t ‘adjusted’. My brain has not computed in any way whatsoever that my mouth is full of plastic.

I am supposed to change them to the next smallest size every week but only if I wear them long enough each day, which I don’t. (Instead of 22 hours a day, I’m averaging about 18.) Besides, I get a headache every time I change them, so I beg for an extension and am allowed to downsize every ten days instead.

Still I find it excruciatingly difficult. The two hours ‘off’ – in my case four – are supposed to be for eating, which means taking the trays out and putting them back in again three times a day, but this takes me ages. I have to put most of my hand into my mouth and rummage around trying to ‘unhook’ the trays. It is painful and clumsy.

How does anyone have a life when they have retainers? I spend all my time obsessed by the things. If I’m not taking them out and putting them in, I’m running my tongue over them uncomprehendingly or worrying about what they look like.

My teeth feel restrained and also more tender, so that even free of the retainers I can’t eat anything hard. All I manage is soup, yogurt and soft eggs.

I used to love melon, radishes, bread, but it is impossible to eat any of that. It just hurts too much. I don’t know if I’ll ever eat an apple again. I am also not sleeping well, always grinding my plasticised teeth together. Plus, I’m convinced everyone’s staring at my mouth – the moulds seem so huge, so visible.

Obviously it is impossible to date wearing them. I look swollen around the mouth area as if I’ve been in a fight, and I still lisp all the time. Kissing is out of the question. Imagine having to take out your trays in front of someone else, hooking your fingers into your mouth and forcibly hoicking them out? Then dribbling everywhere.

I feel like Jaws from the James Bond movies – my fear is that someone might unexpectedly lunge in for a kiss to find themselves enmeshed in my trays.

So instead I date without them in, but then spend the entire evening anxiously counting the hours until I can get back to the bathroom to put them back in. All of it is very clearly the opposite of sexy.

I am assuming that people who live together and/or are in a relationship either don’t kiss or plan their intimacy to the very minute around a schedule entirely devoted to the retainers. Eating three meals and having sex in just two hours a day will test the organisational capabilities of even the most efficient midlife woman.

The only upside is that you drink gallons of water and you barely eat because it’s such a faff. Most people on retainers report losing weight. I recently got down to my ideal weight of 10st 7lb using Mounjaro, but now I’ve lost another half stone because of my retainers.

All this has made me regret my teeth obsession, but also become even more obsessed. I realise that for years, every time I went on a date all I could really focus on was how bad everyone’s teeth were. Every man I met had teeth that looked like piano keys, the colour of Werther’s Original. Most were crooked, jagged, sticking out or retrograding back into their mouths.

The more I noticed it, the worse it became. I then began to look at everyone’s teeth and it soon became obvious to me that some people had great teeth and some didn’t. I realised I was in the latter camp.

I know in the end my teeth will look brilliant – or so my dentist says – even and white with no overbite. However, I will have to wear a retainer every night forever more to keep them in place or they will start creeping back. And the thought of it makes me feel very tired indeed.

It is a big commitment to make, and all in the name, essentially, of vanity. Only time will tell if it’s worth it.

Could new plug-in solar panels help YOU fight soaring energy bills? Our simple formula will tell you exactly how many years it will take to cancel out cost of buying them

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Plug-in solar panels were approved for use by households in England, Scotland and Wales just weeks ago, and already demand is soaring. For many they represent a way to make savings on energy bills as fuel costs spiral again.

As with traditional panels fitted on the roof, these generate electricity to run your home. However, they are substantially cheaper and don’t need professional installation – in fact, they can be connected via an ordinary plug socket.

You can put the panels in gardens, on top of outbuildings or on balconies. For the first time, they’re available in high street retailers such as Screwfix and Argos for less than £700 apiece. Sales have been high, with Octopus selling out of its first kits in just two weeks.

Its panels are about the size of a single mattress – 5ft 11in by 3ft 7in (1.8m by 1.1m) – and weigh 3st 7lb (22kg).

For some households, a plug-in solar panel could pay for itself within a few years. Others might wait more than a decade to recover their investment, while those with very high energy needs could be better off forking out more for a rooftop system.

Solar expert Simon Bones, founder of Genous Retrofitting, says the panels will help households make some savings, but warns that these are likely to be modest as the Government has limited the size and power of panels that you can plug in.

‘The absolute amounts are small,’ he says. ‘And you have to have somewhere to put it. If you’re losing your balcony to a solar panel, it is a question of whether that’s something you want to do.’

Whether solar plug-in will work for you depends on how much sunshine your panels receive, how much electricity you use during the day and what you will have to pay.

Plug-in panels won't save you huge amounts but, with the cost of installation falling and the cost of energy rising, they are fast becoming cost-effective

We’ve devised a simple four-step checklist, called LAMP, to help you work out whether plug-in solar is likely to save you money.

LAMP stands for Lifestyle, Aspect, My electricity and Purchase price, and gives you an estimated payback period.

All you need is a pen and paper to jot down each of your four ratings. You can then use our formula to see the number of years it could take you to generate enough electrical savings to cover the cost.

But first, note that not everyone can use plug-in panels.

Tenants and flat owners can be denied permission by their landlord or freeholder, while not everyone has suitable outside space. For example, if your space is all north-facing, you would struggle to generate much power as you won’t get enough hours of direct sunlight a day.

To best profit from the panels, you will need to use your household appliances when the sun is shining.

Under the new rules, plug-in solar systems cannot include integrated battery storage, unlike bigger, professionally installed systems.

That means you can’t save the electricity your panels generate to use later – you need to use the power as it is produced.

If you regularly run a washing machine, dishwasher, computer or other appliances during daylight hours, you’re more likely to use the electricity your panels generate.

To see if it’s worth it, choose what best describes your household. The percentage that most closely matches your household is your ‘Lifestyle figure’. This estimates the proportion of solar electricity you might use yourself.

Next, consider where your panels will go. An unshaded, south-facing position with the panels tilted towards the sun is generally best.

You can put plug-in solar panels in gardens, on top of outbuildings or on balconies

East- or west-facing panels can still generate a useful amount of electricity, although output is likely to be lower. Vertically mounted panels, such as those on balcony railings, may generate less over a year.

Choose the number that most closely matches your proposed installation position.

Now, to estimate how much energy your solar panel could generate, measured in kilowatt hours, multiply your aspect rating by 700.

This is an approximation assuming you have the maximum 800W system allowed under the current rules. If your system’s maximum is less than this you will need to adjust accordingly.

The figure of 700 comes because the Energy Saving Trust estimates that an 800W system in Manchester, facing south with a tilt of 30 degrees without shade, could generate about 700 kilowatt hours (kWh) a year.

Now track down one of your electricity bills to find the unit rate. This is what you pay for each unit or kWh of electricity.

Every unit of solar electricity you use from your panel saves you from buying a unit from your supplier.

Energy regulator Ofgem’s average rate is 26.32p a unit, but you should substitute your own rate in the LAMP calculations.

Your M figure is the price you pay for electricity.

Finally, work out what you’ll need to spend to get the panels working. This is your P figure.

You’ll need to factor in the cost of the panels as well as extras such as mounting equipment.

Don’t assume that any outdoor socket or extension lead will be suitable. Follow the product instructions and seek professional advice if your electrical installation needs checking or adapting.

Many people may need a qualified electrician to put in a waterproof outdoor socket.

You now have the four pieces of information you need.

Step one: Calculate how much electricity your panels might generate. Multiply your Aspect figure by 700 to calculate how many kWh – often just called ‘units’ – your panels might generate over a year based on how well-situated they will be.

Step two: Estimate how much of that electricity you’ll use. Multiply the figure you got from the first step by your Lifestyle percentage to show how much of the electricity you generate you’ll be able to use by being at home when the sun is shining. For example, if you expect to generate 700 units and are home most days, with regular daytime appliance use (lifestyle score of 60 per cent), you will be able to use 420 units on average.

Step three: Work out your annual saving. Multiply the number of solar-generated units you’ll use (420 in the example above) by the electricity price you pay per unit (let’s say Ofgem’s 26.32p per unit) and divide by 100 to get a figure in pounds. This will show how much you will save by using your own electricity instead of power pulled from the grid.

Step four: Calculate how long it takes to cover the cost. To see how many years it would take to recoup the upfront cost of buying a solar panel, divide the total purchase price and connection cost by your annual savings.

Less than five years: You’re a gold-standard candidate. Your panels could pay for themselves fairly quickly, leaving you with more years of potential savings.

Five to eight years: You’re a good candidate. Plug-in solar could make financial sense, especially if you expect to remain in your home or can take the panels with you if you move.

Eight to 12 years: Think carefully. You may still save money, but recovering the initial outlay is likely to take a long time.

More than 12 years: Poor financial prospect. The savings may be too small to justify the investment.

These figures are all estimates, not guaranteed returns. They assume that electricity prices remain unchanged and exclude maintenance, replacement costs and any export payments.

Let’s take a couple who work from home and have a south-facing balcony but their panels would have to be mounted vertically on the railings.

Their Aspect figure is 0.7, which means – multiplying by 700 – their panels are estimated to generate 490kWh annually. They regularly run appliances during the day, giving them a Lifestyle figure of 60 per cent. Which means they will use about 294kWh of the 490kWh generated annually.

At 26.32p per unit, that would save about £77 a year – the usable 294kWh generated multiplied by the cost per unit.

Plug-in solar panels can be bought on the high street for less than £700

Their kit costs £660, with another £120 needed for electrical work – a total of £780. This gives them a LAMP result of 10.1, meaning that to recoup £780 with savings of £77 a year would take 10.1 years.

They could still benefit, but the vertical mounting and additional connection costs make the investment less attractive.

They would also need the necessary permissions.

Plug-in solar could give more households a relatively inexpensive way to generate their own electricity.

But having a big electricity bill doesn’t automatically mean making the biggest savings. What matters is the total amount of electricity the panels generate, whether you can use it while the sun is shining and how much you have to spend to get started.

Some heavy energy users might be better off with a fully installed kit, which will generate far more electricity.

According to the Energy Saving Trust, a typical 4.5kW rooftop system costs about £7,600 to install, compared with £660 for these new plug-in kits.

However, it can cut bills by hundreds of pounds a year and you can earn money by selling surplus electricity back to the grid through the Smart Export Guarantee. And you could also install a battery to use the solar power whenever you want.

There could be changes coming that make plug-in panels suitable for more people.

Energy provider Octopus is hoping to launch a version with a battery in the first quarter of next year, subject to regulatory changes. That could mean even those who don’t use much electricity during the day can benefit from solar plug-in power.

Plug-in solar kits are now available from many retailers, but prices vary.

Octopus Energy advertises a two-panel kit for £650, while Screwfix sells a comparable two-panel PowerXpress kit for £669. Both have two 460W panels and an 800W inverter.

Cheaper options include Octopus Energy’s single-panel kit at £450 and single-panel kits at City Plumbing from £348 – but these will not generate the 800W maximum the calculations on this page have used.

At the other end of the spectrum, an Argos UKSOL two-panel kit costs £1,199.

But check exactly what you’re getting for your money. Solar kits include an inverter, which is needed to convert the direct current produced to the alternating current of mains supply.

Before buying, check that the exact make and model is listed as compliant with UK standards on the Energy Networks Association’s Connect Direct LCT Device Register. You’ll also need to log your system before using it at the UK Plug-In Solar Registration website.

Your Saliva Could Hold the Key to Detecting a Deadly Cancer

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered distinct bacterial patterns in the saliva of patients with a deadly form of esophageal cancer.

Bacteria living in the mouth may hold clues to a deadly form of esophageal cancer that often goes undetected until treatment options are limited. Researchers in South Africa have identified distinctive bacterial patterns in patients’ saliva, raising the possibility that a simple, inexpensive test could eventually help identify people who need further screening.

The study, published in Communications Medicine, was conducted by scientists at the Sydney Brenner Institute for Molecular Bioscience (SBIMB) at Wits University and Columbia University in New York. Combining bacterial DNA sequencing with machine learning, the team developed a model that distinguished cancer patients from unaffected individuals more effectively than one based on clinical and demographic information alone.

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is especially common in parts of eastern and southern Africa, including South Africa’s Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal provinces. High rates also occur in regions of China and Iran, forming a geographic belt that scientists have struggled to explain.

Unlike esophageal adenocarcinoma, which is strongly associated with acid reflux, obesity, and lifestyle factors, ESCC has less clearly understood causes. It also frequently affects relatively young adults.

“A feature of the cancer is the young age of many patients, with a mean age of 50, and 18% of patients presenting before the age of 40,” says Distinguished Professor Christopher Mathew of the SBIMB.

“This geographical occurrence is also puzzling, and may reflect genetic susceptibility, a specific environmental exposure, or a combination of factors that researchers have still not been able to pin down,” explains Dr. Wenlong Carl Chen, a researcher at SBIMB.

Analysis of data from the Johannesburg Cancer Study identified several ESCC risk factors, including smoking, heavy alcohol consumption, rural residence, lower educational attainment, and the use of biomass or other household fuels.

“Still, they do not fully explain the number of cases, the geographic clustering, or why some patients develop the disease without these obvious exposures.”

To investigate another possible indicator, researchers examined the oral microbiome, the community of microorganisms living in the mouth. DNA sequencing revealed distinct differences between saliva samples from cancer patients and unaffected controls.

“We found clear differences between the saliva bacteria of people with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma and unaffected controls. Using genetic sequencing of bacteria and machine-learning methods, the team identified a distinctive microbial pattern associated with the cancer,” says Chen.

Several bacterial species were more abundant in ESCC patients, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, a microorganism previously implicated in other cancers. The microbiome-based model also performed better than one relying solely on clinical and demographic data.

However, the findings do not establish that these bacteria cause cancer. The microbial changes could instead result from the disease itself, particularly as a growing tumor narrows the esophagus and disrupts normal swallowing.

“But even that could be useful. A blockage in the esophagus develops gradually, often before patients arrive at the hospital, unable to swallow properly. If microbial changes in saliva can help flag that something is already changing, they could one day help identify people who need earlier referral for endoscopy.”

A saliva or cheek swab test could eventually help doctors prioritize patients for endoscopy, particularly in communities with limited access to specialized care. Such a test would not replace endoscopy, however, and the current approach has not been established as an early cancer detection method.

Researchers are also investigating whether inherited genetic differences increase susceptibility to ESCC and whether tumors contain evidence of earlier environmental exposures.

One approach involves studying mutational signatures, distinctive patterns of DNA damage preserved in cancer cells. Certain chemicals, pollutants, smoke exposures, or water contaminants may leave recognizable genetic traces, potentially helping scientists identify contributors to the disease.

“This is critical research which has generated important foundational knowledge that will take us to the next step, and hopefully to the clinic in the long run,” says Professor Michèle Ramsay, Director of the SBIMB.

The saliva model has undergone internal validation but still requires testing in independent populations. Oral bacterial communities vary with geography, diet, and environmental conditions, meaning results from one population may not translate directly to another.

The next research phase will include patients with confirmed ESCC, healthy individuals, and people with benign esophageal conditions that can also cause swallowing difficulties.

These comparisons will help establish whether the bacterial patterns are specific to cancer or reflect esophageal obstruction more generally. The answer will determine whether saliva testing could eventually become a practical tool for identifying patients who need further investigation.

Reference: “A generalizable cross-continent prediction of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma using the oral microbiome” by Shahd ElNaggar, Wenlong Carl Chen, Leanne M. Prodehl, Thomas K. Marumo, Muhammed U. Khan, Christopher G. Mathew, Paul Ruff, Zhezhen Jin, Alfred I. Neugut, Anil K. Rustgi, Anne-Catrin Uhlemann, Tal Korem and Julian A. Abrams, 28 February 2026, Communications Medicine. DOI: 10.1038/s43856-026-01468-y

Der schnörkellose Look der Neunziger ist angesagt wie lange nicht mehr. Vor allem die Gen Z feiert die Dekade als nostalgischen Rückzugsort.

Julia Roberts in Levi’s-Jeans und einem weißen T-Shirt, darüber ein voluminöser schwarzer Lederblazer. Gwyneth Paltrow in einem schlichten, kamelhaarfarbenen Seidenrock, knielang und kombiniert zu einem einfachen Kaschmirrolli im gleichen Farbton. Dazwischen immer wieder Caroline Bessette-Kennedy und Kate Moss in ähnlich puristischen Outfits. Denn die Bilder, die dort als Style-Inspiration herumgereicht werden, stammen zu großen Teilen aus den 1990er-Jahren.

Ob „Ugly Shoes“, Gorp-Core oder Iced Matcha Latte: Valerie Zehethofer widmet sich in ihrer Kolumne Hypes, Trends und Gegenwartsphänomenen und erklärt, was in der Welt des Lifestyle gerade „Das Ding“ ist.

Die Collagen und Fotogalerien tragen Titel wie „Nineties-Core“ oder „90s Minimalism“, sie feiern das betont Simple, Unangestrengte. Modehäuser wie Calvin Klein, Helmut Lang oder Jil Sander machten den Look gegen Ende der 1980er-Jahre populär. Die Marken trat mit reduzierten Schnitten und monochromen Farben gegen die schrillen Eighties an. Das Slipdress von Calvin Klein, ein schmales Spaghettiträger-Kleid meist aus Seide, wurde etwa zum Bestseller und zum inoffiziellen Code einer jungen, urbanen Klientel. Allein Material und Silhouette definierten nun ein Outfit. Laute Farben, Muster und Logos hingegen scheute, wer Auskennerschaft signalisieren wollte.

Warum die Ästhetik gerade jetzt und vor allem bei jungen Menschen wieder so begehrt ist, hat Trendforscherinnen nach eindeutige Gründe. Ein gut sitzender Kaschmirmantel, ein Bleistiftrock oder eine lockere Seidenbluse dienen ihnen als Ticket in eine vermeintlich authentischere, rückblickend wohl auch unkomplizierter wirkende Welt. Überfordert von Krisen, Kriegen und der KI-Revolution spendet das modische Fotoalbum Trost und Sicherheit. Videos tauchen in der 90s-Revival-Blase selten auf. Bemerkenswert, wo doch hektische Ultrakurzvideos die Sozialen Medien längst fest im Griff haben.

Freilich aber war das letzte Jahrzehnt des 20. Jahrhunderts auch eines, in dem sich nicht nur die Modewelt grundlegend veränderte. Der Trend-Pluralismus zog ein, Fast Fashion wurde erfunden, der Laufsteg demokratisierte sich. Die aus heutiger Sicht so verlockende Ordnung der Neunziger-Bilderwelt ist eine trügerische, schon damals sollte der unkomplizierte Stil Ordnung in chaotische Verhältnisse bringen. Den Neo-Minimalistinnen ist das egal und recht haben sie, sich in luxuriösen Basics in ein überschaubareres Universum zu beamen. Schließlich ist die Möglichkeit, sich schnell woanders hin zu träumen, die vielleicht wichtigste Superpower, die die Mode zu bieten hat.

A simple 15-minute treatment could help patients avoid carpal tunnel surgery

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

A simple procedure that takes about 15 minutes could offer lasting relief to people with carpal tunnel syndrome, a painful condition that can cause numbness, tingling, and weakness in the hands. A new study suggests that the treatment, which uses ultrasound to guide an injection of ordinary saline around a compressed nerve, provides longer-lasting benefits than traditional steroid injections, without the risks associated with steroids.

The findings, published in Radiology, a journal of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), point to a promising alternative for patients whose symptoms have not improved with conventional treatments. Researchers say the minimally invasive technique could also help some patients delay or avoid surgery.

Why Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Can Be So Difficult to Treat

Carpal tunnel syndrome develops when the median nerve, which extends from the forearm into the hand, becomes compressed as it passes through a narrow channel in the wrist called the carpal tunnel. Pressure on this nerve can cause pain, tingling, numbness, and muscle weakness, sometimes making everyday activities difficult.

The condition is commonly associated with repetitive hand and wrist movements. Studies suggest that approximately 5% to 10% of people develop carpal tunnel syndrome during their lifetime, and the number of cases is increasing.

One common treatment involves injecting corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and relieve pressure on the nerve. Although these injections can ease symptoms, the benefits often fade, and repeated treatments may be necessary.

"Traditional steroid injection for carpal tunnel syndrome often provides only temporary relief, thus requiring repeats of the procedure," said study co-author Anupama Tandon, M.D., professor in the Department of Radiodiagnosis and Imaging at University College of Medical Sciences and Guru Teg Bahadur Hospital in Delhi, India. "Further, steroids carry risk of localized skin changes, fat thinning and tendon weakening."

A Simple Procedure That Frees a Compressed Nerve

Researchers investigated an alternative technique known as nerve hydrodissection. Rather than relying on medication to reduce inflammation, this approach uses carefully placed fluid to separate the affected nerve from nearby tissues that may be restricting its movement.

Doctors use real-time ultrasound imaging to see the nerve and guide the injection precisely. The fluid helps loosen adhesions, which are areas where tissues stick together, allowing the nerve to move more freely.

Hydrodissection is often performed alongside steroid treatment. However, researchers have had limited evidence about whether the procedure can provide lasting relief without adding steroids.

To investigate, Dr. Tandon joined study lead author Anindita Bose, M.D., assistant professor in the Department of Radiology at Mahatma Gandhi Medical College and Hospital in Jaipur, India, and other researchers in conducting a randomized controlled trial involving 63 patients.

All participants had confirmed carpal tunnel syndrome and had continued experiencing symptoms despite conventional treatment.

The researchers randomly assigned patients to three equally sized groups. One received ultrasound-guided hydrodissection using normal saline, a sterile saltwater solution. Another underwent the same procedure with both saline and corticosteroids. The third received an ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection without hydrodissection.

Carpal Tunnel Relief Lasted Longer Without Steroids

Researchers tracked each patient's progress using standardized measurements of pain, symptom severity, and hand function. They also performed ultrasound examinations to measure the size of the median nerve, an indicator of swelling.

These evaluations took place four weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks after treatment.

After four weeks, all three groups showed significant improvements. However, differences became increasingly apparent as the study continued.

Patients who underwent hydrodissection continued to experience improvements in their symptoms and hand function. Those who received steroid injections alone initially improved but began experiencing recurring symptoms during later follow-up visits.

"At 12 weeks and 6 months, both hydrodissection groups demonstrated further improvement in clinical symptoms and function, whereas patients who received corticosteroid injection alone experienced a recurrence of the symptoms, suggesting that the initial benefit was short-lived," Dr. Tandon said.

Surprisingly, adding corticosteroids to the hydrodissection fluid did not produce a statistically significant additional long-term benefit. Using ordinary saline alone appeared to work just as well over the six-month follow-up period.

Ultrasound measurements provided additional evidence of the treatment's effectiveness. In patients who underwent hydrodissection, the cross-sectional area of the median nerve decreased by approximately 43% to 45%. Among those treated with corticosteroid injections alone, the reduction was only about 15%.

The greater decrease in nerve size suggested that hydrodissection was more effective at reducing swelling around the compressed nerve.

"Most of our patients had been suffering from carpal tunnel syndrome for months to years and did not get much relief despite trying various treatments like pain medications, splints, lifestyle modification and corticosteroids," Dr. Tandon said. "It came as a pleasant surprise to find that a simple procedure of dissecting the median nerve under ultrasound guidance with normal saline provided them long-term relief."

A 15-Minute Treatment That Could Help Patients Avoid Surgery

Beyond providing sustained symptom relief, hydrodissection offered several practical advantages.

The procedure lasted just 15 minutes on average and was generally well tolerated. Researchers observed no significant complications, either immediately afterward or during the follow-up period.

Patients also reported high satisfaction with their treatment. The procedure was inexpensive, required neither anesthesia nor hospitalization, and allowed participants to return to work within an hour.

These advantages could make hydrodissection an attractive option for people seeking relief without undergoing surgery or receiving repeated steroid injections.

The findings are particularly relevant as carpal tunnel syndrome becomes more common worldwide.

"Overall, the study suggests that ultrasound-guided saline hydrodissection is a safe, minimally invasive treatment that can provide sustained clinical and morphological improvement in patients with refractory carpal tunnel syndrome," Dr. Tandon said. "Thus, hydrodissection merits to be a minimally invasive option for mild to moderate carpal tunnel syndrome and can defer and even alleviate the need for surgery."

Larger Studies Will Test How Long the Benefits Last

Although the findings are encouraging, the researchers emphasize that additional studies are needed before the treatment's long-term effectiveness can be fully established.

The trial included only 63 participants, and patients were monitored for six months. Larger studies involving multiple medical centers and longer observation periods could help determine how consistently hydrodissection works and whether its benefits continue beyond the initial follow-up period.

The research team has already begun another investigation comparing different fluids used during hydrodissection, including saline and dextrose, a form of sugar.

The goal is to identify which approach produces the most durable improvements for people with carpal tunnel syndrome.

"This trial features an extended follow-up period to track long-term patient outcomes and establish the true durability of the treatment," Dr. Tandon said.

The study, "Efficacy of US-guided Hydrodissection for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial," was conducted by Anupama Tandon, M.D., Anindita Bose, M.D., Gopesh Mehrotra, M.D., Siddharth Maheshwari, M.D., and Ayush Khandelwal, M.D.

How to Stay on Track While Traveling

📅 Jul 17, 2025  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Staying on track with your fitness and wellness goals can feel extra tough when your routine gets thrown off. Whether you’re hopping on a plane, heading out for a weekend getaway, or just dealing with a packed week that doesn’t leave much room for your usual habits, consistency gets harder. But the good news is you don’t need perfection. You just need a plan that works with your lifestyle. 

Here’s how to make it happen, even when you’re out of your regular routine:

1. Set Mini Goals

Don’t try to hit every single goal perfectly. Instead, focus on 1–2 things that will make you feel your best. Maybe it’s getting 20 minutes of movement in each day, drinking a certain amount of water, or hitting your protein goal. Choose what matters most and commit to that before diving in all at once.

Tip: Use your LSF planner or a simple note in your phone to track these. Seeing that visual reminder each day will keep you grounded.

2. MOVE Your Body 

You don’t need a full gym or perfect setup to move your body. Walks, workouts in our MOVE App, stretching, or a quick ab burner before heading out can all count + can all be done at home. 

Even 10 minutes counts. I promise.

3. Increase Hydration

When you're dehydrated, energy drops, digestion slows, and cravings creep in. Traveling? Bring a reusable water bottle so you can refill it wherever you go. Busy week? Set reminders on your phone to sip throughout the day.

And if you’re flying? Drink even more. Planes can be very dehydrating! 

4. Improve, Don’t Restrict

Trying to be “perfect” while traveling usually leads to stress, not success. Instead of cutting things out, look for easy upgrades. Add veggies where you can. Choose lean protein when it’s an option. Enjoy the fun foods without guilt but also fuel yourself in ways that support your energy and mood.

It’s not all or nothing. 

5. Stick to your Morning Routine 

Even one consistent part of your day can help keep you grounded. Maybe it’s a morning walk, journaling with your coffee, or a quick stretch before the day begins. Having something that starts your day with intention helps everything else fall into place even when you’re off schedule.

Staying on track isn’t about rules. It’s about showing up for yourself, no matter where you are or what your day looks like. Progress happens in the small moments, and it all adds up. You’ve got this babe!

📅 Nov 18, 2024  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

With its wide network of challenging trails and uninhibited views of the Pacific Ocean, the Palos Verdes Peninsula has long been a favorite hiking and mountain biking destination in Los Angeles County. But this year’s unprecedented landslide activity, which has damaged homes and left hundreds without power and gas service, has also impacted the most popular trails on the peninsula.

About 13.5 miles of trails in the Portuguese Bend, Abalone Cove, Filiorum and Forrestal reserves are temporarily closed “for public safety due to land movement,” says Rancho Palos Verdes spokeswoman Megan Barnes. There are no plans to reopen them anytime soon.

The good news is, there are plenty of trails on the peninsula that remain open and safe for hiking. The peninsula has more than 1,600 acres of open space within the four cities comprising it: Rancho Palos Verdes, Palos Verdes Estates, Rolling Hills Estates and Rolling Hills. Some of the trails aren’t well-marked or require stretches of walking on residential streets, but many offer ocean views, a diverse array of native plants and even geological lessons that shed light on the peninsula’s unique terrain.

Bill Lavoie, a long-time trail leader for the Sierra Club’s Palos Verdes-South Bay group, regularly leads weeknight conditioning hikes in the area. He and his cohorts miss the unique beauty and cardio-friendly elevation gains of Portuguese Bend, but they have learned to embrace other trails in the area and happily shared some of their favorites.

Here are five Palos Verdes Peninsula hikes that are more than just worthy substitutes for the closed trails. All of them offer a good workout, interesting scenery and a window into a fascinating, often-overlooked area of Los Angeles County.

Keep in mind that trails within the Palos Verdes Nature Preserve typically close between dusk and dawn daily and for at least 48 hours after a rainfall. For updates on trail conditions and closures, go to rpvca.gov/trailalerts.
