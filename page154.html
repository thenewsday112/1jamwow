Israeli forces carried out a major security operation in the West Bank city of Hebron on Saturday, a day after two Palestinian gunmen killed two Israeli soldiers and wounded three others at a military checkpoint near the city.

The Israeli military said the operation was aimed at locating militants and weapons in the area where the attackers had operated. Both gunmen were killed during Friday's exchange of fire.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu announced a large-scale military response following the attack, saying Israeli forces would conduct an extensive operation in Hebron.

The military said its forces were conducting "precise, targeted and thorough searches" and that the operation was expected to continue for several days.

According to the Israeli military, the operation is focused on the area from which the attackers came. Residents leaving the affected neighbourhoods are being directed through designated exit points, where security checks are being conducted.

The military operation has also resulted in the displacement of hundreds of Palestinian residents.

Hebron Mayor Youssef al-Jaabari and local activist Issa Amro estimated that approximately 250 families had been forced to leave their homes, while Hebron Governor Khaled Dudin put the figure at 150 families.

In the Ein Dir Baha neighbourhood, Israeli troops marked buildings with X symbols after clearing them of residents. Streets were largely deserted, with military vehicles patrolling the area.

Local Palestinian officials said the operation had affected approximately one-third of the city, although the Israeli military described its actions as focused on the neighbourhoods linked to Friday's attack.

Governor Dudin criticised the scale of the operation, arguing that civilians who had no involvement in the shooting were being affected.

"This amounts to collective punishment being imposed on civilians who have nothing at all to do with this," he told AFP.

The mayor said local authorities were working to provide assistance and temporary accommodation to displaced residents.

"We will find them shelter and provide these people with all the help they need," he said.

Israeli forces also restricted vehicle access to Hebron, with gates at entrances to the city closed to traffic. Palestinians were seen crossing checkpoints on foot.

The operation comes just days before Israel's general election on Tuesday, with Netanyahu facing a closely contested race against an opposition bloc led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot.

Netanyahu has pledged a strong response to the killing of the soldiers, signalling that additional military measures could follow.

Israeli National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir has called for the dismantling of the Palestinian Authority, which administers parts of the West Bank under agreements reached with Israel in the 1990s.

Netanyahu has also reiterated his opposition to the establishment of a Palestinian state, saying he had resisted pressure from successive US administrations on the issue.

The Israeli government's security policies in the West Bank remain a major issue in the election campaign, alongside concerns over attacks against Israeli civilians and military personnel.

Meanwhile, tensions across the occupied West Bank remain high amid continuing Israeli military operations and reports of violence involving Israeli settlers and Palestinian residents.

Israeli human rights organisation Yesh Din reported 672 incidents of settler violence over a 26-day period, nearly half of them linked to the olive harvest.

The latest operation in Hebron is expected to continue for several days, with Israeli authorities maintaining that their immediate objective is to address security threats following Friday's deadly attack.

Thousands of people rallied for a fresh anti-government protest in the Albanian capital Tirana on Saturday, with police firing tear gas to disperse the crowd.

In the latest of several clashes between police and protesters since mid-October, government buildings were attacked and dozens of demonstrators were detained. Authorities said that more than 300 protestors and police have been injured.

The protesters had heeded calls from organisers for students and others around Albania to attend the rally. Various groups, including university professors, artists and opposition parties, have announced their support or attendance.

Supporters of the opposition Democratic Party earlier gathered outside the prime minister's office for around an hour and a half, setting fire to pillows that Rama had placed there as a symbolic invitation to dialogue with students.

The demonstrators then headed towards the Ministry of Education, where a separate student protest took place.

For their part, high school and university students are demanding lower tuition fees, greater state investment in education and protesting corruption in the education system. Some have joined a broader anti-government movement calling for the resignation of the prime minister.

Prime minister Edi Rama issued a stern warning against violence, pledging a sustained response from the authorities.

"All those who glorify violence, all those who echo violence, and all those who perpetrate violence must understand that just as the urban terror was intense, concentrated, and prolonged, so too will the response from the state be prolonged and persistent," Rama said.

Anti-government protests started in May, sparked by a luxury hotel project linked to US President Donald Trump's family, which scientists said in July had already caused irreparable damage to a once pristine stretch of Albania's coast.

Anger over the resort development has morphed into a broader push against corruption, amid ongoing demands for Prime Minister Edi Rama to step down.

Rama has blamed "foreign actors" for the violence and accused both Russia and Iran of driving online misinformation.

The prime minister has also called for "dialogue".

The demonstrators have distanced themselves from any political party, and regularly carry signs accusing both Rama and longtime opposition leader Sali Berisha of corruption.

They chanted "Rama in prison" and "Berisha in prison" on Saturday, blaming the entire political class for the crisis.

The prime minister had earlier published photos of around 40 people, identified by their initials, whom he accused of violence, and renewed his call for the heads of dozens of public institutions to resign.

Rome: Clashes broke out on Saturday evening as protesters marched through the Italian capital. Near Largo di Santa Susanna, demonstrators confronted a police cordon, throwing bottles and setting off firecrackers. Several street signs were also knocked down. Police responded with water cannons.

Milan: Two separate demonstrations involving students, activist groups and pro-Palestinian protesters merged into a single large procession heading towards Piazza Leonardo da Vinci, home to the Polytechnic University of Milan.

Earlier, police blocked a march that had set off from the square towards the city centre, forcing demonstrators back towards Corso Venezia. Some participants subsequently joined a second procession that had started at the Piccolo Teatro.

Around 10,000 people were reportedly taking part in the combined march, which was expected to conclude at Piazza Leonardo da Vinci.

Turin: Tensions escalated as protesters clashed with police, prompting officers to deploy tear gas. After marching through the Aurora neighbourhood, demonstrators reached the Mosca Bridge, where police blocked their route towards the city centre.

Some protesters threw stones, bottles and smoke bombs at officers, who responded with tear gas. The procession later resumed along Via Cigna.

Bologna: Emergency services were called to Via Marconi following clashes between demonstrators and police. Paramedics treated an injured young protester at the scene.

Florence: Protesters threw firecrackers and glass bottles at police as tensions rose during a march through the city. Near the Santa Trinità Bridge, at the junction with Via Tornabuoni, some demonstrators attempted to break through a riot police cordon.

Italian authorities have deployed hundreds of additional soldiers and stepped up security across major cities on Saturday as a new wave of demonstrations raises fears of further violence following last weekend's clashes between protesters and police.

Protests are planned in Milan, Rome, Turin, Naples and several other cities, with demonstrators voicing anger over rising living costs, low wages, military spending and government policies.

Milan is on particularly high alert after violent unrest on 17 and 18 October, when clashes with police, vandalism and looting caused widespread damage in the city centre.

Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's government has ordered the deployment of an additional 500 soldiers to Milan, reinforcing the security forces already stationed in the city.

Authorities have also introduced drone surveillance, railway station checks and rapid-response units to prevent further disorder.

Italian police carried out more than 150 searches in Milan, Monza and around 20 other cities on Saturday morning as part of investigations into last weekend's violence.

The searches reportedly targeted individuals suspected of involvement in the unrest, including students and minors.

Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi has suggested that the previous disturbances were coordinated, while authorities are investigating messages exchanged in online groups ahead of the latest demonstrations.

The newly formed "17 October Movement," which has been linked to the recent mobilisation, announced that previously advertised protest locations had been cancelled and that new meeting points would be communicated shortly before the marches.

The decision has complicated security preparations, with police warning of the possibility of spontaneous gatherings in several parts of the country.

In Rome, protesters gathered for a national demonstration under the slogan "Keep Italy out of the war", opposing military involvement and increased defence spending.

In Turin, students and activist groups have called for a march against rearmament, rising fuel prices and the cost of living, while also demanding better access to housing, education and healthcare.

In Naples, demonstrators mobilised against economic hardship and rising prices.

Meanwhile, thousands were expected to march in Castel Volturno, near Naples, against plans to establish a migrant detention and repatriation centre. Religious organisations, charities, migrant groups and agricultural workers are among those supporting the protest.

The latest mobilisation comes a week after violent clashes in Milan and Turin prompted the government to announce tougher security measures.

Police said around 2,000 young people took part in the earlier unrest, which resulted in 45 arrests, including 34 minors.

Meloni has condemned the violence and pledged a tougher response to attacks on police officers and businesses.

The unrest has also renewed debate over economic inequality, social exclusion and the government's approach to public order.

Authorities remain concerned that Saturday's demonstrations could bring together student movements, trade unions and more radical activist groups, increasing the risk of further confrontations.

The protests are taking place alongside demonstrations elsewhere in Europe, including France, Spain, Portugal and Albania, where campaigners are mobilising over issues ranging from living costs to public services and government policies.

Thousands of demonstrators are taking to the streets of Madrid, demanding better public pensions, as a wave of protests sweeps across several European countries amid heightened security and fears of fresh violence.

Organised by the pensioners' movement and supported by trade unions, including the General Confederation of Labour (CGT), the march is making its way from Atocha station through the Spanish capital towards Calle Alcalá, near Sevilla metro station.

Demonstrators are demanding pensions that guarantee a decent standard of living for both current retirees and future generations.

Their key demands include linking pension increases to actual inflation, raising the minimum pension to match the minimum wage, closing the gender pension gap and protecting the public pension system from privatisation.

The CGT is also calling for easier access to early retirement without financial penalties for workers who have contributed for at least 35 years, as well as the repeal of several pension reforms introduced since 2011.

The Madrid demonstration comes as several European countries face a weekend of protests over a range of political and economic grievances.

In France, authorities are deploying 70,000 police officers and gendarmes amid heightened tensions, including demonstrations against Marine Le Pen's National Rally congress in Orléans.

Italy is also stepping up security, with 1,500 officers deployed in Rome and 1,800 in Milan as demonstrators mobilise against the rising cost of living and government policies.

Italian authorities fear renewed clashes following violent unrest on 17 and 18 October, which has prompted tighter security measures in several major cities.

Although the demonstrations have different demands, they reflect mounting political and economic tensions across Europe, with governments facing growing pressure over living standards, public spending and social policies.

Clashes broke out Saturday in Paris and other French cities as demonstrations were held a week after others that descended into street violence, prompting President Emmanuel Macron's government to launch a massive security operation this time around.

Protesters demonstrated over the high cost of living in France, rising fuel prices and a lack of funding for education. They come after a series of protests which started in the capital's high schools last month, with students demanding more funding for education, more teachers in schools and better facilities.

Those protests then went nationwide before expanding to include wider society.

Though the protests were largely peaceful and there was no repeat of the scale of last week's rioting and looting, police arrested nearly 1,000 people, with Interior Minister Laurent Nunez saying "an extremely elevated level of violence" from black-clad "ultra-left" extremists had taken place.

Riot squads fired tear gas at protesters in the capital as well as in Marseille, Lyon, Bordeaux, Lille, Toulouse, Montpellier and Nantes.

Two people were "seriously" injured, Nunez said: one man in Toulouse, who reportedly had fingers blown off, and another person in Clermont-Ferrand. He did not give details of either case.

Authorities had deployed 70,000 security forces across the country for the new marches.

The violence came a week after rioting that saw public buildings vandalised, hundreds of shops looted and 2,700 people arrested, casting a pall over a country that goes to the polls in six months to choose a successor to President Emmanuel Macron after 10 years.

Nunez said Saturday's outcome was more "positive" than a week ago, with more police on the streets acting more rapidly, conducting more pre-demonstration checks and better protecting businesses.

There was nevertheless widespread anger at the government over a protest crackdown viewed as repressive and as an assault on the right to protest.

Protests also took place in Italy, Belgium, Spain and Portugal over different issues, yet with some overlap on inflation and education problems.

Mass protests last Saturday saw widespread violence and clashes with police in several cities, with buildings and vehicles set alight and masked individuals smashing shop windows and looting stores. Unrest continued in Paris last Sunday, as masked youths erected flaming barricades and police charged crowds.

The French government ramped up security operations this weekend, and many businesses took matters into their own hands and boarded up windows or closed for the day.

A last-minute route change to a planned Paris protest prompted pushback from unions and politicians on the left in France, who argued that the move could scatter demonstrators and fuel further chaos.

Police had initially approved a march between Bastille and Nation squares in the east of the capital but overturned the decision on Friday, instead proposing a route between Porte d’Italie and Porte de Vincennes along the city's southeastern edge.

To the south of the capital, authorities also kept a close watch over the hard-right National Rally party's congress in Orléans.

The weekend event, which both Marine Le Pen and Jordan Bardella are attending, drew both supporters and counterprotesters on Saturday.

Police said at around midday Saturday that they had seized multiple items "intended for use in acts of violence or to create a hostile atmosphere," in locations including Orléans, Pau, Rennes, Marseille, and Tours.

Getting initial customers as a startup can be one of the most grueling challenges in business. Even when companies manage to snag some early interest, there’s no guarantee those customers will stick around.

At TechCrunch Disrupt last week, Elia Wallen, founder and CEO of travel startup Engine, Gamma founder and CEO Grant Lee, and Crystal Huang, general partner at Google Ventures, shared tips on how to attract and retain customers.

In the current era — when young AI companies like Cluely and Instinct are building big audiences not just through their products but also through their founders’ colorful online presences — social media can seem like a magic bullet for reaching early customers. But founders who want heighten their business strategy with modern marketing, have to be strategic, according to Lee.

Gamma, which sells AI-powered presentation software, managed to scale its business exponentially with a social media strategy that deployed influencers through its Ambassadors (or “Gambassador”) program. The company adopted the plan before influencer-based marketing was a tried-and-true strategy for smaller companies, eventually reaching a multi-billion dollar valuation.

But simply giving influencers a platform and telling them to promote your brand online doesn’t automatically translate to sales, said Lee. There’s more to it than that.

“If you have a product that can serve many different segments or use cases, then you want to basically have creators that can target each one of those,” Lee said. “If you have folks that are in consulting versus people that do all their marketing and create all the decks for their CMO, there’s all these different segments you might want to go after.”

Lee said sometimes founders seem to treat creator marketing or influencer marketing as a sort of set it and forget it thing.

“I think you’re better served by really spending a lot of time with these creators, because if they’re not able to understand how to articulate the value of your product, it doesn’t matter how much money you spend, it’s just going to fall flat,” Lee added.

The effectiveness of social media depends largely on the type of business. Other ways of reaching customers can be just (if not more) effective.

Wallen would know. His company Engine, which manages lodging and travel bookings for midsize businesses, bootstrapped its way to $150 million in bookings before taking on any outside funding. They also had no visible social media strategy at all. For Wallen, traditional sales tactics can be far more useful than a positive TikTok video.

“We did events and we smiled and dialed,” said Wallen. “We still have a lot of people picking up the phone. A lot of people who didn’t grow up with that or don’t understand it think that it’s outdated or it doesn’t work but it does. It’s a hard thing. There are a lot of companies that just refuse to do it or don’t know how to do it well. But they’re missing out on a very big offline client base.” He added: “The phone still works.”

Wallen also shared an in-person tactic that his company deployed during its scrappy early days: hiring a magician named David to perform tricks for prospective clients at events and encourage them to visit the Engine’s booth. In short, old-fashioned personal engagement can work just as well, if not better, than the latest social media tricks.

Huang, who has managed droves of startups and shepherded them to success, said that once you have a customer, a lot of value can be derived from looking at how they use the product.

“Can you look into usage data and figure out if it’s recurring, if it’s increasing, if they are referring their friends?” Huang said. “It can come across in early usage and retention information as well. And then also, do you see some super fans, right, who even want to work at the company, promote the company, be attached to the brand?” She added: “You need to have ways to create evangelists who are really passionate about the product.”

On Friday, OpenAI said that it will train its models using cached copies and simulated versions of the internet instead of the live web because the models are too unpredictable to be trusted with live internet access.

The frontier lab announced its new policy two weeks after its rival, Anthropic, began limiting its newest and, in theory, most capable models from accessing the live internet. Researchers adopted these policies after months of incidents in which models pursuing training tasks broke into external systems in search of answers, collaborated on obscure message boards, and developed exotic hacking strategies.

“Based on the current state of alignment research, monitoring systems, and the security of internet infrastructure, we believe the responsible approach is to conduct frontier model training with no internet access,” the company said in a blog post.

OpenAI said that as recently as September 20, one of its frontier models exploited a misconfiguration to access the internet. That led the company to pause training work for the rest of the month so it could develop and test new approaches to securing its models.

The decision to train these models in sandboxes cut off from the internet is notable because employees at frontier labs have insisted that doing so would be nearly infeasible.

Keeping AI data centers separate from the internet “would need research teams to essentially be embedded inside of data centers in Iowa or wherever else around the world,” former OpenAI researcher Steven Adler told Fortune this month. “It’s a lot of overhead, and it isn’t clear to me that the safety benefits of it are large enough to merit just how large a tax it would be for a company to do it unilaterally.”

“In nearly every post I make on X, without fail, I get between five and ten comments about my inability to secure a sandbox, or asking why the hell we can’t just unplug the model from the internet,” an anonymous security engineer at OpenAI wrote in a September post on X. “These comments reflect a widening gap between where a lot of organizations and people are today and what the frontier labs are dealing with. There is also a general misunderstanding of how model training and evaluation work at these labs.”

The engineer, whose role has been confirmed previously by OpenAI, described how complex the training runs launched by OpenAI engineers are, noting that they require access a variety of resources and tools across many domains, and that thousands of researchers build, modify and test the environments. “So is it as easy as just putting it in a sandbox? No, it’s not,” he concluded.

Now, OpenAI says that “we must always be able to prevent a frontier model from achieving unauthorized, arbitrary access to the live internet.” That comes after the company deployed swarms of agents in red-teaming exercises that attempted to escape from their sandbox. That effort revealed a range of vulnerabilities that convinced the lab to adopt its new approach.

To meet that requirement while still reaching its training goals, the lab said it created a “a vast set of indexed and cached web content with which our models can interact, without risk of affecting external systems.”

Whether that strategy will perform as well as letting models run freely on the open internet remains to be seen, but it is noteworthy that both frontier labs have determined their most powerful technology can’t be left unsupervised on the digital commons.

At Harrow's Bengaluru campus, students wear the traditional Harrow hats and uniforms

On the outskirts of Chandigarh city in India's north-western Punjab state, a 473-year-old legacy British public school opened its first all-girls international campus earlier this year.

In contrast to the prominent Gothic-style central building on the school grounds in Bedfordshire, England, the Indian premises are more modern, with purpose-built academic blocks. But a conspicuous red-brick clock tower bears the imprint of the school's British heritage.

The Bedford School, Mohali, is the newest in at least half a dozen British public schools (elite, fee-paying private schools with a long history), including three of the country's oldest, that have set up shop across the length and breadth of India. Their expansion comes amid tighter market conditions in China and the introduction of VAT on private school fees in Britain from 1 January 2025.

Since 2023, Bengaluru - India's Silicon Valley and a hub of software and start-up companies - has been home to the prestigious Harrow School, alma mater of India's first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru. Shrewsbury School has a campus in Bhopal in central India, while Wellington College has chosen to set up base in Pune, a western city that calls itself the "Oxford of the East" for its well-regarded universities and colleges.

Millfield, Queen Elizabeth's School and a few others are reportedly on their way with satellite campuses in the coming years.

For decades, India has been home to well-established local boarding schools modelled on the colonial British public-school system, serving elite, aristocratic families. But this market has rapidly expanded with newly wealthy Indian families increasingly aspiring to give their children more global exposure.

"Our international curriculum is quite a driver for many of them," Caroline Pascoe, head of school at Harrow in Bengaluru, told the BBC.

Harrow and Bedford are among the few schools in India that follow an all-through Cambridge curriculum - a global education framework recognised at 10,000 schools worldwide - from primary through to A-levels.

Bedford School is the latest in at least half a dozen British public schools that have entered India

Harsimran Singh Kocchar, whose own extended family runs a chain of schools across India, says he decided to send his five-year old daughter Parineet to Bedford Mohali because of the "holistic focus of the school on character development, confidence and not merely academics".

He said he was also drawn to the fact the school did not have a "VIP culture" unlike many Indian equivalents.

"All kids are treated the same. Children of ministers and bureaucrats don't get greater privileges," said Singh.

Beyond the growing ranks of well-heeled local families, non-resident Indians who have either returned home after long stints abroad, or those still in the UK or Middle East wanting their children to connect to their roots, are also a significant market for these schools, says Pascoe.

Sixty per cent of Harrow's staff in Bengaluru are international and, like the others, it has imported its unique traditions to its Bengaluru campus: teachers are called Beaks, and students wear the traditional Harrow hats and uniform and take part in The Long Ducker - a cross-country race that happens across its schools globally. There is also a house system - a hallmark of traditional British education.

These quintessentially parochial quirks are blended with local customs such as singing the Indian national anthem every morning or celebrating Ganesh Chaturthi, a major Hindu festival.

Schools such as Harrow in Bengaluru promote extracurricular activities like music and drama

While India's growing economic credentials and burgeoning population of newly minted millionaires are a natural tailwind for expansion, this growth is partly being driven by a slowdown in China.

Much of the expansion of British public schools abroad in the past decade was driven by China and Hong Kong - half of the 151 satellite campuses operated by 50 UK schools are situated in these countries.

But that growth has slowed following changes in regulation, and "increased scrutiny, and even resistance, from national governments", according to a 2025 study by the UK-based Private Education Policy Forum (PEPF, external) think tank.

A new 2024 Chinese law that required schools to integrate patriotic education into all subjects further challenged expansion and even led to planned campuses being cancelled, prompting these schools to consider other locations like India and Vietnam, according to PEPF.

Moreover, in the 2024 Autumn Budget, Britain introduced a 20% VAT on private school fees. Naomi Atkins, founding principal at Bedford School Mohali, says this has forced many private schools to look overseas.

"They are in a desperate situation. Even those that hadn't previously looked for international expansion are now suddenly active in India," she told the BBC.

Well-heeled local families and non-resident Indians are a major target market for British schools in India

But India has not been an easy market, she admits.

Most of these schools are set up in partnership with local investors or operators.

While in the past schools in other countries might have been able to do deals at 10% of revenue - the fee that they would charge the local partner-investor - margins in India may have shrunk to as low as 1% in some cases, says Atikins.

There has also been some over-estimation of the size of the market, she says.

Even though India has nearly 250 million children enrolled in schools, English education is deeply entrenched in the country and competition from local players is much more intense compared with other non-English speaking countries like China, Korea or Vietnam.

India's per capita income is also far lower and the high price-points of the British schools - which can charge up to Â£15,000 or more for full boarding - can limit growth.

"I've worked in Indonesia, Malaysia and China but Indians are much more price-sensitive - they like a bargain," says Atkins.

That is why Bedford Mohali has set its fees at roughly half those charged by other British schools.

Between 2011-12 and 2022-23, the satellite campuses of British schools generated Â£152.4m in profit, according to PEPF, and this profit has grown substantially over the years.

But in India, it appears growth is likely to be slow and steady rather than explosive.

The country's old colonial roots and new money afford the legacy institutions a significant business opportunity, but it is unlikely to be big enough to offset the loss of the Chinese market, at least immediately.
