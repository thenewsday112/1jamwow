Trump will Selenskyj als Präsidenten der Ukraine weghaben

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Der US-Republikaner legt nach: Die Ukraine gebe den Vereinigten Staaten nichts zurück - obwohl sie viel empfangen habe. Und ihr Präsident sei bei den Friedensbemühungen "keine echte Hilfe".

US-Präsident Donald Trump hat die von Russland angegriffene Ukraine erneut fehlender Dankbarkeit bezichtigt. In Washington sagte der Republikaner: "Wir haben der Ukraine viel gegeben - sie hat uns nichts gegeben. Vergessen Sie das nicht." Er fügte hinzu, der ukrainische Präsident Wolodymyr Selenskyj sei bei den Friedensbemühungen "keine echte Hilfe". Es sei "an der Zeit, dass jemand anderes im Namen der Ukraine verhandelt".

Derweil meldet das Portal "Axios", Selenskyj habe sich beklagt, US-Verteidigungsminister Pete Hegseth verzögere absichtlich die Lieferung von Patriot- und weiteren Luftabwehrraketen, obwohl andere NATO-Staaten diese schon bezahlt hätten. Auf der Nachrichten-Website heißt es, Selenskyj habe den Vorwurf bei einem virtuellen Treffen mit republikanischen wie auch demokratischen Senatoren der Vereinigten Staaten erhoben.

Die Zeitung "Washington Post" schreibt unter Berufung auf eigene Informationen, der Stopp betreffe auch Lieferungen anderer Partner der Ukraine, die an eine Zustimmung der USA gebunden seien. Ein hochrangiger Beamter aus dem Verteidigungsministerium trat dieser Darstellung entgegen. Bei dem Programm, in dessen Rahmen NATO-Staaten US-Waffen für die Ukraine kaufen, habe es keine Änderungen gegeben, sagte der ungenannte Pentagon-Vertreter der Deutschen Presse-Agentur. Dasselbe gelte für den Austausch von Geheimdienstinformationen.

Selenskyj und der US-Sondergesandte Steve Witkoff wollen an diesem Dienstag in Norwegens Hauptstadt Oslo miteinander reden; Trumps Schwiegersohn Jared Kushner soll per Video zugeschaltet werden. Witkoff will im Anschluss den russischen Präsidenten Wladimir Putin in Moskau unterrichten. Er und Kushner sind die Repräsentanten der Vereinigten Staaten bei Bemühungen um einen Friedensprozess zwischen Russland und der Ukraine. Dazu waren beide schon mehrfach mit Putin zusammengetroffen.

Der gastgebende Ministerpräsident Jonas Gahr Støre erklärte, Selenskyj habe Norwegen und Finnland gebeten, ein Treffen "im Rahmen der laufenden diplomatischen Bemühungen zu einem für die Ukraine kritischen Zeitpunkt" zu organisieren. Ziel sei es, den Prozess "in konstruktivere Bahnen" zu lenken. Auch der finnische Präsident Alexander Stubb nimmt an dem Treffen in Oslo teil.

Die Kampfhandlungen gehen unvermindert weiter. Durch einen russischen Luftangriff in der Nähe von Kyjiw wurden nach Behördenangaben mindestens drei Menschen getötet. Die Leichen seien entdeckt worden, als Rettungskräfte den Brand in einer Produktions- und Lagerhallte im Kreis Obuchiw südlich der Hauptstadt gelöscht hätten. Ein weiterer Mensch wurde der Militärverwaltung zufolge bei Attacken in Saporischschja im Südosten der Ukraine getötet.

Bei ukrainischem Beschuss wurde nach inoffiziellen Berichten in der Region Tambow rund 400 Kilometer südöstlich von Moskau die Pumpstation einer Treibstoffpipeline getroffen. Der Gouverneur des Gebiets sprach von einem Brand an einer Industrieanlage.

Papst Leo XIV. bemühte sich erneut um einen humanitären Waffenstillstand in der von Russland besetzten ostukrainischen Stadt Oleschky. Nach einem Anruf bei Putin am Freitag telefonierte das Oberhaupt der katholischen Kirche in der Sache mit Selenskyj. Leo habe die Hoffnung zum Ausdruck gebracht, dass die Zusammenarbeit beider Seiten die Lieferung von Nahrungsmitteln und medizinischer Hilfe ermöglichen werde, teilte der Vatikan mit. Ebenso müsse die Zivilbevölkerung des Gebiet freiwillig und sicher verlassen können.

Russland begann vor mehr als vier Jahren mit seiner großangelegten Offensive in der Ukraine; Attacken im Osten des Landes hatte es bereits seit 2014 gegeben. Ukrainische Gegenangriffe richten sich vor allem gegen Industrie- und Militärziele im russischen Hinterland. Moskau nimmt seinerseits vor dem Wintereinbruch die ukrainische Energie- und Verkehrsinfrastruktur wieder verstärkt ins Visier.

Kritiker werfen Trump seit längerem vor, mit Blick auf den Ukraine-Krieg einen russlandfreundlichen Kurs zu fahren. Am Montag sagte der US-Präsident vor Journalisten, er sei offen für einen Dreiergipfel mit Putin und dem chinesischen Staatschef Xi Jinping - ohne Selenskyj.

Am Freitag hatte Trump bereits beschlossene Sanktionen gegen Russland kurz vor deren Inkrafttreten ausgesetzt. Die Kehrtwende erfolgte nur eine Woche, nachdem er ein umstrittenes Abkommen mit Putin über die Lieferung von russischem Diesel geschlossen hatte - und gut zwei Wochen vor den anstehenden Kongresswahlen in den USA. Bei den sogenannten Midterms am 3. November stehen Trumps Republikaner wegen gestiegener Lebenshaltungskosten und der vor allem wegen des Iran-Kriegs stark gestiegenen Spritpreise erheblich unter Druck.

AI takes on great experiment in self-rule

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Slowly but surely, the leading AI companies are creating a national self-governing structure to oversee their technology and its societal impact.

  • With the federal government largely hands-off, Anthropic, OpenAI and others are self-regulating their technology and now setting up their own plans to redistribute their wealth.

Why it matters: Not since the age of the robber barons have America's industrial titans wielded such sweeping power to shape society — and decide how their private fortunes should serve the public good.

  • In the modern era, Washington has largely exercised those powers through regulation, taxation and public spending.

The big picture: President Trump has no interest in new regulations. Instead, he has blessed a basic framework for the AI companies to regulate themselves — and potentially each other — outside of formal government monitoring.

  • Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Elon Musk's XSI, Meta and Nvidia are working on different versions of this. This is all voluntary and rests on the public trusting companies the size of industrialized nations to responsibly police themselves.
  • These companies are in a frantic race to create human-surpassing superintelligence. All are pressing ahead, even as their leaders — particularly at Anthropic — warn of potentially catastrophic risks and profound economic disruption.
  • At the same time, some AI leaders are promising to direct a portion of their vast wealth to benefit those outside their companies.

Zoom in: At least three competing visions are emerging for sharing the spoils of the AI boom, each with a different answer to who should control the wealth.

  • Anthropic: The company. On the eve of its blockbuster IPO, Anthropic pledged 5% of its shares to a new public benefit fund that could grow into one of the largest philanthropic institutions in history. At a hypothetical $2 trillion valuation, its stake would be worth $100 billion — more than the Gates Foundation's current endowment. Including earlier founder and employee pledges, roughly 20% of Anthropic's shares are earmarked for the public good.
  • Musk: The people. Musk responded to Anthropic's announcement by urging the company to give shares "directly to the public." On Monday, Musk pledged to give every American child a share of SpaceX next year — a staggering $10.5 billion giveaway at current prices. Musk called it the "first of many," though he offered no details on how the unprecedented distribution would work.
  • OpenAI: The government. CEO Sam Altman has floated giving the U.S. government a 5% stake in OpenAI through a sovereign wealth fund — potentially handing Washington a multibillion-dollar interest in one of the world's most powerful AI companies. Altman envisions an Alaska-style fund that could distribute the industry's investment returns directly to Americans, though the plan remains preliminary and would likely require congressional approval.

Reality check: The numbers are staggering. So are the assumptions about future wealth, billionaire follow-through and the public's willingness to trust its benefactors.

  • Bill Gates and Warren Buffett launched the Giving Pledge in 2010, a voluntary promise by billionaires — including Musk — to give away most of their fortunes. Gates built a philanthropic empire, only to become a lightning rod for conspiracy theories.
  • Anthropic's ambitions depend on extraordinary growth projections — its own employees envision 17% GDP growth in 2030 — while Musk has a history of making spectacular promises that fail to materialize.
  • Anthropic's roots in effective altruism, a philosophy deeply distrusted by the Trump administration and its allies, also raise a more fundamental question: Who gets to define the public good?

Between the lines: No industry requirements or consensus exist on how best to redistribute their expected wealth. But Trump Accounts — which allow families, businesses and individuals to invest in tax-advantaged funds for children — are emerging as a favorite vehicle for free-market AI leaders.

  • Billionaire investor Brad Gerstner, a driving force behind the program, is championing a vision of broad-based ownership in which ordinary Americans share directly in the economic upside of the AI revolution.
  • SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell and her husband in July pledged roughly $320 million in company shares to 2 million children through Trump Accounts — a potential blueprint for distributing an even larger slice of Musk's fortune.

What to watch: The Trump administration hasn't fully relinquished its regulatory authority, potentially complicating the emerging experiment in AI self-governance.

  • After Anthropic disclosed that its AI had submitted a false homicide tip to Philadelphia police and improperly interacted with government websites, the White House declared that AI companies must report security incidents and remedy any harm.
  • "This notification and remediation process is not optional," Trump's Super Intelligence Force warned — a striking assertion of federal authority just weeks after the president blessed a voluntary system of industry oversight.

Axios' Zachary Basu and Bradley Olson contributed reporting.

Empathy divides Americans by religion, with white evangelicals standing apart

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Data: PRRI, 2026 American Values Survey; Chart: Russell Contreras/Axios

White evangelical Protestants are the only major religious group in which a majority says empathy can become dangerous when it leads people away from a society guided by God's truth, according to a new survey.

Why it matters: The survey lands amid a quiet civil war within American Christianity over who deserves empathy.

  • Conservatives ranging from evangelical pastors to Elon Musk have framed empathy as a vulnerability in debates over immigration, racial justice and LGBTQ+ rights.
  • In a February 2025 interview with Joe Rogan, Musk called empathy "the fundamental weakness of Western civilization," while acknowledging its potential good and warning against its weaponization.

By the numbers: The annual American Values Survey from the nonpartisan Public Religion Research Institute (PRRI) released Tuesday found that 42% of white evangelical Protestants agreed that empathy is a foundational moral value that helps build a healthy society.

  • Separately, 54% agreed that empathy can become dangerous when it leads people away from a society guided by God's truth.
  • In every other religious group, PRRI found that at least 66% agreed with the foundational-value statement, including 73% of white mainline Protestants and Hispanic Catholics.
  • Empathy's foundational value was endorsed by 83% of religiously unaffiliated Americans, 80% of respondents grouped under other non-Christian religions, 77% of Jewish Americans and 65% of Latter-day Saints.

The intrigue: The survey found a 30-point partisan gap: 84% of Democrats agreed with the foundational-value statement, compared with 54% of Republicans. Independents were at 70%.

  • White evangelicals stand out among religious groups, but PRRI reports religious and partisan results separately.
  • The survey doesn't establish how much white evangelicals contribute to the Republican-Democratic gap.

Zoom in: The divide also tracks with PRRI's Christian nationalism measure. Among people PRRI classifies as Christian nationalism adherents, 38% agreed that empathy is foundational, compared with 94% of rejecters.

  • PRRI classifies respondents as Christian nationalist adherents or sympathizers based on support for a U.S. government and laws grounded in Christianity and the view that Christians should exercise dominion over society.
  • Six in 10 adherents agreed that empathy can become dangerous, compared with 6% of rejecters.

What they're saying: "As Christian nationalist leaders attack the sentiment of empathy, a disturbing 'empathy gap' has opened, separating Republicans and white evangelicals from most other Americans," PRRI President and Founder Robert P. Jones said in a statement.

  • Jones said the findings raise questions about the moral foundations that let people from different backgrounds share a democracy.

Methodology: PRRI conducted the American Values Survey online Aug. 20–Sept. 7, 2026, among 5,200 adults in Ipsos' KnowledgePanel and 339 additional adults recruited through opt-in panels to increase sample sizes in smaller states. The survey covered adults ages 18 and older in all 50 states and the District of Columbia.

  • The margin of sampling error is ±1.6 percentage points at the 95% confidence level for results based on the entire sample.

Why Calling Anti-Trans Hate a “Distraction” Is So Troubling

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s a familiar line in the midterms: Anti-trans attacks are a “distraction” from “kitchen-table” issues. That sends a chilling message about whose rights are worth fighting for.

Over the past decade, open bigotry against immigrants, trans people, Muslims, and vulnerable communities of all kinds—has moved even more firmly to the center of US politics. At the same time, many people trying to combat the forces churning up this hate have settled on a familiar line: that the attacks on these groups are a political “distraction” from the “kitchen-table” issues facing Americans. It’s become such a common refrain that a recent poll indicated a majority of US adults endorse it. And it’s a line being used over and over again by Democratic candidates throughout this year’s midterms.

It’s easy to see why this framing has taken hold. By painting inflammatory positions as desperate attempts to evade discussion, proponents hope to disarm them. The conventional wisdom is that somewhere in the imagination of the average voter, there are core issues that really matter—usually to do with the cost of living. These, they contest, should be the rightful focus of political debate.

But embedded in this taxonomy of “distractions” and “core issues” is a series of assumptions about whose opinion matters in an election, and more importantly, whose rights are worth fighting for. Many of us in the groups being most commonly targeted by the far right are left to wonder if this dogmatic pursuit of the least controversial platform is less a strategy than a cultivated apathy—or, worse, whether our ostensible allies ever cared for us at all if it is so easy to cast us aside.

I have lost count of how many times I’ve left work over the past five years only to realize one push notification later that I had more rights in the morning than I did in the afternoon. The week after Trump’s passport executive order, I kept forgetting to breathe. While social media swirled with speculation about what this was meant to distract the public away from, visions of a grim future unspooled in my head. When it feels like your world is ending and the most people can be bothered to say about it is that something actually important must be happening somewhere else, the clear message is that what is happening to you is a second-tier issue—that you don’t really count.

The distraction discourse also doesn’t account for what happens when politicians supposedly on the “good” side embrace bigotry themselves. Is the response to, say, the recent decision by South Carolina Democratic Senate nominee Annie Andrews to release an anti-trans ad to dismiss it as a distraction—or to ignore it entirely? This wasn’t just a rhetorical shift. This was an otherwise progressive politician being dragged into the realm of authoritarian prejudice—the kind of choice that has potentially life-altering consequences for those of us who live here under the bus. Shouldn’t that matter?

On a practical level, the distraction strategy has also failed Democrats repeatedly. In 2024, strategists convinced Democrats to court the center exclusively. Despite retroactive claims to the contrary, no overtures to pro-trans policy were made by the Harris campaign. Her answer on the question of protecting gender-affirming care was middling. Her foreign policy on Palestine was hawkish. Rather than attack xenophobia at its roots, she bragged that her policy on immigration in many ways would be harsher than Trump’s. None of us need to be reminded that she lost that election decisively. Those she was cynically attempting to court were mostly unpersuaded, while those of us whose lives were determined by concessions made in pursuit of the biggest possible tent were edged out of what we’re told is our only political home.

Yet, in the aftermath, those who had crafted this approach claimed inexplicably that the opposite had happened—that Harris had lost because she had pandered too much to the left. Today, while Democrats pivot past an imaginary center towards the right, Republicans continue their pursuit of minority groups undaunted. If it is a distraction for Democratic politicians, it appears to be a permanent cornerstone for the GOP.

Moreover, claims to “distraction” fail to understand how reactionary politics work. Cultural issues, broadly speaking, are used to glue together value systems in the service of creating a larger, unified voting base. Project 2025 was particularly terrifying because, however small and insignificant each of those individual positions may have appeared on their own, together they created a cohesive framework governing every aspect of life. Xenophobia, gender policing, and the criminalization of reproductive freedoms are animating principles of fascism. They give life to it.

Today’s campaigns are light on facts and heavy on emotional appeals. It matters that both of Trump’s terms were won by appealing to emotions while Democratic candidates focused on pragmatics. Emotional appeals are so central to the conservative platform that more money has been poured into anti-trans advertising than any other issue, and those expenditures show no signs of slowing down. They believe they’ve struck gold, in part because they do so in the absence of much opposition.

The fact that these ads do not appear to be moving the immediate political needle—Republicans are significantly underperforming in advance of next month’s elections, after all—doesn’t negate the impact they have had, particularly when it comes to enforcing near-total silence or, worse, active complicity from Democrats toward the attacks on trans people. As Democrats continue to fold on trans rights, they have allowed far-right actors to cement anti-trans and anti-immigrant animus into national policy.

Research suggests that rather than appear more reasonable, parties who cave on contested territory only convince the general public that their opposition to those parties was correct. There is no ground the party can cede that will satiate fascism, and a party that espouses equality but fails to defend it is morally hollow.

While it’s true that bombast historically has been the refuge of desperate politicians, many of them went on to reshape the world in their own brutal but otherwise perfunctory images. History’s villains clearly show it is possible to galvanize large swaths of the public through hate in order to seize and maintain power.

When Trump first declared that he would “secure the border” in 2015 by erecting an enormous wall across the entire southern US border, this too was labeled a distraction even well into 2019—an unserious proposal to mask the absence of cogent policy. Democrats had already shown themselves to be quite committed to border warfare, with former President Obama having escalated deportations during his two terms far above his predecessor. But building a wall was exactly what Trump ultimately did. As migrants drown trying to survive concertina wire and gunfire from newly erected border fortifications, we must concede that this was not merely a performance. Trump’s anti-immigrant policies have metastasized. Eight years after his initial promise to build that wall, Trump’s running mate made the astonishing claim that Middle America was besieged with immigrants eating the pets of their neighbors. That man is now vice president.

A plurality of voters do not draw such a careful division between pragmatic policies and those rooted in emotional appeal. The attention violent crime is given vastly outweighs the impact it has on the average voter. The most violent crimes—those involving high-capacity firearms—remain fundamentally unaddressed. Meanwhile, thinly veiled anti-Black racism couched in “law and order” rhetoric has pushed US policy ever rightward for 40 years.

Even if we accept that at one point the issues in question had once been chosen to “distract”, they became intimately woven into core policies of the GOP. They are part and parcel of every minute of Fox News. There is historical precedent. When abortion, homophobia, and anti-feminism were centralized as pillars of the conservative platform during the “Moral Majority” in 1979, it may not have been a driving force behind American politics quite yet. But soon it was, and it laid the ground for the modern far right to germinate and take root.

These issues weren’t used to draw attention away from the policy failures the party had championed. They were instead used to make these more enticing, to appeal to a broader audience and signal the new direction of conservative policy. They harnessed the anger of conservatives towards the anti-war movement, the rise of feminism, and the gathering successes of the civil rights movement, and directed this energy toward institutional capture. Policing bodies, and specifically reproduction, was a brass ring to hold out to those who felt they had otherwise lost control of their lives. As church-aligned politicians embraced anti-abortion policies to build voter power, these policies began to impact how those voters saw not only the ballot but the church itself. A feedback loop was created, reshaping the modern conservative movement less toward small government, more toward the promise of a bulwark against Gomorrah.

At the point at which every major university has had its curriculum reconfigured to comport with the approved national history, where major landmarks have been taken down, renamed or otherwise defaced to obscure painful facets of our history, where gender policing has been enshrined by executive order down to middle school soccer games, is it still distraction or does it yet count as policy? Facing these policies head-on, not as a passing phase but an entrenchment of authoritarianism, is an existential imperative. Marginalized communities whose lives are increasingly imperiled by this entrenchment cannot wait. While it will be a very long time before any of us can breathe deeply again, a bold and affirmative defense of human rights is necessary for our survival.

Jessica Kant is a writer and licensed independent clinical social worker based in Boston.

Inside India’s Gen Z Cockroach Janta Movement

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Young Indians are challenging the purge of voter rolls by Modi’s right-wing government. Shrishti Khanna, who was detained by Delhi police while covering the protests, explains.

A student activist aligned with left-wing organizations holds a poster of Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi amid the ongoing protest in solidarity with the youth-led Cockroach Janata Party demanding the resignation of the country’s chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, over alleged voter roll irregularities linked to the “special intensive revision,” in Kolkata on October 11, 2026.

Shrishti Khanna told me we would talk about developments in India that night. Then I didn’t hear from her.

When she resurfaced the next afternoon, she explained why: “I passed out after a tussle with the police.”

She had spent the previous days in Delhi documenting the youth-led protests as an independent journalist and increasingly found herself caught up in the state’s response to them. On October 10, police detained her and four other reporters near Jantar Mantar, the city’s designated protest site, and took them to a police station in Sarai Rohilla, several miles away in north Delhi. No one told them what offense or legal provision they were being held under, she says. They were told only that they would be released at sunset. The detention itself, she says, was rough: Three or four female officers lifted her off the ground and shoved her against a police van.

Now, she told me, she was home and “just sore everywhere.”

“Well, until the next call for mass gathering,” she added.

The protests have become a rare public challenge to Prime Minister Narendra Modi, whose Hindu nationalist Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) has governed India since 2014. Many are organized by the Cockroach Janta Party, which began in May as an online joke after India’s chief justice, Surya Kant, likened jobless young people to cockroaches. Kant later said he meant holders of fake law degrees. By July, the movement had helped force the education minister to resign over leaked entrance exams.

Its current target is a nationwide revision of the electoral rolls that has deleted roughly 130 million names, according to Election Commission figures. The commission says it is removing the dead, the relocated, duplicates, and voters officials could not trace; critics say eligible citizens, disproportionately Muslims, have been struck off. Since protests demanding the chief election commissioner’s resignation began on October 2, Delhi Police have banned large gatherings, detained protesters, and opened an inquiry into harassment complaints from women journalists.

Khanna is a Mumbai-based writer and political activist whose work on India-Israel relations, authoritarianism, and digital surveillance has appeared in The Wire, The New Arab, and Azad Essa’s Hostile Homelands newsletter. I asked Khanna what has driven her generation into the streets.

Shilpi Chhotray: For readers in the US who are just meeting the Cockroach Janta Party: What is it, and why has it caught fire with young Indians?

Shrishti Khanna: The Cockroach Janta Party is a youth-driven protest movement that uses satire, Internet culture, and the language of political resistance to challenge the Modi government and what protesters see as the erosion of democratic rights in India.

I think part of its appeal is that it speaks a language young people already understand. Humor, memes, and political satire can make participation feel accessible, especially to people who haven’t previously been involved in organized politics.

SC: Your generation has grown up almost entirely under Narendra Modi. How has that shaped the young people now on the streets?

SK: For many young Indians, Modi’s government is not simply one political administration among several. It is the political environment in which they have grown up.

They have witnessed the normalization of Hindu majoritarianism, the shrinking space for dissent, attacks on universities and student movements, and the increasing use of state institutions against critics. They have also grown up in a political environment where television news, social-media algorithms, and political propaganda play an enormous role in shaping public opinion.

This creates a contradiction. Some young people have internalized the government’s political worldview because it is what they have been exposed to for most of their lives. Others are beginning to question the institutions and narratives they once considered normal.

I wouldn’t describe this generation as uniformly progressive or anti-government. Young Indians are politically divided. The significance of these protests is that some are beginning to challenge a political order that, for much of their lives, appeared almost permanent.

SC: Something that resonates with me as an Indian American is the generational divide over Indian politics. What happens when a young person’s political awakening puts them at odds with the people who raised them?

SK: In India, political ideology often enters the home through religion, caste, nationalism, and family expectations. So when a young person begins questioning the Modi government, Hindutva, or the treatment of minorities, it can become much more than a disagreement over electoral politics.

For some families, support for the BJP has become closely tied to their understanding of national identity, religion, and patriotism. Criticizing the government can therefore be interpreted as criticizing the family or even the nation itself. Young people may face pressure to remain silent, accusations of being anti-national, or restrictions on their participation in protests.

But I also think we should recognize that political disagreement within families is shaped by material dependence. A student who relies on their parents for housing, education, and financial support does not necessarily have the freedom to express dissent without consequences.

Political awakening can be isolating, but it can also lead people to build communities and political relationships outside the structures in which they were raised.

SC: Let’s get to the voter rolls. Roughly 130 million names have been removed. From the outside, that sounds bureaucratic. Why has it become a question of power for your generation?

SK: The voter-roll revision, or Special Intensive Revision, is not simply a bureaucratic exercise. It is a question of who gets to participate in India’s democracy and who gets excluded.

There are serious concerns that the exercise has disproportionately affected Muslims, marginalized communities, and eligible voters whose names have been removed despite their right to vote. These are often people who already face significant barriers when it comes to documentation and proving their eligibility.

What makes this particularly alarming is the timing. The BJP government has benefited politically from elections held after these revisions, which have taken place close to polling dates. When eligible voters are removed from electoral rolls, they can lose their opportunity to participate in an election that may determine who governs them for the next five years.

SC: And if those voters are later restored?

SK: Here’s the most disturbing part: Even if those voters are later found to have been wrongfully excluded, the election is not automatically conducted again. The BJP may already have won, formed the government, and consolidated its power. Restoring someone’s name to the electoral roll afterward does not restore the vote they were denied.

This raises fundamental questions about electoral legitimacy and whether voter-roll revisions are being used to facilitate electoral manipulation. We cannot establish that an election was rigged simply from the number of deletions, but wrongful exclusions on a significant scale, particularly if politically targeted, can undermine the fairness of the result.

SC: You’ve seen the state’s response up close. On October 10, you were detained while covering the protests. Take me through that day.

SK: On October 10, I was in Delhi documenting the protests as an independent journalist. I was near Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Marg, close to Jantar Mantar, at approximately 10:30 in the morning. I was not participating in the protest. I was there to report on what was happening, alongside other journalists.

Despite that, Delhi Police detained me along with four other reporters and took us to the police station at Sarai Rohilla. We spoke to Inspector C.P. Bhardwaj, but we were not given any clear explanation of why we had been detained. We weren’t informed of any specific offense or legal provision under which we were being held. Instead, we were simply told that we would be released at sunset.

What concerns me is that we were there in a professional capacity, documenting events of public interest. Our detention was subsequently reported by The Quint, and Digipub News India Foundation issued a statement condemning the detention of journalists, explicitly naming me. The question is why journalists carrying out their work were detained in the first place, and why the police could not provide a clear legal justification for restricting our freedom.

SC: When you messaged me, you said you were “just sore everywhere.” What happened?

SK: The police were very rough, and that was the case with not just me but everybody.

For me, there were three to four female police officers who came toward me when I was reporting, and they picked me up, all of them. I was lying down on the ground. I was resisting as much as I could, but they picked me up from everywhere. My pants were literally falling, and I had to scream that my clothes were falling, please help. They paused for a second, but then they shoved me against the police van. There were so many people being detained. It was a big crowd. They didn’t want me to go anywhere.

SK: Women were subjected to a lot of violence, and also sexual assault and groping. The groping and sexual assault, fortunately, did not happen to me.

My hair was fortunately tied up, but I saw people who had ponytails or longer hair have their hair pulled so officers could get a grip on them and pick them up. SK: Yes, absolutely. Reporting on Palestine has fundamentally shaped how I understand state violence, surveillance, and the role of journalism, including here in India.

Palestinians are subjected to systems of mass surveillance, facial recognition, automated targeting, and military control. These technologies do not remain confined to Palestine. India is particularly important here because it is Israel’s largest arms customer. The two countries have extensive military, intelligence, and surveillance cooperation.

And then there is what Israel has done to Palestinian journalists. Gaza has become the deadliest conflict for journalists ever documented by the Committee to Protect Journalists. These were people documenting what was happening to their own communities, often because international journalists were not even allowed into Gaza. SK: When I look at protests in India, I see some deeply concerning parallels in the methods of surveillance and repression, although the scale and circumstances are very different.

We have seen Delhi Police deploy facial recognition systems and surveillance vehicles at Jantar Mantar. We have seen journalists detained despite identifying themselves as members of the press. And we have seen how the language of national security and maintaining public order can be used to justify restrictions on protest and documentation.

SC: Is that a challenge for the Cockroach Janta Party itself?

SK: One of the most important questions for a movement like CJP is whether it can move beyond opposing Modi personally and confront Hindu majoritarianism as a political structure.

A movement cannot meaningfully claim to defend democracy while remaining silent about the systematic marginalization of Muslims or the persistence of caste oppression. If young people are demanding voting rights and democratic accountability, those demands must extend to communities whose rights have been under attack for years, not only to those who have recently begun experiencing repression.

SC: Let’s talk about the name. It started as an insult from India’s chief justice. Why did the cockroach stick, and what does humor let young people do that conventional political language doesn’t?

SK: There is something politically interesting about taking an insult from someone in a position of institutional power and turning it into a collective identity.

The name itself is deliberately absurd. It plays on the naming conventions of Indian political parties, particularly the Bharatiya Janata Party, or BJP, while turning the cockroach into a symbol of political survival and defiance.

Humor allows young people to ridicule authority instead of fearing it. But humor also has limitations. A meme can bring thousands of people onto the streets, but it cannot necessarily explain why unemployment exists, how caste and class shape access to education, or how political institutions protect existing power structures.

I think the question is whether humor becomes an entry point into political education and organizing, or whether the movement remains dependent on viral moments. Because the state can survive being mocked. What threatens entrenched political power is sustained, organized resistance.

SC: The Congress party and other opposition parties are now marching over the voter rolls too. What happens when a youth-led movement starts to intersect with established parties?

SK: There are both opportunities and risks. On one hand, established political parties have organizational networks, resources, parliamentary representation, and access to institutions that a new youth-led movement may lack.

On the other hand, there is always a danger that the movement’s demands become absorbed into electoral competition, particularly if political parties begin treating young protesters as a constituency to be mobilized rather than people with independent political demands.

SC: So what would it take for this moment to become lasting political power?

SK: I think we need to distinguish between opposing the BJP and building an alternative political project.

A movement cannot measure its success only by the resignation of a minister, the removal of an election official, or even a change in government. Those may be important victories, but they do not automatically transform the material conditions that produced the anger.

For this moment to become lasting political power, there needs to be sustained organizing beyond Delhi and beyond social media. There needs to be engagement with workers, farmers, students, Dalits, Adivasis, Muslims, and communities already resisting displacement, exploitation, and state violence. The movement also needs internal democracy, political education, and accountability to the people it claims to represent.

Ultimately, the question is whether young people are being mobilized for a moment or organized for a struggle that continues long after the headlines disappear.

Mamdani Is Facing Heat Over the NYPD. Here’s What He Should Do.

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The mayor is being given a series of false choices over his public-safety agenda. But there is another way forward.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani speaks during an unveiling ceremony for a new 9/11 Memorial Wall at One Police Plaza on September 9, 2026, in New York City.

In recent weeks, New Yorkers have been presented with a series of false choices about how Mayor Zohran Mamdani should address public safety and the power of the NYPD.

Some have said that we can either support the police wholeheartedly or see crime and disorder rise. Others have warned that the city must implement conventional procedural police reforms or the NYPD will abuse its power. Others have suggested that the mayor must fire Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch or surrender all hope of creating a more just city.

There is perhaps no more powerful or politically sensitive entity for a New York City mayor to handle than the NYPD. Moving in any direction on policing invites anger from some portion of the mayor’s constituents. But there is a way for Mamdani to fulfill the public-safety policy pledges he campaigned on without derailing his administration. (Full disclosure: I served on the mayor’s transition team after his election, though I have no ongoing role in his government.)

Ten months into his tenure, Mamdani has avoided making an enemy of the NYPD—an accomplishment for any mayor, let alone a democratic socialist. But that peace has come at a cost: letting a variety of problems at the agency fester, or even worsen.

Since Mamdani took office, the NYPD has significantly increased low-level arrests, leading to higher headcounts at Rikers Island and backlogs in the court system. NYPD officers have repeatedly performed crowd-control duties that have facilitated ICE raids throughout the city. The Strategic Response Group unit continues to take an aggressive and at times violent approach to protests. The city’s independent Stop and Frisk Monitor has accused the NYPD of continuing to engage in unconstitutional stop-and-frisk activity and systematically underreporting the number of stops. NYPD officers accused of misconduct continue to face little or no consequences for their actions. And the NYPD continues to put the names of people accused of no crime into the racially discriminatory “Gang Database.”

Mamdani’s failure to act on these issues contradicts several of his campaign pledges. He said he would dismantle the Strategic Response Group, end the Gang Database, close down Rikers Island—which requires reducing low-level arrests—and invest heavily in a new Department of Community Safety to enhance the city’s capacity to prevent crime before it happens. The good news is that the mayor can still follow through on every one of these pledges—while avoiding some of the false choices currently on offer.

One thing Mamdani definitely does not need to do is reach for ultimately hollow versions of “police reform” that we already know won’t work. During the campaign, he rightly avoided talk of changes to training, symbolic policy adjustments, or other forms of proceduralism that have consistently failed to produce results over the last 10 years. These kinds of reforms produce the worst of all worlds: They don’t meaningfully change anything, but they still antagonize police unions and other entrenched forces.

Mamdani has mostly avoided these traps in office. (He did pledge more support for the Civilian Complaints Review Board, and has followed through on that to some degree, but this is a bit of an empty gesture since the police commissioner routinely obstructs the board’s investigations and overrules its findings.) Unfortunately, in response to the anger about the NYPD’s conduct in recent weeks, the mayor has suggested a pointless review of “use of force” policies that is destined to produce nothing of substance.

Police accountability organizers have also begun calling for the firing of Commissioner Jessica Tisch following the ICE shooting in Marble Hill and the subsequent violent NYPD response to protests there. While I counseled against retaining Tisch as early as the summer of 2025, firing her, without significant changes to public-safety policy, would be a hollow victory. If the mayor is serious about a new path for creating safety, he should focus on the substance of his agenda, not just the symbolism of who is in charge. The police commissioner works for him and should be instructed to make some major changes or, yes, step aside—the stance taken by New York City DSA. But the emphasis should be on the changes, which should be implemented in a responsible, transparent, and comprehensive manner.

As Mamdani made clear on the campaign trail, police are not the only tool for producing safety. The safest communities have low crime not because of intensive policing but because people’s everyday needs are being met. Delivering on the mayor’s affordability agenda and improving government services are important long-term strategies for enhancing public safety and community well-being.

But we also need immediate strategies to directly address crime and disorder conditions. That means following through on the mayor’s pledges to robustly fund the Department, now Office, of Community Safety, by expanding community-centered strategies to address violence, mental health crises, gender-based and domestic violence, hate crimes, and subway safety. The leadership of the new office is working hard to do this, but results have been slow to manifest, and the mayor has undercut their work with messaging that centers police as the primary tool for producing safety. Instead of leaning into this narrative, Mamdani can both dial back his material and rhetorical support for police and improve public safety through these essential evidence-based interventions.

Here are some of the changes that the mayor could make to enact his campaign pledges, respond to real community concerns about NYPD abuse, and maintain and possibly improve public safety.

The NYPD must be instructed to dial back low-level “broken windows” arrests. These are placing a tremendous burden on already poor and vulnerable communities. They are used to target almost exclusively poor people of color, many of whom are experiencing homelessness, untreated mental health and substance use disorders, and lack basic incomes.

The mayor must immediately enhance services for these populations so that everyday conditions improve for them and the communities they live in. That means adopting a robust Housing First approach that puts people in permanent housing with support services rather than endlessly cycling them through shelters, jails, hospitals, and transitional programs that fail to take into account their need for long-term stability and cost more than permanent housing. New York City currently has units designated for people in crisis, but there are often too many restrictions on accessing them and a lack of appropriate services to help people maintain stable housing.

The city also needs to expand community-based mental health and substance use facilities, including Clubhouses, Sobering Centers, Mental Health Crisis Respite Centers, and medically based drug treatment. And the mayor should look at a minimum basic income policy to create a floor for people who have been left out of the job market.

The mayor should create a plan to phase out the use of the gang database and gang suppression policing more broadly. Most of the people on the database are there simply because the police suspect they may be connected to illegal activity and violence, not because they have committed any concrete offense. There is no independent evidence that these practices reduce crime—but plenty of evidence that the harassment and criminalization of young people can actually make violence more likely.

The mayor should follow through on his prevention pledge and enlist intensive neighborhood violence reduction programs to work with these young people in real time—both to diffuse disputes and connect them with stable housing, help with mental health and substance use, and income supports. This means expanding and improving the current Crisis Management System by opening new sites and increasing their access to essential crisis stabilization services. This system has already been shown to drive down gun violence. Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg is already doing something along these lines, referring young people on the margins of violence to well-established programs to help steer them in a positive direction rather than surveilling and harassing them until they can be arrested and incarcerated.

The mayor does need to fulfill his promise to eliminate the current Strategic Response Group, but just as importantly, he needs to address the policies that guide the unit’s activities. Ever since it was created under Mayor de Blasio, the SRG has seen its mission primarily as one of suppressing any dissent that it perceives to be either too disruptive to daily life or too politically radical. Former NYPD chief of department John Chell recently posted that the SRG is hated because it has successfully “dominated” protesters in the service of “protecting and serving the true community members.”

The NYPD must be forced to accept that disruptive protest is a necessary and even desirable aspect of democracy and that people who engage in First Amendment activity are also legitimate community members. This means that the goal of protest policing must not be to suppress disorder at all costs.

Instead, the mayor should pursue a policy of “negotiated management” that emphasizes good communication with demonstrators before and during protests and tolerance of some disorder to help maintain an overall peaceful protest. This can be achieved in part through civilianizing the city’s approach as much as possible by creating a civilian office of community engagement that works with groups before demonstrations, issues permits, and has a visible presence during demonstrations to help keep protests on track. This can be used for the vast majority of protests that the SRG currently manages through intimidation and suppression.

That doesn’t mean that the NYPD will have no role to play in the immediate future. There will still need to be some kind of capacity to manage the possibility of violent protest and to make, when necessary, mass arrests in a responsible manner, but the SRG and its anti-terrorism focus are the wrong unit with the wrong approach.

Not all of this can be done overnight. It requires new investments, program development, and community consultation. But the mayor should begin publicly committing to move forward with these changes and implementing them as they are ready, rather than continuing to lean into a police-centered narrative about public safety and superficial calls for procedural reform that amount to little more than box-checking.

So What Is Socialism, Anyway?

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a world dominated by neoliberal market fundamentalism, “socialism” is what will make a more moderate, regulated form of capitalism work for most people.

In electoral democracies, politics is most often about the future: making things better than they currently are. Hope pervades as the dominant emotion, accompanied by a tenacious sense that nothing will actually change. Thirty-five years after the collapse of actually existing communism, right-wing political leaders around the world have resurrected the hoary images of the Red Menace in demagogic populist appeals to their followers to stop the drift toward the left of young people fascinated by a language of progressive political possibilities.

Most Americans are opposed to what they think is socialism without having any clear idea of the variety of meanings attached to that word. In the last decade, a majority of the young have become disillusioned by the failure of the liberal elite and of liberalism more generally and have been gravitating toward something that they see as an alternative to the capitalist status quo. A year ago, a Gallup poll showed that only 54 percent of Americans favored capitalism, while 39 percent had a positive opinion of socialism. Democrats viewed socialism more positively than capitalism—66 percent to 42 percent, respectively—while independents were modestly more pro-capitalism than pro-socialism (51 percent vs. 38 percent). Republicans remained overwhelmingly pro-capitalist (74 percent vs. 14 percent). In earlier polls, three out of five Americans supported a national health plan, sometimes called Medicare for All, in which all Americans would get their insurance from a single government plan. Even more people favored the government providing free tuition to anyone attending a two- or four-year university. Two-thirds believed that taxes should be raised on the rich to curb the runaway polarization of wealth in the country.

Conservatives once again are deploying the word “socialism” as a weapon against the left. So, we might ask, what is socialism anyway? Why are young people calling themselves democratic socialists, and why are such candidates as Zohran Mamdani able to win elections? Have we arrived at a moment when it has become possible to separate the enduring appeal of socialism’s humanist values that have inspired so many in the past from the dark history of the Soviet experiment and the legacy of Stalinism?

While the right in the United States and Europe gleefully and without much reflection deploys the term socialism to denounce everything from social welfare programs to environmental regulations, it is possible to examine the word historically and distinguish the cluster of core meanings that still possess a power to animate and mobilize millions from its frequent and deliberate misuse. Like words such as “liberalism” and “conservatism,” socialism works, as literary critics would put it, as a “floating” or “empty signifier,” that is, a word with multiple, indeterminate, and contested meanings. Yet it has a history, and despite its frequent misuse—after all, even the Nazis called themselves “national socialists”—the word indicates an aspiration to move beyond capitalism toward a more egalitarian, socially just political and economic order.

At its most fundamental level, socialism is the political philosophy that believes that public policy ought to promote primarily the common good rather than simply individual or corporate interests. From the early 19th century, socialism involved democratic politics—the empowerment of all the people, social justice for all, and equality (not only of opportunity, as liberals believe, but of reward as much as is practically possible). Socialism, then, stood (and for many still stands) opposed to the proposition so central to classical liberal (now conservative) economic ideology that individual greed will magically produce the greatest good for the greatest number.

The indispensable critic of modern market economics, Karl Marx, argued that capitalism, with its enormous productive capacities as well as its fatal contradiction between the abundance and poverty it engendered, would eventually give way through revolution or evolution to the collectivist egalitarianism of socialism. Here his optimism and idealism may have gotten the better of his usually sober realism about the social world. More perceptively, he warned that social environments—in his words, modes of production—shape human ambitions and possibilities.

Along with the society of competition and greed that capitalism fostered, it also created a corresponding “human nature” that demanded ever-increasing accumulation, exploitation of the natural world, and the separation of individuals from their common purpose. While it nurtured the possibility of a more socially connected economy, capitalism daily tore asunder the empathetic bonds of men and women, rendering them egoistic atoms in a state of Darwinian competition. Such a system, particularly in its neoliberal variant, which proudly proclaims that the market can do everything if left to itself, perpetuates the class division between a wealthy minority and the vast majority without capital and assets. Capitalism operates for the benefit of the shareholders, and those who can pay, not for the general good. The material world and the moral world move in opposite directions.

For those who embrace its positive meaning, socialism is a utopia—not in the sense of an unattainable goal but rather of a direction in which people might direct their political desires. In what was practically a valedictory essay, the Marxist historian of socialism George Lichtheim (1912–1973) wrote:

Socialists are at a minimum committed to economic planning that goes counter to the operation of an economy in which private firms predominate and profits are distributed among shareholders and managers. A system of this kind necessarily perpetuates the class division between a wealthy minority and a propertyless majority. A capitalist economy, moreover, can only respond to “effective demand” backed by actual purchasing power. It operates for the benefit of paying customers only and does not recognize the existence of other people. In consequence it normally fails to satisfy basic material needs except in a roundabout way that is not to everyone’s taste. To put it crudely, the logic of the system entails the production of luxury goods for the few, rather than the provision of food, clothing, and decent housing for the many.

To most Americans, who by world standards are extraordinarily affluent, this stark statement of one understanding of capitalism may seem exaggerated. To the global poor, it reflects the lives they know.

Yet the normative assertion that society and the economy ought to work for the many, not only the few—the socialist critique of capitalism—has always been more potent and persuasive than the presentation of alternatives to the way economies work in our own time. The intermediate solution outside of the Soviet-style communist systems and neoliberal capitalism has been the welfare state: capitalist market economies but with state regulation and provision for the sick and less able in a democratic polity. Social democracy, or what Bernie Sanders calls “democratic socialism,” which runs from the American New Deal to Swedish socialism, preserves the market but directs state policy toward the public good with the conviction that individuals and markets may operate with a far different logic when left on their own. As Lichtheim understood, “Taken by itself this is a democratic rather than a socialist line of reasoning,” for it “leaves the wage relationship unaltered” and the means of production in the hands of capitalists. Certainly an advance on unfettered neoliberal capitalism, social democracy, nevertheless, must be distinguished from socialism, in which profits would go, not to the owners of capital, but to society at large.

The state ownership of Soviet-style “socialism” (what might more accurately be called “Stalinism”) and the state capitalism of fascism were understood by many Western Marxists to be perversions of the authentic social ownership of socialism. “If socialism were to become permanently identified with the kind of life imposed after 1945 on Eastern Europe,” Lichtheim wrote, “few sane people would want it.” For him, socialism meant the means of production were to be owned by the whole community and operated democratically.

Today, in our world of plutocrats and oligarchs with their political allies embedded in the state apparatus, it is unlikely that popular ownership of the means of production is attainable. In a more limited sense, socialism might be seen as the democratic intervention into the economic as well as the political sphere to reorient production and consumption toward public ends. The critics of Obama, Biden, and the Democratic left have a point. A healthcare system that eliminated fee-for-service medicine (which Obama did not propose) and provided guaranteed healthcare for all (which he did) would be a kind of socialism, whether administered by government or, as in Switzerland, by regulated nonprofit private insurers. Municipal services that work for the common good, such as fire departments and public schools, would be socialist, as are the national parks and the post office. Imagine if entry into the grandeur of Yosemite were calculated on a monetary basis. What would it cost to view El Capitan if we calculated a rare resource and high demand? And, by the way, can FedEx deliver a first-class letter from Ann Arbor to California for 50 cents and make a profit?

Socialism is defined both by its ends and its means. Its ends are policies defined by the public good, a movement toward greater democracy and equality, well-informed participation in politics and economics by its citizenry, and programs, both governmental and in the private sector, that aim toward greater material security for all. Inequality and unearned advantages and privileges conferred by the ownership of property are the enemies of socialism, and therefore policies aimed at the common good are in tension with the power of the propertied who shortsightedly look to the profit of corporations and individual owners of capital.

The means to socialism, as the bitter experience of the Soviet Union perversely demonstrated, cannot be coercive but must be consensual. Socialism cannot be imposed; it has to be acceptable and accepted, that is, democratically chosen. That, of course, may be the greatest obstacle to a socialist future, given the naturalized hegemony of bourgeois normality.

Utopian or realist, socialists have to be frank that their values of equality and social justice run up against the desires of people already shaped by centuries of capitalism. Marxists believe that social environments shape human ambitions and possibilities, and consequently, along with the society of competition and greed that capitalism fostered, that competitive, individualistic system also created a corresponding “human nature” that demanded ever-increasing accumulation, exploitation of the natural world, and the separation of individuals from their common purpose. Socialist values run up against the way people currently think, feel, and want, embedded by the lived experience of capitalism.

The regulated economy of socialism would likely compel people to give up the search for ever-greater output, ever-higher standards of living, the latest iPhone, and increasing consumption of goods and exploitation of the natural world. And what about the majority of people around the world who have not yet attained minimal standards of comfort, health, or even survival? Both in the statist economies that imploded by 1989 and in democratic capitalist societies like Obama, Trump, and Biden’s America, there was and is, as Lichtheim reminded us, an “inherent conflict between two quite different and possibly irreconcilable goals: economic growth and social equality.”

It has been said that capitalism is intolerable and socialism impossible. That may be the inescapable dilemma of our age. But as historians continually remind us, nothing is fatally determined or inevitable. We should not mistake the present for the future. There is always a degree of choice, a rational, radical middle road to a better future. As someone who has studied the USSR and international socialism for over half a century, I have come to think that concepts like socialism and capitalism, which at first seem to define opposing economic systems, are, in fact, intimately, even fatally, connected.

The current debate in the United States around socialism does not in fact involve socialism in its classical meaning. Even among strong supporters of open borders, an end to the carceral state, and defunding the police, few are openly advocating nationalizing major industries or turning factories over to workers. Here, it is actually an argument between adherents of two competing ideas of capitalism: one, the neoliberal, libertarian model favored by the Republicans of a system responding almost exclusively to market imperatives and eschewing regulation and state interference; the other, advocated by most Democrats, proposes a return to New Deal Keynesianism, a capitalism that requires regulation in the interest of the people’s welfare and protection from naked market forces. Raising the red flag of fear, conservatives contend that responsible governmental regulation is “creeping socialism,” which will lead a free society along the “road to serfdom.”

Realistically, in a world dominated by neoliberal market fundamentalism, in which inequality between the top and bottom of society grows daily, “socialism” is what will make a more moderate, regulated form of capitalism work for most people. Looking at the past of failures and the present of confusion, socialism offers the possibility of a different logic to be applied to public policy, a democratic intervention into the economy, redirecting it toward a public good and the salvation of the planet. We have learned from the Soviet experiment that there is no real socialism without democracy, and we are daily coming to the realization that there can be no real democracy—the empowerment of all the people—without socialism.

The Clarion Call of “NAZA”

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor’s new film not only documents the horrors of the present but gives us a glimpse of a possible future too.

More than 74,000 Palestinians have been killed by Israeli troops in Gaza over the past three years. There are more than 20,000 children among the dead—an unfathomable number. We may never know exactly how many people were killed and how many died of secondary causes, such as sepsis or cancer. The environment, with packs of ranging dogs and the atomizing effects of kilotons of explosives, has done its part to efface those facts.

NAZA, a new documentary by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, works in a somewhat different direction. It seeks to document the particulars of what did take place, to preserve the truth through a series of interviews with two dozen perpetrators—primarily Israeli intelligence officers—on a rooftop in Tel Aviv. By focusing their interviews on these intelligence officers and data scientists, the men who built the air force’s targeting algorithms, Abraham and Szor anticipate and undermine any arguments that the atrocities committed in Gaza occurred on the ground and were strictly the result of targeting Hamas fighters. Instead, we learn that the massive death toll occurred through calculated and dispassionate appraisals of when it would be easiest to kill a particular Palestinian—usually when that person was at home with his family—and that all of the “collateral” deaths (tens or even hundreds of them, depending on the target) resulted from these deliberate policy decisions.

Early in NAZA, we see a view of the Israeli occupation of Gaza in the 1970s. We are treated to propaganda from another era, quaint depictions of a smiling, benevolent mid-career Israeli army officer describing his good works to the attentive and smiling Arabs, somehow in their fellahi garb—not an ahistorical detail, but an unrepresentative one. What we are watching is the installation of some of the first telephone infrastructure in Gaza, built by the Israelis and designed from the outset with surveillance in mind.

A voiceover in Hebrew explains that the system will, in the years to come, serve as one of the best ways for Israel to monitor Palestinians and control them. What we are seeing is the modern surveillance state, the one we all now inhabit, at an early stage in Gaza.

A half-century after those images were first recorded, we are taken to 2023—into a world whose currency is destruction on a massive, historical scale. One in which Microsoft’s cloud infrastructure is used to capture millions of hours of private phone conversations between Palestinians in Gaza every day.

Most of the shots in the film are constructed elegantly, with Abraham and his interlocutor facing each other in shadow. The backdrop consists of the lights and glass towers of a modern city—the city of Tel Aviv. Close-up shots punctuate these conversations, capturing intimate moments across various high-rises—a hand brushing crumbs from a table; someone watching the evening news in an apartment. Here, Israelis enjoy their privacy and experience life just as many people in Europe and the United States do. But not so for the Palestinians living in Gaza, a mere 37 miles or so to the south.

Each of the film’s two dozen interviews explores different contours of the mass murder. There is Lavender, the software program that “identifies” Palestinian targets for execution, often permitting the murder of 20 or even 500 people in order to assassinate a single man, and Where’s Daddy?, another software program used to track Palestinians on a kill list; and then there is the unit tasked with targeting diplomatic threats on behalf of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s office. There are men who describe themselves as “cogs” in a far more elaborate machine, and others who acknowledge their instrumentality in the destruction.

One of the men that Abraham interviews, perhaps mindful of the setting, describes how Al Rimal, a neighborhood he helped destroy in Gaza City, resembled Tel Aviv—tall glass modern towers, now gone forever. The viewer is thus invited to imagine NAZA: Tel Aviv; 2,000-pound bombs are indifferent to where they land and whom they maim or kill, after all.

There is little new information in NAZA (the title is an acronym taken from the phrase nezek agavi, or “collateral damage” in Hebrew). Abraham has reported most of his findings about the operations of the Israeli army in Gaza in Local Call and +972 Magazine over the course of the past three years. For example, a portion of the film is devoted to the workings of Lavender, a story that Abraham had already reported in April 2024, when only 34,000 lives had been taken.

But herein lies the challenge for the film. American and Israeli Zionists have attached themselves to the term NAZA to illustrate Israel’s righteousness. They ask, reasonably enough, why any damage would be described as “collateral” if the goal were to inflict wholesale destruction—one of the conditions of the genocide charge. The very use of the term indicates that not all damage is desirable; some of it is simply unavoidable. Abraham, for all his deftness as an interviewer, fails to directly tackle the sleight of hand involved in the NAZA descriptor. The rejoinder here is maybe too obvious: that the existence of programs like Lavender are little more an exercise in implausible deniability. A low or high error rate is beside the point when mass murder is the goal. We know—based on the statements of Israeli leaders themselves, and through the behavior of the perpetrators—that mass murder was the intention all along. The extermination of civilian life was the goal; the software merely put some distance between the operators and their works.

Yet there is still plenty to learn from NAZA, not least about the perpetrators themselves. One man describes the experience of watching through a drone camera as other Israeli soldiers execute starving Palestinians at an aid-distribution site. He and the others, at various points, speak as a proprietor would: These Palestinian lives belong to them. But they are not valuable or prized possessions, or even ones that could be usefully employed at a job site building settlements. The Israelis in the film relate to the Palestinians as disobedient and tiresome animals.

This attitude conveys the internalization of the phrase “human animals,” articulated by the war criminal and former minister of defense Yoav Gallant. It also explains the plaintive statement made by one of the perpetrators in the film: that neither he nor any of the others in his unit were “Arab haters.” They were just people doing a dirty job because, to paraphrase another of the interviewees, “If I don’t do it, someone else will.”

The enormous effort made by the Israelis in NAZA to describe their country’s trauma after the Hamas attack on October 7 can thus be summarized less as “How could you?” and more as “How dare you?” For them, the Palestinian offense is real, and so too is the injury to Israeli pride, and so they must correct the nature of the power relationship between the two groups, the balance between subject and object, and the value of a Jewish Israeli life as compared with those of the subalterns next door.

There is a genre of Jewish Israeli cultural production—films in particular, but also novels and TV shows—that is described as “shoot-and-cry.” Typically, an Israeli man (it’s almost always a man) behaves badly and then sheds tears about it. Those tears, and the inevitable self-reflective soliloquy or dialogue that follows, are meant to contrast his and his society’s high ideals with his and his society’s base behavior.

We learn that there is high ground to be staked in the regret, the rueful contemplation of one’s own actions. Whatever the individual does, whatever his society requires that he do, his humanity is recoverable through his tears. The genre is designed to demonstrate to Israeli or Western audiences that the perpetrator’s humanity can be restored, despite everything. Or that he never relinquished it in the first place. He is an object of pity, a by-product of a savage reality.

NAZA avoids this trap. The perpetrators are given no real opportunity for self-indulgence. Abraham and Szor seem to understand that lamentations cannot erase what these Israelis have done in Gaza, what they continue to do.

Abraham’s critical distance as an interviewer is appropriate to the subject matter and the crime. He doesn’t cozy up to the perpetrators; he doesn’t try to sympathize or empathize with them. He doesn’t indulge their apparent desire to self-abnegate for the greater good, or whatever they think they’re doing. He asks questions; they answer. And when they evade or obscure things in their response, he asks follow-up questions.

Abraham and Szor have been criticized for veiling the identities of the people they interviewed, but I think this criticism misses the mark. With hundreds of thousands of perpetrators in Israel, there is no shortage of targets for prosecution, which is a project of the Hind Rajab Foundation. The perpetrators’ identities are anonymized in NAZA so that the filmmakers can collect the evidence, the testimonies, and the confessions that document what Israel is doing in Gaza.

Abraham and Szor’s effort is consistent with the goals of the Archive Genocide project. Palestinians will obtain justice, but it may come more slowly than many of us would like, and it may require a different set of tactics and methods in different settings.

One of the criticisms of NAZA made by Palestinians has been that it’s Oscar bait—a movie that instrumentalizes their pain and serves mainly as a monument to the filmmakers’ egos. I disagree with this assessment.

Still, there are frissons. At several points in the movie, the perpetrators are invited to draw the UX interface of their computer panels, where they sat and decided who (and how many other people) to kill. The scene is shot from above to capture what’s being drawn. The pens are high-quality felt pens with black ink—not the kind of pen I have on my desk, or the pen I’d grab from my kitchen as I rush out the door in the morning. The paper, too, is canvas-like, thick—not a standard item on the shelf.

What is one to make of the fact that a cinematographer planned these shots, knowing what they would contain? That care and artful attention were given to something that could have been depicted with a Bic and ordinary notepad? I understand: All art forms require art, and all artists must make art. If, in depicting a devastating fact, an artist or filmmaker suffuses the scene with quality—a sensibility that may be appreciated by the discerning viewer or member of the grand-prize jury—well, who can blame them? And yet…

I mentioned the surveillance state earlier. We learn through NAZA about some of the capabilities that modern states possess. The Israelis on camera reveal that they record Palestinians on their phones. They track movements and peruse digital photographs. They feed geolocation data into a computer model to map social interactions and surmise behaviors. And then they target people on that basis.

The tools of totalitarianism exist, and they are here to stay. What the Israelis have developed and utilized to eliminate life in the overwhelming majority of Gaza, every other government either possesses or will possess in the future. We are charged, as citizens of a constitutional democracy, with legislating protections for our civil liberties. I only hope that NAZA acts as a clarion call for people around the world. The film shows us one version of the future, which may be, in the end, one of its most meaningful achievements. But it also reveals how far along the path we’ve traveled. The threat has arrived—no quaint benevolence here—and it must be stopped.

Ahmed Moor is a writer and fellow at the Foundation for Middle East Peace.

On March 3, 2020, Sen. Lindsey Graham walked into a private dining room at Charlie Palmer Steak in Washington, D.C. Graham’s time was expensive. He was meeting with two executives and a lobbyist from a Hawaiian naval defense contractor called Navatek. And he had required them to commit to $40,000 in campaign contributions for a 45-minute meeting.

Navatek’s CEO, Martin Kao, was confident that was all the time he needed.

“Shoot all he has to really do is stop by and say ‘yes,’” Kao wrote to a Navatek lobbyist and a fellow executive the day before, in an email seen by ProPublica. “That should only take a minute and he’s on his way. We don’t need to pretend we give a shit about each other.”

At the dinner, Graham ate little and talked a lot. But Kao got the “yes” he was hoping for: Graham agreed to steer a $10 million government contract Navatek’s way in his native South Carolina, Kao later recounted to the FBI. In exchange, he said, Graham asked Kao to pledge a total of $100,000 to his reelection campaign.

In early 2025, the FBI opened a sweeping investigation into bribery on Capitol Hill with Graham’s steakhouse dinner at the center. Kao confessed to federal agents that his business model consisted of bribing public officials in exchange for government contracts, and that the research Navatek was providing to the government was often worthless.

The scheme involved some of the most powerful figures in Congress, he said — in both parties. In South Carolina, Kao told agents, Navatek made a campaign cash-for-contracts deal not only with Graham, but also with the state’s top Democrat, Rep. James Clyburn, and another influential Republican, Rep. Joe Wilson.

Kao told agents that Graham in particular left little room for ambiguity: “This is how much I want, and how much I am going to give you,” Kao remembered Graham saying, according to notes one agent took during their debrief in late 2024.

Kao’s admissions would constitute a vast criminal bribery scheme if they could be proved in court — a challenge, given recent Supreme Court rulings that have made it more difficult to win corruption cases. Kao himself posed another hurdle. He was facing prison for fraud and campaign finance violations and was speaking to the FBI in the hopes of reducing his sentence.

But the FBI agents who took Kao’s confession — two experienced anti-corruption investigators — could see that much of it lined up with a trove of records they had seized from Navatek. They forged ahead. The FBI took the formal step of naming two former Navatek executives who had worked with Kao as subjects of a bribery investigation. The agency did not designate Graham, Clyburn and Wilson that way, but the agents were actively investigating their interactions with Kao. The agents indicated to their superiors that the lawmakers could become subjects in the future, according to records viewed by ProPublica.

The details of this investigation have not previously been reported.

President Donald Trump returned to the White House and almost immediately gutted the specialized FBI and Department of Justice teams dedicated to taking on public corruption — an act of revenge for having previously investigated him. The two experienced agents on the case were swept up in the purge, and the career prosecutor assisting them quit after Trump appointees forbade the DOJ’s anti-corruption section from filing any new cases. Today, there is no one left to look into Kao’s explosive allegations, as the Trump administration has abandoned any pretense of prosecuting public corruption. The case is dead.

ProPublica has unearthed the investigation by reviewing thousands of pages of legal documents, internal company emails, evidence the FBI used to vet Kao’s claims and dozens of interviews with people who saw how Navatek operated, including an eyewitness to Graham and Kao’s secret steakhouse dinner.

This story is part of a series based on our reporting. Last month, ProPublica reported that the FBI had probed the conduct of Maine Sen. Susan Collins, who steered millions in government funding toward Navatek while Kao gave her illegal campaign donations. Collins denied wrongdoing and dismissed Kao as a “convicted liar.”

Kao is indeed the only person involved to serve prison time. He pleaded guilty to making illegal campaign donations and a loan fraud scheme in 2022. In 2025, he was sentenced to 87 months in prison.

Graham died at 71 earlier this year. His sister, Darline Graham, who was appointed to fill his seat, retained most of his senior staff, including those who dealt with Navatek. She even took up some of his committee assignments, including appropriations. Darline Graham is running for a full term in office this November.

Clyburn, 86, is one of the most powerful Democrats in Washington. He is widely credited with tipping the scales for Joe Biden during the 2020 Democratic presidential primary. Wilson remains influential on national defense spending from his senior perch on the Armed Services Committee.

“Whatever a donor may think or say, a donation would never cause Congressman Clyburn to increase his support for the donor’s request,” Jami LaRue, Clyburn’s communications director, wrote in response to detailed questions. “The Congressman has always striven to assist the University of South Carolina and all of the colleges and universities in his district when called upon to further their important missions. The crimes and lies of an imprisoned fraudster will not cause the Congressman to waver in his support for these fine institutions.”

David Snider, Wilson’s communications director, did not address a detailed list of questions sent by ProPublica. “Congressman Wilson does and has always abided by campaign donation regulations set forth by the Federal Election Commission,” Snider said in an email.

In an emailed statement, Taylor Reidy, who was Lindsey Graham’s communications director and now holds the same post for his sister, wrote that “any allegations of quid-pro-quo behavior or wrongdoing by the late Senator Lindsey Graham or his staff are simply false.” She said the accusations were the “ravings of a convicted felon” and that her office was never contacted by the FBI about the matter. After Kao’s arrest, they disgorged his donations, she added.

The two executives who were subjects of the FBI investigation, Duke Hartman and Joshua Knight, were never charged. Hartman, the other executive at the dinner meeting with Graham, is now the CEO of a new naval research company called Integer Technologies that he co-founded with Knight after Kao’s arrest. Integer was recently recognized as one of the fastest-growing government contractors in the country. It also became the ultimate beneficiary of the Graham dinner meeting, picking up the contract Graham allegedly agreed to facilitate that night.

Hartman and Knight did not respond to detailed requests for comment.

“[It’s] corruption at its best,” Kao wrote to ProPublica from federal detention in South Dakota, before cutting off contact. “But I am the only one rotting in prison.”

Kao’s dinner with Graham was part of his grandiose plan to make Navatek a major defense contractor.

He had become the CEO at an uncertain moment in the company’s history. Founded in Hawaii in the 1970s, the company originally thrived on a close relationship with the state’s powerful senior senator, Daniel Inouye. When Kao assumed ownership in 2019, it had been several years since Inouye had died, and Navatek was in need of a new patron in Washington.

At times, Kao hardly seemed the person to pull this off. He had started out as the company’s CFO and was openly uninterested in its technology. He told bombastic lies about his personal life. Former employees said he regaled them with tales of selling newspapers for members of the Bloods and Crips street gangs as a youth. In a deposition, Kao later admitted that wasn’t true.

But what he lacked in engineering chops he made up with ambition. Once, Kao held a company town hall using a map of the U.S. dotted with future Navatek offices as his backdrop, a former employee recalled. “He would talk about how we’re a $10 million company, and in the next three years, we’re going to be a billion-dollar company,” said a former engineer. “He was an absolute megalomaniac.”

Kao hatched a plan to expand Navatek’s presence across the country, making new friends in Congress along the way. He courted politicians using something he called “the method,” as ProPublica detailed in September.

Navatek would open an office in the state or district of a powerful member of Congress and partner with a local university to concoct a proposal for federally funded research. Then, aided by a well-connected lobbyist and the sheen of credibility from its association with the university, Navatek would approach the member for federal research funding.

Kao told the FBI he would strike deals in small, face-to-face meetings with the members or their trusted staff. Navatek — through executives, family members and lobbyists — would make generous campaign donations. In exchange, the member would add detailed language to the defense budget to fund the company’s work.

That legislative language would not name the company. But it would describe specific work that Navatek was already planning to do behind the scenes, making it the only logical choice for the eventual government contract. The lawmakers’ staff would also backchannel with the Navy after the bill was passed to make sure contracting offices knew to direct the money to Navatek, emails seen by ProPublica show — even though the contracts were in theory competitive.

Kao “wanted to build a company as big as he could as fast as he could,” a former executive later testified in court. For a while, he succeeded. Under Kao, Navatek went from having to “rebuild,” as the executive put it, to winning $40.7 million in new government contracts in 2020.

Hartman, the other Navatek executive who went to dinner with Graham, was in charge of maintaining friendly ties in Congress while tracking the millions of dollars the company had requested in research funds. He kept careful tabs on the company’s progress using dozens of color-coded spreadsheets. “If anyone knows the impropriety of the business model, it’s him,” Kao told the FBI, referring to Hartman.

Later, these spreadsheets provided the FBI a detailed picture of which congressional offices worked closest with Navatek. In the tables devoted to South Carolina, Navatek denoted Graham as a “key supporter.”

The company primarily targeted lawmakers from smaller states who sat on appropriations committees, like Collins, Kao explained to the FBI, because they wielded huge power over the budget while having fewer “constituents to ‘feed.’”

The senior senator from South Carolina was another prime target. “You can get access to Lindsey Graham,” Eric Schiff, a former Navatek executive, said in an interview.

Kao, Hartman and other Navatek executives approached the offices of Graham, Clyburn and Wilson — all powerful members of the South Carolina delegation — in the beginning of 2019. The company had recruited the University of South Carolina for a joint research project, and together they asked the lawmakers to find $9.5 million to fund the work. If it succeeded, Navatek planned to ask Congress to re-up the funding for two more years as well as green-light two more projects, internal company records show, for a total of up to $90 million in South Carolina alone.

Yet Navatek had “zero people” capable of doing the research it was pitching, said a former executive with direct knowledge of the work.

Its $9.5 million “power and energy” proposal promised cutting-edge research on how to distribute the limited power on board a ship between multiple energy-intense systems, like radars, lasers and rail guns. Graham’s office later boasted that the work would lead to new technology that would “ensure that our fleet is ready for the fights of the future.”

In reality, Navatek’s initial research “looked like a college senior’s graduation project,” the executive said. “Something you go get from RadioShack.” Graham’s office referred questions about the quality of the work to the Navy and the university. The Office of Naval Research, which awarded the contract, did not respond to questions.

But for Congress, that lack of expertise wasn’t a problem. Throughout 2019, the FBI noted, as the ensuing year’s budget came together, both Graham’s and Clyburn’s offices took steps to make sure it included Navatek’s funding.

In May 2019, Willie Lyles III, Clyburn’s legislative director, alerted Navatek that the House Appropriations Committee would endorse new funding for a “modular and scalable integrated power and thermal system capable of powering a directed energy weapon system of 100 or more kilowatts,” according to an email flagged by the FBI. Three days later, that exact language appeared in the House Appropriations Committee report. Lyles did not reply to a request for comment.

In September, the Senate Appropriations Committee — Graham’s committee — released a budget report that set aside $9.5 million in funding for “Talent and Technology for Navy Power and Energy Systems.” Agents noted that Navatek may have ghostwritten the technical description that accompanied this line item because “an almost identical paragraph” appeared in Hartman’s internal spreadsheets two months before the Senate report became public.After the Senate advanced its version of the budget in the fall, Navatek dropped $4,799 to send bourbon to a handful of lawmakers, including Graham and Collins, the FBI found in company records — a likely violation of Senate gift rules. Both Collins’ and Graham’s offices denied receiving bourbon. Navatek later sent Clyburn a fruit basket from Edible Arrangements for $102.60.

Congress passed the final spending bill containing $9.5 million for Navatek’s pet project on Dec. 19, 2019.

At that point, Kao and other Navatek executives had not made any donations to Clyburn, Wilson or even Graham.

That would change within days of Congress passing the budget, when an executive pledged $750 to Wilson’s campaign at a fundraiser held at Bullfeathers, a Capitol Hill watering hole for lobbyists and Hill staffers, emails show. In the following weeks, Kao and his lobbyist gave Wilson’s campaign $3,500.

And bigger checks were imminent. Kao kicked off 2020 by making plans to spend $300,000 at a slew of political fundraisers, according to text messages filed in court and a recent deposition. Navatek’s lobbyists at that time were seeking private meetings with Graham, Wilson and Clyburn, where Kao planned to ask for significantly more federal funding. Kao eventually met with all three, and each asked for $100,000, he told the FBI.

During that same period, Kao and Hartman met a top fundraiser for Collins at a Corner Bakery in Washington, D.C. As ProPublica reported, that meeting led Kao to donate $150,000 to the pro-Collins super PAC. Kao told the FBI that the money was a bribe in exchange for the senator’s support for more Navatek funding. The head of the super PAC and Collins have denied Kao’s version of events.

Kao told the FBI it was not unusual for donations to come after the favor: “We became clever in our own right and figured out how to dangle the carrot,” he wrote in a memo seen by ProPublica, “until we saw some assurance that the Ask was in the budget.”

To secure a meeting with Graham, Kao hired Michael Tongour, a veteran South Carolina lobbyist who was old law school friends with the senator and family friends with Clyburn and Wilson. Graham was up for reelection against a well-funded challenger, Jaime Harrison, and had a crowded fundraising schedule, said Tongour, but they eventually found a time.

“The Graham fundraiser says that it [sic] you want to reserve a date for Senator Graham to attend a private dinner, the campaign wants to get $40,000 in contributions,” Tongour wrote to Kao, Hartman and lobbyist Andy Winer, in an undated email seen by ProPublica. Winer, whom Kao described as Navatek’s chief lobbyist, chimed in to say that Graham could stay only 45 minutes, and Kao replied that all they needed to hear was “yes.”

“These discussions are very frank and oddly not at all awkward,” Kao told the FBI. “I suppose once we got that far, everyone knows the deal.”

Graham showed up late to his March 3, 2020, dinner with Kao, Hartman and Tongour. A fundraiser working for Graham appeared first and accepted an envelope from Kao containing checks made out to Graham’s campaign, Kao said. Kao did not know the fundraiser’s name; in an FBI debrief, he simply referred to the person as the “money man.” After that person departed, Graham entered.

Graham, Kao recalled to the FBI, was “very abrupt” and “straight to the point”: He asked how much Navatek wanted, to which they said $10 million a year for three years. Graham asked Kao to pledge $100,000 in donations in exchange for his support, Kao told the FBI.

Navatek was juggling multiple proposals in South Carolina worth around $10 million, and it’s not clear which one Kao was describing.

Congress had just approved the $9.5 million for the power and energy project, but Navatek still needed the Navy to award the money in the form of a contract. The company was also about to ask Congress to fund the same project for a second year at $10.5 million. On top of that, Navatek was planning to pitch a new research proposal, at $10 million, to study the transport of supplies inland from ships using autonomous vehicles. It was another area where Navatek had “zero past performance,” the former executive said.

The same week of the steakhouse dinner, Graham’s office was working behind the scenes with Navatek and the University of South Carolina to make sure the Navy sent the first $9.5 million to the right company.

A day before the dinner, an official with the Office of Naval Research emailed Graham’s office to ask about the money, which “the Senator may have an interest in.” Craig Abele, Graham’s national security adviser, called her a few days later to confirm that Graham’s office had intended the money to be awarded to the Navatek partnership, emails show. “Spoke to ONR this morning,” Abele wrote March 6. “Should be set.”

The company received the contract, which it split with the university, in July 2020.

Abele became Graham’s deputy chief of staff and still holds the position for Darline. He did not reply to an email requesting comment. A Graham spokesperson confirmed to ProPublica that their office had indeed added the $9.5 million to the budget at the request of the university and Navatek. The spokesperson said the research was supported by the Navy and rejected the notion that Graham was motivated by campaign cash.

Graham’s spokesperson said requiring a fundraising minimum for a dinner was normal practice.

“[It’s] corruption at its best. But I am the only one rotting in prison.”

The checks Kao handed over at the dinner added up to about half of the $40,000 he had agreed to raise for the dinner, according to Tongour. The rest came in May, campaign records show.

“My understanding at the time was that the contributions associated with the event were lawful political contributions,” Winer said. “I never advised Kao to make a political contribution in exchange for official action. I never participated in, witnessed or had knowledge of any illegal political contribution.”

After Kao’s 2020 indictment, the University of South Carolina broke off the partnership. “University officials were not aware of any political contributions from Navatek,” Jeff Stensland, a university spokesperson, wrote in an email. “Our sole objective in partnering with them was to expand our research enterprise.”

One week after the dinner, on March 10, a University of South Carolina lobbyist sent written requests to Graham’s office for $20.5 million in appropriations: $10.5 million to continue Navatek’s power and energy research and $10 million for its new autonomous vehicle research.

Tongour confirmed many elements of Kao’s story. In a recent interview with ProPublica and subsequent emails, he remembered Kao giving the envelope full of checks to Graham’s fundraiser and even agreed that Graham kept the meeting short. “I don’t recall that he stayed more than 30 minutes,” Tongour said in an email. “Certainly not for appetizers, entrée, and dessert.”

But he denied Kao’s most significant claim: that Graham and Navatek struck an explicit, potentially illegal deal for contracts.

“I would remember that forever,” Tongour said. Graham supported Navatek’s requests, he said, because they were good for the state. “If I had seen that kind of quid pro quo, I would have quit earlier.” Tongour fired Navatek as a client later that year, immediately after Kao’s arrest.

Navatek considered Graham its chief patron in South Carolina, internal records show, just as it viewed Collins in Maine. But the company also wanted support in the House.

For that, Navatek turned to Clyburn, a member of Democratic party leadership and one of the most powerful figures in Congress. Agents found Navatek emails setting a meeting with Clyburn for early March. The goal, Kao said, was to make sure that the House draft of the budget “mirrored” whatever Graham inserted in the Senate version.

Over a meal, Clyburn asked Kao how much he was donating to Graham and then asked for the same amount, $100,000, Kao told the FBI.

The FBI tallied $20,000 in contributions that Kao and his family members made to Clyburn a few days after their scheduled meeting. A month later, Hartman arranged for a $10,000 gift to Clyburn’s alma mater, South Carolina State University, according to an email seen by ProPublica. Hartman noted it was “Congressman Clyburn’s favorite school.”

The spokesperson for Clyburn confirmed the dinner occurred but did not respond to questions about what was discussed.

Kao and Hartman struck a similar bargain with Wilson, Kao told the FBI, at a private lunch he secured by donating to his campaign. The FBI pulled records of $3,500 in donations Kao and Tongour made in late 2019 and early 2020.

Wilson has an influential role on the House Armed Services Committee, which authorizes defense spending. Wilson was “very clear and knew what the ask was,” Kao told the FBI. He wanted however much Clyburn was getting in campaign contributions in exchange for his support, Kao said.

Tongour denied this. “There was no ‘pay to play’ with Joe Wilson or his staff either,” he wrote in an email, adding that he donated because Wilson “is a friend and I want to see him re-elected.”

Kao claimed to the FBI that he paid for meals with all three politicians, but campaign finance records appear to show the lawmakers’ campaigns picked up the check.

By August 2020, Hartman had gotten word that Graham’s and Wilson’s committees would each support part of the company’s requests, internal company records show. Clyburn was not on key committees while he was a party leader. In one of his myriad spreadsheets, Hartman projected that the final budget could include up to $15.5 million of the $20.5 million the company had lobbied for.

Kao told agents he was gearing up to make more significant donations to Graham, Clyburn and Wilson by the end of the year. Instead, on Sept. 30, 2020, he was arrested.

Kao reflected on his time at Navatek in a deposition taken last month inside a federal prison. The company’s founder has sued Kao, saying his stewardship defrauded Navatek of millions.

“It’s nothing more than government contracting done through placating senior appropriation members for contracts,” Kao said. “The whole thing is a grand lie.”

Kao’s arrest plunged Navatek into disarray. Federal prosecutors charged him with defrauding a federal loan program; not long after, he was also charged with making illegal donations to the Collins campaign and super PAC. Lobbyists, universities and congressional offices abruptly cut off contact. “Don’t call us,” one congressional office told an executive, he later testified.

The Collins campaign said it disgorged Kao’s contribution. Yet the senator, facing a tight reelection bid, has had to field questions following the ProPublica report about her support for Navatek and why the super PAC supporting her accepted the $150,000 donation from the shell company in the first place.

As Navatek sank, Hartman began building himself a lifeboat. In 2021, he and Knight started a new military research company headquartered in South Carolina called Integer Technologies. Integer’s first project was one that Navatek had brokered with Graham.

Hartman didn’t face any criminal charges at the time and left Navatek largely unscathed. He would later settle a civil lawsuit brought by his former employer, which contended he played a role in Kao’s campaign finance and loan fraud schemes, for $425,000. He admitted no wrongdoing.

In September 2024, FBI agents flew to Hawaii for what would be their final interview with Kao. They pressed him for details about what happened in South Carolina, quizzing him on his dinner with Graham and a second in-person meeting he said he had with Clyburn. Agents wanted Kao to help them find more records that would back up his account and asked him to point them toward lobbyists in D.C. who he said facilitated the pay-for-play scheme.

As the year came to a close, FBI agent Michelle Ball felt she had enough material to move forward. The FBI had “uncovered a lengthy history” of a government contractor making donations “in exchange for government contracts,” she wrote in a Dec. 9, 2024, memo viewed by ProPublica.

In federal corruption probes, agents often begin by focusing on lower-level targets with a goal of working their way up to more prominent figures. Ball named Hartman; his business partner, Knight; and their company, Integer, as the formal subjects of the probe, as they prepared to potentially issue subpoenas and gather more evidence. They had Graham, Clyburn and Wilson in their sights. Ball devoted entire sections of the memo to detailing the lawmakers’ conduct. And she flagged the case as a sensitive matter that might implicate politicians in the future.

In the summer of 2024, Kate Fifield, a prosecutor in the Justice Department’s anti-corruption section, had been assigned to join the case. Fifield was wrapping up a high-profile prosecution of a retired Navy admiral who traded a government contract for a future job. Turning her attention to Kao, she and the FBI agents weighed their options. They considered getting high-level approval to deploy undercover agents. Proving an exchange of cash for favors often requires the kind of inconvertible evidence that undercover recordings can provide.

But before the investigation took flight, the new Trump administration began to demolish the Justice Department. The president’s handpicked leaders ordered prosecutors to drop a corruption case against New York Mayor Eric Adams, triggering a wave of resignations in the Public Integrity Section where Fifield worked, and revoked the section’s authority to bring new cases.

Then Trump’s FBI director, Kash Patel, began to purge the anti-corruption unit in the Washington D.C., field office, where many agents had investigated Trump.

Ball was fired in October 2025. Kevin Gounaud, another agent who had been working with her on the investigation, was pushed out in early 2026. Through their lawyer, the agents declined to comment.

The corruption investigation, just launched, suddenly went dark.

The White House referred ProPublica’s questions to the FBI and DOJ.

The Department of Justice didn’t respond to detailed questions about the investigation.

“Under President Trump, the Justice Department is rooting out the weaponization, corruption, and institutional rot that ran rampant under Joe Biden’s two-tiered system of justice,” DOJ spokesperson Audrey Cook said.

Kao’s claims are “half baked” and “misleading,” FBI spokesperson Ben Williamson said. “Additionally, as we have said repeatedly, personnel are only removed at this FBI if they have been found to have acted unethically, undermined the mission, or engaged in weaponization of law enforcement.”

Meanwhile, Integer has rapidly expanded. Last year, it reported $81 million in contracts and announced that number would climb to $375 million by 2030. The company inked partnership deals with universities in New Hampshire, Louisiana and Mississippi. The University of South Carolina called its partnership with the firm a “model for public-private success.”The company continues to forge ties with powerful politicians. In late 2024, Hartman posted a photo of himself with Speaker of the House Mike Johnson on his LinkedIn. “It was great to visit with the Speaker of the House today,” he wrote. “It’s good to know there are people in positions of power who seem to really care for the people they serve.” Johnson’s office did not reply to an email requesting comment.

The occasion was a new research partnership in Louisiana. Hartman thanked his lobbyist for making it happen.

On March 3, 2020, Sen. Lindsey Graham walked into a private dining room at Charlie Palmer Steak in Washington, D.C. Graham’s time was expensive. He was meeting with two executives and a lobbyist from a Hawaiian naval defense contractor called Navatek. And he had required them to commit to $40,000 in campaign contributions for a 45-minute meeting.

Navatek’s CEO, Martin Kao, was confident that was all the time he needed.

“Shoot all he has to really do is stop by and say ‘yes,’” Kao wrote to a Navatek lobbyist and a fellow executive the day before, in an email seen by ProPublica. “That should only take a minute and he’s on his way. We don’t need to pretend we give a shit about each other.”

At the dinner, Graham ate little and talked a lot. But Kao got the “yes” he was hoping for: Graham agreed to steer a $10 million government contract Navatek’s way in his native South Carolina, Kao later recounted to the FBI. In exchange, he said, Graham asked Kao to pledge a total of $100,000 to his reelection campaign.

In early 2025, the FBI opened a sweeping investigation into bribery on Capitol Hill with Graham’s steakhouse dinner at the center. Kao confessed to federal agents that his business model consisted of bribing public officials in exchange for government contracts, and that the research Navatek was providing to the government was often worthless.

The scheme involved some of the most powerful figures in Congress, he said — in both parties. In South Carolina, Kao told agents, Navatek made a campaign cash-for-contracts deal not only with Graham, but also with the state’s top Democrat, Rep. James Clyburn, and another influential Republican, Rep. Joe Wilson.

Kao told agents that Graham in particular left little room for ambiguity: “This is how much I want, and how much I am going to give you,” Kao remembered Graham saying, according to notes one agent took during their debrief in late 2024.

Kao’s admissions would constitute a vast criminal bribery scheme if they could be proved in court — a challenge, given recent Supreme Court rulings that have made it more difficult to win corruption cases. Kao himself posed another hurdle. He was facing prison for fraud and campaign finance violations and was speaking to the FBI in the hopes of reducing his sentence.

But the FBI agents who took Kao’s confession — two experienced anti-corruption investigators — could see that much of it lined up with a trove of records they had seized from Navatek. They forged ahead. The FBI took the formal step of naming two former Navatek executives who had worked with Kao as subjects of a bribery investigation. The agency did not designate Graham, Clyburn and Wilson that way, but the agents were actively investigating their interactions with Kao. The agents indicated to their superiors that the lawmakers could become subjects in the future, according to records viewed by ProPublica.

The details of this investigation have not previously been reported.

President Donald Trump returned to the White House and almost immediately gutted the specialized FBI and Department of Justice teams dedicated to taking on public corruption — an act of revenge for having previously investigated him. The two experienced agents on the case were swept up in the purge, and the career prosecutor assisting them quit after Trump appointees forbade the DOJ’s anti-corruption section from filing any new cases. Today, there is no one left to look into Kao’s explosive allegations, as the Trump administration has abandoned any pretense of prosecuting public corruption. The case is dead.

ProPublica has unearthed the investigation by reviewing thousands of pages of legal documents, internal company emails, evidence the FBI used to vet Kao’s claims and dozens of interviews with people who saw how Navatek operated, including an eyewitness to Graham and Kao’s secret steakhouse dinner.

This story is part of a series based on our reporting. Last month, ProPublica reported that the FBI had probed the conduct of Maine Sen. Susan Collins, who steered millions in government funding toward Navatek while Kao gave her illegal campaign donations. Collins denied wrongdoing and dismissed Kao as a “convicted liar.”

Kao is indeed the only person involved to serve prison time. He pleaded guilty to making illegal campaign donations and a loan fraud scheme in 2022. In 2025, he was sentenced to 87 months in prison.

Graham died at 71 earlier this year. His sister, Darline Graham, who was appointed to fill his seat, retained most of his senior staff, including those who dealt with Navatek. She even took up some of his committee assignments, including appropriations. Darline Graham is running for a full term in office this November.

Clyburn, 86, is one of the most powerful Democrats in Washington. He is widely credited with tipping the scales for Joe Biden during the 2020 Democratic presidential primary. Wilson remains influential on national defense spending from his senior perch on the Armed Services Committee.

In May 2019, Willie Lyles III, Clyburn’s legislative director, alerted Navatek that the House Appropriations Committee would endorse new funding for a “modular and scalable integrated power and thermal system capable of powering a directed energy weapon system of 100 or more kilowatts,” according to an email flagged by the FBI. Three days later, that exact language appeared in the House Appropriations Committee report. Lyles did not reply to a request for comment.

In September, the Senate Appropriations Committee — Graham’s committee — released a budget report that set aside $9.5 million in funding for “Talent and Technology for Navy Power and Energy Systems.” Agents noted that Navatek may have ghostwritten the technical description that accompanied this line item because “an almost identical paragraph” appeared in Hartman’s internal spreadsheets two months before the Senate report became public.After the Senate advanced its version of the budget in the fall, Navatek dropped $4,799 to send bourbon to a handful of lawmakers, including Graham and Collins, the FBI found in company records — a likely violation of Senate gift rules. Both Collins’ and Graham’s offices denied receiving bourbon. Navatek later sent Clyburn a fruit basket from Edible Arrangements for $102.60.

Congress passed the final spending bill containing $9.5 million for Navatek’s pet project on Dec. 19, 2019.

At that point, Kao and other Navatek executives had not made any donations to Clyburn, Wilson or even Graham.

That would change within days of Congress passing the budget, when an executive pledged $750 to Wilson’s campaign at a fundraiser held at Bullfeathers, a Capitol Hill watering hole for lobbyists and Hill staffers, emails show. In the following weeks, Kao and his lobbyist gave Wilson’s campaign $3,500.

And bigger checks were imminent. Kao kicked off 2020 by making plans to spend $300,000 at a slew of political fundraisers, according to text messages filed in court and a recent deposition. Navatek’s lobbyists at that time were seeking private meetings with Graham, Wilson and Clyburn, where Kao planned to ask for significantly more federal funding. Kao eventually met with all three, and each asked for $100,000, he told the FBI.

During that same period, Kao and Hartman met a top fundraiser for Collins at a Corner Bakery in Washington, D.C. As ProPublica reported, that meeting led Kao to donate $150,000 to the pro-Collins super PAC. Kao told the FBI that the money was a bribe in exchange for the senator’s support for more Navatek funding. The head of the super PAC and Collins have denied Kao’s version of events.

Kao told the FBI it was not unusual for donations to come after the favor: “We became clever in our own right and figured out how to dangle the carrot,” he wrote in a memo seen by ProPublica, “until we saw some assurance that the Ask was in the budget.”

To secure a meeting with Graham, Kao hired Michael Tongour, a veteran South Carolina lobbyist who was old law school friends with the senator and family friends with Clyburn and Wilson. Graham was up for reelection against a well-funded challenger, Jaime Harrison, and had a crowded fundraising schedule, said Tongour, but they eventually found a time.

“The Graham fundraiser says that it [sic] you want to reserve a date for Senator Graham to attend a private dinner, the campaign wants to get $40,000 in contributions,” Tongour wrote to Kao, Hartman and lobbyist Andy Winer, in an undated email seen by ProPublica. Winer, whom Kao described as Navatek’s chief lobbyist, chimed in to say that Graham could stay only 45 minutes, and Kao replied that all they needed to hear was “yes.”

“These discussions are very frank and oddly not at all awkward,” Kao told the FBI. “I suppose once we got that far, everyone knows the deal.”

Graham showed up late to his March 3, 2020, dinner with Kao, Hartman and Tongour. A fundraiser working for Graham appeared first and accepted an envelope from Kao containing checks made out to Graham’s campaign, Kao said. Kao did not know the fundraiser’s name; in an FBI debrief, he simply referred to the person as the “money man.” After that person departed, Graham entered.

Graham, Kao recalled to the FBI, was “very abrupt” and “straight to the point”: He asked how much Navatek wanted, to which they said $10 million a year for three years. Graham asked Kao to pledge $100,000 in donations in exchange for his support, Kao told the FBI.

Navatek was juggling multiple proposals in South Carolina worth around $10 million, and it’s not clear which one Kao was describing.

Congress had just approved the $9.5 million for the power and energy project, but Navatek still needed the Navy to award the money in the form of a contract. The company was also about to ask Congress to fund the same project for a second year at $10.5 million. On top of that, Navatek was planning to pitch a new research proposal, at $10 million, to study the transport of supplies inland from ships using autonomous vehicles. It was another area where Navatek had “zero past performance,” the former executive said.

The same week of the steakhouse dinner, Graham’s office was working behind the scenes with Navatek and the University of South Carolina to make sure the Navy sent the first $9.5 million to the right company.

A day before the dinner, an official with the Office of Naval Research emailed Graham’s office to ask about the money, which “the Senator may have an interest in.” Craig Abele, Graham’s national security adviser, called her a few days later to confirm that Graham’s office had intended the money to be awarded to the Navatek partnership, emails show. “Spoke to ONR this morning,” Abele wrote March 6. “Should be set.”

The company received the contract, which it split with the university, in July 2020.

Abele became Graham’s deputy chief of staff and still holds the position for Darline. He did not reply to an email requesting comment. A Graham spokesperson confirmed to ProPublica that their office had indeed added the $9.5 million to the budget at the request of the university and Navatek. The spokesperson said the research was supported by the Navy and rejected the notion that Graham was motivated by campaign cash.

Graham’s spokesperson said requiring a fundraising minimum for a dinner was normal practice.

“[It’s] corruption at its best. But I am the only one rotting in prison.”

The checks Kao handed over at the dinner added up to about half of the $40,000 he had agreed to raise for the dinner, according to Tongour. The rest came in May, campaign records show.

“My understanding at the time was that the contributions associated with the event were lawful political contributions,” Winer said. “I never advised Kao to make a political contribution in exchange for official action. I never participated in, witnessed or had knowledge of any illegal political contribution.”

After Kao’s 2020 indictment, the University of South Carolina broke off the partnership. “University officials were not aware of any political contributions from Navatek,” Jeff Stensland, a university spokesperson, wrote in an email. “Our sole objective in partnering with them was to expand our research enterprise.”

One week after the dinner, on March 10, a University of South Carolina lobbyist sent written requests to Graham’s office for $20.5 million in appropriations: $10.5 million to continue Navatek’s power and energy research and $10 million for its new autonomous vehicle research.

Tongour confirmed many elements of Kao’s story. In a recent interview with ProPublica and subsequent emails, he remembered Kao giving the envelope full of checks to Graham’s fundraiser and even agreed that Graham kept the meeting short. “I don’t recall that he stayed more than 30 minutes,” Tongour said in an email. “Certainly not for appetizers, entrée, and dessert.”

But he denied Kao’s most significant claim: that Graham and Navatek struck an explicit, potentially illegal deal for contracts.

“I would remember that forever,” Tongour said. Graham supported Navatek’s requests, he said, because they were good for the state. “If I had seen that kind of quid pro quo, I would have quit earlier.” Tongour fired Navatek as a client later that year, immediately after Kao’s arrest.

Navatek considered Graham its chief patron in South Carolina, internal records show, just as it viewed Collins in Maine. But the company also wanted support in the House.

For that, Navatek turned to Clyburn, a member of Democratic party leadership and one of the most powerful figures in Congress. Agents found Navatek emails setting a meeting with Clyburn for early March. The goal, Kao said, was to make sure that the House draft of the budget “mirrored” whatever Graham inserted in the Senate version.

Over a meal, Clyburn asked Kao how much he was donating to Graham and then asked for the same amount, $100,000, Kao told the FBI.

The FBI tallied $20,000 in contributions that Kao and his family members made to Clyburn a few days after their scheduled meeting. A month later, Hartman arranged for a $10,000 gift to Clyburn’s alma mater, South Carolina State University, according to an email seen by ProPublica. Hartman noted it was “Congressman Clyburn’s favorite school.”

The spokesperson for Clyburn confirmed the dinner occurred but did not respond to questions about what was discussed.

Kao and Hartman struck a similar bargain with Wilson, Kao told the FBI, at a private lunch he secured by donating to his campaign. The FBI pulled records of $3,500 in donations Kao and Tongour made in late 2019 and early 2020.

Wilson has an influential role on the House Armed Services Committee, which authorizes defense spending. Wilson was “very clear and knew what the ask was,” Kao told the FBI. He wanted however much Clyburn was getting in campaign contributions in exchange for his support, Kao said.

Tongour denied this. “There was no ‘pay to play’ with Joe Wilson or his staff either,” he wrote in an email, adding that he donated because Wilson “is a friend and I want to see him re-elected.”

Kao claimed to the FBI that he paid for meals with all three politicians, but campaign finance records appear to show the lawmakers’ campaigns picked up the check.

By August 2020, Hartman had gotten word that Graham’s and Wilson’s committees would each support part of the company’s requests, internal company records show. Clyburn was not on key committees while he was a party leader. In one of his myriad spreadsheets, Hartman projected that the final budget could include up to $15.5 million of the $20.5 million the company had lobbied for.

Kao told agents he was gearing up to make more significant donations to Graham, Clyburn and Wilson by the end of the year. Instead, on Sept. 30, 2020, he was arrested.

Kao reflected on his time at Navatek in a deposition taken last month inside a federal prison. The company’s founder has sued Kao, saying his stewardship defrauded Navatek of millions.

“It’s nothing more than government contracting done through placating senior appropriation members for contracts,” Kao said. “The whole thing is a grand lie.”

Kao’s arrest plunged Navatek into disarray. Federal prosecutors charged him with defrauding a federal loan program; not long after, he was also charged with making illegal donations to the Collins campaign and super PAC. Lobbyists, universities and congressional offices abruptly cut off contact. “Don’t call us,” one congressional office told an executive, he later testified.

The Collins campaign said it disgorged Kao’s contribution. Yet the senator, facing a tight reelection bid, has had to field questions following the ProPublica report about her support for Navatek and why the super PAC supporting her accepted the $150,000 donation from the shell company in the first place.

As Navatek sank, Hartman began building himself a lifeboat. In 2021, he and Knight started a new military research company headquartered in South Carolina called Integer Technologies. Integer’s first project was one that Navatek had brokered with Graham.

Hartman didn’t face any criminal charges at the time and left Navatek largely unscathed. He would later settle a civil lawsuit brought by his former employer, which contended he played a role in Kao’s campaign finance and loan fraud schemes, for $425,000. He admitted no wrongdoing.

In September 2024, FBI agents flew to Hawaii for what would be their final interview with Kao. They pressed him for details about what happened in South Carolina, quizzing him on his dinner with Graham and a second in-person meeting he said he had with Clyburn. Agents wanted Kao to help them find more records that would back up his account and asked him to point them toward lobbyists in D.C. who he said facilitated the pay-for-play scheme.

As the year came to a close, FBI agent Michelle Ball felt she had enough material to move forward. The FBI had “uncovered a lengthy history” of a government contractor making donations “in exchange for government contracts,” she wrote in a Dec. 9, 2024, memo viewed by ProPublica.

In federal corruption probes, agents often begin by focusing on lower-level targets with a goal of working their way up to more prominent figures. Ball named Hartman; his business partner, Knight; and their company, Integer, as the formal subjects of the probe, as they prepared to potentially issue subpoenas and gather more evidence. They had Graham, Clyburn and Wilson in their sights. Ball devoted entire sections of the memo to detailing the lawmakers’ conduct. And she flagged the case as a sensitive matter that might implicate politicians in the future.

In the summer of 2024, Kate Fifield, a prosecutor in the Justice Department’s anti-corruption section, had been assigned to join the case. Fifield was wrapping up a high-profile prosecution of a retired Navy admiral who traded a government contract for a future job. Turning her attention to Kao, she and the FBI agents weighed their options. They considered getting high-level approval to deploy undercover agents. Proving an exchange of cash for favors often requires the kind of inconvertible evidence that undercover recordings can provide.

But before the investigation took flight, the new Trump administration began to demolish the Justice Department. The president’s handpicked leaders ordered prosecutors to drop a corruption case against New York Mayor Eric Adams, triggering a wave of resignations in the Public Integrity Section where Fifield worked, and revoked the section’s authority to bring new cases.

Then Trump’s FBI director, Kash Patel, began to purge the anti-corruption unit in the Washington D.C., field office, where many agents had investigated Trump.

Ball was fired in October 2025. Kevin Gounaud, another agent who had been working with her on the investigation, was pushed out in early 2026. Through their lawyer, the agents declined to comment.

The corruption investigation, just launched, suddenly went dark.

The White House referred ProPublica’s questions to the FBI and DOJ.

The Department of Justice didn’t respond to detailed questions about the investigation.

“Under President Trump, the Justice Department is rooting out the weaponization, corruption, and institutional rot that ran rampant under Joe Biden’s two-tiered system of justice,” DOJ spokesperson Audrey Cook said.

Kao’s claims are “half baked” and “misleading,” FBI spokesperson Ben Williamson said. “Additionally, as we have said repeatedly, personnel are only removed at this FBI if they have been found to have acted unethically, undermined the mission, or engaged in weaponization of law enforcement.”

Meanwhile, Integer has rapidly expanded. Last year, it reported $81 million in contracts and announced that number would climb to $375 million by 2030. The company inked partnership deals with universities in New Hampshire, Louisiana and Mississippi. The University of South Carolina called its partnership with the firm a “model for public-private success.”The company continues to forge ties with powerful politicians. In late 2024, Hartman posted a photo of himself with Speaker of the House Mike Johnson on his LinkedIn. “It was great to visit with the Speaker of the House today,” he wrote. “It’s good to know there are people in positions of power who seem to really care for the people they serve.” Johnson’s office did not reply to an email requesting comment.

The occasion was a new research partnership in Louisiana. Hartman thanked his lobbyist for making it happen.

Last-Minute Undas Trips 2026: Where You Can Still Go

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Manila to Caticlan seat on Friday 30 October was going for PHP 3,704 when we checked fares on 18 October. The same route on Sunday 1 November? PHP 2,238. Same route, same island, 40 percent cheaper, just because most of the country will be at the sementeryo that day instead of at NAIA.

That gap is the whole game for last minute Undas trips this year.

If you didn’t lock in a trip back in September, you haven’t run out of options. You’ve just run out of the obvious ones: the Friday night bus to Baguio, the Saturday morning flight out. What’s left is still good, as long as you travel on the days everyone else avoids, pick routes where seats are still plentiful, and book the return before you get excited about the outbound. Below are the routes that still had real seats from Manila when we checked, what they cost in pesos, and the one trap that catches most late bookers.

Some links in this post are affiliate links. If you book through them we may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you, which keeps this blog running.

Undas 2026 is a three-day weekend. Under Proclamation No. 1006, series of 2025, Sunday 1 November (All Saints’ Day) and Monday 2 November (All Souls’ Day) are special non-working days. Saturday 31 October is not a declared holiday.

Here is what was still bookable from Manila when we checked on 18 October. These are the lowest one-way fares we found, before checked bags:

The short version: Cebu, Boracay and Iloilo are the easiest last-minute wins, with round trips under PHP 5,000. Bohol and Davao still work at a slightly higher price.

Fares move every hour, so treat these as a snapshot. The pattern matters more than the exact peso.

Every Undas rush has the same shape. Everyone leaves Manila on Friday and Saturday, and heads back when the holiday ends on Monday. The Parañaque Integrated Terminal Exchange (PITX) alone expects nearly 1.9 million passengers between 25 October and 4 November, with Friday 30 October the single busiest day at around 200,000, according to Bombo Radyo.

So flip it. Go to the cemetery early, on Saturday 31 October, or on Sunday morning if you pick an afternoon flight like Cebu at 12:20pm or Kalibo at 4:55pm. Then fly out on the holiday itself and come home after the crowd.

The fares back this up. Compare Friday 30 October with Sunday 1 November on the same routes:

That is roughly 30 to 40 percent off just for shifting by two days.

The catch is leave. Fly out Sunday, back Wednesday 4 November, and you need Tuesday and Wednesday off. One day of leave if you come back Tuesday. For a lot of us that’s a fair trade, since you skip the Monday return rush entirely.

One honest warning: returns move fast this close to the holiday. On the morning of 18 October, the only Wednesday 4 November return from Iloilo we could see was PHP 6,451 on PAL. A few hours later, a Cebu Pacific round trip for 31 October to 4 November was showing at PHP 4,426. Hong Kong was still lopsided at PHP 4,214 out and PHP 6,974 back. Price your return first, every time, and check again before you pay.

Buses are where last minute Undas trips get hardest, because they don’t price up the way planes do. They just sell out.

The Land Transportation Franchising and Regulatory Board (LTFRB) has issued around 1,200 to 1,300 special permits for extra buses, minibuses and vans this Undas, valid until 5 or 6 November, as the Daily Tribune reported on 18 October. More units means more seats, but not necessarily at the times you want.

If your heart is set on Baguio or La Union, our Undas long weekend guide has the operator-by-operator fares and routes. That guide expected the good departure times to go first, so be ready for a very early or very late bus.

Honestly? This year, flying is often the easier last-minute play. A PHP 2,238 Caticlan seat costs about twice a premium Baguio bus seat (PHP 850 to 1,055 in that guide), with no NLEX traffic.

Cebu is the route that barely notices Undas. Manila to Cebu was going for PHP 2,440 to PHP 2,561 on every day from Thursday 29 October to Sunday 1 November, with returns from PHP 2,331 on Wednesday 4 November. In our check, the price barely moved across the whole long weekend.

That makes it the safest choice if you want certainty over a beach.

Three days in Cebu work well even without a plan. Walk the old city around Magellan’s Cross and the Basilica del Santo Niño in the morning, eat lechon for lunch, and take a day trip south or a ferry to Bohol if you want an island. Cebu also works as a base if Bohol flights from Manila are too pricey: fly to Cebu, then cross by fast ferry.

The room is the part to sort quickly. You can compare rooms in Cebu City across Booking.com, Agoda and others and filter for free cancellation, which matters when you’re booking this close.

Iloilo is one of the cheapest places to fly into this Undas: PHP 2,087 on Sunday 1 November on AirAsia, and PHP 2,078 on Tuesday 3 November on Cebu Pacific. It’s also one of the most food-forward short trips in the country. Batchoy at the La Paz market, the Molo church, the river esplanade at sunrise.

As a round trip it held up too: Saturday 31 October to Wednesday 4 November on Cebu Pacific was PHP 4,426 when we last checked. Two ways to make more of it.

Stretch the trip. If you can work remotely or burn a few more leave days, flying back on Tuesday 10 November was showing PHP 2,326 one way on Cebu Pacific. Undas becomes a week in Western Visayas.

Add Bacolod. Fly into Iloilo, cross to Bacolod by fast craft, and fly home from there. OceanJet runs the crossing in about 1 hour 30 minutes for PHP 550 in tourist class, with departures from Iloilo at 7:20am, 11:00am, 2:30pm and 5:30pm on its February 2026 schedule, as listed by Suroy.ph. Budget another PHP 30 to 40 for the terminal fee. You can check Iloilo to Bacolod ferry times and seats on 12Go.

We couldn’t find a Bacolod to Manila fare for 2 to 5 November in the fare data on 18 October, so check that leg before you commit. If it’s sold out or pricey, do Bacolod as a day trip from Iloilo instead and keep the round trip.

For the Bacolod end, our DIY guide to Bacolod covers what to eat and see in the City of Smiles. In Iloilo, our list of budget hotels in Iloilo is a good starting point, or see what’s still available in Iloilo City for the Undas weekend.

Here’s the surprise of this year’s fare check. Boracay was one of the cheapest last-minute Undas trips from Manila: PHP 2,238 to Caticlan on Sunday 1 November, PHP 2,164 back on Wednesday 4 November. Kalibo was even lower on the way out at PHP 1,975, though its Tuesday return was PHP 3,523. Caticlan is the closer airport: it’s a 5 to 7 minute tricycle ride to Caticlan Jetty Port, according to Tripwis.

The flight is the easy part. The room is not.

Boracay only lets tourists cross with a confirmed booking at a Department of Tourism-accredited hotel. A March 2026 summary of the entry rules by Respicio & Co. puts it plainly: “Entry is strictly permitted only to those with a confirmed booking at a DOT-Accredited Establishment.” The same summary lists the fees for Filipino tourists at about PHP 150 environmental fee, PHP 150 terminal fee and PHP 50 for the boat, so roughly PHP 350 on top of your fare. Treat those as approximate and confirm at the Caticlan jetty port.

So the order is: room first, flight second. Never the reverse. Compare rooms in Boracay, then check the property against the DOT’s accredited list before you pay. A cheap room at a non-accredited place can leave you stuck at the jetty.

Bohol still works at a slightly higher price: PHP 3,395 out on Sunday 1 November and PHP 3,292 back on Wednesday 4 November, both on Cebu Pacific. Around PHP 6,700 round trip for the Chocolate Hills and Panglao is reasonable for a holiday weekend.

Davao was showing a Saturday 31 October to Thursday 5 November round trip for PHP 6,211 on Cebu Pacific (the return leaves just after midnight, so effectively Wednesday night). A longer trip, but People’s Park, the durian stalls and a day at Eden Nature Park are all easy without much planning. Our guide to things to do in Davao City has more.

Hong Kong is the one international option worth considering this close, because Philippine passport holders can visit visa-free for up to 14 days. HK Express had a Sunday 1 November flight for PHP 4,214, though it leaves at 2:20am, so really the night of the 31st. The return is the expensive bit again: PHP 6,974 on Wednesday 4 November with Cebu Pacific. Add the PHP 1,620 economy travel tax and you’re at roughly PHP 12,800 before a bed. Rather than paying roaming rates, get a Hong Kong eSIM from Airalo before you board.

Watch for rental scams. Last Undas, the PNP warned about fake Airbnb listings and “rent-tangay” car rental schemes. PNP spokesperson Brig. Gen. Randulf Tuaño’s advice was simple: if an offer is too cheap, it’s likely a scam (Philstar, 30 October 2025). Late bookers are the easiest targets, because panic makes a “last room available, GCash only” message look like luck. Book through a platform that holds your payment, never by direct transfer to a stranger’s account.

Book refundable where you can. Weather is still a factor in late October and early November. Pick free-cancellation rooms, check PAGASA before you fly, and know your airline’s rebooking rules.

Leave extra time for the airport. If you’re flying on 1 November, roads near the big cemeteries get busy from early morning. Give yourself more time than usual to reach NAIA.

Going abroad? The PHP 1,620 economy travel tax is still collected as far as we can tell: the bill to abolish it was still pending in the Senate in late August, as Suroy.ph reported. Check TIEZA’s travel tax page before you fly, and consider cover for the trip. SafetyWing travel medical insurance works for short trips too.

Is 31 October 2026 a holiday? No. Only Sunday 1 November and Monday 2 November are special non-working days under Proclamation No. 1006. Saturday 31 October is an ordinary Saturday.

What is the cheapest day to fly during Undas 2026? In the fares we checked on 18 October, Sunday 1 November was the cheapest outbound day on most routes, around 30 to 40 percent below Friday 30 October to Caticlan, Bohol and Kalibo.

Can I still find a bus to Baguio or the provinces? Possibly, but avoid Friday 30 October, the projected PITX peak. Buy only from the operator’s counter or website, and expect early-morning or late-night departures.

Is Boracay possible on short notice? Yes, if you secure a room at a DOT-accredited hotel first. Flights were from PHP 2,238 one way on 1 November.

Do Filipinos need a visa for Hong Kong? No. Philippine passport holders can visit Hong Kong visa-free for up to 14 days.

Last minute Undas trips are still very possible this year. You just have to travel against the crowd. Visit your loved ones early, fly out on 1 November, take a day or two of leave, and price the return before you book anything. Cebu, Boracay and Iloilo are the safest bets under PHP 5,000 in flights, and if you’re still deciding, our full Undas long weekend guide has the bus routes and the trips abroad.

Booking even earlier would have been better, sure. But a quiet Sunday flight to the beach while everyone else sits on NLEX? That’s not a bad consolation prize.

Googlebook review: Google’s new laptops are woefully underbaked

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Guardian journalism is independent. We earn a commission if you buy something through an affiliate link. Learn more.

Great hardware let down by software flaws on these pricey machines that combine ChromeOS with Android

Google’s attempt to create a new type of laptop to compete with Windows and macOS machines is finally here, combining the best of ChromeOS and Android into the new Googlebook. With advanced links to Android phones and the best of Google built in, can these Googlebooks really compete with a MacBook Air or Surface Laptop? I took two for a spin to find out.

For years, Chromebooks have been the boss of budget laptops, but Googlebooks take a different, far more premium approach. Made by a range of traditional PC makers, including Acer, Asus, Dell, HP and Lenovo, they start at £999 (€1,099/$899/A$2,299) – a far cry from the £160 Chromebooks.

While Google does not make these machines directly, it has set strict hardware quality standards that the laptops must reach. That includes backlit keyboards, haptic trackpads, fingerprint readers, crisp touchscreens, decent speakers and modern fast chips with at least 16GB of RAM.

It is a list of specs you should expect with a premium Windows PC, but those start at a much higher price, primarily thanks to RAMageddon.

As such, the Acer Googlebook 14 and the Asus Googlebook 14 I’ve been testing have excellent hardware with satisfying keyboards, great trackpads and quality screens.

The Acer is a convertible that allows the screen to fold over the back of the device turning into a tablet of sorts. It is 1.14kg and 13.9mm thick, with a glossy 14in 3K OLED screen and a smooth aluminium-magnesium body, costing £999.99.

The Asus is a traditional laptop with a matt 14in 3K OLED screen and a magnesium-alloy body that weighs just 0.99kg and is 11mm thick, costing £1,199.99.

Top quality hardware aside, anyone familiar with a Chromebook would be forgiven for thinking it was ChromeOS running on these machines. It has the familiar taskbar at the bottom, Chrome-based web apps and access to Android apps.

But the software is essentially Android underneath, which means getting it up and running is just like setting up an Android phone: scan the QR code with your phone, enter a passcode and transfer your apps and accounts. It is all done and dusted in about four minutes, which is faster than setting up a modern Windows 11 machine even if you know all the shortcuts.

Access to the full gamut of Android apps is the Googlebook’s biggest strength and its greatest weakness. Practically any app you have on your phone can be installed and run on the laptop. But almost all those phone apps are just that, mobile versions, with most not even aware that they are not installed on a phone.

Those that have at least some tailoring to the laptop form come in a few different types, categorised by Google broadly under “compatible with desktop”, which means they should support mouse and keyboard controls; “optimised for desktop”, which means they should scale properly to the laptop’s display; and “made for desktop”, which are a handful of Android apps redesigned with the laptop in mind.

Only the last category really seems to make any difference to the quality of the app, and even then most of them are still not of the same calibre as their equivalents found on Windows or macOS.

Even Google’s apps are a mixed bag. Google Calendar can only show one account at any one time, so you can’t see your personal, work and shared calendars on one screen like you can on Android.

Google Docs starts as its own app, but when you actually open a document it just becomes another Chrome window hidden behind the Chrome logo on the task bar, not the Docs logo.

The Files app shows your Google Drive files, but you can’t pin locations to the side bar to quickly access your commonly used folders, making it a time-consuming dig through endless folders to find things each time.

Even the Chrome browser is not quite up to par with its Windows or macOS counterpart, lacking features such as split-screen tabs, the auto-hiding vertical tab bar or the ability to have multiple profiles, such as a personal and work account.

Google has an app-gap problem and one that will only be solved if developers are convinced Googlebook OS is going to take off and be worth spending time and money making better apps for.

The other big selling point is deep integration with your Android phone. Google calls this “better together”, which is a collection of features that mirror capabilities of iPhones with Macs or Microsoft’s Link to Windows.

Some of the features are like magic. You can search the files on your phone straight from the Files app on the laptop without plugging it in, see your notifications from your phone, continue tasks on your laptop from your phone and even use apps cast from your phone.

As useful as these features sound, many are not up to scratch. Streaming apps from your phone is so slow that I feel as though I can type entire sentences before they appear on screen in chat apps, and it is restricted to one app at a time. If you click on a synced notification from your phone, it will open in a cast app, even if you have the full app installed on your Googlebook. Even viewing files on your phone from the laptop is irritatingly slow.

The only apps I’ve reliably managed to get to appear with the ability to continue from where I was are Google Docs and Chrome on the phone.

It also took me two hours of troubleshooting to get an up-to-date Google Pixel 11 Pro to actually connect with the Googlebooks, despite using the phone to set them up in the first place.

The other big selling point for Google is its Gemini integration into Googlebook OS. The Gemini app is just like it is on macOS and Windows. But it is the Magic Pointer feature, which appears when you wiggle the mouse to click or highlight something on your screen to send it to Gemini, that is the most novel.

You can use it to get Gemini to summarise text, explain something, search for things or generally chat. But I often hit a road block, such as Gemini refusing to generate images or saying it cannot add a date into my calendar in the mode Magic Cursor opens, which oddly isn’t within the desktop Gemini app. Sometimes it works, sometimes it doesn’t, and it is simply not joined up.

Magic Cue has been imported from Google’s Pixel phones, which is meant to pop up with helpful information such as calendar availability or a location in maps when you highlight a date or a place in a chat or email. But unlike on a Pixel phone, I have yet to get it to successfully activate in two weeks of testing.

Google’s super-charged voice dictation system called Rambler, also from the recent Pixels, does at least work as described. Press the dictation key and just speak naturally. Rambler will condense your speech into concise sentences removing filler words and errors.

Googlebooks are full of excellent ideas about how a modern laptop should work. The hardware of the Acer and Asus machines I’ve tested is top notch, but the software just isn’t ready for prime time.

When it works it’s great, but it is filled with limitations that might be acceptable on a super-cheap laptop but never on a £1,000 premium machine.

Chrome is not as good as it is on a Mac or Windows PC. Google’s various apps are generally better on an Android phone and the third-party app ecosystem is a mess of poorly optimised mobile apps. Even the best desktop-optimised Android apps pale by comparison to their counterparts on Windows or macOS.

The Android phone-linking system should be the killer feature, but it is partly broken or simply not up to scratch. Even the Gemini integration is flakey.

Most of the Google-specific issues and software bugs are eminently fixable. But the app gap will take serious time, effort and money to convince third-party developers to fix.

Unless you want to spend at least £1,000 to be a beta tester, your money is better spent elsewhere for now.

Sabadell y CaixaBank son los bancos que más condicionan el bonus; Bankinter, el que menos

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Nota: Datos de cotización y análisis actualizados a día de hoy.

BBVA prima las empresas, Unicaja usa indicadores sintéticos y Santander simplifica, pero la rentabilidad es el indicador estrella de la retribución variable.

El objetivo en todos los casos es el mismo: decidir si los directivos se merecen cobrar su bonus o no y en qué cuantía. Pero los caminos no pueden ser más diferentes. Santander, BBVA, CaixaBank, Sabadell, Bankinter y Unicaja tienen seis fórmulas distintas para valorar la retribución variable de sus presidentes ejecutivos y consejeros delegados y las hay con hasta 11 criterios de concesión.

Y eso solo para determinar el bonus anual a corto plazo de forma cuantitativa. Es el más significativo, pero los bancos también tienen reglas cualitativas, así como retribuciones plurianuales y remuneraciones a largo plazo con diferentes métricas y criterios de concesión.

Sabadell es el que tiene el bonus anual más condicionado. Los directivos deben cumplir nueve requerimientos para cobrarlo, pero eso es sobre el papel, porque la realidad es que se convierten en 11 en el cómputo final. Hay un criterio que está subdividido en tres.

El año pasado eran menos. «En el marco de la revisión de los objetivos del grupo Banco Sabadell para el ejercicio 2026, y en línea con las recomendaciones supervisoras orientadas a reforzar la gestión prudente del riesgo y la solvencia, se ha incorporado un nuevo indicador estratégico: la generación de capital», explica la entidad.

El objetivo es reforzar «el compromiso con la mejora de la solvencia y la creación de valor para el accionista», añade. Porque no se trata solo de incrementar el capital de máxima calidad (CET1), sino que también computan los dividendos devengados durante el periodo.

El nuevo criterio pesa un 10% y se suma al beneficio neto, el margen de intereses más las comisiones, los gastos, la calidad del servicio, la cuota de mercado y la sostenibilidad, todos ellos con idéntica ponderación del 10% en el bonus.

Eso deja la rentabilidad sobre capital tangible (ROTE) como el criterio estrella en Sabadell, porque supone el 15% del total. No está solo. Es la métrica clave para la mayoría de los bancos. Solo Bankinter no lo usa y BBVA lo cambia por el retorno sobre el capital regulatorio (RORC).

Hay otro indicador en Sabadell que pesa un 15%, pero es el que se subdivide. El objetivo general es medir la calidad de los activos del banco y para ello se analiza el coste del riesgo (5% de peso), la morosidad (otro 5%) y la ratio de cobertura (el 5% final).

CaixaBank está muy cerca de Sabadell. Sus directivos son evaluados con 10 criterios. El ROTE y el apetito por el riesgo llevan la voz cantante, con un 20% de peso cada uno, seguidos de la ratio de eficiencia, la calidad del servicio, la cuota de mercado y la morosidad.

El impulso que eleva el número de indicadores llega por la sostenibilidad, en la que CaixaBank incluye cuatro criterios con una ponderación del 2,5% cada uno. Es la forma que ha encontrado el banco de medir este parámetro en todos sus frentes, tanto los financieros, como los de gobernanza o los sociales. El número de mujeres en puestos directivos es uno de ellos.

Justo al lado contrario está Bankinter. Solo dos métricas determinan el bonus. Es cierto que el proceso total es más completo y obliga a pasar por tres fases antes de que salga la cuantía final a cobrar, pero el punto de partida es el más simple de toda la banca. El margen de exploración antes de provisiones pesa un 60% y el beneficio neto (el año pasado era antes de impuestos) supone el 40% restante.

BBVA y Unicaja están en un terreno intermedio. El primero tiene seis criterios, liderados por la rentabilidad. El presidente, el consejero delegado y buena parte de los altos directivos se juegan el 35% de su bonus anual a corto plazo con ella.

El beneficio, la satisfacción de los usuarios, la captación de clientes y la sostenibilidad también ponderan en la paga de BBVA, pero se trata de indicadores muy habituales en los bonus españoles. El último es diferente. En una apuesta por los clientes corporativos, la entidad otorga un peso del 10% a los ingresos por comisiones de las empresas. Es el único banco que lo hace.

Unicaja le da a la rentabilidad una importancia incluso mayor que BBVA. Es el 45% de su bonus anual a corto plazo. El resto se lo reparten cuatro criterios, pero el banco malagueño usa indicadores sintéticos para abarcar más espacio y meter en el mismo punto aspectos no tan parecidos entre sí.

La sostenibilidad, por ejemplo, mide con una métrica única los objetivos de descarbonización y la eficiencia energética de la nueva producción hipotecaria. Otro criterio agrupa el número de clientes activos, su satisfacción y la de los empleados.

Santander ha decidido simplificar. Su bonus tiene este año menos condiciones que el anterior. La parte cuantitativa de la paga se ha quedado con cuatro indicadores de los que el ROTE es la estrella, una vez más. Su peso es del 35%.

Pese a la importancia que tiene la solvencia en el sector bancario, solo hay dos entidades que la utilizan para medir a sus directivos. Sabadell es una de ellas. La otra es Santander. Esta última le otorga un 25% de ponderación, así que es el segundo factor por importancia para la remuneración variable. Los otros dos son los costes y los clientes activos. Bankinter también usa el capital, pero no en la primera fase.

Santander tiene una segunda parte del bonus. Son los criterios cualitativos. Permiten mejorar la paga, aunque también empeorarla, y han pasado de cuatro el año pasado a dos en el actual.

Con muchos o pocos criterios, los bancos tienen en común cumplir o sobrepasar los objetivos del bonus. Así fue el año pasado, en el que todas las entidades lo pagaron a sus directivos.

El más espléndido fue Santander, que les dio el 145% de la remuneración variable objetivo. En Unicaja fue el 135% y en BBVA ascendió al 115%. CaixaBank y Sabadell empataron en el 110%, con Bankinter en el 99%.
