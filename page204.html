The very bad news for the GOP: Donald Trump’s record unpopularity means its once-presumed lock on the US Senate continues to look more and more tenuous. Why You Should Always Read the Label on Your Cleaning Spray

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Until I started reviewing kitchen gear five years ago, I rarely read product manuals or user guides. I’m sure that’s due in large part to my ADHD, as my brain tends to move more quickly than step-by-step instructions allow. Plus, I like to figure things out on my own, thank you very much!

But my rogue, DIY, quick-start method hasn’t always worked out in my favor. I inadvertently killed my first Dyson stick vacuum (that I saved for months to buy) because I failed to educate myself on proper filter maintenance. And I’ve owned my car for a decade and still have no clue what a couple of the buttons actually do.

While user error can make for a funny punchline, it can also get expensive. For example, it might seem silly to read the manual for a nonstick frying pan or enameled cast-iron Dutch oven, but that’s where you’d learn that putting it in the dishwasher or using metal utensils can destroy or degrade the cooking surface.

In the case of cleaning products, the stakes can be even higher—especially in this era of, er, less restrictive food safety regulations.

Before you get to the instructions on the back of a cleaning spray bottle, you need to pay close attention to what’s on the front, because not all kitchen cleaning sprays (no, not even all all-purpose cleaning sprays) are created equally.

Though they might have some very minimal, naturally occurring disinfecting properties from ingredients like vinegar or essential oils, basic cleaning sprays like Mrs. Meyer’s and Aunt Fannie’s have one job—and that is to clean the surfaces in your kitchen. And by “clean,” I mean they contain surfactants, which help to dislodge grease and gunk so you can more easily wipe away a mess.

Mrs. Meyer’s Clean Day All-Purpose Cleaner Spray

Aunt Fannie’s All-Purpose Cleaning Vinegar Spray

But simply removing discernible debris won’t leave you with a germ-free countertop, which is why you also need a disinfectant to kill bacteria and viruses (not to be confused with a sanitizer, which only kills bacteria). And if you want to ensure your counters are so clean you could eat off of them, I recommend using both. If you spray a disinfecting solution on top of a spill, it’s not disinfecting the counter underneath the spill—it’s sanitizing the surface of whatever is stuck to your counter.

Here’s the real kicker, and the reason it’s important to read the instructions on the back of your disinfecting (or sanitizing) spray: In order for the product to kill the number of germs the label claims, it needs to sit on the surface in question for anywhere from about two to 10 minutes. This period is called the “dwell time” or “contact time,” and the product needs to remain visibly wet for however long it takes to be truly effective.

For example, Lysol's All-Purpose Cleaner (which is truly all-purpose when used properly), is a sanitizer at 10 seconds, but it only "kills 99.9% of viruses and bacteria" as the front of the label claims, if it’s left to sit for 10 minutes. The little asterisks after that claim lead you to the back label, which details the dwell time along with the common bacteria and viruses it kills.

All-Purpose Cleaner Spray, Lemon Breeze Scent, 32 oz.

“Use as directed” shows up four times in the online product description for Clorox’s Multi-Surface Disinfecting Mist, which also claims to kill 99.9% of viruses and bacteria. It even has a specific instruction for kitchens (to rinse off of any food-prep surfaces after a 10-minute dwell time).

The moral of the story? Use your cleaning products as directed, which unfortunately does require reading the fine print on the label. While you’re at it, you might want to consider replacing your microfiber cleaning cloths after a 2010 study found that microfiber becomes less effective at removing contaminants from surfaces the more it gets used.

Our Best Thanksgiving Yet

If you listen in on Test Kitchen conversations, you’ll hear the editors say that Thanksgiving is our Super Bowl, our World Cup, and our Fashion Week. For food people, it’s the culmination of a year’s worth of prep, planning, brainstorming, and, as my colleague Hana Asbrink describes, Post-it Note–filled recipe scrums. But I wouldn’t call it stressful.

We look forward to basking in the day’s many gravy-soaked superlatives. We delight in stories that showcase celebrating family, friendship, and community while giving guidance for making the most of the holiday season. Of course, it’s a busy time, but it also brings so much joy.

I revel in the little tasks that make the day feel even more special, like sorting my china patterns and silverware, checking napkins for grease stains, and sending table linens to the cleaners for some extra TLC.

I’ve never really bought into Thanksgiving Day angst. I find the shopping stress and pressure-packed meal planning to be exaggerated myths perpetuated by pop culture. After all, the mac and cheese, potatoes, pies, and pound cakes are similar to the celebratory dishes that punctuate the year’s other holidays.

Of course, traditions vary between cultures and communities. What makes a classic Thanksgiving recipe is a matter of heritage, geography, and nostalgia. But even tradition can use a little zhuzh. New recipes, such as our Cranberry Meringue Pie and A Beautiful Cabbage Galette can add excitement—and practicality—to the holiday.

The Bon Appétit Test Kitchen has created 18 dishes, including breakfasts and variations for sides and salads to complement your family staples. We even asked five sommeliers for their recommendations for the best wines to pour on the big day.

These all add up to the best Thanksgiving ever.

Behind the Settler Attack on Palestinians and a Washington Post Journalist

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On Friday, a group of Israeli settlers attacked Palestinian farmers who were harvesting olives in the West Bank. In the process, they also beat up an American Washington Post photographer, Heidi Levine, who was there to cover the annual olive harvest. The attack prompted rare criticism of the Israeli government from the United States Embassy in Jerusalem, which requested an investigation. The office of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said that an investigation was warranted, while also casting doubt on what occurred. (On Saturday, after settlers also attacked a group of European diplomats during a visit to the West Bank, Netanyahu raised the prospect that it was part of a “coordinated campaign to smear Israel.”) Over all, in 2026, violence against Palestinians in the West Bank has increased significantly from prior years; the next Israeli election is in late October.

I recently spoke by phone with Jasper Nathaniel, a journalist who covers the West Bank for his Substack, and who was present during the assault on Levine. During our conversation, which has been edited for length and clarity, we discussed how the Israeli government has been increasingly aiding extremist settlers, the changing attitude among Israeli settlers toward Americans in the West Bank, and what happened during the attack on Levine and others.

How many times have you been to the West Bank in the past couple of years?

This is my fifth trip since January of 2024. Each trip is around a month, sometimes a little more, sometimes a little bit less. For this trip, I arrived on October 6th, and I am definitely staying past the election, which is on October 27th.

How have things changed over those nearly three years?

There are real material changes, because there are lots of villages that I visited as recently as last year that just don’t exist anymore. They have been literally wiped off the map and replaced by either an outpost, or in some cases a new neighborhood of a settlement, or a closed military zone. [Outposts are officially unauthorized by the Israeli government, although many of them eventually become retroactively legalized.] So there have been actual material changes to the geography and the demographics.

Since this government came in, in 2022, and certainly since October 7, 2023, the settlers have been emboldened. And the threshold for when they turn to violence has just gotten lower and lower. In other words, it used to be the case that many settler attacks would start with settlers coming into a village and accusing Palestinians of something like stealing a goat, and then they would go house to house, kicking down doors looking for the goat, and then eventually it would turn into a fight.

Now what you’re seeing is, literally every single day, huge mobs of settlers put on masks, wear all black, pick up clubs and assault rifles, and launch coördinated attacks. And these attacks are not random at all. The way that the map has changed in the West Bank is precisely what Bezalel Smotrich, the finance minister, has laid out numerous times when he’s described how Israel is going to create Jewish sovereignty across the West Bank. So, for example, take the attack on Friday in Khirbet Abu Falah. There is an outpost that exists there, and it’s not there for no reason. It’s a strategic piece of land because it cuts off a point between two villages. It creates continuity between different settlements, and the Israeli government wants that piece of land, and so the settlers are out there doing their dirty work by terrorizing the Palestinians. They have effectively taken over all of the farmland there. You may see some Israeli officials condemning Friday’s attack because it was so gnarly and involved an American, but, at the end of the day, the most violent settlers are working toward a state project of taking over land in the West Bank. And so, yes, the violence has increased both in intensity and in scale.

The settlers seem to be acting with more confidence, brazenness, and violence. How would you define their mentality?

I think the biggest thing is that they appear to be much more organized. I’ll give you an example. In Masafer Yatta, which is in the Hebron Hills, there’s been settler violence there for a very long time, but only in the past couple of years has what is essentially a gang formed. It’s run by a guy named Shimon Atiya, and he runs a farm, and there are lots of young men and teen-agers who work for him, and some of them actually live with him. And these guys go from village to village, vandalizing property, letting their livestock feed on Palestinian land, and beating up residents in some cases.

They appear like a gang because what happens is, first, there will be a couple of maybe twelve- or thirteen-year-old kids who come and try to plant an Israeli flag somewhere. And then when people try to stop them, a couple of older kids will come and start pushing and shoving and punching, and then maybe Atiya or one of his right-hand men show up with a gun and start waving the gun around and actually scaring the crap out of people to make them leave. So I am seeing a level of organization with what are essentially settler militias that I did not witness three years ago. And part of that is because the settlers are so well supported and resourced by the government and by the military. I mean, they have the ability to basically create these little org structures.

A guy like Atiya or Elisha Yered or Amishav Melet has a direct connection to the I.D.F., or a commander in the I.D.F. that they work directly with. And that I.D.F. commander is responsible for giving them an official I.D.F. guard, who basically serves as their personal security and their enforcer in some cases. [In a statement, the I.D.F. said, “The claim that the IDF provides personal security guards to the leaders of the outposts is entirely unfounded.”] So settlers have this sort of direct material connection to the military that, in years past, may not have existed at all. At other times it was probably more tacit, but now it’s direct. There’s a guy in the I.D.F. central command whose job is to meet with outpost leaders and make sure they have everything that they need, and that includes money, too.

And so then that money is used to pay shepherds to work for them. And part of what the shepherds do is they bring their livestock onto Palestinian land to destroy their vegetation, so they kill off the farmland. So they’re operating in this very strategic way that, from a distance, appears to be random. And I think at one time in the past it was more random, but now you can just see that it’s a well-oiled machine.

So the approach by authorities has changed from letting violence happen to actively working with the perpetrators?

Absolutely. I think that’s the biggest change. Smotrich, in addition to being the finance minister, also heads up something called the Settlement Administration, which he created within the Ministry of Defense so that he could basically take charge of the military occupation of the West Bank and clear all the red tape involved in authorizing new settlements, or new roads, or things like that. So, for example, it is now much, much, much easier for a settler leader who wants to create a new outpost to get whatever resources he needs for that, because all that red tape that used to be there doesn’t exist anymore. Now it’s a bunch of messianic settler ideologues like Smotrich in the government, who are just rubber-stamping most of what they want.

And that also trickles down to the fact that these settler groups are unbelievably well armed. Settlers have been basically deputized into these groups called Hagmar, where they just become settler-soldiers, who are basically wearing full I.D.F. attire and have rifles. Smotrich has what is called a sovereignty plan, which he unveiled last year, to basically squeeze Palestinians into six major population centers, which include Ramallah and the other big cities. And the way that the settler violence has unfolded recently, the way that the outposts are popping up and the villages are being emptied—it’s in direct alignment with his plan.

I have heard people say that the attack on Heidi Levine only got so much attention because she was American, when in fact this type of thing happens constantly to Palestinians, including during the attack on Friday, with much less attention. This is surely true, but I also think it’s important to understand how the settlers and the Israeli government view the risks of attacking Americans, especially American journalists. How has this question changed since you started making these trips, which was during the Biden Administration?

A Palestinian American who I talked to about this told me that, under Biden, if an American was beat up or they had their home burned or something, or an American was brutalized by the I.D.F., somebody from the Embassy would come and send their condolences, and then they would leave, and the victim would never hear from them again. With the Trump Administration, they have not even been making the trip. In other words, there’s no material difference between the two. It’s just that one of them was putting a dressing on it to act like there was some care.

I think that Palestinian Americans are a good measure for this because they look like other Palestinians, but they have blue passports. And so it’s sort of like you’re controlling for a variable here. There are Palestinian communities in the West Bank which are, like, eighty per cent Palestinian American. Because of their dual citizenship, they used to be pretty insulated from the violence of the occupation, from the settlers. But that privilege disappeared on October 7th. And now those Palestinian Americans are coming to terms with the fact that they’re actually just Palestinians. There’s no distinction.

As far as Americans who are not also Palestinian, like myself, or Heidi—I mean, look, it’s anecdotal, but I will tell you that, in my five trips, the level of violence that I have directly faced myself has quite literally increased. My first three times, nobody ever actually laid hands on me. I was in cars that were attacked. I was chased. I had guns pointed at me, but nobody ever struck me. The last time I was here, in June and July, I was attacked multiple times. This trip, I’ve been here for ten days, and to tell you the truth, I’ve lost count of how many times I’ve been attacked already. And, listen, I don’t look Palestinian. I’m usually shouting in English. A lot of the time I’m wearing a press vest or I’m shouting that I am a journalist, and they just don’t give a shit. They don’t even blink. It doesn’t faze them. They still come after me just like they would anyone else.

It was the olive-harvest day in Khirbet Abu Falah, a Palestinian community in the West Bank. And, like I said, there’s this outpost that exists on the top of a hill that overlooks the olive fields, and the Palestinians are routinely attacked by settlers that live there. So, because of these attacks, the olive fields have basically been off limits for the Palestinians, but this is their livelihood, and this is the harvesting season. And so, basically, what a lot of these communities do is pick a day, or a couple of days, when everybody will go out together, and the hope is that there will be protection in numbers.

But, from the minute we got there, we were watching the settlers at the top of the hill prepare for an attack. I mean, we were literally watching them suit up, put their masks over their heads, pick up their clubs, and it looked like a group of people getting ready for battle, which is exactly what they were doing. The olive harvest is supposed to be this peaceful and celebratory family affair, but in this case, because of the risk of attack, it was only men who went to harvest. There were no women or children there, and they were just racing as fast as they could to get the olives off the tree, because we were watching the settlers get ready to attack. About an hour into it, they start charging down the hill, and initially they are just hurling really big rocks, and they have these slings that they use. So these are really big rocks that are coming in from a distance, really fast, and, if you get hit by one, it can do real damage. So first they’re just charging down with rocks. There’s probably forty to fifty of them. And you have to keep in mind that the olive fields—and this is the case in a lot of Palestinian villages—are basically the buffer between the outpost and the village itself, the residential core of the village. And so, if the settlers are able to push right past the olive fields, they’re now in the village, and they’re going to start ransacking homes and basically doing a pogrom.

So, in other words, the Palestinians had an interest in defending themselves, not just to defend themselves so they could continue picking olives but because it’s existential. It was sort of a remarkable thing to witness, but dozens of young Palestinians just put masks on and picked up rocks and charged back at the settlers, and they beat them back. And so there was about ten to fifteen minutes where it was like a battle. The settlers would make a push and hurl rocks, and then the Palestinians would charge back. Then things changed when the settlers started setting trees on fire, and so now there’s smoke in the air, and the Palestinians are having a harder time pushing forward because of that. And then the military showed up and started tear-gassing the Palestinians. So, once there’s tear gas—you can’t run through tear gas. So everybody’s basically now in retreat, and then out of the tear gas emerges a white pickup truck with, I think, two or three settlers, in black hoodies, holding rifles, shooting them into the air. And, as soon as we heard machine-gun fire, everybody just turned around and ran.

And you’re running along with everyone else?

Oh, yeah. I was right at the front line and, as soon as I heard the machine-gun fire, that’s it.

When you say you’re at the front line, you’re observing as a journalist here?

Yeah. I’m just there taking cover and filming what’s happening. And so, as everybody’s running backwards, that must be when Heidi was caught. I’m not sure if she fell or if she was just overtaken. I mean, she’s sixty-three years old, and these settlers are maybe a third or fourth her age. But, basically, after we retreated and the charge stopped, I look back, and I see somebody in a press vest on the ground with five or six settlers on top of them, just beating them over and over with clubs. It was truly barbaric. At one point, we saw them lift her up by her collar, and I was, like, “Oh, are they helping her up?” And then somebody came and kicked her and knocked her back down, and then they continued beating her. They stole everything off of her: her phone, her camera, her multiple cameras, her wallet, her passport.

At this point, I actually didn’t know who was being beaten, but I was worried this person was going to be killed, and we were trying to figure out how to get to her because there was still this mob of settlers attacking us, plus there was tear gas in the air. A group of young Palestinian men appeared and led the way, started throwing rocks, and pushed the settlers back. And so the reason that the settlers stopped attacking Heidi is that they were forced to retreat. And that’s how I was then able to reach her. I saw her and I was shocked. I was, like, “Why is my Jewish mother here on the front lines of this battle?” I helped her up. She was in really, really bad shape. At first, I didn’t think we were going to have to run, because the settlers were retreating, but then they charged us again, and so then we had to run. I mean, in the video, you hear her say, “Let me die, let me die.” Fortunately, we were able to get her to the back, and some Palestinian paramedics picked her up. Again, the whole time, the only reason we were not completely overtaken is because these young Palestinian men were protecting us with rocks.

It ended after that happened, which was the last of the major settler violence that day. But then the I.D.F. soldiers who have been there, who’ve been tear-gassing the Palestinians and providing cover for the settlers, stormed the village. Rather than go after the settlers who have just nearly killed a woman, they stormed the village and started firing their weapons, and they shot a Palestinian man in the leg. The bullet shattered his femur and left countless bullet fragments that surgeons were unable to remove. He had to have a major surgery. I heard the gunshots, ran over, and was trying to document it, and soldiers were pushing me around, trying to throw me on the ground. One soldier aimed his gun at the paramedics. The New York Times reported that they were demanding the wounded man show his I.D. or he wouldn’t be able to go to the hospital. And then at some point, he was put in an ambulance, which took him away, and then the soldiers hung around for a bit. They shot a little more tear gas, and then they left. And then, actually, what was cool was that the Palestinians then went back out to their olive harvest. I mean, frankly, the feeling on the ground was that, despite two really grave injuries and other injuries, it was sort of a win for the Palestinians, because they did beat the settlers back and return to their olive fields.

The reason I keep bringing up the Palestinians throwing rocks to protect Heidi is because, I mean, I can’t emphasize enough how much these young men are . . . On Friday, they put their lives on the line. There were soldiers there, and soldiers will shoot when somebody is throwing rocks. These young men put their lives on the line to protect a sixty-three-year-old Jewish American journalist. And, I mean, these guys are heroes. If they were not doing that, so many villages would’ve been overtaken. They are literally the only defense. [On Saturday] I was in a village called Jalud, and the exact same thing happened. We got attacked by settlers, and it was these young men, whom we call the shabab, who fought them back with rocks. And the reason I just want to put a fine point on this is because, for me, just in terms of where things are going with Israel-Palestine politically, I think that a sort of discursive shift in how we talk about Palestinians’ right to defend themselves needs to happen.

To what degree is the election, which is in less than ten days, on people’s minds, either in terms of how Palestinians are thinking it will or won’t change what’s happening, or in how Israeli settlers are thinking about it, or just in the broad politics of what all this means internally for Israel?

It is top of mind for everybody. For the Palestinians, they are all over the map when it comes to their politics. Some of them don’t believe that there’s any version of an Israeli government that would allow them to live a decent life, but most Palestinians, especially the ones in vulnerable communities, feel that if Netanyahu is out, and that means Smotrich and Itamar Ben-Gvir are out, there will at least be a degree shift in the level of overt support that these settlers are getting from the military and from the government. Nobody thinks that a new government is going to roll back the settler movement. And nobody thinks that Gadi Eisenkot, the opposition leader, is going to even dismantle the outposts or anything. But the hope is that settlers will get fewer guns, and less support to just build roads wherever they want. Still, Eisenkot went to an illegal outpost earlier this year, a notoriously violent one, and met with the leaders there, in what one can only assume was a political meeting, a signal to them that they didn’t have to worry if the government changed.

The other way that it’s affecting things right now is that there’s a feeling that the settlers are racing against the clock, that if the government changes they’re not going to have as much support, and so they need to basically steal as much land as they possibly can before the clock runs out.

Exactly. And, by the way, that is the entire philosophy of Smotrich, who is running the ethnic-cleansing campaign in the West Bank, and who has said he wants to change the DNA of the system, meaning changes in the bureaucracy and the governing mechanisms, which will make it impossible to undo this stuff.

I think that the last thing I’ll mention is that there’s a real fear about what’s going to happen immediately after the election, because, if Netanyahu wins, the settlers are going to be doing celebratory pogroms. Is it Safe to Travel to Paris Right Now Amidst the France Protests?

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If there is one thing that the French know how to do, it is fight for their rights. It’s not for the goodness of politicians' hearts that university fees in France are still $200 for a bachelor's degree but thanks to a population that refuses to back down when their rights are being threatened.

Since September, French high school students have been protesting for better conditions, especially in suburban areas often forgotten by institutions. The movement has reached almost unprecedented proportions, with other sectors joining the students, demanding better learning conditions and fighting against the cost-of-living crisis, hike in fuel prices, and erosion of social welfare.

I have seen many large-scale gatherings during my years in France, but nothing like what’s unfolding right now. It’s important, however, to distinguish between peaceful demonstrators and those involved in vandalism, violence, or looting. Although not always of this magnitude, violence and looting have also marred some previous France protests, whether it’s the country winning a football match or students fighting for their rights. If you're visiting Paris soon or planning an upcoming trip, here's what to know.

High school students have called for days of action on October 22 and 24, as well as November 3 and 5, raising the possibility of future disruption in the coming weeks. With further demonstrations planned for Saturday October 24, the government is preparing a substantial security operation in central Paris that will deploy armored vehicles, specialist police and gendarmerie units, and military personnel from Operation Sentinelle.

On Thursday October 22, the Préfecture de Police banned unauthorized demonstrations and gatherings between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. in several parts of central Paris, including areas around the Louvre, Champs-Élysées, Élysée Palace, Bastille, République, and major railway stations.

Meanwhile, on October 22, several Metro stations were closed from 10 a.m. at the request of the Préfecture de Police, including Bastille, Saint-Paul, Place d'Italie, Campo Formio, Saint-Marcel, Quai de la Rapée, Gare d'Austerlitz, Bréguet-Sabin, and Chemin Vert. Services on Metro Line 10 were also interrupted between Gare d'Austerlitz and Jussieu. These closures follow restrictions earlier in the week at major transport interchanges, including République and Châtelet-Les Halles, where trains were unable to stop. Travelers should check the latest RATP Group updates before setting off, as further closures may be introduced at short notice.

Beyond Paris, localized curfews have been introduced in several other French cities, including Lyon, Bordeaux, Lille, and Nice. In Lyon, restrictions covering part of the central Presqu'île district, from the Hôtel de Ville to Place Carnot, were imposed between 9 p.m. and 6 a.m. on Monday and were renewed through Thursday morning. In Bordeaux, Lille, and Nice, the curfews and restrictions have primarily been put in place for minors, rather than all residents and visitors.

Demonstrators gather across Paris as part of a broader protest movement over rising living costs and college tuition.

Paris is far from the only French city to have been affected by the recent unrest. On Saturday October 17, demonstrations took place across the country, with an estimated 550,000 people taking to the streets according to the French authorities, although organizers put the figure at 800,000. While many of the marches remained peaceful, violent outbreaks have occurred in major cities such as Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux, and Lille.

In Lyon, more than 20,000 people gathered for demonstrations that descended into violent clashes with police. One such incident took place at Place Bellecour, where a branch of the city's tourist office was vandalized and set on fire after a group entered without authorization. Dozens of shops across the Presqu'île, Lyon's central peninsula, were also damaged and looted, while in Limoges, the historic Hôtel de Ville was devastated by a fire during the unrest. The 19th-century building, parts of which are protected as historic monuments, suffered extensive damage to its roof structure and central gable, with local authorities warning that it had not yet been saved.

Elsewhere, protesters made their way into Marseille's town hall, damaging the interior, while similar incidents were reported at municipal buildings in Lille and Angers. This violence has raised concerns within France's tourism industry, particularly as the country enters the Toussaint school holidays. According to Le Monde, some hotels have already reported cancellations and early departures, although the overall impact on bookings remains limited for now.

Does this mean Paris—and France as a whole—is a no-go zone? No, it doesn’t. Certain streets and neighborhoods may be affected by demonstrations, police operations, or temporary closures, but the disruption is not necessarily city-wide. You can still come to Paris during this time and enjoy yourself, provided you keep informed and keep your wits about you when navigating the city. You might have to change some of your plans, but the good thing about Paris is that you can be here for decades, like me, and still discover new beautiful corners.

When visiting a city, we all tend to want to stay as central as possible, but this is not always a good idea when protests are happening. Avoid booking a hotel close to main squares or places of particular significance such as République, Bastille, Nation, and Place de Clichy. Paris is not big and quite easy to navigate from wherever you are—at this time, choose to stay in quieter, residential areas. I suggest areas on the Rive Gauche like the 15th and 14th or the 13th arrondissements. I particularly like the 15th as it has a lot of cute boutique hotels, and it’s close to some lovely places to visit like the Musée Bourdelle.

“Travelers should also think beyond their immediate surroundings. If a primary transportation route becomes inaccessible, do they know how to reach their accommodations or contact someone for assistance?” says John Gobbels, CEO of air medical transport and security response membership program Medjet. “Maintaining situational awareness and having a contingency plan can make a meaningful difference when circumstances change unexpectedly.”

Although authorized peaceful demonstrations are planned in advance, looters do not give a heads up before they take to the streets, so make sure you keep an eye on French news. “Once in the country, travelers should check local news, government travel advisories, and transport updates before heading out, particularly when demonstrations are expected near major attractions or transport hubs," suggests Gobbels. ”Keep your phone charged, save emergency contacts offline, and be prepared to adjust your itinerary as conditions warrant. If you encounter a demonstration, avoid the crowd, move away calmly, and follow local authorities’ instruction.”

For metro closures and issues with public transport, check the Bonjour RATP website, while the Préfecture de Police also publishes information about demonstrations, traffic restrictions, and security measures—it’s worth following on social media platforms for immediate information. When it comes to news outlets, Franceinfo and Le Monde are reliable, so it’s good to keep an eye on their websites. Although outlets such as Le Monde publish in English, their French-language coverage may offer more frequent local updates.

I know it’s sad not to be able to stick to your plans, but for your safety, it is better to avoid certain areas and attractions while the France protests are unfolding. Big institutions like the Louvre—which was blocked by students on Monday October 19—and the Élysée Palace are better avoided; instead, visit some of the city's niche sights and smaller museums. You could visit the beautiful Musée Gustave Moreau in the 9th or the Musée Zadkine in the 6th, depending on where you are staying. Big touristy spots such as the Eiffel Tower are open and busy—if you don’t feel comfortable but still want a picture with the monument in the background, head to Île aux Cygnes to see the tiny Statue of Liberty and the Tower in all her glory, from a safe distance.

Thoroughfares in French cities large and small, from Paris to Limoges, were filled with as many as 800,000 people, according to organizers.

State intelligence services underestimated the level of participation, as impromptu marches crisscrossing city centers were able to overwhelm police forces. There were more than 2,000 arrests reported across the country over hours of heated clashes that saw the burning of public buildings and the ransacking of private businesses like luxury retail stores in Paris and Bordeaux. Though the vast majority of protesters were peaceful, some violent demonstrators did engage in acts of vandalism, like the burning of the neo-Renaissance city hall building in Limoges. As of Sunday, 500 people were still in police custody in the capital alone.

“How can French people do this to France?” said President Emmanuel Macron on Sunday evening, responding to the weekend’s rioting in a televised address. Blaming “ultra-left groupings” and “delinquents,” the president offered little in the way of major policy announcements, indicating instead that police prefects would be encouraged to consider strict protest bans.

The convergence between students and this weekend’s marches was not surprising. A segregated, underfunded education system is only one of the many grievances bringing French high school and university students out in force. From artificial intelligence and climate change to diminishing expectations over an uncertain future, they are calling attention to many of the demands heard in youth-led movements like Spain’s recent housing protests, anti-conscription demonstrations in Germany, and India’s so-called “cockroach” movement.

For many, it’s also a political coming of age.

The impasse is only exacerbated by growing fears over the trajectory of French politics, which after a decade under President Emmanuel Macron has seen the far right make considerable gains in both institutional and cultural power. Going from vocal outsiders to insider kingmakers, Marine Le Pen’s Rassemblement National is in many respects already setting the agenda when it comes to immigration, Islam, and the demonization of progressives. The imminent risk today is the all-too-possible scenario of a far-right victory in the spring 2027 presidential elections.

To some, it’s a sign of the movement’s hijacking by the left: Many students have made no mystery of their fears of where the normalization of Le Pen is taking the country they’re inheriting.

“The ideas of the far right are becoming mainstream while those who denounce the country’s slide into fascism are being discredited,” Soumaya Sarré told The Nation. Sarré is the spokesperson for the Fédération Lycéenne des Quartiers, a collective established in Saint-Denis in early October that seeks to represent the specific needs of students in the working-class and ethnically diverse banlieues.

Saturday’s marches were again filled with protesters chanting antifascist slogans and carrying banners denouncing the rise of the far right. The chorus from a 1985 punk anthem, “Porcherie” (“Pigsty”)—“the youth shit on the National Front”—remains a staple today, and has been heard at marches and occupations since the student movement began in September. The Front National, founded in the 1970s by Marine Le Pen’s father, Jean-Marie, was rebranded as the Rassemblement National (National Rally) in 2018.

“The situation is already bad, but we know that if the Rassemblement National wins, it will be even worse,” said Marius Mesnil, co–general secretary of the Union Syndicale Lycéenne. “We know they won’t do anything to fight the racism plaguing the police. In fact they’ll only encourage it,” he added. “What the RN wants is more leeway for the police, more weapons, and even harsher crackdowns.”

The threat of far-right rule is more real now than ever. All opinion polling shows Le Pen towering above her rivals in the April 18 first-round vote, often by double-digit leads. These predictions remain hypothetical, but in possible second-round face-offs, the prognosis is just as grim: Le Pen, a four-time presidential candidate, maintains an advantage over every possible contender.

France’s student revolt highlights a generational rift. Many of the young, first-time, and future voters who have demonstrated in recent weeks are at odds with a voting population that skews in favor of older, more right-wing citizens. Conservative media have tried to turn right-wing influencers into representatives of a supposedly dormant thirst for order among French youth. “Rioters once again ruined the October 17 protests in defense of purchasing power,” argued Marine Le Pen in a social-media post on Saturday.

Yet young French people are considerably less on board with the idea of a Le Pen presidency than their elders. Upwards of 49 percent of voters between the ages of 18 and 24 are estimated to support the leader of the left-wing party La France Insoumise, Jean-Luc Mélenchon, even if a still-considerable 28 percent favor Le Pen.

“I’m not sure whether this movement will amount to a political revolution,” said Mesnil. He nonetheless points to a recent study suggesting that 80 percent of young people intend to vote in the next presidential election, potentially bucking the trend in an age group usually marked by high rates of abstention. “This movement has significantly politicized young people,” Mesnil said, holding out hope that “this could potentially translate into institutional change in 2027.”

But the risk remains that they’re speaking into a vacuum.

“Support for the student movement is very weak among the right-wing electorate and elderly voters,” said Gilles Ivaldi, a political scientist and expert on the far right. “The Rassemblement National’s calls to restore order through things like a new ‘rioters-pay’ law are appeals to that base.”

He continued: “Retirees are the largest pool of voters and are the electorate that votes the most consistently. This generational divide is not new. A growing problem in France is that politics is increasingly being made by and for elderly people.”

In keeping with its law-and-order mantra, Le Pen’s party distanced itself from this fall’s protests from the beginning. It has played off the reduction of the demonstrations to an outburst of violence in the banlieues, castigating the outgoing Macronists for laxism. Speaking from Madrid on October 13, on the third day of countrywide student marches, Rassemblement National president Jordan Bardella called for an immediate end to protests and for tougher legal penalties against minors.

Le Pen is also likely counting on the coming presidential election to curb frustrations that have led to the ongoing cascade of occupations, protests, and rioting. “French people can no longer bear being trapped between state austerity and tax bludgeoning on the one hand and insurrectionist activists on the other. They must hold on for a few more months,” Le Pen wrote on the social-media platform X.

“The malaise in France has reached such depths that while it’s now spilling out onto the streets, it will definitely be reflected in the voting booths come April,” said Ivaldi, who teaches at Sciences Po. Between its rigid “security”-obsessed platform and its proposals to address the cost-of-living crisis by slashing payroll charges and sales taxes, the RN hopes to be seen as a firm party of order for a country trapped in a cycle of economic decline and social unrest.

For now, the perception of the ongoing demonstrations maps onto the country’s preexisting political divides.

The absence of widespread outrage at the scale of the police clampdown on this fall’s protests is itself revelatory of the climate induced by Le Pen’s rise. Ubiquitous images of brutal riot police interventions, and even accounts of sexual harassment endured by detained protesters, are being drowned out in a country that is growing accustomed to violence.

Right-wingers can point to studies claiming that nearly three-quarters of the public favor doing away with underage protections for rioting infractions. Still, other opinion polls suggest that some 55 percent of the broader population approve of the student marches, with 72 percent looking favorably on the protesters’ demands.

“October 17 came at just the right time,” said Sarré. “The high school movement can bring other causes and sectors together, like the ‘yellow vests,’ railway workers, and farmers. This convergence can only amplify our voices.”

But Le Pen’s appeal as a rampart against disorder also has receptive listeners, especially in a media ecosystem and political class drifting toward the hard right. Nowhere is this slide more evident than in the designation of La France Insoumise as the bête noire of the ongoing unrest. “Mélenchon has turned these young people into enemies of everything and everyone,” Le Pen said on October 15. The investigative news outlet Le Canard enchaîné reported on October 13 that the Macronist prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, pushed for criminal prosecutions for “inciting rebellion” against Mélenchon and party leaders. “I don’t know who will hear me given the circumstances, but my message is clear: Those who share our struggle have no place in looting and vandalism,” said Mélenchon on Sunday, in an implicit recognition of the party’s isolation.

Between Le Pen and the Macronist center, a shared front against social unrest has the advantage of distracting from the meager responses to protesters’ material demands. Education Minister Edouard Geffray announced an “emergency plan” that students and teachers have widely derided as insufficient. Seriously answering popular outrage over declining living standards is anathema for a government otherwise fixated on containing the budget deficit, which at 5.4 percent of GDP is the highest in the eurozone.

Le Pen’s party is equally eager to come across as guardians of fiscal discipline. This month, the Rassemblement National presented the outlines of its budget plan, widely criticized by economists, that calls for draconian DOGE-style cuts worth up to €140 billion by 2032. Le Pen has also promised the creation of a constitutional “brake” on deficit spending. As financial markets grow increasingly restless about France’s budget woes, she wants to send the message that a far-right government would be responsible toward creditors.

Students and cost-of-living protesters have set themselves the unenviable task of defying the laws of political gravity now at work in the country. As one widely used slogan for the October 17 demonstration put it, “It’s time to break out of the contemporary world.”

The Supreme Court on Monday agreed to hear a challenge to the Reconstruction-era ban on distilling alcohol in private homes.

Why it matters: Americans can legally brew beer or make wine at home, but making whiskey, vodka or other spirits can land them in federal prison. The Supreme Court could change that.

Driving the booze: SCOTUS will review Department of Justice v. McNutt, an April ruling by the conservative 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals that found the nearly 160-year-old home-distilling ban unconstitutional and an overreach of Congress' taxing power.

  • Distilling in your home carries a penalty of up to five years in prison, a $10,000 fine or both.
  • The 6th Circuit reached the opposite conclusion in a separate April ruling for Ream v. Department of the Treasury, arguing Congress can restrict distilling at home to protect federal alcohol tax collections.

What they're saying: Advocates argue that Congress overstepped their powers by banning distilling in the home through a measure meant to prohibit fraud from commercial distillers, per the Associated Press.

  • The government says Congress acted within its authority as the restriction was intended to stop producers from evading federal excise taxes, per AP.
  • Lifting the ban, the government argues, could undermine Congress' authority to enforce federal tax laws.

Friction point: Both appeals courts agreed Congress' taxing power alone doesn't authorize the ban. They split over whether it is a permissible way to collect liquor taxes under the Constitution's Necessary and Proper Clause.

Reality check: You didn't hear this from us, but the practice of distilling hooch and moonshine still happens in the U.S. despite the federal prohibition.

  • Some states, like West Virginia, allow some home distilling. But, much like marijuana, those state laws don't override federal law.
  • Advocates hope the Supreme Court will strike down the federal ban and its associated criminal penalty.

Why distilling drinks at home is banned

Flashback: Congress banned distilling spirits in homes and connected properties in 1868, during the Reconstruction, as part of a crackdown on widespread tax evasion.

  • The restrictions also cover certain premises where beer or wine is made.

Yes, but: Federal law allows Americans to brew beer and make wine at home, but not distill spirits.

  • Distilling spirits requires a process that gives people a more concentrated drink and can involve fire hazards and dangerous contamination risks.

What happens if SCOTUS overturns the ban?

What's next: Even if SCOTUS strikes down the ban, this doesn't mean people can start distilling automatically.

  • Federal alcohol-production rules could limit what people could do at home. The government currently requires permits, registration and tax compliance for the production of distilled spirits.
  • States could also impose their own bans, which would limit what people can make in their homes.

The bottom line: SCOTUS could make it easier for Americans to produce their own liquor, but overturning the ban wouldn't necessarily mean anyone could legally set up a still in their backyard … yet.

“What would Jesus do if he visited the United States Senate?”

It’s a question one Senate hopeful has repeatedly asked on the campaign trail. To anyone who’s followed the last 40 years of American politics, this merging of preaching and politics may not sound particularly novel. But the candidate’s answer—and his party affiliation—marks a surprising departure from the usual partisan scripts.

“I think he would tell the senators on the floor, ‘Depart from me,’” James Talarico has told audiences. “For I was hungry and you cut my food assistance. I was sick and you kicked me off Medicaid. I was a stranger and you deported me and my family.”

Talarico, a Presbyterian seminarian, is running on a compassionate, liberal Christianity rarely seen since the right gained a stranglehold on faith-forward politics decades ago. But in recent years, Republicans, previously the bastions of good old-fashioned, Focus on the Family traditionalism, have rolled over to the dark underbelly of their movement. Exit: modest family values. Enter: barstool conservatism.

In doing so, they’ve created an opening for Democrats who make their religious convictions central to their politics. And unlike the Moral Majority of years past, these liberal candidates are preaching a faith that’s inclusive, optimistic, and winning support on both sides of the aisle.

Entrenched though it’s been, the “God gap” between Democrats and Republicans is a relatively recent creation. The Republican Party platform from 1972 contains no references to God, and in 1976, evangelicals voted for the Sunday-school teachings of Jimmy Carter.

That all changed in the 1980s with Reagan’s embrace of Jerry Falwell, who urged his fellow evangelical pastors to bring politics to the pulpit and push their congregations toward conservative candidates. The conflation of Republican beliefs and Christian identity had officially begun, and after decades of work from the likes of Pat Robertson, James Dobson—culminating in eight years of President George W. Bush—there was no stopping the merger.

But just when the God gap had reached its peak, Trump slithered in. While his extramarital affairs and long history of alleged sexual misconduct always made for an uneasy allegiance with the religious right, the president has more recently drawn fire from Christian groups for posting an AI image depicting himself like Jesus, suspending refugee programs, and almost allowing the livestream execution of a prisoner. And though he says the Bible is his favorite book, it’s hard to believe he can stomach a narrative that elevates another power above his own. The lack of pictures probably doesn’t help, either.

Regardless, white evangelicals continue to approve of Trump and his political juggernaut in shockingly high numbers, albeit less resoundingly than they did last year. But across the country, Republican candidates replicating his cheap political playbook aren’t faring as well.

Nowhere is this more apparent, of course, than the messy Senate brawl in Texas.

In one corner, we have MAGA adherent Ken Paxton, perhaps the single most morally bankrupt Senate candidate in the country—and it’s generally a competitive category. A self-identified Christian, Paxton cannot name what church he attends.

In the other corner, Talarico—a theologian, oratorical talent, and former underdog in this race—has embraced the campaign slogan “It’s time to start flipping tables,” a direct reference to the passage in the Bible when Jesus admonished merchants for corrupting a sacred temple with exploitative commerce. It’s David and Goliath if Goliath were indicted on two counts of securities fraud.

But Talarico isn’t the only Democrat closing the “God gap.” Senator (and Reverend) Raphael Warnock set the stage with his Baptist pride and 2021 Senate win—and he’s not ruling out a 2028 run for the presidency. And at least six Democrats running for office this cycle are ordained clergy, including Kansas’s Adam Hamilton, who, with the support of prominent local Republicans, has turned his deep-red state into a battleground.

It’s not just Christians, either. Mayor Zohran Mamdani openly celebrates his Muslim faith, despite Islamophobic backlash. And Senate hopeful Abdul El-Sayed continues to embrace his Muslim identity ahead of his contentious election in Michigan. Their example is broadening the definition of what a “faith-first candidate” means in America.

And in turning toward faith, Democrats are far from alone. After years in slow free fall, the share of Americans who identify as Christian is no longer declining. Meanwhile, 31 percent of Americans now see religion as gaining influence—still a distinct minority, but that’s the highest number Pew has measured in 15 years.

Perhaps, with everyday life increasingly filled with misinformation, AI deepfakes, and broad losses in institutional confidence, more Americans are seeking comfort beyond empiricism. It’s no surprise, then, that Gen Z, facing the host of problems we’ve left them with, might be more religious than other generations.

By long ceding the pulpit to Republicans, liberals only made it easier for the right to smuggle cruel policies and ugly bigotries into our political mainstream under the guise of religion. With this new crop of faith-fluent Democrats, however, the left may have found a potent way to fight back. After all, as Talarico has said, quoting scripture, “You can’t love God and hate other people.”
