Musk says SpaceX did restrict Starlink, as Ukraine says the tech has been restored

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The roots of Russia's invasion of Ukraine go back decades and run deep. The current conflict is more than one country fighting to take over another; it is — in the words of one U.S. official — a shift in "the world order."Here are some helpful stories to make sense of it all.

Elon Musk listens as President Donald Trump speaks during an event at the U.S. Institute of Peace, Oct. 8, 2026, in Washington. Julia Demaree Nikhinson/AP hide caption

Elon Musk says that his firm SpaceX did restrict Ukraine's use of the Starlink satellite system, after several days of controversy over the technology.

In a post on social media site X, Musk said that Ukraine had been violating Starlink's terms of service by using it for offensive military activity.

On Saturday, Ukraine said that Starlink and the military's use of the related Starshield technology were back to high operating speeds after more than a week of disruptions.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on X that the restoration began overnight.

"The most important thing today is that Starlink and Starshield operations have been restored to the speeds we had before the restrictions," Zelenskyy said. "The speeds are now where they need to be."

The Starlink network has been critical for Ukraine in its defense to Russia's full-scale war.

Ukraine uses Starlink for drone operations, logistics such as delivering food and water to soldiers, and frontline communications. Zelenskyy said the satellite service also helps defend Ukraine from Iranian-made Shahed drones used by Russia.

The U.S. and Ukraine had been at odds over who to blame for the more than week-long disruptions to Elon Musk's SpaceX operated satellite technology.

Zelenskyy told NBC's Meet the Press Sunday that Ukrainian officials believed the U.S. government was behind the disruptions and restrictions to Starlink and Starshield. He said SpaceX put the restrictions in response to a "message from [the] American government."

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy answers media questions before his meeting with Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo in Kyiv, Ukraine, on Thursday. Efrem Lukatsky/AP hide caption

In an Oct. 14 post on X, Musk stated that Starshield is owned and controlled by the U.S. government.

"Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS," he said.

On Saturday, in a separate post on X, Musk said Ukraine's military had broken Starlink's terms of service.

"Using Starlink, a civilian commercial service, for offensive drone strikes to kill people has never been allowed, nor should it be," Musk wrote. "Starshield, a different system, is controlled by the US government. What they decide to do is up to them."

"Ukraine military violated Starlink terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes to kill people," he continued. "That loophole was closed last week."

Musk says that Starlink "remains critical to the defense of Ukraine," adding that if it were turned off, the Ukrainian front line would "collapse immediately."

Musk said Starlink had been crucial to preventing Russia's invasion of Ukraine from succeeding fully in the early months of the war, when Russia destroyed or jammed other means of communication. He insisted that SpaceX "has done more to defend Ukraine than any private company on Earth BY FAR."

He also appeared to hit back at his critics, as well as those who suggest "no realistic solution to the conflict" in Ukraine. In a subsequent brief exchange, when a user wrote, "Zelensky needs to go," Musk agreed: "yes."

The Trump Administration has denied interfering with Ukraine's Starshield access.

Speaking to reporters Friday, President Trump declined to answer a question regarding who ordered the restrictions.

"I wouldn't tell you if I did know," Trump said.

Trump also defended Musk, dismissing concerns that the SpaceX CEO played a role in the limitations.

"Elon will never do anything wrong," Trump said. "I know him very well. He's a fair person."

On Sunday, Democratic Senator Adam Schiff told NBC's Meet the Press that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth should testify before the Senate to clarify the facts surrounding the Starlink limitations.

If the Democrats win the Senate in the upcoming midterm elections, "Defense Secretary Hegseth should be brought in. Anyone who has knowledge of what happened," Schiff said.

"You've got the president saying one thing, you've got Elon Musk saying another thing, you have them both saying different things at different times," Schiff added. "Somebody is lying here. Maybe they're all lying in different ways, but we need to find out."

Ukraine's Defense Ministry said Saturday its monitoring systems were officially recording the restoration of Starlink and Starshield operating speed. It said connectivity remained stable for projectiles and interceptors travelling at speeds of more than 250 mph.

The restrictions had capped the speed at about 100 mph. This hindered Ukraine's ability to intercept drones and disrupted missions.

Ukraine Defense Minister Yevhen Khmara said the restoration is the result of "direct dialogue" by Zelenskyy.

"This result is important, above all, for our front line, for service members, for drone operations, logistics, and communications," Khamara said.

In his post about the restoration, Zelenskyy thanked "all Americans" including members of Congress, the State Department and President Trump.

The restoration accompanied a renewed push for peace talks led by the U.S., with Russia agreeing to a trilateral meeting that will include representatives from Ukraine and the U.S. next month.

Russia and Ukraine also agreed to a localized ceasefire in the Russian occupied city of Oleshky in southern Ukraine. The White House said the ceasefire was brokered by First Lady Melania Trump, with an agreement for humanitarian aid and civilian evacuation as there is a growing food crisis in the city.

But Ukraine and Russia still continue to trade attacks elsewhere.

Ukraine attacked a chemical plant in the Russian city of Tolyatti on Saturday. A Russian drone struck a residential building in Kyiv's Obolon District Saturday causing a fire.

Published: 13:50 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 13:52 GMT, 25 October 2026

Wes Streeting has warned Britain must be ready for war in little more than 1,000 days as Russia steps up its threats against NATO allies.

The Defence Secretary cautioned that the UK's enemies could be 'in a position to attack' by 2030 as he urged the country to 'prepare for the worst'.

The chilling message came ahead of Wednesday's Budget, at which Chancellor John Healey will reportedly unveil billions of pounds on new defence spending.

He is said to be poised to put more money into the state-owned National Wealth Fund and British Business Bank, with some of the cash then invested in UK defence firms.

The move - which will raise the percentage of the UK's GDP spent on defence - is intended to signal a 'downpayment' on Mr Healey's aim of reaching 3 per cent of GDP on defence spending by 2030.

But the Chancellor is still not expected to lay out full plans for reaching the 3 per cent level, or 3.5 per cent by 2035 - a NATO target, until the Government's spending review next year.

This is despite Mr Healey having quit as defence secretary in Keir Starmer's administration over the issue earlier this year.

Tory shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith has called on Mr Healey to resign if he fails to set out how the Government will reach the 3 per cent mark.

Writing in The Telegraph, Mr Streeting said 'the gravest dangers of the 20th Century are returning, armed with the technologies of the 21st'.

He warned of the 'growing threat of state sponsored terrorism, sabotage and hostile acts designed to disrupt our lives, undermine our security and spread fear in our communities'.

'To prevent the worst, we have to prepare for it,' he added. 'In the first instance, that means expanding our capabilities so our adversaries think twice about attacking us.

'Should that fail, we must ensure that Britain is ready for war.'

The Defence Secretary said he was '100 per cent confident' that Mr Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham would meet the NATO defence spending target.

He added there was a 'lesson' to be drawn from the Ukraine conflict 'where tactics and technologies on the battlefield today are a world away from when the war began just a few years ago'.

'Our adversaries' willingness to harm us is not new. What has changed is their capacity,' Mr Streeting wrote.

'By 2030 their force strength and war economy could put them in a position to attack.

'That gives us a little over 1,000 days to complete our mission: to be ready with our allies to fight and win. This Government will make sure it is mission accomplished.'

The Sunday Times reported that the increased funding for the National Wealth Fund and British Business Bank would benefit drone manufacturers and military tech firms.

But the move could be controversial as it pushes increased defence spending to long-term borrowing, rather than spending cuts elsewhere - such as to the welfare bill - that the Tories have called for.

James Cartlidge, the Tory shadow defence secretary, said: 'Last year Kemi Badenoch and I announced our plans for a sovereign defence fund.

'A key part of this was to make the National Wealth Fund switch its investment from Net Zero to defence.

'So I welcome Labour copying our idea, particularly if that means getting more cash to UK start-ups and defence tech businesses.

'The main reason we proposed using National Wealth Fund cash as part of our multibillion-pound sovereign defence fund was to accelerate the uncrewed, AI-driven transformation of our Armed Forces.

IHS-Chef Bonin für „differenzierte Abschlüsse“ bei KV-Verhandlungen

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Der Chef des Insituts für Höhere Studien ortet die Forderungen der Arbeitnehmer beim Handels-KV „am oberen Rand“ und denkt an eine Modifizierung der „Benya-Formel“. Die Gewerkschaft ist dagegen.

IHS-Chef Holger Bonin plädiert bei den Lohnverhandlungen für „differenzierte Abschlüsse“. Die Lage in der österreichischen Wirtschaft sei „sehr, sehr unterschiedlich“, sagte der Wirtschaftsforscher am Sonntag in der ORF-„Pressestunde“. Er verwies etwa auf den Handel, der aufgrund des Onlinehandels und des veränderten Kaufverhaltens eine „komplett andere Situation“ habe als Teile der Industrie. Bonin plädierte u.a. auch für eine Modifizierung der sogenannten Benya-Formel.

Die vom früheren Gewerkschafter Anton Benya erstellte Formel bildet seit den 1970er-Jahren die Grundlage für die jährlichen Lohnverhandlungen. Laut dieser Formel wird die jährliche Inflation - also der durchschnittliche Verbraucherpreisindex - und ein Teil des gesamtwirtschaftlichen Produktivitätszuwachses gefordert.

Im Handel, bei dem vergangene Woche die Lohnverhandlungen begonnen haben, sei man nach wie vor mit der steigenden Konkurrenz durch den Onlinehandel konfrontiert, „das Kaufverhalten der Bürgerinnen und Bürger hat sich massiv verändert“, sagte Bonin. Im Handel habe man eine „komplett andere Situation“ als etwa in einem erfolgreichen Segment der Industrie, „wo exportiert wird“ und wo man davon profitiert, „dass der Welthandel wieder steigt“.

Seinen Wunsch nach „differenzierten Abschlüssen“ würde die „Benya-Formel“ aber eigentlich ohnehin besagen: „Wir orientieren uns an der Inflation und schauen uns dann an, wie sich die Produktivitätsentwicklung in den einzelnen Branchen eigentlich entwickeln sollte.“ Man müsse vermehrt auf die Produktivitätskomponente schauen, sagte der Chef des Wirtschaftsforschungsinstituts. Denn man habe diese Produktivitätskomponente „oft am Durchschnitt der Wirtschaft festgemacht“ und „nicht so sehr geschaut, wie geht es denn bei einzelnen Branchen“.

„Wir schauen bei dieser “Benya-Formel' immer nur nach hinten zurück„, man sei damit “immer zu spät„ - und gleiche “nachholend„ die Inflation aus. Damit habe man “zu sehr hohen Abschlüssen gegriffen„, “ohne zu sehen, dass die Entwicklung der Wirtschaftsleistung entsprechend nicht so gut ist„. Es gehe um Erwartungen und den Blick auf die Wirtschaftsaussichten.

Zu den Forderungen der Arbeitnehmerseite bei den Handels-Kollektivvertragsverhandlungen sagte Bonin, der Wunsch nach 3,8 Prozent Lohnplus sei „am oberen Rand dessen, was eben möglicherweise erfüllbar ist für Teile des Handels“. Auch innerhalb des Handels gebe es „sehr unterschiedliche Unternehmen“.

Die Produktivitätsentwicklung im Handel sei auf längere Frist aber negativ. „Und das muss man eben in irgendeiner Form bei den Handelsverhandlungen berücksichtigen.“ Die Löhne würden sich nicht mehr so dynamisch wie in anderen Bereichen der Wirtschaft entwickeln. Gestartet waren die KV-Verhandlungen am vergangenen Donnerstag, am 3. November wird weiter gefeilscht.

Man müsse damit rechnen, dass die Löhne im Handel über einen längeren Zeitraum nicht mehr nach oben gehen. Wenn man etwa in die Innenstädte schaue, dann merke man, „dass es immer weniger Geschäfte gibt“ und etwa durch Restaurants ersetzt werden. Diese Veränderungen werde man nicht mehr zurückdrehen können. „Am Ende müssen wir sehen, das wird ersetzt durch andere Dinge“, so würden etwa im Bereich der Freizeitindustrie „sehr dynamisch Jobs“ entstehen.

Gefragt - mit Blick auf die Demonstrationen in Frankreich -, ob man nicht auch den sozialen Frieden etwas einpreisen müsse, sagte Bonin, der soziale Friede werde nicht dadurch gewahrt, dass man auf Dauer Bereiche der Wirtschaft fördert, die nicht langfristig tragfähig sind. Man müsse den Strukturwandel sehen, da könne man nicht dagegen agieren. Die Frage sei vielmehr: „Wie schaffe ich es, mit meinem Sozialstaat dafür zu sorgen, dass die betroffenen Menschen sozial gesichert eben in die neuen Berufe kommen, wo die Zukunftschancen sind.“ Kritisch äußerte sich Bonin unter anderem auch zum Mietpreisdeckel und der Spritpreisbremse, auch plädierte er - langfristig - für eine Anhebung des Pensionsantrittsalters, vor allem aber müsse man „mehr über die Frühverrentung nachdenken“.

Der Gewerkschaftsbund ÖGB wies die Aussagen Bonins zurück: „Die Kritik an Kollektivverträgen und ‚Benya-Formel‘ sowie Forderungen nach einem höheren Pensionsantrittsalter gehen aus Sicht des ÖGB an der Lebensrealität der Beschäftigten vorbei“, sagte ÖGB-Bundesgeschäftsführerin Helene Schuberth. Bonins Kritik an „angeblich zu hohen Lohnabschlüssen“ greife zu kurz. „In Österreich werden Kollektivverträge seit jeher nach Branchen verhandelt. Die jeweilige wirtschaftliche Lage wird dabei berücksichtigt“, so Schuberth. Gleichzeitig mussten die Lohnabschlüsse der vergangenen Jahre massive Kaufkraftverluste infolge der hohen Inflation ausgleichen, betonte sie. „Wer die 'Benya-Formel' angreift, greift direkt die Beschäftigten an. Jeder Angriff auf die kollektivvertragliche Lohnentwicklung ist ein Angriff auf ihre Kaufkraft, ihre Leistung und ihren gerechten Anteil am Wohlstand.“

Weitere Lohnzurückhaltung wäre aus Sicht des ÖGB „auch wirtschaftlich kontraproduktiv“. Denn niedrigere Reallöhne würden weniger Kaufkraft und damit weniger privaten Konsum bedeuten. „Gerade in einer schwachen Konjunktur braucht Österreich aber Nachfrage und keine weitere Schwächung der Einkommen.“

Kritisch sieht der ÖGB auch Bonins Forderungen nach höherem Pensionsantrittsalter bzw. einer Eindämmung der frühzeitigen Antritte. „Das Antrittsalter für die Korridorpension wird bereits schrittweise von 62 auf 63 Jahre angehoben. Eine weitere Erhöhung sowie ein höheres gesetzliches Pensionsantrittsalter ignorieren die Situation älterer Arbeitnehmer:innen am Arbeitsmarkt.“ (APA)

I'm 62, covered in tattoos, and refuse to dress my age. Getting older doesn't mean getting boring.

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This as-told-to essay is based on a conversation with Lonni Pike. It has been edited for length and clarity.

Eleven years ago, when I was 51, I got sober after years of struggling with addiction. I felt vibrant again and ready for my life to start.

What I was met with was messaging on TV, social media, and even in day-to-day life that I had missed the train. I was too old, too boring, and I would just need to move along.

Everything associated with women and aging seemed to be negative. Even women themselves were negative about aging, and started hiding away, blending in, or getting treatments to chase youth.

But that wasn't going to be me. I was tenacious and determined to live the life I had another chance at.

Loving fashion, I started finding clothes that told the world who I was. Fashion is much more than clothing — it's the way people feel about themselves.

Most women my age had given in to the expectation that an aging woman is meant to be drab and boring.

For a bit of fun, I went to a clothing store and asked a staff member to help me find something to wear. She chose a pair of tan pants and a button-up, plain top, with a pair of loafers.

We are told not to show our arms because they are too flabby, not to wear miniskirts because our legs should not be seen, and to avoid bold colors so we don't stand out in a crowd.

Try a Level Up action pack.

Build a simple, steady morning ritual that supports energy, recovery, and the long game.

Add BI in Google so our reporting is easier to find when you’re searching for what matters.

I'd break out of this noise and dress in a way that made me feel good about myself.

With the help of my son, who studied broadcast journalism, I started posting on social media about what I was wearing.

I remember people telling me I looked like I was dressing like a teenager, or asking why I was wearing men's clothing. I often responded by saying that clothes don't have an age or gender, and that I was dressing to represent myself.

I've now created a community, mostly of women, who are watching my content and being reminded that aging isn't something to fear. It isn't the end.

I shaved my head because I hated my hair and felt that a buzz cut would make me feel more like I felt inside.

Three years ago, I started a gaming channel, and I've become a TikTok gaming live pro and Twitch partner.

My sons had always played video games, and it looked really fun. But I knew it was a notoriously young crowd. I asked if I was too old to game. It was when I found another gamer, Tactical Grandma, who was around my age, that I knew I should try it.

I started solo hiking because, after getting sober, I needed that time alone outside, moving, to reflect. Slowly, I built up my fitness.

In October, I'll run my first 5 K. This was a goal I had after watching people run a half-marathon. I thought half-marathoners would be young and super fit, but there are so many different ages and shapes.

Having gotten tattoos for years, I was determined to continue getting them as I aged. They are my life's journey inked out on my skin. I don't worry about the wrinkles or a changing body shape that might alter how they look. The canvas the tattoos are on doesn't have to be perfect — I love the tattoos for what they represent, not for how aesthetically pleasing they are to other people.

Next month, I plan to get bird tattoos along the right side of my neck.

As I continue to age, I want to be louder. Not "in your face" kind of loud, but I do want to talk more frequently about aging.

Women can be comfortable getting older. They don't need to be perfect to be beautiful. In the world of AI, injectables, and cosmetic procedures, this is a powerful message.

It seems that growing old is becoming a dying art. That's why I want to pop up in their feeds to say that none of it's true. Aging doesn't have to be something we dread.

Sometimes, my body doesn't keep up with my brain. Running had been hard and ruined my feet. Gaming for eight hours a day has been hard on my body. And that's OK — I'll pivot and pursue something else instead.

I tried new things in my 50s and 60s, and I'll continue fearlessly trying more until I can't anymore.

Volkswagen to build $4bn Audi SUV factory in US despite job cuts at home

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

VW investment, creating 4,500 jobs in Georgia, comes amid Trump’s damaging tariffs, intense Chinese competition and costly shift towards EVs

Audi has said it will spend $4bn (£3bn) to build its first factory in the US, as its parent company, Volkswagen, struggles with the impact of steep tariffs imposed by Donald Trump.

The German brand’s chief executive, Gernot Döllner, joined Trump on stage at a campaign rally on Saturday night to announce the factory in Macon, Georgia, in an unusual move by a big European brand just days before the US midterm elections.

The Republican party under Trump is trying to retain control of both houses of Congress, but it is polling poorly as it struggles with higher fuel prices caused by Trump’s war on Iran, as well as his trade war against some of the US’s biggest trading partners.

Trump has long tried to convince voters that his tariffs are helping to increase the number of manufacturing jobs in the US, and has seized on any new factory investments. However, US vehicle and parts employment has fallen since Trump took office last year.

Audi, Volkswagen’s most important premium brand, has suffered from Trump’s 15% tariffs on imports from the EU as well as the president’s efforts to cut sales of battery electric vehicles in favour of petrol cars.

It said it aims to build 200,000 SUVs at the new US factory, creating 4,500 jobs. The factory will build the Q7, Q8 and Q9 SUVs, shifting some production from Bratislava in Slovakia.

Volkswagen has said Trump’s tariffs cost it up to €5bn(£4.2bn)a year, before the impact of lower sales. The German manufacturer this month said it was still struggling with “continued high pressure from import tariffs”. The carmaker was also forced to shift some production of its cheapest cars, such as the Jetta saloon and Taos SUV, from Mexico to the US.

The new factory may add to tensions in Germany, where Volkswagen has started to cut as many as 100,000 jobs and close factories. It has blamed the job losses on intense competition from Chinese carmakers, as well as the tariffs and a need to invest heavily in the shift to EVs for markets other than the US.

Döllner said he accepted the invitation to speak at Trump’s rally out of respect for the US.

The World’s 10 Most ‘Cursed’ Places: A Guide to Dangerous Destinations

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Home → Features → Natural Sciences → Geography

Some of these places look like dream destinations until you learn the disasters hiding underneath

Some places frighten you when you arrive. Others spook you just reading about them.

In Atlas of Cursed Places, Olivier Le Carrer delivers for both types of people — those with a morbid fascination for macabre locations and those who want to stay as far as possible from them.

The destinations he mentions are not all haunted in the candlelit sense. The “curses” here come in many forms: natural hazards, industrial nightmares, religious terror, mass death, environmental ruin, and places where legend has grown over real suffering like moss on a wall.

“The honest traveller is surprised to observe that the red sand is ringed by snow-capped mountains and impenetrable forests populated by tetchy bears,” writes Le Carrer in a chapter on the so-called Nevada Triangle. Le Carrer adds that any offer of an excursion by plane should be politely refused.

The reason is simple: thanks to the turbulence generated by the rugged landscape, more than 2,000 aircraft have gone missing there over the past five decades — more than that of the fabled Bermuda Triangle.

The author’s white-knuckle ride across America and around the globe features 40 stops ranging from Golgotha, the site outside Jerusalem of Christ’s crucifixion, to the Japanese suicide venue, Aokigahara Forest, and Australia’s Cape York, where crocodiles noted for explosive attacks approach speeds of 30 km/h.

The result is a travel guide in reverse: a gazetteer of places where the curious should keep one hand on the emergency exit. Here are just ten ‘cursed’ places I picked from the book.

Were it not for the people going about their business, Jharia might look like a place recently shelled. Smoke rises from cracks in the ground while the earth sags. The landscape has the exhausted look of a battlefield.

Except here, as Olivier Le Carrer notes, the fire “does not come from the sky.” It comes from below.

For more than a century, old coal seams beneath this district of Jharkhand in India have been burning. Such fires are not rare in abandoned mines, but Jharia is home to roughly half a million people. They live above or beside what Le Carrer calls a “steadily expanding underground inferno,” made up of dozens of separate blazes.

At first, residents even found uses for it. Steam from fissures could cook a pot. But the seeping coal fumes weren’t so useful when some houses split and roads started to buckle, all in an atmosphere of toxic fumes.

At the edge of Russia, near the Norwegian border, the Kola Peninsula first offers the usual northern temptations. There’s the never-ending tundra, the lovely fjords, and the frigid terror of polar night.

Zapadnaya Litsa has no shortage of abandoned barracks, broken factories, smashed windows, and military debris. Le Carrer gives it a harsher title: “the antechamber of hell.” The true horror lies lower down, in the fjords, where the retired monsters of the Northern Fleet wait.

Dozens of decommissioned, Cold War-era vessels — including massive Typhoon and fast Alfa class submarines — were abandoned here, left to rust and decay while awaiting complex and hazardous dismantling processes. Le Carrer mentions the old nuclear submarines sit “like harmless museum exhibits,” although they are anything but. The base once served a superpower. Now it keeps watch over radioactive fuel in quantities Le Carrer describes as “thirty times greater than that of the Chernobyl reactor.”

The Maldives sells the dream of a flawless lagoon. Thilafushi receives the bill.

Le Carrer labels it “The Toxic Lagoon,” a grim counterpoint to the country’s postcard image of overwater villas and transparent seas. In 1992, authorities began turning this narrow island near Malé into a landfill, a dump for all the garbage produced by the archipelago.

As the number of tourists to the Maldives skyrocketed, trash arrived by the ton. Migrant workers at the site burned what they could. Heavy metals, batteries, and chemicals seeped toward the water. When the island ran out of space, waste sometimes spread into the shallows, creating drifting rafts of garbage in a lagoon marketed to the world as paradise.

Le Carrer calls the result the “highly visible abscess of the infection.” Apparently, even Eden produces rubbish, and someone has to stand downwind of it

From Venice, Poveglia is almost insultingly close: a small green island in the lagoon, overlooked by a bell tower and abandoned buildings.

It might look charming but Le Carrer calls it the “Island of Death,” and the legends have done much of the work. In Veneto, Le Carrer writes, people say that “when a bad man dies, he wakes up on Poveglia.”

Stories claim plague victims and lepers were exiled there and thousands of bodies lie beneath the soil. A psychiatric hospital later added a second layer of horror. In the most lurid versions, a mad doctor tortured patients and threw himself from the campanile.

The archives confirm a quarantine station, later care homes, and then closure in 1968, but not every bloody detail the internet now repeats.

More recently, Poveglia (often called the world’s most haunted island) turned into a bizarre real estate battle. In 2014, the Italian state offered a 99-year lease on the island at an auction. A Venetian citizens’ group, Poveglia per Tutti, crowdfunded to buy it for public use, but a businessman named Luigi Brugnaro won the auction with a €513,000 bid. The deal eventually stalled, keeping Poveglia locked in a bureaucratic and spectral limbo.

The Sunda Strait is beautiful in the way a sleeping dragon is beautiful.

Between Java and Sumatra, the sea route carries ships through shallows, oil installations, new school pirates, and one of the most famous volcanic hot spots on Earth: Krakatoa. In 1883, after months of eruptions and tremors, the volcano exploded with such violence that its sound reportedly traveled thousands of miles. To this day, it’s considered the loudest thing in history. Tsunami waves smashed into nearby coasts. The official death toll reached about 40,000.

Krakatoa did not vanish after the disaster. Its offspring, Anak Krakatoa, rose from the sea in the 20th century and has continued to remind the strait that geology works on its own schedule.

Indonesia sits along one of the world’s most active tectonic regions, where subduction feeds volcanoes and tsunamis. To build, sail, and live there is to negotiate with forces that predate every port, bridge, and shipping lane.

Le Carrer calls Cape Horn the Sailor’s Nightmare.” In less than two centuries, some 800 ships wrecked near this dark rock at the end of South America, taking perhaps 20,000 lives with them. The region is blanketed by gray sea, cold rain, poor visibility, brutal wind, and “always the feeling that one’s troubles are only just beginning.”

Nobody came here for romance. Sailors came because commerce told them to. Before the Panama Canal, rounding the Horn was part of the long Atlantic-to-Pacific ordeal. Ships clawed their way through the Roaring Forties and Furious Fifties (strong, continuous westerly winds circling the Southern Hemisphere) that whip around the planet and the sea. With almost no land to stop them, the winds grew monstrous.

In 1914, the canal cut thousands of miles from the voyage and spared many ships the old punishment. Cape Horn, Le Carrer writes, “got what it deserved.”

Still, the place keeps its talent. It merely torments fewer people now.

The Houtman Abrolhos islands sit off Western Australia, low and reef-ringed, a trap disguised as an archipelago.

In 1629, the Dutch East India Company ship Batavia struck the reefs with more than 300 people on board. The wreck should have been the disaster. It became only the opening scene.

While a rescue party left for Java, assistant merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz took control of the survivors. What followed can only be described as organized murder. Cornelisz, a failed apothecary turned company official, began arranging resources by his own twisted arithmetic. Those he considered useless were sent to islands without fresh water, drowned quietly, or killed more openly once his intentions became impossible to miss. His men murdered “by whatever violent means” they had available. Women were spared only to be abused.

Rescue arrived from Java after more than two months. Cornelisz and six accomplices were hanged. Nearly 200 others were already dead.

The reef wrecked the ship. The crew did the rest.

Can a wildlife sanctuary suffer from too much nature? Kasanka National Park is one of Africa’s smallest reserves. Driven to the brink by poachers, conservationists successfully revived the park, reintroducing elephants and hippos. But nobody expected the sky to turn black. Every October, five million straw-colored fruit bats descend on a tiny forest within the park. Le Carrer describes the phenomenon as the “African remake of one of Alfred Hitchcock’s most unsettling films”.

These aggressive mammals strip the trees, devouring 330,000 tons of fruit annually. The massive swarm also attracts predators. Pythons, crocodiles, and birds of prey crowd the area, feasting on fallen bats. Ecologists study this colossal migration to understand shifting global wildlife patterns, yet the bats’ final destination remains unknown. The site remains a terrifying spectacle for anyone brave enough to visit.

Aokigahara lies at the foot of Mount Fuji, where beauty and dread share the same trees.

The forest is often called a suicide forest. It covers barely fourteen square miles, yet it claims around one hundred lives each year.

Le Carrer describes a dense, dim “sea of trees,” where the ground is uneven, mossy, and broken by crevasses. Paths are scarce and people can easily get lost. More painfully, many have gone there intending not to return. Authorities and volunteers have tried to intervene, but people looking to end their lives still venture here, as if lured by a siren call.

Pop culture has been blamed, notably Seichō Matsumoto’s 1960 novel Black Sea of Trees, where two lovers end their lives at Aokigahara forest. But locals insist the forest’s dark appeal predates modern literature. They believe the “yuurei — the ghosts of the departed who wander the earth in search of paradise — are well acquainted with Aokigahara”. Historically, families practiced ubasute here, abandoning elderly relatives to die.

In modern Carthage, amid villas, cypresses, and bougainvillea, one of antiquity’s darkest arguments lies underfoot.

The Tophet of Carthage, also known as the Salammbo, has yielded twenty thousand funerary urns, many containing the remains of very young children. Ancient writers accused the Carthaginians of sacrificing children to the gods Baal and Tanit. Some modern scholars have argued instead that the site may have served as a cemetery for infants who died naturally.

Ancient Carthaginian families allegedly offered their firstborn males to secure the gods’ favor. Despite these gruesome rituals, Carthage ultimately fell. The Romans destroyed the city in 146 BCE, and according to myth sowed the ruins with salt such that nothing would grow there. Le Carrer asks rhetorically if the city fell because “the earth of the tophet was never able to digest all that blood.”

"Flou" et "prématuré" : plusieurs pays européens, dont la France, s'interrogent quant à l'entrée du Canada dans l'Union en tant que "membre associé"

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Article rédigé par Clément Périé

l'essentiel Alors que le rapprochement entre Bruxelles et Ottawa semble se concrétiser, plusieurs voix dissonantes s'opposent à l'entrée du Canada en tant que "membre associé" dans l'Union européenne. La France est notamment dans cette position, aux côtés de l'Allemagne et de l'Italie qui pointent du doigt l'actuel manque de clarté concernant le statut qu'obtiendrait le pays d'Amérique du Nord.

Le rapprochement entre l'Union européenne et le Canada se poursuit. À l'origine de l'initiative, la présidente de la Commission européenne Ursula Von der Leyen qui, depuis septembre 2026, propose le statut de "membre associé" à l'UE pour le pays nord-américain. Concrètement, le statut de membre associé permettrait au Canada de devenir un vrai partenaire économique des 27 États membres. Ainsi, le Canada aurait la possibilité de participer directement à la base industrielle de défense européenne, aux projets arctiques conjoints et de coopérer aussi sur les thématiques de l'intelligence artificielle et sur les services financiers avec les autres membres de l'UE, expliquait Euronews en septembre dernier.

À lire aussi : Donald Trump trouve "ridicule" la proposition d'Ursula von der Leyen de faire du Canada le "premier membre associé" de l'UE, "s'ils le font, ce serait un acte hostile"

Ce rapprochement du Canada avec l'Union européenne s'est notamment accéléré après les menaces répétées du président américain Donald Trump qui clamait vouloir faire du Canada le 51e État des États-Unis. Le pays est d'ailleurs depuis plusieurs mois enlisé dans une guerre commerciale avec son voisin américain alors que le président des États-Unis qualifie de "ridicule" ce rapprochement entre l'UE et le Canada.

Mais ce rapprochement entre Bruxelles et Ottawa pose tout de même certaines interrogations au sein des États membres de l'Union européenne. En effet, plusieurs États membres, dont la France, sont sceptiques concernant ce nouveau statut avancé par Ursula Von der Leyen et dont le Canada pourrait prochainement profiter.

Aux côtés de pays comme l'Allemagne et l'Italie, la France souligne le caractère "flou" et "prématuré" de ce statut alors qu'un diplomate européen souligne auprès de Politico que "nous avons mis la charrue avant les bœufs". Ainsi, ces États membres reprochent à la Commission européenne d'avoir annoncé une nouvelle catégorie de partenariat avec le Canada sans détailler de manière précise les contours juridiques et politiques de ce statut. Ils reprochent également l'absence de débats et de discussions avec l'institution européenne concernant cette thématique.

À lire aussi : ENTRETIEN. Rapprochement entre le Canada et l'UE : Gaz, nucléaire, minéraux critiques... "la question énergétique est au cœur des échanges futurs"

Au-delà du manque de clarté concernant le statut proposé au Canada, des divergences existent entre États membres concernant les domaines dans lesquels le pays américain pourrait participer. Par exemple dans le secteur de l'énergie nucléaire, la France, la Slovaquie et la Roumanie sont favorables à un approfondissement des relations dans le domaine alors que l'Autriche s'y oppose catégoriquement.

Les représentants de l'UE et du pays outre-Atlantique doivent d'ailleurs se réunir le 29 et 30 octobre prochains lors d'un sommet à Montréal pour clarifier ce statut de "membre associé" et ses conséquences pour les relations entre le Canada et les 27 États membres.
