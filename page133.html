Europe is entering a “new era of green marketing” as more cities turn their backs on climate-damaging advertising.

It all started with Amsterdam, which became the first capital city in the world this year to ban billboards promoting fossil fuels and meat on 1 May – five years after the law was first proposed.

The ban is part of the city’s targets to reach carbon neutrality by 2050 and to halve meat consumption during the same period. Other Dutch cities, such as Utrecht, the Hague, Zwolle, Delft and Nijmegen already have similar bans in place.

Many other cities including Helsinki, Stockholm and Bristol have introduced bans on promoting fossil fuels or other carbon-intensive products such as aviation, shipping, fast-fashion and construction. However, many of these don’t include meat.

While the climate impacts of burning fossil fuels are widely understood, the environmental effects of animal agriculture get less attention.

A recent report from European non-profit Changing Markets found mounting evidence that departments within the UN Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) have a “bias in favour of the livestock sector” – as lobbyists seek to undermine climate action.

This includes high-ranking members of the FAO delivering speeches at the World Meat Congress and affiliated scientists from the animal agriculture industry attempting to discredit studies that promote plant-rich diets.

But there is no hiding that food and agriculture contribute one-third of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, second only to burning coal, oil and gas.

Animal-sourced foods in the EU are also believed to make up between 81 and 86 per cent of total GHG emissions of food production. However, they only supply an estimated 21 per cent of calories and 64 per cent of protein.

Over recent years, scientists have called for countries – particularly those in the West with access to plant-based alternatives – to reduce their meat consumption to tackle this.

Most notably, the 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission report – compiled by 70 leading experts from 35 countries – found that around 15 million deaths could be avoided each year if the world shifted to predominantly plant-based diets.

Experts say this could also cut non-CO2 GHG emissions from agriculture – such as methane from livestock and nitrous oxide from fertilisers – by 15 per cent by 2050.

When factoring in the entire food lifecycle – such as reducing deforestation, transportation and refrigeration, and restoring nature – the total drop in emissions would be much higher.

Amsterdam’s meat ban proved controversial, with many people on social media branding the ruling “ridiculous” – or arguing that their meat consumption isn’t linked to what they see on billboards.

“I can’t honestly remember the last time I actually purchased something directly because of an advertisement,” one Reddit user wrote. “Probably [when I was] a teenager.”

But Linda Aspey, a climate psychology specialist and board member of Climate Psychology Alliance, tells Euronews Earth that much of advertising’s influencing power comes from “repetition and cueing”.

“One study found that seeing meat imagery (in this case, a Big Mac) increased people’s desire to eat it, and its everyday presence likely reinforces meat as an ordinary choice,” she says.

“This may be reinforced by someone’s identity, culture of livelihood where eating meat is simply a part of that, and carries little conscious tension.”

Meat consumption in the Netherlands, for example, has been on the decline. In 2024, the Dutch ate less meat (including meat products) than at any time in the past 20 years, according to Wageningen Social and Economic Research’s ‘Meat Report’.

“For others, there's an uncomfortable pull between valuing animal welfare or the environment and still eating meat, and advertising may make that pull easier to set aside,” Aspey explains.

“What the evidence does show is that advertising typically shapes what feels normal for most of us, to varying degrees, which in turn can shape behaviours.”

Amsterdam is yet to announce any data on how the ban has impacted meat consumption in the country – but experts predict there will be a correlation.

When Transport for London (TFL) in the UK removed junk food advertising from its network, weekly household purchases were estimated to fall by nearly seven per cent, confectionery by almost a fifth.

This was measured through actual shopping data, not consumer surveys.

“The effect wasn't dramatic as a ban changes the constant prompts that people see around them day to day, without necessarily changing why people eat what they eat, whether that's habit, taste, livelihood or budget,” Aspey explains.

“As with tobacco control, such restrictions tend to work best combined with other measures, not alone.”

Despite the known climate-link, the EU has historically propped up foods like red meat with generous subsidies.

A recent report from charity Foodrise found that the EU’s common agricultural policy (CAP) directed more subsidies to the production of high-emitting animal-sourced foods than plant foods in 2020.

That year, animal-sourced foods received around 77 per cent of total CAP subsidies, accounting for €39 billion out of the total €51 billion spent.

The study found that beef and lamb, which are consistently identified as the biggest culprits of climate damage, were given around 580 times more in subsidies compared to legumes such as lentils and beans in 2020.

Going from funding these foods to actively stripping them from public advertising may feel too drastic – but experts say it could become part of a “mix of strategies” to tackle climate change.

“Most research points to sustainable diets needing meaningfully less meat than the UK eats now, for both planetary and personal health, and marketing that is weighted heavily towards meat – especially mass produced and cheaper – makes that shift harder," Aspey says.

Forget pricey haunted houses and tourist-packed ghost tours. Some of the most atmospheric places to spend Halloween are historic cemeteries, where crumbling mausoleums, elaborate sculptures and famous names provide all the spooky scenery you need – and entry is often free.

A new ranking of 30 cities has revealed the best places to explore Europe’s most fascinating burial grounds, from Paris to Oslo.

The "Tombstone Tourism Index", by travel eSIM app Saily, took into account five separate factors when looking at cemeteries: famous “residents”, visual merit, heritage status, crowd-free experience, and transit access from the city centre.

Each category was given an individual percentage of importance to reveal the most tourism-worthy cemeteries.

"Every October, people queue for hours at haunted attractions while walking straight past the real thing – some of the most atmospheric, story-filled places in any city," said Matas Cenys, head of product at Saily.

"Historic cemeteries are open-air museums with sculpture and incredible stories, and they don't charge entry. Halloween is a good excuse to discover them, but they're worth visiting any day of the year."

While the legendary Parisian cemetery has some 70,000 graves across 44 hectares, a staggering 1,445 notable people are buried here, many of whom attract visitors year-round.

Père Lachaise is the eternal resting place of the likes of Chopin, Édith Piaf, Oscar Wilde, Jim Morrison, Marcel Proust, Gertrude Stein, Molière, Balzac, and Sarah Bernhardt, to name just a few.

The grave of The Doors singer Jim Morrison is especially popular, while Oscar Wilde's lipstick-covered tomb has needed a glass barrier.

More than 4,500 photos have been shared on Wikimedia Commons, making it the most documented cemetery on earth. It’s also free to visit, and just 15 minutes from Paris’ centre.

Make sure you download the official app to help navigate this huge space and avoid getting lost, especially in spooky season.

Think of Norway’s capital and cemeteries don’t necessarily come to mind. That’s likely because the Vår Frelsers Gravlund has only 123 Google reviews, making it the index’s best-kept secret.

Despite that, the cemetery is just 10 minutes from the centre of Oslo, and is home to the father of modern drama Henrik Ibsen, ‘The Scream’ artist Edvard Munch, painter Christian Krohg and polar explorer Fridtjof Nansen.

These four figures who define Norway’s history are all buried within walking distance of each other in a striking cemetery, landscaped in the Victorian era. It’s well worth adding a visit to your Scandinavian bucket list.

This Edinburgh cemetery is even less known than Vår Frelsers Gravlund, with just 90 Google reviews, despite the fact that it sits just steps from the city's busiest street.

The Dean Cemetery, a Category A listed site, lies between Queensferry Road and the Water of Leith, and is filled with elaborate Victorian monuments.

The people buried here are among those who built modern Edinburgh, including architect William Playfair, philosopher Dugald Stewart, and portrait painter Henry Raeburn.

The Olšany Cemetery in Prague is a veritable maze of crumbling Baroque tombs, elaborate Art Nouveau monuments and overgrown pathways, making it an especially good spot for a wander during spooky season.

Franz Kafka, one of Prague’s most famous literary figures, is buried here, adding another reason to seek out this historic burial ground.

Pair a visit with nearby Vyšehrad Cemetery, where Czech cultural icons including composers Antonín Dvořák and Bedřich Smetana, artist Alfons Mucha and writer Karel Čapek are laid to rest.

Arguably the most atmospheric cemetery in the UK, Highgate Cemetery in London is actually a little controversial as it’s not free to enter, unless you’re a grave owner or hold a grave owner's pass.

Nevertheless, it remains very popular and for good reason.

Karl Marx was laid to rest here, alongside George Eliot, Christina Rossetti, and Michael Faraday.

The Victorian Gothic architecture is particularly impressive – think overgrown mausoleums, ivy-draped angels, and an Egyptian Avenue.

The whole cemetery is Grade I listed, has some 950 notable burials, and is hugely photogenic, meaning it’s well worth the visit if graveyards are your thing.

Russia has been selling off gold throughout this year. September brought a further reduction in its reserves of the precious metal of around 6.2 tonnes, bringing the total reduction since January to about 62 tonnes.

Only once in its modern history has Russia disposed of gold at a similarly rapid pace, when in 1998 it sold 118 tonnes from its reserves in just three months.

According to Adam Stroniawski, managing director of Polish precious metals dealer Mennica Skarbowa, "Russia has a serious budget shortfall because of its war in Ukraine and wants to inject more funds into the defence sector". He tells Euronews that Russia is selling gold outside the London bullion market, saying that "the sell-off of reserves will take place in Hong Kong, and the main buyer is China's central bank".

Stroniawski concludes that the impact of these transactions on the global gold price would therefore be negligible.

According to Hong Kong trade data, from January to July around 100 tonnes of Russian gold reached Hong Kong – nearly three times as much as in the same period last year.

The expert does not rule out Russia returning to the group of countries buying gold and rebuilding its reserves, just as it did after its 1998 sell-off.

A few days before the Russian central bank published its data, investors learned that Kazakhstan also plans to sell part of its gold reserves.

The country, which straddles two continents, was among the world's biggest buyers of gold in the first eight months of 2026. It has now announced that by the end of the year it intends to reduce its gold holdings so that gold accounts for 75% of its total reserves.

"The liquidation of part of Kazakhstan's gold-denominated foreign reserves may seem surprising, because for years the country was one of the biggest buyers of bullion," says Stroniawski.

He says the shift may be temporary, driven by the need to plug a budget deficit or take profits.

"Kazakhstan has been buying gold for a long time, so it will now be selling at a significant profit," he adds.

The expert stresses that both the scale of the planned reduction – from around 78% to 75% of total reserves – and the spreading of sales over time mean that these transactions, too, will not have a major impact on global gold markets.

The change in approach by Russia and Kazakhstan raises questions about whether this is the beginning of a new trend among central banks, and whether Poland might follow in their footsteps. In recent years Poland has significantly increased its gold reserves and could take profits while gold prices remain high. In Stroniawski's view, however, this scenario is highly unlikely.

"This stems from internal factors, with government policy and central bank policy moving in different directions. In this parliamentary term it is unlikely that reserves will be liquidated," says Stroniawski.

Indeed, the National Bank of Poland has been actively buying gold for several years, and Poland's current reserves are already close to the target of 700 tonnes. According to the expert, this does not have to mean the end of gold purchases by the central bank. He says large gold reserves improve the country's credit standing and help to stabilise the currency.

"At present, with its level of gold reserves, Poland ranks 10th in the world. In my opinion, reaching 700 tonnes will only be one stage. It does not mean the end of purchases or the start of a sell-off," says Stroniawski.

"At the moment, many investors who bought gold a few years ago may be thinking about taking profits," the expert says. He adds that the precious metal saw significant price increases in recent years, although its price has weakened in 2026.

Gold prices rose 25.5% in 2024 and 67.4% in 2025 in US dollar terms, according to the World Gold Council. However, they fell 4.4% in the first nine months of 2026.

Stroniawski sees temporary corrections in the price of gold as not affecting its long-term upward trend. He believes buying by central banks and investors who treat gold as a safe haven and a reserve asset will support further price rises.

"When it comes to gold, I wouldn't assume it will ever be cheaper. However, buying it delivers reliable profits only over the long term," Stroniawski emphasises.

Burnham has previously committed to the red lines in Labour’s 2024 election manifesto, which ruled out the return of free movement, single market membership or a customs union.

It means any fundamental change in the relationship is likely to have to wait until after a general election, expected by 2029.

Burnham acknowledged freedom of movement had “very significant challenges associated with it”, but he suggested that European migration had changed in the past decade.

“It was a type of immigration that perhaps wasn’t as difficult [as now],” he told the Sunday Times. “Revolving-door migration where people would come and then go back is different. Burnham will meet EU leaders at a summit in Brussels on 20 November.

Migration was one of the dominant topics of debate during the 2016 Brexit referendum campaign. In the 12 months to June 2016 – the month of the Brexit leave vote – net migration was at a near-record high of 335,000, according to the Office for National Statistics.

The Conservative campaign platform mentions climate just once, stating “climate decisions” should be based on three principles: protecting people from extreme weather, reducing emissions in B.C. and beyond and delivering measurable results.

In the end, a majority of voters threw their support behind the Conservatives, handing the former opposition party the reins of the province for the next four years.

“Together, we seized this opportunity, you guys, and together we voted for a better British Columbia,” Doerkson told an electric crowd in Vancouver. During his victory speech he promised to “supercharge” the province and “cut red tape like it’s never been cut before.”

Despite choosing to call an election while the Conservatives were in chaos, the BC NDP will now form the official opposition. Eby held on to his Vancouver—Point Grey seat but in his concession speech offered his resignation as party leader.

The BC Greens maintained their seat count with party leader Emily Lowan winning the Saanich North and the Islands riding where she was seeking a seat in the legislature. Incumbent Green MLA Jeremy Valeriote was re-elected in West Vancouver—Sea to Sky.

Here’s a breakdown of some of the promises Doerkson and the Conservatives made on the campaign trail.

One of Doerkson’s first public statements after becoming interim leader of the BC Conservatives was to declare his intention to repeal B.C.’s Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, or DRIPA.

The pledge, which is a top priority for the Conservatives, appears in the party’s platform under a section titled “protect private property rights” — despite the fact that the act doesn’t deal with private property.

During the campaign, Doerkson was vague about what his government’s next steps would be after repealing the act. Ditching the law will not eliminate the B.C. government’s consultation obligations with First Nations or eliminate friction related to Indigenous Rights and Aboriginal Title. Even in a post-DRIPA province, the constitutionally guaranteed rights of First Nations can be hashed out in the courts, as they have been for decades.

“Simply just repealing it isn’t going to do anyone any service,” Dallas Smith, president of the Nanwakolas Council, told The Narwhal in an interview during the early days of the election campaign.

The Conservative platform promised to pass a law requiring information about Aboriginal Title claims and ongoing negotiations to be made public. The proposed law would also publicize information about legal actions and agreements related to First Nations Rights and Title.

The party’s platform also promises to “restore equal access to Crown land and provincial parks,” a pledge that appears to target annual temporary closures of Pipi7íyekw/Joffre Lakes Park, which restrict visits to members of Líl̓wat and N’Quatqua First Nations, whose unceded territories encompass the park. These reconnection periods have been taking place since 2023 and are intended to allow the nations to conduct traditional harvesting, as well as ceremonial and cultural practices.

Under a BC Conservative government, reconciliation with First Nations will focus on “practical co-operation, clear and fair treaty agreements and economic opportunities,” the platform says. It emphasizes opportunities for First Nations to benefit from projects through revenue sharing and ownership opportunities.

The Conservatives have promised to make B.C. an “energy superpower” by doubling liquefied natural gas (LNG) production by 2032 and tripling it by 2035. With LNG Canada the only LNG export facility currently operating in B.C., the province is already on track to more than triple the province’s LNG production by 2035 with five additional LNG projects currently in development.

On the campaign trail, Doerkson lamented the number of LNG projects proposed under the BC Liberal government of the 2000s and 2010s and said his party is keen to see if any can be revived.

The Conservative energy platform also pledges to get rid of B.C.’s industrial carbon tax and replace it with new rules that it says would ensure B.C.’s gas exports are competitive with the United States and Qatar.

A BC Conservative government would take a new approach to tallying B.C.’s greenhouse gas emissions, one that runs counter to the 2016 Paris Agreement. The Conservative platform proposes taking credit for “emissions avoided through cleaner B.C. exports,” using the example of exported gas replacing coal.

Doerkson has also promised to use deregulation as a tool to spur economic development, partly by bringing back a ministry dedicated to getting rid of rules, processes and laws it deems burdensome or unnecessary. For every new regulation a Conservative government makes, it would axe two existing regulations, according to Doerkson.

Beyond boosting natural gas and LNG production, the BC Conservatives are promising to boost B.C.’s energy self-sufficiency, a commitment that would mean building a considerable amount of additional energy generation capacity. The province currently balances its energy needs through a mix of energy imported from other jurisdictions (mostly Alberta and western U.S. states) and locally generated power.The strategy, basically, is to use local sources when they are cheap and convenient and buy imported power — such as solar-generated power from California — when it is plentiful and cheap. To date, the strategy has worked out well, though recent drought conditions have put a squeeze on those margins.

The Conservatives are committed to boosting B.C.’s energy production “from every available source,” according to its platform.

Boosting biomass production is a key part of the Conservatives’ energy pitch. The party is proposing to increase the use of logging and mill waste to make renewable fuels. On renewables, the Conservatives support wind, solar and small-scale hydro power “where economics and reliability make sense,” as well as geothermal projects where a business case can be made — i.e. with little investment from the provincial government.

Notably, the 2026 B.C. Conservative platform did not mention nuclear power, an energy source the party touted as part of the solution to B.C.’s energy crunch during the 2024 election.

Beyond increasing exports, a Conservative government would consider opening the door to more natural gas power production in B.C. by making it eligible for BC Hydro’s call for energy project proposals.

The Conservatives are also promising to “restore choice in energy for home heating and cooking” — a commitment to attacking restrictions and bans on natural gas use implemented by local governments — and overhaul B.C.’s Energy Step Code, which sets energy efficiency standards for new buildings.

On the campaign trail, the BC Conservatives promised to do many of the same things the NDP government did to bolster the province’s floundering forestry industry — secure support from the federal government to offset the impact of U.S. tariffs on softwood lumber, boost policies aimed at getting more woodfibre to mills and provide cash for mills to upgrade their operations.The Conservatives’ forestry platform promised to make more trees available for harvest — but does not address how the party would ensure companies actually harvest the maximum amount allowed, something that has not happened in years due largely to the dwindling availability of economically viable trees to log. The Conservatives are also promising to focus on streamlining permitting, reduce costs for forestry companies and support value-added manufacturing.

Conservative forestry policies would double government revenue from stumpage fees by 2028-29, according to the campaign platform. The 2026 provincial budget did not include a specific revenue estimate for stumpage but estimated total forestry-related revenues will increase 3.3 per cent per year through 2029.

The BC Conservative platform claims conservation is a conservative value but doesn’t offer many specific conservation-focused commitments. The party pledges to increase enforcement in parks and wilderness areas with more conservation officers in the field, and to establish sustainable fish and wildlife population objectives.

The platform promises a science-based approach to wildlife management with input from hunters, anglers and First Nations. It also repeats a 2024 campaign commitment to develop a made-in-B.C. species-at-risk law. The Conservative platform doesn’t mention B.C.’s existing commitment to protect 30 per cent of lands and waters by 2030, known as 30-by-30. In May 2024, former BC Conservative leader John Rustad told The Narwhal he would abandon the goal.

When the B.C. legislature began its summer break at the end of May, the BC NDP held 47 seats, the BC Conservatives held 39, and the BC Greens held two. There were five independent MLAs, all of them formerly members of the BC Conservative caucus.

The BC Conservatives had just elected a new leader, Kerry-Lynne Findlay, a former Conservative Party of Canada MP and cabinet minister. Findlay won the leadership race to replace John Rustad, who resigned as the party’s leader in December 2025 after weeks of caucus infighting. Findlay’s victory was narrow; she won 51 per cent of party members’ votes, barely beating out second-place finisher Caroline Elliott. Elliott is set to head to the legislature after winning the West Vancouver—Capilano riding for the BC Conservatives.

The BC Conservatives had a spectacularly chaotic summer. Findlay’s leadership lasted just 113 days, ending in her resignation on Sept. 20. Before she stepped down, 13 MLAs had left or been removed from the BC Conservative caucus and one more — Abbotsford—Mission MLA Reann Gasper — stepped down to allow Findlay to secure a set in a by-election.

Doerkson was appointed the party’s interim leader the day after Findlay stepped down.

After Eby’s snap election call, the by-election was cancelled, Findlay exited the party and 10 of the 13 MLAs who had left the caucus signed up to run again.

“I think David Eby is going to regret this,” Doerkson said at a Vancouver press conference following the snap election call. “Because this election actually offers an opportunity for hope and for real change.”

He was right. The Conservatives won a historic victory, though Doerkson’s future as premier is uncertain. His party’s constitution bars interim leaders from running to become a permanent leader, though the constitution can be changed. Tweaking the rules to allow Doerkson to remain premier will require the support of two-thirds of party members at an annual general or special general meeting. The Conservative Party’s board of directors committed to “pursue the appropriate means” to keep Doerkson as party leader in the event of an election win.

A shark went astray and captured Korea's heart - will she finally make it back to sea?

As soon as a large shark was spotted in a canal in Busan - a bustling shipping hub and beach city on South Korea's south-eastern tip - marine biologist Kim Hyun-woo was drafted in to rescue it.

He got the call in mid-September, on his way home from a business trip tracking shoals of mackerel to aid fishing vessels. Despite being one of the country's leading shark experts, he never thought he'd be asked by the coastguard to actually save one.

South Korea is no stranger to sharks but one this size, in a shallow, concrete canal right by the world's seventh busiest port, was unheard of - until now.

With an excited crowd already gathering at the waterfront, his team from the National Institute of Fisheries Science had to move fast.

The first task was to determine the species.

Some sharks are more dangerous than others. The star of the 1975 blockbuster Jaws, the great white shark, is known to attack and kill humans.

The specimen in Busan was found to be a female dusky shark, a species linked only to two deadly attacks. Still, Kim feared someone in the crowd might jump or fall into the canal.

The dusky shark was first spotted in the canal in mid-September

A small coastguard ship made an initial attempt using an electrical deterrence device, which generates a weak current that sharks avoid.

They're usually used after sightings near popular beaches. But after five hours, officials determined this particular shark was too large and fast for the device.

Kim changed tack, deciding instead to wait for the shark to let herself out. But that did not happen.

As authorities looked for ways to guide her out of the canal, the stranded creature went viral.

Just hours after she appeared, the council had sent everyone in the city an emergency text alert, urging pedestrians and residents to "exercise caution for their safety".

People rarely visited the park along the canal but because of the message, the place was soon brimming with people eager to spot the shark.

Adoring fans named her Bukangi, from "buk hang", the Korean name of the nearby harbour. Within days, a business trademarked it and Bukangi pins, plushies and T-shirts began selling online.

City authorities joined in, livestreaming the shark and making her their official mascot.

A banner was hung at the entrance of the canal, asking the public to sign a petition "on behalf of Bukangi" to demand the national government's support for the construction of a new 30,000-seater baseball stadium by the canal.

Shark spotting became a team effort among locals and visitors. The unspoken rule was that children were given the front row, and whoever saw Bukangi had to point her out to newer arrivals.

Some self-appointed defenders even patrolled the canal at night to make sure no-one was shining torches or lasers at Bukangi.

A poster encourages support for a baseball stadium "on behalf of Bukangi"

More than 1.6 million visitors ultimately flocked to the canal.

The local mayor said the shark brought "the kind of publicity even billions of dollars cannot buy" to the port.

"It's really rare these days for people to come together," said Kim Joo-eun, who came to see the shark with her two nieces. "I feel that Bukangi brought us all together, cheering for herâ¦ It's a lot of fun."

But officials couldn't leave Bukangi in the canal.

As September drew to an end, she hadn't been seen feeding for days and was spotted with bloody abrasions on her snout from bumping into the concrete walls.

Prominent marine biology professor Choi Yoon told local media that experts were particularly worried about a "critical" injury around the shark's eye.

Kim Joo-eun and her nieces were among Bukangi's many visitors

At the end of September, Kim's team mounted another rescue attempt. The marine biologist looked back to a 2004 incident in Massachusetts, when water jets were used to drive a great white shark away from a shallow pond.

But when the team tried this on Bukangi, she kept circling back.

On 2 October, they decided to install a net spanning the width of the canal to stop her from returning. She managed to swim around the net and was back within hours.

At this point, Kim started thinking the shark might have problems with her snout's electromagnetic sensors, which she uses for navigation. Or she might have become confused by the loud noises emitted by ships in the harbour.

The reason Bukangi was even in the canal may be a sign of warming seas, the oceans ministry suggests.

It believes she may have followed plentiful fish to warmer waters. Some 50 large sharks were recorded off South Korea's east coast in the first half of the year; four times as many as last year.

The canal leading up to the Busan port where Bukangi was stranded

With each failed attempt some of Bukangi's newfound fans began demanding she be left in peace.

On Thursday, several showed up with placards and shouted "leave Bukangi alone" and "remove the net".

When a security guard told them to stop, one man cried: "I love you, Bukangi!"

"I am obsessed with her," said Jeong Yo-won, a woman in her 40s. She called the shark a "source of hope and joy" and insisted, "I don't think we should evict her mercilessly like this."

Jeong protesting against Bukangi's "eviction", alongside a man demanding officials "protect Bukangi's right to self-determination"

Others thought it was time for her to return home.

"I would stare at her for hours because I was so enamoured by her," a woman said after sighting the shark on her third attempt. "I hope Bukangi would stay, but I prefer that she lives a long and healthy life."

People joked the council should "make an official ID for Bukangi and give a pension", Shin Seungmin said.

"She brought so much fun with her. But she should go and live her normal shark life."

Bukangi became a sensation, drawing the curious and the concerned

Staying in the canal would seriously jeopardise Bukangi because of falling autumn temperatures, Kim explains. Usually this prompts sharks to migrate to warmer waters further south.

"This canal is far from something we can call a habitat," he adds. "A wild animal cannot stay in a space made strictly for humans."

Eventually, as public scrutiny grew, the oceans ministry took over the rescue operation from the fisheries institute. Around 40 personnel, including divers from Busan's aquarium, were mobilised. A vet was on standby, along with an ambulance in case the divers were attacked by Bukangi.

On Wednesday divers installed two layers of nets, and Bukangi was successfully herded out of the narrow canal into open water. But overnight, she swam back into the canal, diving under the outer net.

This left her trapped between the two layers.

Then on thursday, rescuers removed the outer layer, and Bukangi was seen swimming further and further towards open sea.

Although she did occasionally circle back, the rescue operation had ended.

Staying in the canal is dangerous for Bukangi, says marine biologist Kim Hyun-woo

There have been no sightings of Bukangi in the last few days, which experts say is a positive sign.

Prof Choi believes she may be adjusting to wider, deeper waters.

She is unlikely to have travelled far, he adds, and could reappear over the coming week.

But he is also worried about any injuries she may have sustained. Trapped in the canal, it "kept scraping against [the walls], stripping the skin off its snout and even its pectoral fins", he told local media.

For now, South Korea is holding its breath, hoping Bukangi can find her way back to her home - the open sea.

Hay menos afluencia que en pleno verano y las temperaturas suelen resultar más cómodas para hacer turismo y actividades al aire libre.

Julio y agosto son los meses más adecuados si quieres aprovechar al máximo los lagos, los ríos, la costa y las actividades en los Alpes, aunque también coinciden con la temporada de mayor demanda. En invierno, en cambio, el viaje cambia por completo y resulta más interesante si buscas nieve, esquí o una visita urbana. En las zonas de montaña hay que tener en cuenta además que algunas carreteras pueden verse afectadas por la nieve y que los días son más cortos. Las estaciones están muy marcadas y ten claro que en verano puede hacer mucho calor y en invierno mucho frío.

Para una primera visita, lo más recomendable es disponer de entre 8 y 10 días. Con una semana puedes conocer algunos de los lugares más importantes, pero tendrás que asumir un ritmo bastante intenso moviéndote de un lado para otro.

Si solo tienes tres o cuatro días, puedes centrarte en Liubliana, el lago Bled y alguno de los atractivos del Karst. A partir de dos semanas, la ventaja está en poder viajar con más calma, hacer senderismo y añadir regiones menos conocidas y alejadas.

En siete días conviene centrarse en los grandes imprescindibles. Una ruta bastante equilibrada puede comenzar en Liubliana y continuar hacia la zona del Karst, con las cuevas de Postojna y Škocjan y el castillo de Predjama. Desde allí puedes bajar hasta la costa para conocer Piran y después dirigirte hacia el norte para dedicar los últimos días al lago Bled, Vintgar, Bohinj y los Alpes Julianos.

Es un recorrido intenso, pero permite hacerse una buena idea de la variedad del país. Si te lo puedes permitir, lo ideal es contar con un poco más de margen.

Diez días permiten ampliar bastante el recorrido. A la ruta anterior puedes añadir Maribor y Ptuj, en el este, y dedicar más tiempo a la zona alpina. También merece la pena reservar un par de jornadas para el valle del Soča y Bovec, especialmente si te interesan las rutas de senderismo y las actividades al aire libre.

Una buena estrategia es dividir el viaje en varias bases y hacer excursiones desde ellas. Así reduces los cambios de alojamiento y evitas pasar buena parte del día metido en el coche.

Con más de dos semanas puedes dedicar varios días a los Alpes Julianos y al valle del Soča, hacer rutas a pie y explorar otras zonas del país, como Logarska Dolina, Velika Planina o algunas de sus regiones termales.

La última huelga general no fue el 14 de noviembre de 2012, aunque, en función de algunas declaraciones, pudiera parecerlo. Retroceder hasta 2012 obvia el precedente más claro del próximo paro por la vivienda: las huelgas generales feministas de 2018 y 2019 que movilizaron al país. La convocatoria era heterodoxa: lejos de la imagen de los paros tradicionales que paraban la industria, el movimiento feminista llamaba a darle una vuelta completa al concepto de huelga. ¿Para qué sirve una huelga? ¿Qué hacemos con los trabajos de cuidados que no se pagan pero que sostienen la economía y que hacen mayoritariamente las mujeres? ¿Y con el consumo? ¿Pueden ser los paros herramientas que superen las reivindicaciones laborales para plantear propuestas que transformen la sociedad? La huelga del 11 de noviembre nace de preguntas similares y bebe de una movilización que resultó un punto de inflexión.

El 8 de marzo de 2018 arrancaba la primera huelga general feminista de la historia de nuestro país. El primer paso lo había dado el movimiento feminista argentino un año antes al convocar el Primer Paro Internacional de Mujeres, que supuso fuertes movilizaciones en el país y que fue replicándose en otros lugares. La convocatoria desbordaba la idea tradicional de huelga, por el fondo y por las formas.

Por el fondo, porque no se trataba de responder a un conflicto laboral, sino de generar un movimiento que pusiera sobre la mesa las violencias machistas, la desigualdad legal y simbólica o las discriminaciones de todo tipo que sufren las mujeres. Por la forma, porque no solo apelaba al empleo asalariado, sino a los trabajos de cuidados no remunerados y al consumo. Porque si una fábrica para, alguien sigue haciendo la comida, atendiendo a los niños o cuidando de las personas dependientes.

“Las huelgas de 2018 y 2019 abrieron la posibilidad a hacerlas desde un lugar diferente. Había una necesidad de ampliar el sujeto de la huelga, el concepto de trabajo, pensarlas desde otro lugar distinto al del centro de trabajo, construirlas de manera más abierta y horizontal”, explica Ana Useros, que ya entonces participaba en la Comisión 8M, y que resume lo sucedido entonces como un proceso que “resignificó” el concepto de huelga.

Por eso, prosigue, cuando se planteó la posibilidad de un paro general por la vivienda, el vínculo con el 8M fue “instintivo”: “Pensamos 'esta es de las nuestras' porque no es una huelga laboral o de producción y ya, es una huelga por la vida que se piensa desde los movimientos sociales y se organiza desde las bases hacia fuera y a la que después se suman los sindicatos”.

En Barcelona, otra activista refiere: “Fui detenida por casualidad a tres calles de mi casa el día que teníamos un juicio”.

El primer encuentro de David Agus con Elizabeth Holmes, durante una conferencia sobre salud, resultó toda una sorpresa. La Elizabeth Holmes que conoció en la sala de espera era una persona agradable y cercana; la Elizabeth Holmes a la que entrevistó sobre el escenario era, en cambio, alguien totalmente distinto.

A24 anuncia un documental secreto sobre Elizabeth Holmes, la empresaria que estafó con su máquina de analizar sangre

“El acento cambió: una voz muy grave, muy seria”, recuerda Agus, oncólogo médico, por teléfono desde Los Ángeles. “Fue como si hubiera conocido a una persona y, acto seguido, entrevistado a otra completamente diferente. Esa transición, de la sala de espera al escenario... nunca había vivido algo así”.

Holmes no es como tú o como yo. Era aún una adolescente cuando fundó Theranos, promocionando una tecnología capaz de realizar análisis médicos utilizando solo unas pocas gotas de sangre. Cultivó una imagen muy característica: cabello rubio, una mirada fija que no parpadeaba, una voz ronca y un jersey negro de cuello alto, a modo de homenaje a Steve Jobs, cofundador de Apple.

Holmes cautivó a inversores y miembros de su consejo de administración, entre ellos Rupert Murdoch, Larry Ellison, Carlos Slim y los exsecretarios de Estado Henry Kissinger y George Shultz. Se convirtió en la multimillonaria hecha a sí misma más joven de Estados Unidos. Pero todo era una ilusión, una estafa basada en el engaño. La brillante carrera de Holmes terminó en desgracia y prisión.

Margaret O’Mara, profesora de historia en la Universidad de Washington en Seattle y autora de The Code: Silicon Valley and the Remaking of America, afirma: “Esto no se puede inventar. Si un guionista hubiera intentado crear algo así de la nada, la gente habría dicho: ”No, es una historia inverosímil“. Sin embargo, es real. ¿Quién es la verdadera Elizabeth Holmes? Es una pregunta que aún no tiene respuesta”.

Quizás la respuesta se encuentre en You Can See Everything, un documental que desafía la realidad —estrenado en Estados Unidos este viernes y que Avalon traerá a los cines de España el 12 de febrero de 2027—, en el que Holmes se enfrenta cara a cara con el comediante del absurdo Nathan Fielder (How To with John Wilson, Los ensayos), quien codirige la obra junto con Lance Oppenheim. El documental fue filmado durante los 34 días previos a su ingreso en el campo penitenciario federal de Bryan, en Texas, para comenzar a cumplir una condena de 11 años por fraude electrónico y conspiración.

Ese destino parecía impensable en 2003 cuando, a los 19 años, Holmes abandonó la Universidad de Stanford y fundó Theranos, con la promesa de revolucionar la multimillonaria industria de los análisis de sangre mediante el uso de microfluídica para realizar cientos de pruebas a partir de unas pocas gotas de sangre.

Encontró un público receptivo entre los inversores que deseaban que Silicon Valley ofreciera algo más que “amigos de Facebook”. O’Mara explica: “Su ascenso destaca por ser el de una mujer emprendedora en una época dominada por hombres; además, estaba desarrollando una tecnología que parecía tener el potencial de cambiar el mundo”.

“El rechazo hacia el sector tecnológico —el techlash— aún no se había gestado, pero cuando surgían críticas contra Silicon Valley, el planteamiento era: ”Oigan, tienen a todas estas personas brillantes, ¿y lo único que hacen es crear aplicaciones? ¿Por qué no hacen algo con más sentido?“. O bien: ”Tienen a todas estas personas brillantes, pero resulta que todas son hombres“”.

O’Mara añade: “Ella desafiaba las expectativas de género; además, para convertirse en una especie de ”Steve Jobs femenino“, adoptó una imagen menos femenina y aparentó ser mayor de lo que era”.

Holmes dirigía la empresa junto a Ramesh 'Sunny' Balwani, un empresario 19 años mayor que ella, mientras mantenían su relación sentimental en secreto. Juntos lograron recaudar 945 millones de dólares (unos 844 millones de euros). Theranos no había publicado ningún dato validado por la comunidad científica, pero Holmes se mostraba carismática y persuasiva en las salas de juntas.

Agus, profesor de medicina y director ejecutivo del Ellison Medical Institute, comenta: “¿Sabes cuando entras en una sala, hay diez personas sentadas a una mesa debatiendo y, de repente, alguien toma la palabra y todos los demás dicen: ”Sí, vamos en esa dirección“? Ella tenía esa capacidad”.

“Vi cómo era capaz de influir en los demás. Esa cualidad es excelente si eres un Steve Jobs, pero resulta muy problemática en el ámbito sanitario, donde las decisiones deberían basarse en datos, no en la influencia de ciertas personalidades”.

Para 2014, Theranos estaba valorada en 9.000 millones de dólares (unos 8.040 millones de euros). A los 30 años, Holmes fue nombrada la mujer más joven en convertirse en multimillonaria por méritos propios en Estados Unidos, con un patrimonio neto de 4.500 millones de dólares. Fue aclamada en portadas de revistas y en listas de personas influyentes.

La revista The New Yorker escribió: “Aunque es capaz de citar a Jane Austen de memoria, ya no dedica tiempo a las novelas ni a sus amigos; no tiene citas, no posee un televisor y no ha tomado vacaciones en diez años. Su refrigerador está prácticamente vacío, ya que realiza la mayor parte de sus comidas en la oficina. Es vegana y, varias veces al día, consume una mezcla triturada de pepino, perejil, col rizada, espinaca, lechuga romana y apio”.

El ambiente embriagador sedujo a figuras políticas veteranas como Shultz, quien había servido de puente entre Washington y Silicon Valley durante la era de Ronald Reagan. A pesar de no contar con conocimientos especializados en diagnóstico de laboratorio, se unió a la junta directiva de Theranos en 2011, cuando ya tenía más de 90 años. O’Mara comenta: “Le atrajo la idea de que había una joven brillante intentando hacer algo con más sentido que simplemente crear una aplicación. Pero no se realizó la debida diligencia necesaria”.

En su biografía In the Nation’s Service, el periodista Philip Taubman describe cómo el entorno de Shultz temía que su entusiasmo se hubiera mezclado con un afecto personal hacia Holmes. Shultz hablaba con ella por teléfono casi a diario y la invitaba a las cenas familiares de Navidad. Holmes fomentaba esa atención, acercándose mucho a él cuando estaban sentados juntos.

Shultz incluso animó a su nieto, Tyler Shultz, a realizar unas prácticas de verano en Theranos antes de incorporarse a la empresa a tiempo completo. Con el tiempo, Tyler llegó a la conclusión de que Holmes estaba exagerando las capacidades de su tecnología. Trasladó sus preocupaciones al Wall Street Journal, cuya investigación sobre la empresa resultó fundamental para desentrañar su red de engaños.

Holmes sospechó que Tyler estaba detrás de las revelaciones. Sus abogados fueron tras él y fue sometido a vigilancia. Aterrorizado, buscó la protección de su abuelo. Sin embargo, George Shultz se negó a romper su relación con Holmes. Según Taubman, intentó —sin éxito— mediar entre ambos en lugar de apoyar a su nieto. George Shultz falleció en 2021, a los 100 años.

Los científicos también albergaban dudas sobre las grandilocuentes promesas de Holmes. Agus recuerda: “El problema con la sangre es que se coagula y, cuando trabajas con sistemas de microfluidos, estos se obstruyen. Ella nunca me dio una explicación de por qué eso no suponía un problema para su tecnología. Para mí, esa fue la primera señal de alerta. La situación era como la del cuento del emperador desnudo: o bien ella era extremadamente reservada, o bien sus explicaciones carecían de sentido”. John Ioannidis, profesor de medicina e investigación sanitaria en la Universidad de Stanford, trabajaba casualmente en un edificio que anteriormente había albergado la sede de Theranos. En 2015, publicó un comentario en el Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) en el que señalaba la llamativa ausencia de pruebas revisadas por pares que respaldaran las afirmaciones de Theranos.

Él relata: “Querían que me retractara, que me reuniera con Elizabeth Holmes y que, tal vez, escribiéramos juntos un nuevo artículo para JAMA en el que yo dijera que 'había visto la luz'”. Cuando Ioannidis les instó a publicar datos de validación, Theranos aludió a más de cien solicitudes de patentes. “Analicé esas patentes y, en su mayoría, carecían de sentido. Una de ellas tenía un título parecido a 'Cómo realizar investigación clínica': era vago y poco específico. Nada de eso cubría el vacío sobre lo que realmente se estaba haciendo”.

Sin embargo, al evaluar las motivaciones de Holmes, Ioannidis ofrece una perspectiva matizada: “No creo que nadie pueda saber qué pasaba por su mente. Mi interpretación más probable es que ella creía que iniciaría esa ola —atraer capital, fondos, talento, experiencia— y convertiría esto en realidad”.

“No creo que fuera una simple charlatana. Estaba impulsada por el deseo de poder, pero en Silicon Valley hay muchísima gente así. Ella entendió que, para lograr su objetivo, primero debía obtener poder y, después, atraer a las personas y la tecnología necesarias para materializarlo”.

Holmes dejó su puesto como directora ejecutiva en 2018, año en el que la empresa colapsó. El juicio contra ella culminó en 2022 con una condena a más de 11 años de prisión por fraude y conspiración. El escándalo fue documentado en un libro, varios podcasts y la miniserie biográfica The Dropout, protagonizada por Amanda Seyfried.

You Can See Everything ofrece una mirada íntima, y a menudo desconcertante, a la vida doméstica de Holmes junto a su pareja, Billy Evans, y sus dos hijos pequeños durante los 34 días previos a su ingreso en prisión, en mayo de 2023.

Los micrófonos de solapa captan hasta la más mínima respiración de Holmes mientras lee cuentos a sus hijos antes de dormir, a veces durante 12 horas al día. Por su parte, Evans aparece en pantalla desnudo en la ducha y batiendo huevos mientras corrige la forma en que ella cocina. Ambos se obsesionan con los perfiles que los medios publicaron en el pasado, argumentando que los periodistas inventaron la imagen pública que ella proyectaba.

La revelación más importante ocurre fuera de cámara: incluso mientras se preparaba para entrar en prisión, Holmes y Evans planeaban activamente una nueva empresa de tecnología médica. En 2025, dos años después del inicio de su condena, Evans buscó una financiación de 18,5 millones de dólares (unos 16,5 millones de euros) para Haemanthus, una compañía que supuestamente posee patentes de un dispositivo capaz de analizar muestras de sangre, orina y saliva mediante láseres.

¿Engañado una vez, la culpa es del otro; engañado dos veces, la culpa es mía? En un momento en que Silicon Valley invierte sumas sin precedentes en inteligencia artificial, O’Mara sostiene que las lecciones de Theranos van mucho más allá de la propia Holmes. “Esta historia sirve como advertencia y, a la vez, como una lección moral más amplia sobre Silicon Valley: el peligro de depositar tanta confianza y poder en manos de una sola persona joven o de un círculo reducido de individuos”.

Lo que el caso de Amber Heard explica sobre cómo las demandas por difamación buscan silenciar a las víctimas de abusos

“La cultura de los fundadores y la obsesión de Silicon Valley —que contribuyeron a convertir a Elizabeth Holmes en quien fue— siguen vivas, coleando y más fuertes que nunca. Nunca habíamos visto el poder y la riqueza tan concentrados en tan pocas manos. Ni siquiera durante la 'Edad Dorada' de Estados Unidos habíamos presenciado algo así”.
