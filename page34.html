Eisenkot leads but deadlock looms...

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Just two days before Israelis head to the polls, the four latest election surveys show a similar picture: Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party leads Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud, but neither of the two major political blocs has enough seats to secure a majority in the 120-seat Knesset.

With the election campaign entering its final stretch, the battle to cross Israel's 3.25% electoral threshold could significantly affect the distribution of seats and the balance of power in the next Knesset.

A comparison of the four polls published over the weekend, along with their average results, shows that neither Netanyahu nor his political rivals currently has a clear path to forming a government.

According to the final Maariv poll before the election, the opposition's change bloc has strengthened to 54 seats, compared with 48 for Netanyahu's bloc.

The shift comes as Ofer Winter's People of Israel party fails to cross the electoral threshold, receiving 3% of the vote, just below the required 3.25%.

The poll also shows United Torah Judaism and Otzma Yehudit losing one seat each, while Likud, Shas, and the Religious Zionist Party (RZP) each gain a seat. Overall, Netanyahu's coalition bloc has lost three seats compared with the previous week's poll.

Among the opposition parties, the most significant change is the rise of former prime minister Naftali Bennett's B'Yachad party, which gains two seats to reach 15. The Democrats, led by Yair Golan, also gain one seat, bringing their total to 10.

Meanwhile, Eisenkot's Yashar remains unchanged at 21 seats, and Avigdor Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu remains at eight.

The poll also finds that the joint Reservists Party and Economic Party led by former communications minister Yoaz Hendel and economist Yaron Zelekha narrowly crosses the electoral threshold, securing four seats.

The Channel 13 election poll gives Netanyahu's bloc 50 seats, compared with 54 for the opposition's change bloc.

Eisenkot's Yashar leads with 22 seats, followed by Netanyahu's Likud with 19. Bennett's B'Yachad ranks third with 14 seats.

Ofer Winter's People of Israel receives 3.1% of the vote, falling short of the electoral threshold. The joint Reservists Party and Economic Party receives 3%, while Moti Leitner's Haredi Public Party receives 1.3%.

All three fall below the 3.25% threshold required to enter the Knesset.

A poll published by Israel's public broadcaster KAN 11 shows Eisenkot's Yashar losing one seat but maintaining its lead with 22.

Netanyahu's Likud also loses one seat, falling to 20, while Bennett's B'Yachad gains two seats to reach 14.

The joint Reservists Party and Economic Party secures four seats.

Meanwhile, Winter's People of Israel fails to cross the electoral threshold, along with the Haredi Public Party and the Noam Party.

The poll points to a further decline for Netanyahu's bloc, which falls to 50 seats, while Eisenkot's bloc stands at 52.

The Arab parties gain one seat, bringing their combined total to 14, while the Hendel and Zelekha list holds four seats.

Under this distribution, neither of the two major blocs reaches the 61 seats needed for a parliamentary majority without additional support.

The Channel 12 poll puts Eisenkot's Yashar first with 23 seats, followed by Netanyahu's Likud with 21.

Bennett's B'Yachad ranks third with 15 seats.

Three parties fail to cross the electoral threshold, according to the poll. The joint Hendel-Zelekha list gets 2.4% of the vote, while the Haredi Public Party gets just 0.5%.

Winter's People of Israel drops to 2.5%, keeping it out of the Knesset.

The seat distribution among the political blocs highlights the difficulty Netanyahu faces in securing enough parliamentary support to form a government.

The coalition bloc holds 50 seats, compared with 56 for the opposition bloc, while the Arab parties win a combined 14 seats.

Neither bloc, therefore, reaches the 61 seats required for a parliamentary majority without additional support.

Ahead of the election for Israel's 26th Knesset, the average of the four latest polls published by Channel 12, Channel 13, Channel 14, and Maariv shows Eisenkot's Yashar holding a lead of just over two seats over Netanyahu's Likud.

Across the four polls, Yashar averages 22 seats, compared with 19.75 for Likud.

Bennett's B'Yachad follows with an average of 14.5 seats, ahead of Golan's Democrats with 9.5 and Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu with eight.

The joint Reservists and Economic Party list crosses the electoral threshold in two of the four polls, securing four seats in each. Its estimated average support stands at 3.10%, just 0.15 percentage points below the 3.25% electoral threshold.

By contrast, Winter's People of Israel fails to cross the threshold in all four polls, averaging 2.88% support.

Among the parties in Netanyahu's coalition bloc, National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir's Otzma Yehudit averages 7.75 seats, while Shas and United Torah Judaism each average 7.5. The joint RZP and Zehut list averages seven seats.

The Arab Joint List averages 9 seats, while the Ra'am party averages 5.5.

Across the four polls, the opposition parties average 54 seats, compared with 49.5 for Netanyahu's bloc. The Arab parties average 14.5 seats combined.

The average gap between the two major blocs is 4.5 seats, and neither has reached the 61-seat majority needed to form a government.

NFL House closes as riots disrupt game weekend...

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The NFL shut down its NFL House fan hub in Paris on Saturday as France braced for another day of mass protests, the latest disruption ahead of the league's first regular-season game in France. The Pittsburgh Steelers and New Orleans Saints kick off Sunday at 9:30 a.m. ET in Saint-Denis.

The league notified fans of the closure through its OnePass app Saturday morning, citing "potential accessibility issues," a day after it shut its city-center pop-up shop for the same reason.

The Saints also delayed their own fan gathering Saturday, announcing that "Saints House has been postponed until Sunday." The Steelers canceled their Steelers House event at a bar in the Bastille neighborhood on Thursday.

"Following recent events in the Bastille neighborhood, and after careful consideration, we felt it appropriate not to move forward with the planned Steelers House fan event in Paris," the team told fans.

That same day, Nicki Ewell, the NFL's vice president for global events, said no league fan events had been affected. Dani Watt, the wife of Steelers linebacker T.J. Watt, questioned the decision to play in Paris on her Instagram Story earlier in the week.

French Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said as many as 70,000 officers would be deployed nationwide Saturday. Paris police approved one march from Bastille to Nation and banned all other demonstrations in the city. Authorities closed 24 metro stations, the Louvre and the Orsay Museum.

The unrest began in mid-September with high school students protesting overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages and outdated facilities, then spread as trade unions and the yellow vests movement joined over the rising cost of living. The Oct. 17 demonstrations drew more than 500,000 people nationwide, and authorities arrested more than 2,000 people in Paris.

The U.S. State Department issued a security alert this week urging "Americans in Paris for the NFL game" to "exercise caution and be aware of their surroundings."

Peter O'Reilly, the NFL's executive vice president for club business, international and league events, said there was never serious consideration of canceling or moving the game. Economic woes transform Midwest into massive midterms battleground...

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Democrats could be poised for a revival in the heartland, as furor over high costs and doubt about President Donald Trump's willingness to change the nation's trajectory threaten to loosen the GOP's grip on working-class voters.

A decade after the Trump era began, when he first turned Midwestern political battlegrounds into reliably Republican states, the fallout from policies of his second term have rippled through the agriculture sector and broader economies across the Great Plains and the Rust Belt.

Iowa and Ohio, which have not been competitive since former President Barack Obama was on the ballot, are in play. And ruby-red states like Kansas, Nebraska and Indiana are not exempt. The shift is happening up and down the ballot, with tight contests for the House, Senate and governor blanketing the middle of the country.

From spring planting to fall harvest, anxiety over Trump's war with Iran and the fallout from his trade battles has turned into alarm.

In conversations across the region, voters point to the soaring cost of gas and diesel, as well as high grocery prices and interest rates. A whipsaw tariff policy has forced farmers to pay more for fertilizer, while some manufacturers, footing higher bills for imported components, have laid off workers. Trump's decision to allow imported beef has infuriated ranchers. And spending cuts targeting healthcare and grant programs have caused further strain.

Promises to revitalize the nation's manufacturing sector remain unfulfilled. And even before the Iran war sent costs soaring, several of these battleground states were in fiscal decline.

Iowa, for example, recorded the weakest annual growth rate in the country in 2025, according to the federal Bureau of Economic Analysis, a searing indictment for Republicans who have had control of state government for more than a decade.

"I think everybody in agriculture is tired, would be the best way to put it. They're just tired," Steve Kuiper, a farmer and president of the Iowa Corn Growers Association, told CNN. "They feel very beat up right now, demoralized."

It has all fueled a hunger for change — and created an opening for Democratic candidates.

"A lot of the promises the president made to bring costs down, to not start new foreign wars, to deliver on his economic message, have not come to fruition," said Beau Bayh, the third-generation scion of Indiana's most famous Democratic political family. If he wins his race for secretary of state, he'd be the first member of his party to win statewide in Indiana in 14 years.

Trump knows better than most presidents that midterm elections are a reflection — and referendum — on the party in power. He experienced it in 2018, when Republicans held control of the Senate but lost their majority in the House — including three seats in Iowa. GOP officials tell CNN they fear a repeat of that scenario.

The US Capitol building seen on October 1 in Washington, DC. - Anna Moneymaker/Getty Images

The travel plans of both parties tell the tale, with Trump set to visit Michigan on Sunday after making stops in Ohio, Kansas, Nebraska and Iowa in a monthlong campaign blitz. Obama traveled to Michigan and Ohio on Saturday, rallying support in two states he once won.

Polls suggest voters are willing to punish the Republicans in power in Washington, even though they also hold dim views of the Democratic brand.

"It's not that they've been overwhelmed by the Democratic Party, it's just that they're really, really pissed off," Tom Vilsack, a Democratic former Iowa governor, told CNN. "They want to send a message of disappointment. When you're upset with someone, you want to find a way to send a message. You don't swallow it, you send that message."

Vilsack, who served two terms as agriculture secretary in the Obama administration, said economic frustrations across the agriculture and manufacturing sectors have been rising long before the Trump era. But he said the promises Trump made during his three campaigns, particularly when he pledged to bring prices down only two years ago, are feeding the anger.

"He builds up the hope that it's going to be fundamentally different," Vilsack said. "That was interpreted that he's going to bring back the good times. He failed to do that."

Former Minnesota Gov. Tim Pawlenty, who was the last Republican to win that office in his reelection two decades ago, said Trump would be better off talking about rising costs and how he intends to change course.

"It's going to be a blue wave," Pawlenty told CNN. "We just don't know if it's knee-deep, waist-deep, or going to hit us right in the face."

The House is all but certain to fall from GOP control, he said, but added: "The real chance for Republicans to salvage something out of this is to hang on to the Senate and that's a real possibility."

In Iowa and Ohio, Democrats have a shot at winning Senate races that are crucial to the net four-seat gain they need to claim a majority — a goal that eluded them in 2018. Their odds look even better in those states' governor's races, potentially giving the party a foothold in statehouses where Republicans have trifectas: control of the executive, legislative and judicial branches.

But the Democratic revival could extend even further. Megachurch pastor Adam Hamilton has the GOP scrambling to save the seat of Sen. Roger Marshall in Kansas. An independent candidate, Dan Osborn, has rallied Democratic support and thrown a curveball into Republican Sen. Pete Ricketts' bid for a second term in Nebraska.

And a slew of House seats across the Midwest held by Republicans are in play. Democrats believe they could win as many as three GOP-held seats each in Iowa and Ohio, plus potentially four more in Michigan. The party is also targeting an Omaha-based district in Nebraska and one in southeastern Wisconsin's Driftless Area. If the election breaks in Democrats' favor in a big way, another House seat in Nebraska, as well as one seat each in Indiana and Kansas, could also prove to be much more competitive than either party expected early in the 2026 election cycle.

Republicans aren't conceding any of those races. And they believe they have Democrats on defense in the Upper Midwest — particularly Michigan, where progressive former public health official Abdul El-Sayed is locked in an expensive and bitter Senate race against Republican former Rep. Mike Rogers.

Speaking in Sioux City, Iowa, on Thursday, Trump urged voters to pretend he was on the ballot and vote to ensure a "booming Iowa economy."

"If you get out and vote like you did in 2024, we win in a landslide, you know, we win in a landslide," he said

Even as the president boasts of America's golden age, with an economy envied by the world, he also makes clear he understands the challenges people are facing. At most every stop along the way of his red-state October blitz, he has offered sweeteners to voters if they stick with Republican candidates.

From $8 billion in new aid to farmers announced in Kansas to promises of building the nation's largest steel plant in eastern Iowa to a commitment he made in Nebraska to temporarily ease diesel costs, Trump has made a series of pledges that follow his offer of giving every adult American $5,000 if Republicans retain their grip on Washington. The promises are barely even repeated by GOP candidates, who know they ring hollow with many voters.

Democrats acknowledge the uphill climb for winning all of the narrowly divided Senate races across the Midwest, particularly given their own brand and favorability challenges. But Republican officials wonder whether Trump's extensive travels to many of these states will backfire on down-ballot races.

"When our leader, the president himself, does things that make me cringe, that's difficult to tell people to vote for me," said Jim Howell, a Republican member of the Sedgwick County Board of Commissioners in Wichita, who is also on the November ballot. "I really wish we had leaders that don't make us cringe, but help us consider a way to come together."

A question hanging over the final week of the race is whether Trump's relentless campaigning has awakened Republican voters to rally to his aid one last time in hopes of closing an enthusiasm gap. Iowa could be a place to watch, given Trump's long history of winning there and his decision to hold two rallies in opposite corners of the state in the homestretch.

But the president's approach, a little more than one week from Election Day, has been to double down on the policies that have led many voters to sour on his second term.

In Trump's Sunday trip to Michigan, he'll hold a rally at Port Huron — on the Canadian border. It will put on display his tariff battle with the United States' northern neighbor, even though the party's Senate nominee, former Rep. Mike Rogers, has attempted to break with Trump over the tariffs.

"Here in Michigan, we know that Canada is not our enemy. We need to end this tariff war — now," Rogers said in a television ad his campaign launched this month.

The GOP's best hope might be to lean into the cultural battles that have helped them win over White, rural, working-class voters in recent elections. In Michigan, both Trump and Rogers have attempted to brand the Democratic nominee, El-Sayed, as supportive of extremist Islamist interests.

But Democrats are fighting back. El-Sayed in recent days began airing an ad that features clips of Republicans and conservative media figures, including Fox News hosts, saying nice or helpful things about him and his campaign.

Before those clips play, he lists issues where he suggests common cause with some in the MAGA movement, such as releasing the Jeffrey Epstein files and combatting Washington corruption.

Ohio is another headache for Republicans. The party's nominee for governor, Vivek Ramaswamy, faced sharp criticism from the man he is running to replace, two-term GOP Gov. Mike DeWine, for airing a controversial advertisement featuring the mother of his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton.

Gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy, alongside US President Donald Trump, speaks in Youngstown, Ohio, on October 12. - Kevin Lamarque/Reuters

Both Ramaswamy and the president have dug in, defending the ad — and in doing so have turned the race into a test of whether voters will accept Trumpian tactics from candidates who are not Trump.

And what's receded from the forefront is the issue Ohio voters who spoke with CNN in recent days — including Republicans who support Ramaswamy — said is responsible for the state's sudden competitiveness: the cost of living.

"We all have to get gas. I have to go get gas right now in my car, and that's difficult," said Sheri Vorherr, a 55-year-old mother of five from Loveland who volunteered at a Ramaswamy rally in Cincinnati on Wednesday. She said she helps her oldest daughter buy groceries each week and sees the economic pinch brought on by inflation.

"I don't think it's an easy fix. I think it's things that are going to take time," she said. "I do believe Trump is trying to do that — it's just taking a lot longer."

Arkansas in play?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Arkansas Democratic Senate nominee Hallie Shoffner speaks with voters at a campaign stop in Hope, Ark. Friday, Oct. 23, 2026. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

HOPE, Ark. — For Senate candidate Hallie Shoffner, the most important political label she wears isn't that she's a Democrat. Or that she's not a Republican. Instead, it's that she is an Arkansan.

As a lunchtime crowd ate at Tailgater's Burger Company Friday, Shoffner decried what she called a historically unproductive Congress driven by extreme voices in each party and pitched herself to receptive — if skeptical — voters as a candidate who would better serve their needs.

Arkansas is one of the most conservative states in the country, with nearly two out of three voters supporting President Trump in 2024 and Republican Sen. Tom Cotton in 2020.

But in the final days of the 2026 midterm election, Shoffner and other Arkansas Democrats are crisscrossing the state with a message that seeks to capitalize on the Trump administration's unpopularity, stubbornly high prices and anti-incumbency vibes.

"We're the sleeper race," Shoffner told NPR. "I mean, we're the race that no one's noticed and that no one is going to see coming."

Republicans have taken some notice, pre-emptively pouring millions of dollars into the Senate race, the Little Rock-based 2nd Congressional District and robust get-out-the vote efforts to try and stem the rising tide of dissatisfaction with the party.

But even in a midterm cycle where Senate races in states like Kansas, Iowa, Ohio and Texas are viewed as tossups, and where the landscape of potentially competitive House races stretches deep into conservative territory, Arkansas seems a bridge too far for election forecasters.

Arkansas' 2nd Congressional District is considered "Likely Republican," according to the Cook Political Report, and its Senate race is "Solid Republican."

Hallie Shoffner, the Democratic Senate nominee in Arkansas, has emphasized her farming background and belief that both parties have ignored the people's needs in the state. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

At an outdoor coffee shop in Arkadelphia, Vanilla Hannah said she was there to support Shoffner and talk about her own reelection bid to be a local justice of the peace. She said it's possible that Arkansas could elect Democrats this year because the crop of candidates seems willing to work with people to meet their needs — and there are a lot of them.

"When it comes down to basic needs or whatever, the 'D' or the 'R' after your name don't mean a thing, because we're all suffering and all having the same issues and everybody's ready for a change," she said. "And running for an election is not just running specifically on the Democratic policies or the Republican or the libertarians or whatever. It's just what you believe in, and it seems like more and more people are having a common belief now."

Later, Shoffner visited a small family farm in nearby Bierne where several members of the Hatley family cooked sorghum to make syrup and talked about why they were supporting her campaign.

Shoffner is a multigenerational farmer who decided to shut down her family's operation in 2025, citing financial headwinds from rising input costs and Trump administration tariff and trade policies. That in turn inspired her Senate run and influenced much of her campaign's focus.

Sliding behind the wheel of a side-by-side utility vehicle, Shoffner takes off across the property and recounts her life story that led her away from the farm, into nonprofit work and back home when her dad got sick.

She doesn't buy the argument that her chances are low to win.

"This race isn't about Democrats or Republicans or independents or any of that," she said. "This isn't some kind of weird political alignment, this is a values alignment. This is reorienting representation in Arkansas based on common sense, based on compassion and based on showing up."

She said national Republicans — and Democrats — have made the mistake of ignoring the needs of Arkansas residents, especially with agriculture and trade policies.

"I come from a place, a community, that died in my lifetime," she said. "I was the last one left and I felt left behind. I felt like no one was really talking to me or talking about me. I felt like folks were making decisions about me, but without me, and ultimately they were rewriting the rules."

Now, with record diesel prices, the war in Iran and tariffs, Shoffner said she believes that Arkansas voters of all stripes want something different. She's also glad that the race hasn't seen an influx of outside air support from national Democratic groups.

"I don't need them, I really don't," she said. "This is an Arkansas First campaign, and I'm an Arkansan, and I want to talk directly to Arkansans, and I want them to know that when I go to D.C., the only letters that will mean anything next to my name are the "A" and the "R."

Chris Jones, the Democratic nominee for Arkansas' 2nd Congressional District, says voters are frustrated with promises made by the Trump administration that haven't been kept. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

On the flip side, Chris Jones has the backing of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee as one of dozens of "Red to Blue" flip targets.

At an outdoor jazz night held at a brewery in Little Rock, Jones worked the crowd between visits to polling sites and a high school football homecoming game on a campaign that has seen millions of dollars in outside spending by Republicans.

"They understand that people are in pain," he said. "Folks are hurting. They can't afford gas. They can't afford groceries. And our campaign has always been about affordability and accountability for all. So prices are way too high."

Jones also ran for governor against Republican Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders in 2022 and lost, so he started off with higher name recognition as a serious challenger to Rep. French Hill.

He knows it's a tall task, even in a national environment more favorable to Democrats. Hill is a longtime incumbent whose district only became more conservative after the last Census-led redistricting in 2021.

But he also knows that Democrats, Republicans and independent voters alike are open to looking for something other than the current leadership.

"Here's what's so fascinating: a lot of folks here voted for the president and this administration because they wanted lower prices, they wanted to end foreign wars, and they wanted to end corruption, particularly with the Epstein files," he said. "None of that has happened, so they're super frustrated."

Like many states that have seen the potential for close races next week, Arkansas has a longer history of single-party control that has led to anger and frustration at the status quo and incumbency in a way that extends beyond typical partisan labels or voting history.

"One of the things that we forget, because there's so much that comes out of this administration: It wasn't but 15 years ago that the entire federal delegation of Arkansas was Democrat," he said. "So it's not that long ago. The reason they got in office was because of the conditions that existed, the economic anxiety and pain that people feel — and folks feeling like what they wanted to happen out of Washington was not happening. We're in that same place now."

In addition to the DCCC support, Jones has had campaign visits from former DNC Chair Jaime Harrison, former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg and Rep. Jasmine Crockett of Texas. Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker and Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear have both endorsed his run. And he's heard from people who don't normally support Democrats saying they'll back his campaign.

"Folks are literally hurting, and it's to the point where they're saying, 'my life is so bad that I don't care about party anymore'," he said.

Another factor working in Democrats' favor this election cycle is a surge in Black turnout following a Supreme Court decision that weakened part of the Voting Rights Act.

Multiple states across the South rushed to redraw House maps to gain a partisan advantage, with many of the new districts crafted in a way that minimized Black voting power. Arkansas lawmakers did not redraw their maps — the state has an entirely Republican congressional delegation — but after the 2020 Census did split Little Rock up in a manner that made Hill's district more Republican.

In primaries following the ruling, and early voting this month ahead of the midterms, there has been sustained higher Black turnout in states like South Carolina, Georgia and Louisiana in ways that could affect the outcome in key races across the region.

"I think part of it is that folks realized that there was an imminent threat to their right to vote," Jones said. "And people realized across the country that if we don't show up now, and Black folks realize if we don't speak up now, then our ability to do it later might not be there."

Marcus Hatley, one of the people who met with Shoffner at his family farm, said the increased turnout seemed like a "course correction."

"People are dissatisfied with the way that that happened, and some are crossing sides and voting in a different direction," he said. "And a lot of it has to do with removing, in some of those states, the ability for people of color to have equal representation in their districts. So it looks like to me there's going to be a course correction. I could be wrong, but I believe there's going to be a change."

TRUMP: DON'T 'GIVE A DAMN' ABOUT GAS PRICES...

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

President Donald Trump has said he does not "give a damn" about potential criticism of his remarks boasting that the United States is making a "fortune" from high oil prices, as American motorists continue to face elevated gasoline costs.

Speaking at a rally in Georgia on Saturday, October 24, Trump said advisers had warned him against highlighting the financial gains associated with rising oil prices, suggesting the comments would invite criticism over the cost of fuel for ordinary Americans.

In footage of the rally shared online, Trump claimed the US oil and gas industry was making billions of dollars amid the conflict in the Middle East, presenting higher oil prices as a source of financial gains for the country.

President Donald Trump speaks during a midterm campaign rally at the Guardian Centers on October 24, 2026 in Perry, Georgia.

"Our country is making hundreds of billions of dollars because of what's happened in the Middle East, because they're coming to Texas and they're coming to Oklahoma and they're coming to Louisiana and they're taking that oil," Trump said.

"My people say, please don't mention that, Sir, people are paying a little higher," Trump said, adding that he expected gas prices to fall.

"I can just see the fake headlines," Trump said. "President Trump is talking about all the money they're making while people are suffering at the pump."

"But I said, 'I don't give a damn, I'm gonna say it: We're making a frickin fortune,'" he added.

Trump went on to claim that Americans were "paying lower than Biden and Obama and they don't have nuclear weapons," apparently referring to Iran's nuclear capabilities.

Under Obama, the U.S. national average gas price peaked at $3.93 per gallon in May 2011, and under Biden, it peaked at $5.06 in June 2022, according to govargus.com.

There has been widespread backlash to Trump's comments online. California Governor Gavin Newsom reposted footage of Trump's remarks at the rally commenting: "Congratulations to Big Oil on their very successful, record-profits presidency."

Newsweek contacted the White House via email Sunday for comment.

The national average price of regular gasoline stood at approximately $4.29 per gallon on October 25, according to the American Automobile Association (AAA).

That was down from approximately $4.49 a month earlier but significantly higher than the $3.06 average recorded a year ago. The latest figures represent an increase of around $1.23 per gallon over 12 months, equivalent to roughly 40 percent.

AAA's reported national averages for other fuel types were approximately $4.80 per gallon for midgrade gasoline, $5.20 for premium and $6.35 for diesel.

Prices vary considerably across the country, with motorists in some states paying substantially more than the national average. The figures also illustrate the distinction between the financial benefits Trump attributes to the energy sector and the costs facing households at the pump.

Although gas prices had eased from their September levels, the year-over-year increase meant drivers were still paying considerably more to fill their tanks than they had a year earlier.

Trump has repeatedly pointed to the potential financial benefits of higher oil prices for American energy producers and oil-producing states.

But higher crude oil prices can also put pressure on consumers by increasing the cost of gasoline, diesel and transportation. The extent to which domestic producers benefit depends on factors including production costs, market prices and the wider economic effects of more expensive energy.

There have been numerous protests across the country protesting the Iran war and its impact on gas prices.

Signs protesting the Iran war and its impact on gas prices are seen during a protest organized by VoteVets outside of the U.S. Capitol on May 20, 2026 in Washington, DC.

As previously reported by Newsweek, nearly three-quarters of likely voters disapprove of how President Trump has handled gas prices, with the midterm elections days away and his overall approval rating at the lowest point of his second term.

A New York Times/Siena poll found that 73 percent of likely voters disapprove of Trump's handling of gas prices, compared with 24 percent who approve. The poll surveyed 3,494 voters, October 12-19.

No issue in the poll scored worse for the president, and the frustration runs well past Democrats: 77 percent of independents disapprove, along with 42 percent of Republicans and 43 percent of people who voted for him in 2024.

Massachusetts Senator Elizabeth Warren posted on X on Friday: "I'd like to report a fraudster in the Oval Office. Trump promised to lower costs on Day 1. But instead he launched a war that increased the prices of everything from gas to groceries."
