A Single Enzyme Could Hold the Key to Treating a Devastating Brain Disorder

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Blocking an enzyme’s production restored brain growth and healthy behaviors in mice engineered to develop Angelman syndrome, pointing to a possible treatment target.

Children with Angelman syndrome begin to show difficulties with thinking and movement at around 6 months of age. The rare condition occurs in approximately one in 20,000 births and is caused by the absence of a gene normally inherited from the mother. Researchers know which gene is missing, but they have struggled to explain why its absence causes these problems, making treatments elusive.

Research in mice now points to a chain of events that connects the missing gene to a stress response that interferes with brain growth. A team led by Jason Yi, PhD, an assistant professor of neuroscience at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, traced that response to a buildup of a normally harmless enzyme. The study was published in Nature.

The missing gene, UBE3A, produces an enzyme called ubiquitin ligase, which helps keep cells orderly by marking unneeded proteins for disposal. It is active throughout the brain and participates in 60 different cellular processes. Identifying which of those processes contribute to Angelman syndrome required narrowing that broad field of possibilities.

Yi and his colleagues developed modified cells in the laboratory with UBE3A deactivated, then looked for proteins that accumulated without it. They identified 23 candidate proteins whose levels increased. One stood out for its strong connection to ubiquitin ligase’s normal housekeeping function: asparagine synthetase, an enzyme that helps cells produce essential proteins.

In mice genetically modified to develop Angelman syndrome, elevated levels of asparagine synthetase activated the brain’s stress response. Yet the enzyme does not interact with the usual triggers for that response, and its overabundance does not otherwise damage the cell. The consequences instead arise indirectly, as the stress response sharply reduces protein production and growth.

“The neurons are tricked into thinking that they’re in stress when they’re not — that’s bad because they will then not grow normally,” Yi said, noting that most children with Angelman syndrome have smaller brains than their peers of the same age. “It’s a reaction from the brain trying to conserve resources to protect itself. It does suggest that there is a dysregulation in the brain we might be able to correct.”

Blocking the production of asparagine synthetase restored normal brain growth and healthy behaviors in the mice. The team had corrected a later step in the biological sequence set off by the missing gene, allowing healthy development to resume.

The study is the first to link an increase in asparagine synthetase to a neurological condition. It adds to evidence that an imbalance in proteins can contribute to neurodevelopmental disorders by triggering stress responses, even when the proteins themselves are not directly damaging cells.

Yi said medications could potentially target the stress response triggered by asparagine synthetase, but much remains to be learned before work on a possible treatment can begin. His team is investigating precisely why the enzyme activates that response, where in the brain the response is most active, and when during development medical interventions would be most effective.

“We still don’t know a whole lot about the disease,” said Yi. “We haven’t been able to identify all of the things that the missing gene is doing and rescue the things that it is not doing in Angelman’s syndrome. But I think our data are the most complete so far to begin answering those questions.”

Reference: “Persistent neuronal stress signalling underlies Angelman syndrome phenotypes” by Kwang-Soo Kim, Cory Gardner, Alissa Cullen, Jalin A. Stelzer, Kevin Cho, Ashish Sharma, Mati T. Nemera, Travis Law, Huma Naz, Guoyan Zhao, Harrison W. Gabel, Gary J. Patti, Erik S. Musiek, Tae-Hyuk Ahn and Jason J. Yi, 21 October 2026, Nature. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11165-4

Support for this work came from the Simons Foundation (Bridge to Independence award #387973), the Whitehall Foundation, the Hope Center for Neurological Disorders at Washington University (Pilot Award), and the National Institute of Mental Health (R01MH122786 and R01MH144153).

I was questioned by the police after taking a selfie with my daughter. For a year, I saved frantically for legal bills and didn't turn my heating on

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published: 15:12 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 17:51 GMT, 25 October 2026

I walked from my solicitor's office into the bright Plymouth sunshine, as she confidently assured me that she would hear from the police within a couple days.

It was September 2025 and I had just attended an interview under caution with two officers.

From the moment I heard about it, I took the police investigation extremely seriously, instructing one of the best local criminal defence firms, known for representing people accused of offences ranging from burglary and drug importation to posting problematic tweets.

Now, having done everything required of me, all I could do was wait for the police to do their part.

Little did I know that it would be another 13 months before I learned the outcome of this investigation. And even then, I discovered it by accident.

Last September, I wrote in the Daily Mail about how I was being investigated for harassment over four X posts.

The first was a factual update: 'Former Devon + Cornwall Police officer Harry Tangye has been charged with stalking and harassment after an investigation led by West Yorkshire Police.'

As a journalist, I routinely share police news so that people find me on X and DM me their own stories, which I sell to media outlets.

The second tweet was equally mundane, as it simply quoted and linked to a Plymouth Live article about Tangye and his partner being arrested in Newquay.

Then, in a conversation with another X user, I referred to the defendant by his initials and said I believed his 'bail had been extended to early May'. This bland remark was presented to me as the third piece of 'evidence'.

And the final piece of 'evidence' was a simple selfie of me and my daughter on a beach near my sister's house in Newquay. I hadn't tagged or mentioned the complainant, and it's literally one of Cornwall's busiest beaches.

The selfie Rebecca took with her daughter on the beach, which prompted police to investigate her for 'harassment'

Because this ex-copper lived nearby and claimed it made him feel 'fearful and distressed', someone in a position of authority at the force decided the matter warranted a formal criminal investigation.

So, after being abruptly informed that a failure to attend a voluntary interview would lead to my arrest, I made the two-and-a-half-hour journey to Plymouth, where two police officers questioned me at my solicitor's office.

I sat silently while the PCs, diverted from the traditional business of keeping the city centres safe, questioned me about the apparently alarming acts of sharing local press articles and taking my daughter to the beach.

One might assume that the police would only reasonably need a day or so to review an interview under caution concerning four social media posts. Handily, I'd even saved them the trouble of listening to lengthy answers by providing a written statement instead.

Yet the days turned into weeks, then those weeks turned into months, without a word on the outcome.

For 13 months, I continued to believe that I was under criminal investigation, with the possibility of a prosecution looming over me.

Nobody joins the police dreaming of a career investigating social media posts. I can imagine that my case was just another entry on a computer system to the officers dealing with it – and not a particularly exciting one.

Rebecca Tidy spent a year frantically saving to cover her potential legal bills and didn't put the heating on all winter

Every 28 days, my solicitor diligently asked the force for an update. None came.

Another month without an update was of little consequence to them. But every day of uncertainty had practical consequences for my own life.

For 13 months, this uncertainty shaped the decisions I made about money, work and our home. Most importantly, it affected the time and resources I could devote to my daughter, her education and wellbeing.

I knew the career consequences of a harassment conviction could be devastating.

As a single mother, I'm well aware that there is no second wage to fall back on in our home. Nobody else is going to pay my bills, feed my child or keep our household afloat if my income disappears.

So I did what any responsible parent would do and began saving for the possibility of a criminal prosecution.

Before my interview, several Cornish criminal defence solicitors quoted me between £350 and £650 an hour for preparation work.

I realised that by setting aside £1,000 a month, I would still only get two hours and 13 minutes of legal representation, based on an average hourly rate of £450. A simple one-day trial with two days of preparation would cost more than £10,000 in legal fees alone.

Most harassment cases are heard in magistrates' court where you're automatically ineligible for Legal Aid if your gross annual household income is £22,325 or more.

I instantly culled all non-essential purchases including my monthly Mounjaro prescription, quarterly Botox top-up and lip filler. Haircuts and highlights went next.

Then I swapped my beloved weekly Waitrose grocery shop for Lidl whenever possible.

But as the months passed, it wasn't only me going without. Mabel's clothes increasingly came from Vinted, bought second or third-hand. I didn't book our annual summer trip to Italy.

Every time I was about to spend money, I found myself thinking that I could be charged the following week and regret spending it.

I was even too nervous to pay to have the hot water fixed when it broke last winter.

Looking back, perhaps I should have known that I wouldn't be charged for sharing three published news reports. But the police told me they suspected I'd committed a crime, so I took them seriously.

Of course, I desperately hoped that the Crown Prosecution Service would draw a firm line between distress caused by the consequences of a complainant's own arrest and charge being public knowledge, and distress caused by criminal harassment.

Nevertheless, I couldn't rule out the possibility that they might take a different view, leaving me to argue my case in court.

The police had already threatened me with arrest if I failed to attend the interview, after all. I wasn't about to gamble my livelihood on the assumption that common sense would prevail.

It sounds slightly ridiculous now, but when you're living with that uncertainty, your brain often reaches for the worst-case scenario.

Every spare penny went into the pot of money I believed I might need to defend myself.

I worked an awful lot more too. There were evenings when I should have been listening to Mabel read or helping with her schoolwork, but instead I was sitting at my laptop trying to earn more money. She deserved better.

Then, last week, an unexpected email arrived from Devon and Cornwall Police. It contained a partial response to a Subject Access Request I had made 18 months earlier, asking for copies of the personal information they held about me.

I wasn't expecting it to tell me anything about the harassment investigation. But buried among the disclosure was an entry that stopped me in my tracks.

The police had decided to take no further action against me less than a fortnight after my interview under caution, 13 months previous. They hadn't even submitted their file to the CPS for a charging decision.

Their own record stated: 'I will seek to update Suspect of this outcome.'

I wasn't actually relieved to discover that the case had been closed. Instead I was furious. In my view, an investigation over four social media posts should never have been opened in the first place.

Even the wording of their comment infuriated me. How, exactly, do you 'seek' to update somebody? It required a quick update to my solicitor, not a search-party.

It turns out I am far from the only person to have experienced problems with the way Devon and Cornwall Police handles investigations.

Last month, His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire and Rescue Services escalated the force to Level Three of its Policing Performance System after Inspectors found that cases were left unallocated for weeks and that too few investigations were being carried out or supervised effectively.

In some cases involving sexual offences and concerns for children's safety, investigative work had remained unallocated for almost two months.

The watchdog ordered the force to take immediate action.

I find myself wondering how many other people are anxiously awaiting the outcome of a Devon and Cornwall Police investigation that has, in fact, already ended.

Throughout all of this, I did everything the police demanded. I instructed a solicitor, travelled two-and-a-half hours to Plymouth for the interview, provided a written statement and pursued updates afterwards.

I did my utmost to protect my child from the harm this investigation could cause, attending the interview rather than risk her seeing me arrested, and then saving money and working longer hours to ensure we had a financial safety net if I was prosecuted.

But I couldn't protect her from being left in limbo long after the investigation had ended. Telling somebody that their case is closed  is surely one of the most basic responsibilities of the police and takes minutes, yet failing to do it can shape an entire family's lives for months or even years.

I kept my end of the bargain. Devon and Cornwall Police didn't keep theirs. And it wasn't only me who lived with the consequences.

Devon and Cornwall Police have been contacted for comment.

Netanjahu hatte mit seinen Aussagen kurz vor der bevorstehenden Parlamentswahl die Kritik seiner politischen Gegner auf sich gezogen. Sie warfen ihm vor, damit stillschweigend eingestanden zu haben, dass seine Politik negative Auswirkungen auf das Land habe.

As Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy came under fire for releasing an attack ad featuring his Democratic competitor’s estranged mother, a mysterious news site was running a Facebook ad boosting the controversial video.

The Buckeye Bulletin, which bills itself as an Ohio-based news platform committed to “reliable journalism” and “strong editorial standards,” popped up suddenly this spring and began pumping out a steady stream of pro-Ramaswamy content.

A closer look at the Buckeye Bulletin’s Meta advertising data shows that its Facebook and Instagram accounts are managed by the Lincoln Media Foundation, a nonprofit organization affiliated with the Orange County-based Lincoln Club — the oldest and largest conservative donor organization in California. IRS documents show that the club and foundation are associated organizations and share at least two key executives.

The Buckeye Bulletin, Lincoln Club and Lincoln Media Foundation did not respond to requests for comment.

The Bulletin appears to be a partisan site posing as a local news outlet ahead of the midterm elections, a strategy political scientists refer to as “pink-slime” journalism.

This is not the first time a pink-slime outlet has been linked to the Lincoln Media Foundation. In 2024 Max Read, director of civic innovation for the Institute for Strategic Dialogue, identified seven such alleged outlets.

With the exception of one Los Angeles-focused site, those outlets all targeted critical states for the 2024 presidential election such as Georgia, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. Read reported that the media group had spent more than $63,000 on Facebook and Instagram ads, resulting in up to 18 million impressions during the last election cycle.

“[The sites were] very clearly designed to try to influence the information environment ahead of the presidential election,” Read told The Times. “They had all been started early in the year and used advertising on Meta platforms to juice their engagement numbers.”

When asked by The Times to take a look at the Buckeye Bulletin, Read said it appeared to be “pretty much copy and paste the same tactic that they used in 2024, even down to the aesthetics of the Facebook page and Instagram account.”

“It seems like they realized this worked at some level in 2024 and is worth doing again this year,” he said.

Earlier this year, CalMatters identified another outlet tied to the Lincoln Media Foundation called the California Courier, which had been circulating Facebook advertisements criticizing Gov. Gavin Newsom and the statewide redistricting ballot measure Proposition 50.

The Courier is part of a growing ecosystem of news publications that publish biased articles in an attempt to surreptitiously influence elections.

Since the Buckeye Bulletin’s Facebook and Instagram pages launched in March, the outlet has shared posts touting endorsements of Ramaswamy — a biotech billionaire and former presidential candidate — as well as his platform for addressing Ohio’s healthcare crisis and his stance on religious freedom in schools.

Simultaneously, the outlet’s social media posts have highlighted the refusal of his opponent — Amy Acton, Ohio’s former public health director — to debate, comments on how she “shut down” the state’s economy during the COVID-19 pandemic and a video with a watermark from right-wing political activist Laura Loomer.

Last week, the outlet turned attention to Ramaswamy’s controversial campaign ad featuring Acton’s estranged mother, Donna Arthur. In the ad, Arthur calls her daughter a liar and urges voters not to support her.

However, the ad did not acknowledge that Arthur lost custody of her children after their stepfather, Arthur’s husband, was indicted for raping Acton when she was a child — an omission that drew fierce condemnation from Democrats and some Republicans, including Ohio’s Republican Gov. Mike DeWine. Others, including President Trump and Vice President JD Vance, an Ohioan, have defended the ad.

Arthur and her husband fled after his 1978 indictment to Pennsylvania, where he died in 2004 without having been brought back to Ohio to face the charge.

Peppered between political content on the Bulletin’s social media feeds is a smattering of reels related to nonpolitical news, often featuring videos lifted directly from local TV stations. These other posts are used to create a “veneer of genuine local journalism,” Read said.

The language on the Bulletin’s About Us page displays markers of artificial intelligence such as generic mission statements, polished but repetitive phrasing, and vague claims about journalistic standards with no details about the publication’s staff.

Of the last 30 posts shared on the Bulletin’s Facebook as of 5 p.m. Friday, 21 were about state or national politics and 10 were about the gubernatorial race. Engagement on the posts dealing with Ramaswamy and Acton is consistently strong for a page with around 5,600 followers.

The unassuming social media scroller or person who clicked onto the website would have no clue of the Buckeye Bulletin’s apparent connection to a group of wealthy Southern California Republications.

The Lincoln Media Foundation was started by leaders of the historic Lincoln Club, which was founded in 1962 and once counted John Wayne and former President Nixon as members.

Today, the club describes itself as home to “the most prominent business and thought leaders” in California and says it offers members the opportunity to meet world leaders and “experience their hobbies come to life through a combination of extravagance and leisure.”

The foundation notes that it uses a digital approach to reach communities left out of the national conversation, but its website does not disclose providing funding to any news outlets.

IRS records show that the nonprofit’s financial activities kicked up dramatically in 2024, the latest year for which data are available. The Lincoln Media Foundation reported $3.88 million in revenue in 2024, compared with $738,000 in 2023, $453,000 in 2022 and $415,000 in 2021.

The Buckeye Bulletin’s Facebook and Instagram accounts have spent just over $10,000 boosting posts this year, according to Meta’s ad library.

That’s barely a drop in the bucket in Ohio, where hotly contested races for governor and U.S. Senate have drawn hundreds of millions in spending. But presenting a slanted political view under the guise of local news tends to offer more bang for your buck than traditional advertisements, said Georgia Kernell, an associate professor in the departments of communications and political science at UCLA.

“Voters are so sick of ads; they’re getting tons of clear paid advertisements on the radio, on the television and on their social media feeds now,” she said. “Anything that can kind of look different is going to be more likely to keep their attention.”

Presenting as a traditional news source also increases the likelihood of viewers believing that the information presented is well-researched, she said.

Nik Usher, associate professor of communications at the University of San Diego, said it’s unclear how much influence pink-slime journalism is having on voting behavior, but political strategists are willing to try any new approach to possibly make a difference in tight races.

“This is one strategic effort to throw more spaghetti at the wall, hoping it’ll stick,” Usher said.

Creators of these partisan sites further stand to benefit from the fact that artificial intelligence tools are constantly scraping content from news websites without paywalls.

Researchers at NewsGuard recently found that leading AI chatbots are citing pink-slime websites nearly half the time when users ask questions about candidates and issues in the midterm elections.

Democrats and Republicans have increasingly leveraged pink-slime journalism to influence voters in the past few election cycles.

In 2020, the New York Times identified a network of 1,300 news sites that paid freelance writers to produce biased content for Republican groups and corporate public relations firms. In 2024, the left-leaning Star Spangled Media group used a site called the Morning Mirror to promote down-ballot Democratic candidates, according to research from Columbia Journalism School’s Tow Center for Digital Journalism.

More recently, the evolution of AI has increased the ease with which these websites can be populated with content, eliminating the need to pay writers and automating the production of social media posts.

Usher described pink-slime websites as “explosive bombs for public trust” that take advantage of the gap created by the loss of independent local papers and further diminish expectations for news to be fairly reported.

“Once these sorts of sites are pointed out to the public it further undermines distrust in political institutions, political parties, the news media,” Usher said, “especially when AI is associated with this kind of stuff.”

What makes a good spine-tingler? Stephen Graham Jones says it's all in the ending

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Horror writer Stephen Graham Jones invites readers to graft their own nightmares onto his monsters. Alina Art/Tor Publishing Group hide caption

Stephen Graham Jones never intended to be a writer.

"I only planned to drive a tractor and be a farmer," he says.

Jones, a member of the Blackfeet Nation from Midland, Texas, saw either oil fields or cornfields in his future. But he always knew he had the chops to tell a good story.

"I was always spilling Kool-Aid on the couch and telling my mom a story that my little brother did it," he says. "It was all self-preservation, of course, but to tell you the truth, I was reading novels since third or fourth grade and I would get to the end of those novels and I would think, 'I can do that, I can end a story like that.'"

All month, Weekend Edition has been exploring why people are drawn to scary stories, how horror has become big business, and now Jones, a professor of English at the University of Colorado Boulder, shares with us how to create a good spine-tingler.

"I think craft can get you through 95% of a novel," Jones says. "The craft is mechanics. It's the little techniques you use to achieve this or that, and situate the story and the reader. That's all crafting technique."

Jones says the actual art of writing a good story doesn't really come into focus until you get to the wrap-up.

"I always think of it like the story is running along the top of a flat mesa, and it's nighttime and 95% of it is even-footing. But then at the very end, you jump off into the night, into the darkness, and you trust that there's going to be another toe hold out there," he says.

Experts studying fear have theorized that one of the major reasons people are flocking to horror experiences lately — whether theme parks, novels or movies — is that the world feels so uncertain. Jones agrees and says "whether that ending is up or down, that ending is what we're here for."

"Because if this long, dark tunnel of a story has a light at the end, whether it's a train or daylight, that means that the long, dark tunnel we're in in this world is also probably going to have an end."

As much as Jones agrees that a scary present may serve as a gateway to horror, the genre also gives writers — and readers — a particular opportunity to explore the past.

"In our current moment, there's a lot of effort to … cleanse the past of any wrongdoing," he says. "And so I think we need continual reminders that our footsteps to get here are all bloody," and that our history includes such low points as violence against native people and Indian boarding schools.

Jones has been publishing novels, short stories and novellas for decades but his breakout work was "The Only Good Indians." His latest is a sequel to that 2020 hit, titled "Off the Reservation." Both novels follow characters attempting to square their heritage and histories with hauntings and possessions.

Jones says he was initially reluctant to use titles like these because he didn't want to "cede that damaging phrase back into the world," but that an editor said it would help the stories go "wide" and Jones says he was finally convinced by friend that the title was appropriate based on the characters' struggles with identity.

"I reread the novel and every character was wrangling with, 'am I a good Indian in the contemporary world or not?' So I think the novel did earn that title."

Stephen Graham Jones says that writing monsters, ghouls, or — his particular favorite – slashers are about creating characters unique enough to be interesting but with just enough blank space to give readers the space to graft their own demons onto them.

"Then the story matters to them in a completely different way," he says. "Once you get the reader picturing it in their minds without you quite having to say it, that's when the scare can really be effective."

"You build the monster from a bad coach you had, a parent you're having difficulties with, from a bully at school, from a teacher that doesn't like you. You build them from who you're afraid of yourself."

Jones says to write a good story, you have to have something to say. That doesn't mean you preach; just write truthfully and the rest will fall into place, whether you're writing for 15 people, or 15 million.
