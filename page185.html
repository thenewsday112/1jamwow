Scientists discover a surprising secret behind the human brain’s wrinkles

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Most primates have relatively large brains covered in distinctive folds and grooves. However, common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) are an unusual exception. These small monkeys have brains that are almost entirely smooth, with very little folding. Scientists at the German Primate Center (DPZ), the Leibniz Institute for Primate Research in Göttingen, have now identified several cellular processes that help explain this difference.

Using miniature brain structures known as organoids, the researchers discovered that certain neural progenitor cells, which produce neurons, develop differently in marmosets than in humans. These differences involve the cells' structure, the speed at which they divide, and the timing of their development. Together, these changes reduce the number of neurons produced, contributing to the smaller size and smoother surface of the marmoset brain. Published in Science Advances, the findings provide new insights into primate brain evolution and could eventually improve our understanding of human brain development disorders.

Why Some Primate Brains Are Smooth Instead of Folded

Brain size and the extent of surface folding are important features that distinguish different primate species. The human brain, for example, has a highly folded surface resembling the ridges and grooves of a walnut. This arrangement greatly expands the brain's surface area, creating room for billions of neurons that support complex thinking and other advanced mental abilities.

Scientists believe that the common ancestor of modern primates already possessed a moderately sized brain with at least some folding. Yet not all primates living today retain these characteristics.

Common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), small monkeys native to South America that are becoming increasingly important in biomedical research, have brains with almost no visible folds. Researchers at the German Primate Center set out to determine why their brains develop so differently from those of humans and other primates.

"We wanted to understand which changes at the cellular level cause the marmoset brain to grow less and form fewer folds," explains Lidiia Tynianskaia, one of the study's two first authors and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution at the DPZ. "At the beginning of development, the common marmoset brain exhibits the typical structure and composition of a large, folded primate brain. As development progresses, processes must therefore occur that effectively slow down the production of nerve cells."

Scientists Discover What Slows Brain Growth

To investigate these developmental differences, the team compared brain organoids grown from human and common marmoset cells. Their attention centered on neural progenitor cells, the cells responsible for generating neurons during brain development.

These progenitor cells play a major role in determining the eventual size and shape of the brain. The more neurons they produce, the greater the potential for the cerebral cortex to expand and develop folds.

The researchers found that the marmoset's smaller, smoother cerebral cortex is not the result of a single developmental change. Instead, several differences involving distinct types of progenitor cells work together to limit neuron production and brain folding.

"Our investigations have shown that certain progenitor cells in the common marmoset divide significantly more slowly than in humans," explains César Mateo Bastidas Betancourt, also a first author of the study and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group. "Other progenitor cells have a simpler structure than their human counterparts, with fewer processes, and are therefore less proliferative. Both of these factors ultimately result in fewer nerve cells, which contributes to a smaller size and less folding of the cerebral cortex in marmosets."

The scientists also identified important differences in when certain progenitor cell characteristics and behaviors emerge during development. Compared with humans, marmosets have a shorter overall period during which these cells can multiply rapidly.

Together, the slower rate of cell division, simpler cell structures, and shorter window for rapid growth help explain why the marmoset brain produces fewer neurons and develops far fewer folds.

Miniature Brains Reveal Important Developmental Clues

Much of the research relied on brain organoids, small, three-dimensional structures grown from cells that reproduce important features of early brain development. These miniature models allowed scientists to investigate developmental changes across multiple samples and compare the timing of key processes.

"The study combines the advantages of in vivo and in vitro methods," says Michael Heide, head of the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution. "Organoids are well-suited for obtaining statistically robust results because such sample sizes are not feasible in primates. We subsequently repeated some key experiments in fetal brain tissue to confirm the results from the organoids."

One particularly important finding involved the relationship between development in organoids and development in actual marmoset brains. The researchers determined that a marmoset brain organoid grown for 50 days closely matches the developmental stage of a natural marmoset brain at day 90.

Establishing this relationship was essential because brain organoids and actual brains do not necessarily develop at the same rate. By identifying corresponding developmental stages, the scientists could make meaningful comparisons and interpret their experimental findings more reliably.

What Smooth Monkey Brains Reveal About Human Brain Evolution

The findings help explain how relatively small differences in the behavior and developmental timing of brain-building cells can contribute to major differences in brain size and folding among primates.

They also offer a potential explanation for how the wide variety of primate brain shapes and sizes emerged over evolutionary time, including why some species developed relatively smooth brains while others possess much larger, heavily folded ones.

Beyond their evolutionary significance, these discoveries provide a foundation for investigating how the human brain develops and what happens when those processes are disrupted. A better understanding of the cellular mechanisms that control neuron production and brain growth could ultimately help researchers study developmental disorders affecting the human brain.

Greenlight Family Hub (2026): Finally, a Digital Wall Calendar With a Voice Assistant

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I set up a digital wall calendar at a friend’s house a couple of months ago. Her first question was one I’ve constantly asked myself as I test these devices in my own home: “Does it have a voice assistant?”

It feels like the one thing missing from these devices. Digital wall calendars have been on the rise in popularity this year, driven by the popularity of the Skylight Calendar. After testing multiple models in my own home, I can see why. These devices take any and all of the digital calendars you already have (Google Calendar, iCalendar, Outlook Calendar—you name it) and put them onto a single device in your home.

It’s great if you’re like my husband and me, who live by our Google Calendars but don’t want to be tied to our phones when discussing plans. These devices can also double as household management tools with meal planning, chore trackers, to-do lists, photo screen savers that double as a digital photo frame, and even financial management for your kids if you go with the Greenlight Family Hub.

The Greenlight Family Hub has been an interesting device in the world of digital calendars because of how many features it offers. It not only adds financial management, as the company was originally a banking tool for parents and their teens, but family location tracking, which uses the Greenlight app on a family member’s phone to show their location, or a GPS tracker that you can separately purchase for younger children.

But there’s one feature behind the paywall (a $10-a-month membership) that finally satisfies the thing I’ve always wanted: a voice assistant.

Greenlight’s proprietary voice assistant has been both easy to use and has given smart, thoughtful answers. So why isn’t there a voice assistant in every digital wall calendar? And conversely, why haven’t smart displays that do have smart voice assistants gained features like full-page calendars and chore charts?

Part of what makes Greenlight’s hub different from other digital calendars is that the company is using the calendar as a starting point for the offerings it already has. That’s also why it’s called the Family Hub instead of just a calendar like most other devices I’ve tested. Greenlight grew from a banking tool to a way to manage chores and money for kids and teens, and has now become a full family management platform where you can track your family’s calendars, chores, kids’ spending, and even family locations.

Greenlight says it noticed the lack of voice support among competitors’ offerings and baked that into its hub intentionally. “Voice assistants have been around for over a decade, yet when building the specs for Family Hub, we noticed that voice support was absent in the digital calendar market," says Sushil Sharma, chief product officer at Greenlight. "Our goal with Hub was to create an all-in-one command center for busy families to engage with technology in a way that feels natural and seamless. By combining a voice assistant feature with a centralized home display, we are able to limit the number of devices families need in their home.”

It’s also smart for families with younger children—Sharma pointed out that not all kids can read and type fluently, and voice support makes it more accessible for them and the rest of the family. But it’s also a way people are expecting to interact with their home devices after years of smart speakers and smartphones offering voice support.

Greenlight’s assistant reminds me of the modern, AI-powered iterations of Alexa and Google because it’s also powered by AI. Greenlight says its family AI assistant uses frontier large language models with Greenlight’s own proprietary safety tools that it designed with family safety in mind. That means the assistant shouldn’t get into sensitive or inappropriate topics (or accidentally tell your child Santa isn’t real) but will both schedule a timer and answer day-to-day questions. You’ll also see a conversation appear on the right-hand side, reminding me a lot of the Alexa+ experience on an Echo Show device.

Greenlight’s Hub is supposed to only wake at its wake phrase, “Hey Greenlight,” but in the days I’ve been testing it, I’ve found it to be a little sensitive and likely to wake at random. It also tends to keep listening, waiting to see if you’ll continue the conversation or give a new command, so if my family is having a general conversation after asking a question, Greenlight will try to pick up a question from that and answer it. I find myself often ordering Greenlight to stop.

That isn’t an issue just for Greenlight. My Google Gemini-powered smart speaker does the same thing, and both my son and I are quick to yell, “Google, stop!” to get it to stop trying to answer a question we didn’t ask. It’s a problem with these AI-powered voice assistants overall, but I’m finding I’d rather say the wake word multiple times than get interrupted.

When I described to my editor the laundry list of features that the Greenlight Family Hub has, she said it sounded like a “Big Brother machine.” I see why, of course—tracking your location and financial information in a device that sounds like a simple calendar might come as a surprise to most people. It was certainly a surprise to me when I went to set up the hub through the app and ended up stuck on the financial pathway (Greenlight says it’s fixing this issue in the app but I had to have support clear my information so I could set up the hub without setting up a bank card for my child).

Still, I like how much it has to offer, even if it might feel a tad Big Brother-y for some. And while it does have a microphone, it doesn’t have a camera like most smart displays have, so in that sense it feels less creepy to me than other devices I’ve had in my home.

I also really like that most of the features are accessible without needing a premium subscription—notably the photo screen saver feature, which Skylight will charge you for. (Skylight’s business model in general has become charging for storage, for both its calendars and digital photo frames.) Greenlight charges instead for its AI assistant and voice interaction, along with family location sharing. If you’re less voice-inclined than I am, you’d be plenty happy with this hub without the premium subscription.

I am hoping we see more digital wall calendars add at least some kind of voice assistance, as well as offering some control of your smart home devices. Maybe I’m biased coming from a background in smart home technology, but it feels like a huge missed opportunity. Smart displays have existed for years (and Apple just announced its first one) but have lacked the key feature that digital wall calendars have to really make them a touch point in the home beyond your voice. I reached out to both Amazon and Skylight to ask why neither has introduced any such model yet, but both companies declined to comment.

Maybe, for a company like Skylight, it’s the cost of creating an assistant that would work with its platform and needing to change its hardware to include a microphone, but Amazon is well positioned to bring something like this to the market (especially since the newest version of Alexa, Alexa+, is already paywalled). I’m hopeful we’ll see the next generation of smart displays at least make some kind of calendar mode an option; I’d certainly rather see my calendar than another ad or piece of screen clutter on my Echo Show.

Hugging Face’s New AI Biology Model Lets You Chat With DNA

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI platform Hugging Face on Thursday released a new open model for understanding DNA that can run locally on a laptop.

Developed in collaboration with researchers at Zhongguancun Academy in China and the University of Naples Federico II in Italy, the model is trained on genomic sequences and natural language so that people can ask questions about the effects of DNA changes on gene activity and health and get conversational answers in return.

Lewis Tunstall, a senior research scientist at Hugging Face, says the tool is meant to help researchers design new drugs and genetic therapies, and it could potentially aid patients in interpreting their own genomes.

“We've unlocked a new capability in language models, which is fundamentally about being able to chat with DNA,” he says of the new model, dubbed Carbon-X. Using natural language means even nontechnical users “can actually ask questions about DNA that wouldn't be accessible through any other tool.”

Known as essentially GitHub for AI models, Hugging Face provides a library of readymade AI tools and a platform for running primarily open-weight and open-source AI applications for developers, researchers, businesses, and people who want to experiment with AI without building everything from scratch. In September, Nvidia agreed to acquire the company for nearly $13 billion. (Hugging Face was also at the center of a major hack by OpenAI’s models earlier this year.)

DNA is more like computer code than natural language. Instead of 0s and 1s, though, it uses As, Cs, Gs, and Ts to encode information. A human genome contains 6 billion of these letters. While the human genome has been sequenced end to end, scientists still don’t understand how most of it works.

Now, AI models are helping researchers decipher some of those mysteries. Last year, Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome, which predicts the effects of DNA sequences and genetic mutations but does so in a numeric format. It also requires more compute power. Evo 2, a model developed by the Arc Institute and Nvidia, along with academic partners, speaks the language of DNA and can generate entire genomes from scratch.

Carbon-X integrates both DNA and natural language in a conversational interface. Think of it like a ChatGPT for DNA.

The team started with an existing language model and expanded its vocabulary to read DNA in groups of six letters. They then continued training on 300 billion chunks of data, or tokens, that combined DNA sequences, English text, and examples linking DNA to biological descriptions. The DNA sequences came from mammals (including humans), other vertebrates, invertebrates, plants, fungi, and protozoa. Tunstall says the team didn’t train the model on viruses because they didn’t want it to be used for potentially dangerous applications.

Users won’t be able to upload full human genomes. The model was trained to be able to ingest DNA sequences of roughly 50,000 base pairs, so users will only be able to query small parts of the genome at a time.

One of the ways scientists could use Carbon-X is to help understand whether a genetic mutation is benign or disease-causing, a key step in developing a targeted drug or genetic therapy. If an intervention targets the wrong mutation, it could be totally ineffective or, worse, cause unintended harm.

Researchers face restrictions when it comes to uploading identifiable or sensitive patient data to frontier AI models, so Tunstall says his team intentionally designed a small model with 14 billion parameters. That will allow it to run locally on the user’s hardware, rather than rely on cloud-based models. No data is sent to Hugging Face or any other company, he says.

The developers of Carbon-X caution that they haven’t validated the model yet with actual wet lab experiments, so they can’t say how accurate its predictions are.

Hemingway’s Vendetta

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

On June 4, 1952, Ernest Hemingway stood at his typewriter at Finca Vigía, his home outside Havana, and wrote to one of his favorite correspondents, Harvey Breit, of the Times Book Review. The letter wanders from topic to topic: his favorite saint (St. Jude), some has-beens of American literature (John Dos Passos and John Steinbeck), and why he stands while typing. “I write standing up because sitting down broadens the ass,” he says. “I have had a narrow, hard ass all of my life and I keep it and a good left hook as other writers preserve their clippings and I hope to be buried with both of them.”

Hemingway also brings up a thirty-year-old writer and former bullfighter named Barnaby Conrad. Conrad fought bulls in Spain in the mid-nineteen-forties, and published his first novel, “The Innocent Villa,” in 1948. He had sent it to Hemingway, along with a fan letter. “He sounded like a good, straight kid,” Hemingway tells Breit, “and I am pretty sure he did whatever he said he did with bulls.” He says he meant to reply, but wasn’t sure what to say about “not a very good novel.” He clipped Conrad’s letter inside the book and forgot about it.

Now Hemingway has received Conrad’s newly published second novel, “Matador.” It’s bound to be derivative of his own meditation on bullfighting, “Death in the Afternoon,” he tells Breit: “Death” is the ur-text, the book that derives from the art itself, whereas all subsequent books derive from “Death”; and, on the whole, “I am not proud of what it has begotten.” As for “Matador,” “I can’t tell about this book until I read it and I hope the hell it is good.”

Seven days later, on June 11th, Hemingway is back at the typewriter, addressing the same correspondent about the same young novelist. Now that he has read “Matador,” the good, straight kid has become a fraud, a defamer, and, worst of all, a gentleman. The letter is so unhinged that he tells Breit to return it without showing anyone else. He has already written one draft too incautious to send at all.

If Hemingway let off steam in his June 11th letter, it soon built up again. Over the next five years, he bad-mouthed Conrad to his Scribner’s editor Wallace Meyer, his fellow bullfighting aficionado Rupert Bellville, the Chicago Tribune literary editor Fanny Butcher, the New York Post columnist Leonard Lyons, and C. W. Wilcox of Scribner’s Bookstore, in addition to Breit. Conrad, oblivious, continued sending him gushing letters.

As a boy in Hillsborough, California, near San Francisco, Conrad was drawn to what he called the Hemingway model of the intellectual man’s man. While studying art in Mexico City one summer during college, he began attending bullfights. At his third fight, he jumped into the ring, which was illegal but not uncommon, and made rudimentary passes using his Brooks Brothers raincoat as a cape. He got out intact; the raincoat didn’t. A Mexican matador offered to teach him the fundamentals of bullfighting, but during one lesson a bull struck him from behind, smashed his leg—Conrad later said his knee made a sound “like a popping log in a fireplace”—and broke several fingers. Back in the United States, he underwent surgery, and had to wear a brace and walk with a cane. His applications to the V-12 Navy College Training Program and a volunteer ambulance service were both rejected.

After graduating from Yale, Conrad joined the State Department as a code clerk and then got posted to Spain as a vice-consul, in late 1943. There, he befriended two of the towering matadors of the century, Juan Belmonte and Manolete, and began training with them. When Conrad was knocked down at a practice session, Belmonte ran to help him while Manolete lured the animal away. In 1945, billed as Niño de California, Conrad appeared on a program with Belmonte. He ultimately fought about thirty times, ten on programs with Belmonte, and considered resigning from the State Department to become a full-time matador, but another knee injury foreclosed that possibility. When he left Spain, at the end of 1945, Belmonte and Manolete both came to Seville to see him off.

Back in the United States, Conrad turned his adventures in Spain into “The Innocent Villa,” published in 1948 to mixed reviews. He then wrote a novel about a legendary bullfighter on the day of his last fight. Although the book includes a disclaimer that all its characters, apart from a brief appearance by Belmonte, are fictional, its protagonist is transparently based on Manolete, who had been fatally gored in 1947. Published in 1952, “Matador” became a best-seller.

Part of “Matador” ’s appeal grew out of Conrad’s experiences in Spain. Reviewers and feature writers stressed that he had studied under renowned bullfighters, entered the ring, and—the intellectual man’s man—emerged to tell what it was really like.

Not so, according to Hemingway. Conrad is “very skillful,” he tells Breit in the June 11th letter, but “he would never convince the boys.” The “Matador” author photo shows Conrad by a poster for one of his bullfights, and the last line is “Entrada por invitación.” If it was an invitation-only fight, Hemingway says, then “nobody was fighting nothing for money and the bulls were calves.” A calf presented nothing like the mortal danger of a full-grown bull, and killing one in the ring “is the worst form of chickenshit I know.” Hemingway further suggests that Conrad is not just a fraud but a fop, the “Gentleman Calf-Sticker” and the “Dean Acheson of bull fighting,” a reference to the patrician Secretary of State. “Gentleman” was an insult; Hemingway once told the editor Maxwell Perkins that F. Scott Fitzgerald was always a gentleman but “never a man.”

In “Death in the Afternoon,” Hemingway had called bullfighting “the only art in which the artist is in danger of death.” He tells Breit in the June 11th letter that he had fought “the hard way” during Pamplona’s famous bullfighting festival, the Fiesta de San Fermín, entering the ring “six mornings straight” for seven years (a number he would inflate to eleven years in a subsequent letter), facing “every sort of bitch and bastard animal” that had fought “as many as 20 times” in amateur events. (Animals that have fought previously are more dangerous, because they may have figured out that the cape is just a distraction.) This wasn’t calf-sticking, he says: “People got killed for nothing every morning.” Even so, he knew his limits. The matador Maera tried to persuade him to enter the ring with “one famous educated cow” by saying, “Ernesto you son of a bitch you could not be killed by a more distinguished animal.” But, he recalls, “I did not want any part of her.” Some people begged him to stay out of the ring altogether, before it was too late, he says, while others remarked that “if we get killed in July we do not have to be rained on and broke in October.”

As Hemingway acknowledged elsewhere, though, the Pamplona festival was hardly a showplace for grace under pressure. It featured an early-morning melee in which several hundred men would enter the ring. A heifer, a steer, or occasionally a young bull would be released, usually with its horns covered. The men would taunt and tease the animal until it charged, and then either cape it or run. Some men would get knocked down, butted, or trampled. Hemingway’s friend Donald Ogden Stewart broke several ribs in 1924, but “he was in no real danger,” Hemingway would write a few years later, adding, “It was a rough Spanish game but no rougher than boxing or professional football.”

In several descriptions of these amateur events, including some of his own, Hemingway sounds less like a matador than a rodeo clown. When he jumped on the back of one animal, according to his friend Gerald Murphy, it collapsed beneath his weight. In “Death in the Afternoon,” Hemingway claims that, to avoid getting injured, he once grabbed a bull’s horns and rode around the ring on its muzzle while spectators laughed uproariously. In the nineteen-fifties, the Spanish writer José Luis Castillo-Puche asked about his experience in the bullring. Hemingway replied that “even my friends thought it was a laugh riot.”

Hemingway’s prominence in the history of Spanish bullfighting, of course, stems from the page, not the ring. The first year he attended the Pamplona festival, in 1923, he claimed that he and his wife, Hadley, were the only English speakers there. When he wrote about bullfighting that year in the Toronto Star Weekly, he figured that readers needed a pronunciation guide: “ ‘Ole!’—pronounced Oh-Lay!—roared the crowd.” Through his articles, novels, short stories, and “Death in the Afternoon,” he introduced much of the English-speaking world to bullfighting. Everyone who wrote about the topic thereafter toiled in his shadow, and knew it.

Conrad certainly knew it: “Death in the Afternoon” introduced him to bullfighting, as he noted in his letters to Hemingway. But he proceeded to learn a great deal on his own by living in Spain, befriending matadors, and entering the ring. Hemingway followed Spanish bullfighting during visits to Spain between 1923 and 1931. Conrad knew it more intensely for a shorter time, while living there between 1943 and 1945. Though a decade separated their sojourns in Spain, the two men shared friends and acquaintances there. Conrad, accordingly, wasn’t just one more writer working in Hemingway’s shadow. He knew people Hemingway had written about—and Hemingway was aware of it. In “Death in the Afternoon,” Belmonte is the object of sweeping generalizations and grand judgments. Conrad got the impression that Hemingway and Belmonte must have spent extended time together and known each other well; Belmonte, however, said they had met only a few times. Conrad could compare the real Belmonte with the “Death in the Afternoon” “Belmonte.” He might try to do the same with Hemingway and “Hemingway.”

Not that one needed to be a potential fact checker to provoke an unfatherly response from Papa. Throughout his career, Hemingway sniped at other writers—his juniors (Conrad, James Jones), his mentors (Sherwood Anderson, Gertrude Stein), and his contemporaries (Fitzgerald, William Faulkner). He punched up, down, and sidewise. His manly posturing fit a pattern, too. Reviewing “Death in the Afternoon,” in 1933, Max Eastman likened his style to “wearing false hair on the chest.” When they encountered each other a few years later, Hemingway struck Eastman across the face with a book.

Along with being a calf-sticking impostor, Conrad, according to Hemingway, was a malicious defamer. Pacote, the Manolete-like protagonist of “Matador,” is Spain’s greatest bullfighter and plans to retire after one last fight. After his girlfriend calls him a coward, he gets drunk but sobers up before entering the ring and gives the greatest performance of his life. As Pacote delivers the fatal thrust with his sword, the bull raises its head and gores him. He dies in the infirmary.

Hemingway can’t see past the drunkenness. If Manolete was sober as he entered the ring for his final fight, the book is a malicious libel, he tells Breit in the June 11th letter. He tells Butcher, Lyons, and Wilcox the same thing. In a July 15th letter, he urges Breit to write a magazine article about “Matador” ’s slanders and their reception in Spain, given that “the Spanish regard Manolete with practically a religious veneration,” and offers to provide “detailed suggestions how you might build it.” Breit apparently didn’t take him up on it. If “Matador” did provoke any controversy in Spain, Conrad doesn’t mention it in his two memoirs, “Fun While It Lasted” and “Name Dropping,” nor in his autobiographical articles.

Eight years after “Matador,” Hemingway himself issued a harsher indictment against Manolete. In 1960, Life featured his three-part account of the 1959 Spanish bullfighting season, later gathered into a book called “The Dangerous Summer,” which proved to be his last major work published during his lifetime. In it, he blames Manolete, dead since 1947, for debasing bullfighting through vulgar showmanship and outright cheating. Hemingway may have seen his accusation as broadly comparable to the one he saw in “Matador”: dishonor. But there’s a difference between drinking before entering the ring once and corrupting bullfighting for years. There are differences, too, between “Matador,” a work of fiction, and “The Dangerous Summer,” a work of nonfiction; and between Conrad, a novice novelist when he wrote “Matador,” and Hemingway, a Nobel laureate in literature when he wrote “The Dangerous Summer.”

Hemingway’s accusations caused an uproar in Spain and attracted coverage in the United States. Manolete’s friends and admirers defended him from the charge that he had sullied bullfighting—including, in a letter to Life, Barnaby Conrad. After faulting Hemingway for misconstruing Manolete’s style in the ring, he closed on a gracious note: “Mr. Hemingway still writes more interestingly on the bulls than anyone going, whether I agree with him or not.”

Did any of Hemingway’s left hooks even strike their target? Three weeks after Hemingway’s June 11th letter, Breit described Conrad as “young and handsome” in the Times Book Review. In 1956, when Conrad sought permission to quote some Hemingway writings in a bullfighting anthology, Hemingway told Meyer to refuse: “I am predjudiced [sic] against Mr. Conrad.” Scribner’s ignored Hemingway and let Conrad use brief excerpts. Hemingway famously said that “the most essential gift for a good writer is a built-in, shockproof, shit detector.” Perhaps, when reading Hemingway’s denunciations, his correspondents applied their own built-in, shockproof shit detectors.

Though he received no responses from Hemingway, Conrad kept sending him chatty letters. In hopes of expressing his admiration face to face, he once wrote and then phoned to ask if he could visit the Hemingways in Cuba, but Mary Hemingway told him that Ernest was ill. Conrad had no way of knowing that Hemingway was intent on avoiding him. “If I don’t meet him then, at least, I was never his pal,” Hemingway told Leonard Lyons in 1954.

Decades later, long after Hemingway’s death, in 1961, Carlos Baker, Hemingway’s biographer, told Conrad about some of the letters deriding him. After getting copies, Conrad concluded, as he told a reporter in 1991, that Hemingway had been “extremely jealous I had fought bulls and written about it.” Conrad had decorated his beach house in Carpinteria, California, with bullfighting memorabilia: a poster for a fight featuring Niño de California, the mounted head of a bull, his own charcoal sketches of Manolete and Belmonte. To the mix he added framed copies of several Hemingway letters about him originally sent to Meyer at Scribner’s. Conrad apparently didn’t know about the fevered 1952 letters to Breit. It’s a shame. Gentleman Calf-Sticker is hard to beat. ♦

In the upcoming midterms, D.S.A.-endorsed candidates in New York will likely pick up two seats in Congress and six in the state legislature. But their K-12-related proposals tend to be sparse—mostly limited to measures such as blocking charter-school expansion and restricting A.I. use in classrooms.

Educators were the top donors by profession to the Mamdani campaign, so it’s somewhat surprising that his education policy “was sort of a blank space,” as Kyle DeAngelis, who teaches third grade at a public school in Harlem, put it. “I think a lot of teachers got caught up in the thrill of it all and painted on that canvas all of the things we’d like to see change about our profession.” For DeAngelis, these changes would include more funding for social workers and reading specialists, and less classroom time devoted to tests—the city mandates three-times-a-year assessments in math and reading, and the many hours spent prepping for these and for district and statewide tests “eat away at instructional time,” he said.

During a recent episode of WNYC’s “The Brian Lehrer Show,” in a segment called “Ask the Mayor,” the host pressed Mamdani on issues that were raised in a viral essay by the Times journalist Nikole Hannah-Jones about her daughter’s experience in two of the city’s predominantly Black and low-income elementary and middle schools, which Hannah-Jones portrayed as de-facto segregated, badly under-resourced, and rife with indifferent teachers, disruptive student behavior, chronic absenteeism, and grade inflation. Mamdani told Lehrer that members of his administration, including his chancellor, Kamar Samuels, were working on “a plan to deliver a rigorous and high-quality education to every single child” in K-12 schools. But he attached few specifics and no deadline to this pledge. On Wednesday, Samuels and Hannah-Jones appeared together at a town-hall-style event in Manhattan, where Samuels likewise demurred on providing details or a timeline for the plan.

Even some ardent supporters of the Mayor are troubled by what they see as his inaction on K-12 education, especially at a moment when reading and math scores, graduation rates, and public-school enrollment numbers are falling or stagnant. “I am a huge Zohran Mamdani fan,” the education journalist Jennifer Berkshire recently wrote on Substack. “But I have been deeply frustrated by his administration’s lack of a vision for what is not only the nation’s largest school district, but the largest entity, by cost and headcount, under the mayor’s control.”

There are several different and overlapping hypotheses for Mamdani and N.Y.C.-D.S.A.’s seeming reluctance to engage on K-12 issues. In her Substack post, Berkshire speculates, with reason, that the Mayor is loath to consider education reforms that might risk alienating the United Federation of Teachers, the powerful union that represents most public-school teachers in New York City. But that didn’t stop Mamdani from suing the City Council, in August, over its U.F.T.-supported bill to give bonuses to the school system’s woefully underpaid paraprofessionals. (The Mayor believes that the bill violates existing labor law.) Berkshire also points out that D.S.A. membership skews young and child-free—but those demographics did not forestall Mamdani’s push for free child care and universal preschool.

Edgar Alfonseca is an N.Y.C.-D.S.A. organizer and the parent of a four-year-old. He’s currently involved in D.S.A.’s Tax the Rich campaign, which is advocating for new citywide and statewide tax legislation that would fund universal child care across New York State. “There are fourteen of us on the Tax the Rich organizing committee, and right now I’m the only parent,” he told me. He estimates that maybe one in ten D.S.A. organizers are parents of young children. “Nonetheless, every time I have brought up child care as a policy priority, there is a shared positive feeling about it.”

When it comes to the K-12 landscape, several D.S.A. members indicated that the organization and its candidates have to tread carefully in order to minimize any impression that they are politicizing the classroom or indoctrinating students. The media climate is “one of the factors that has tended to cool any education-related D.S.A. organizing,” DeAngelis said, citing Red-baiting outlets such as Fox News and the New York Post. DeAngelis is part of the U.F.T.’s social-justice caucus, the Movement of Rank-and-File Educators (MORE-U.F.T.), whose membership overlaps considerably with N.Y.C.-D.S.A.; last year, a Post article portrayed MORE members as “anti-Israel radical socialists” who are “plotting to take over” the U.F.T.

In July, another Post article kept up this refrain, with a headline warning, “DSA Targets NYC Public School Kids in Radical Takeover.” The article featured mysteriously sourced screenshots from a Zoom meeting in which D.S.A.-affiliated educators discussed agenda items such as “Helping students flourish,” “Healthy, up-to-date, physical spaces,” and “High-quality education, critical thinking, literacy, numeracy, democracy,” among other Marxist-Leninist slogans. One right-wing education activist quoted by the Post described the meeting in terms redolent of sexual exploitation. “Childless apparatchiks seeking access to other people’s children in order to convert them to their beliefs is the definition of grooming. Hands off the kids,” she said.

“Education justice is often used as a cudgel by the right,” Nina Bruckenthal, who teaches math at a Lower East Side high school, and whose name and photo appeared in the Post article, told me. “This is just one example of the dynamics that the administration will face as it begins to tackle education issues.” The episode also captured a persistent cultural appetite for shaming teachers. (“On one hand,” Peter Maas Taubman writes in his book “Teaching by Numbers,” from 2009, “we are held up as saviors, rescuers, saints, and angels, on the other cast as buffoons, sadists, milquetoasts, or incompetents.”)

David I. Backer is an associate professor of education policy at Seton Hall University who advised the Mamdani campaign on education issues. “I understand why the campaign made a strategic decision to fight front and center on other planks, and not engage as much in this very fraught terrain,” Backer, a former high-school teacher, told me. Still, it’s not the case that Mamdani ignored K-12 policy altogether. In April, 2025, the candidate announced his Green Schools for a Healthier New York City plan (Backer helped write it), which would use union labor to refurbish some five hundred of the city’s dilapidated schools with new HVAC and renewable-energy systems; it also proposed transforming a similar number of asphalt schoolyards into green spaces for students and their communities.

Since Mamdani’s election, there’s been no public-facing follow-through on the Green Schools plan, as Jenille Scott of the New York-based nonprofit ALIGN recently noted in an essay for City Limits. “We want classrooms with clean, chemical-free airflow and comfortable temperatures, where kids can focus on their lessons instead of getting sick and missing classes,” Scott wrote. She cited research showing that proper classroom ventilation and climate control can have dramatic positive effects on student absenteeism, test scores, and disciplinary referrals. “Our schools are falling apart and our kids are falling behind,” Scott added. This sentiment is hardly limited to American cities—the recent student demonstrations in France, which have driven hundreds of thousands of people into the streets, originated as a protest over poor classroom conditions at a single high school in a Paris suburb. (“The work to build a more sustainable school system, while started on the campaign, has already begun and will continue,” a representative from the Mayor’s office stated in an e-mail.)

If the Green Schools plan ever does come to fruition in New York, it might serve as an exemplum of what Mamdani calls “pothole politics”—his homage to the “sewer socialism” that flourished in Milwaukee for roughly half of the twentieth century—by which a municipal government can maintain the citizenry’s trust by diligently maintaining the city itself: filling potholes, shovelling snow, tearing down scaffolding, repairing public rest rooms. “As Democratic Socialists, we have to demonstrate that we can solve the small but very tangible problems of the city,” DeAngelis told me. He looks forward to the arrival of pothole politics in his Harlem classroom, where one of the windows is broken and has been covered in duct tape for more than a year, in a system with an annual budget of around forty-five billion dollars. “There’s a fundamental dissonance between a figure like that and the everyday reality of being a New York public-school teacher,” he told me.

The biggest splash Mamdani has made in the K-12 public pool came last month, when he declared a one-year moratorium on students’ use of generative A.I. through eighth grade. The announcement marked a breakthrough for the AIM Coalition, an umbrella organization of predominantly left-leaning groups, including N.Y.C.-D.S.A. and MORE-U.F.T., that oppose most A.I. in education. But the D.O.E. is pressing on with five A.I. pilot programs for high-school students, which AIM is vehemently protesting, and teachers are still generally free to use A.I. tools. In short, a wildly reviled and starkly unnecessary techno-corporatist intrusion on public classrooms is the most conspicuous place where New York’s first avowedly socialist mayor has intervened in the city’s schools, and even that intervention has been rather mild.

In the mid-nineteen-sixties, the sociologist James Coleman led a survey of public-school students and teachers, under the auspices of the Civil Rights Act, on the topic of equal educational opportunity. After sorting through hundreds of thousands of responses, Coleman and his team came to the conclusion that family environment—the size and stability of one’s family, the educational and economic status of one’s parents—was the “most important variable” in predicting how a student would fare in school. The report also found that children benefit from attending school with peers who come from diverse racial and socioeconomic backgrounds, and observed the most positive effects of diversity in children from low-income and minoritized families.

The Coleman report’s conclusions have been corroborated by generations of studies analyzing the effects of poverty and demographic isolation on children’s health, behavior, cognitive development, and academic achievement. This body of research forms some of the latticework of a social-reproduction theory of education, in which public schools are largely perceived as reflecting and reinforcing the socioeconomic structures of their surrounding community.

This way of seeing is both embedded and rejected in Hannah-Jones’s wrenching Times essay, which dominated conversations among parents and teachers for weeks after its publication. The article describes Hannah-Jones’s daughter and her peers “languishing in classrooms with educators who did not take their academic potential seriously and largely refused to teach.” Their predominantly Black and low-income elementary school, contra Coleman, “exposed the fallacy that caring families are more important than competent teachers”; Hannah-Jones felt “wholly defeated by a system of inequality that may begin outside the school walls but is magnified within them.” Society, Hannah-Jones goes on, has abandoned its duties to children in racially and socioeconomically segregated schools, unfairly blaming “their academic limitations on the dysfunctional culture and poor choices of their marginalized families.”

It’s true that findings such as Coleman’s have been weaponized by conservative forces against more generous funding for socioeconomically disadvantaged schools. But leftists, working off the same evidence, have long argued for precisely the opposite. In a 2005 paper titled “What ‘Counts’ as Educational Policy?,” the late CUNY professor and Marxist theorist Jean Anyon examined how poverty-level wages, substandard housing, spotty public transportation, and unequal property-tax structures “create environments that overwhelm the potential of educational policy to create systemic, sustained improvements in the schools.” She argued for a more holistic response, one in which labor, housing, and taxation policies—the kinds of proposals that are championed by the D.S.A. candidates of today—were also understood as education policy. A trove of research has shown, for example, that SNAP benefits and child-related tax credits can have significant impact on children’s test scores and graduation rates.

“It might sound like a bit of a dodge,” Corey Robin, a professor of political science at Brooklyn College, told me, “but when you push for better housing, when you push for health care, those are education interventions.” Universal high-quality child care and preschool, too, can be seen as K-12 policy, offering benefits for kids’ social-emotional regulation and executive-function skills that they can carry with them to primary school and beyond. Preschool teachers can also spot cognitive delays and other learning differences that might otherwise go unaddressed until much later. “Then it’s not a first-grade or third-grade teacher who’s suddenly trying to play catch-up on things that upper-middle-class families have been throwing resources at from the get-go,” Robin said.

This is a wraparound approach to K-12 policy, evocative of the line that Mary Simpson used while canvassing with Gays for Zohran: “Anything that is good for us is good for our kids.” It has gained relatively little traction in mainstream education discourse, perhaps because it is premised on systemic and structural change to a degree that strikes more centrist temperaments as naïvely optimistic at best, implicitly fatalistic at worst. In her Substack piece, Berkshire argued that class-based analyses such as Anyon’s have rendered the left avoidant and “visionless” on education. Berkshire approvingly quoted the billionaire philanthropist John D. Arnold, who posted to X, “We can’t wait to solve all societal issues before demanding higher performing schools.”

Arnold, who made his money trading energy at Enron and who has donated millions to charter schools, listed alternative solutions for better public education, including school choice, standardized testing, consolidation of underperforming schools, reëxamination of tenure, and “greater accountability at the district, school, principal, and teacher levels.” In other words, we can rescue schools in 2026 if only we adopt the bold reforms of the federal No Child Left Behind Act and the mayoral administration of Michael Bloomberg, both of which débuted a quarter century ago. It’s a moribund formulation, as stale as the air in a poorly ventilated classroom.

Education debates in the United States are undoubtedly warped by pressures from the right, whether it’s the neo-HUAC inquisitions of the New York Post or the billionaire-driven voucher program in Texas that has become central to the state’s current gubernatorial race. (New York’s governor, Kathy Hochul, recently opted into President Trump’s private-school voucher program, which siphons students and funding from public schools; Hochul’s decision drew immediate condemnation from the U.F.T. and the D.S.A.) But this discourse is also misshapen by forces of the neoliberal center, which tends to see education outcomes not as products of their environment but as self-contained, quantifiable, ripe for privatization, and measurable largely in terms of teacher performance and test results. A whole-system framework for education reform in New York—of the kind that Democratic Socialists could conceivably articulate, package, sloganize, and turn into buttons and protest signs—may seem utopian or unrealistic. But one advantage it would have over competing proposals is that it’s never really been attempted before.

“Something that the Mamdani administration could say is, Let’s try an affordability agenda that gives everyone the basics of what they need, and then we’ll see if kids do better in school,” Backer said. “If kids and their parents could get to school and work on time, if they had good food, if they had good health care, good child care, solid housing, if they had a school that wasn’t making them sick—we’re ready to try it. Are you?” ♦

A Philosopher of War Changes His Mind

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I first met Michael Walzer about a decade ago. I was a nobody graduate student from Catalonia, finishing my dissertation. Walzer was a professor emeritus at the Institute for Advanced Study, in Princeton, and the former co-editor of the left-wing magazine Dissent, but he was most interesting to me as a philosopher—and, especially, as the author of “Just and Unjust Wars,” an attempt to delineate the rights and wrongs of warfare in both its causes and its conduct. In the nearly fifty years since it was published, no other book on the subject has matched its influence. I e-mailed him out of the blue, eager to discuss its ideas, and he invited me to his office. We talked about philosophy and politics on a walk in some nearby woods.

I returned to his work several years later, when a publisher wrote to me about another of his books, “Thick and Thin: Moral Argument at Home and Abroad,” which I had praised in a talk. They had acquired the rights to publish it in Catalan and asked me to write a prologue. I said yes, but found that I also wanted to write about Walzer’s career and his legacy. I wrote to him again, this time as a journalist, proposing a profile. Early in 2023, he invited me to his apartment, in New York, where he had just moved with his wife of nearly seventy years, Judy Borodovko Walzer—like him, a retired academic. In May of that year, Walzer and I sat in their kitchen and talked amid boxes waiting to be unpacked and paintings waiting to be hung.

He had recently turned eighty-eight. Walzer has a gentlemanly demeanor and a sense of irony that makes him difficult to pin down; he dislikes abstraction and hypotheticals, and prefers to speak and write without jargon. He learned to read, in the early nineteen-forties, from the left-leaning afternoon paper PM, he told me, which, he added, “had the best maps” of the Second World War. He remembers his grandmother rejoicing at the Soviet victory in the Battle of Stalingrad. His parents, the children of Jewish immigrants, were living in the Bronx, where his father worked in a defense factory. Later, Walzer’s father got a job, through his in-laws, at a jewelry store in Johnstown, Pennsylvania, where the family joined a Reform congregation. The rabbi was a Zionist, and Walzer shared a memory—though he wasn’t sure if it was accurate—of his parents weeping with joy listening to the news that the United Nations had voted in support of creating a Jewish state in what was then the British mandate for Palestine. In Johnstown, Walzer went to a consolidated high school, with kids from the well-to-do borough of Westmont and from the working-class township of Upper Yoder. After graduation, “half the boys” went to college, he said, and half went to fight in Korea. “It was a lesson in the class system,” he told me. He went to college at Brandeis, then did a Ph.D. at Harvard.

After Korea came Vietnam. When the United States sent combat troops, in 1965, Walzer was teaching political theory at Princeton, where he helped organize one of the first teach-ins against the war. The following year, he returned to Harvard, and, in 1967, he became the co-chair of the Cambridge Neighborhood Committee on Vietnam, whose members went door to door collecting signatures for a referendum opposing the war. In June, Israel launched a preëmptive strike against Egypt, setting off the Six-Day War, and proceeded to occupy the Sinai Peninsula, the Golan Heights, the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip. Many of Walzer’s anti-Vietnam allies opposed all of this, but he thought that Israel’s initial attack was justified; in his view, the country faced an imminent threat and needed to act. Seeking to explain why this was right, and why the war in Vietnam was not, he turned to the mostly Catholic tradition of just-war theory, and began work on what became his most famous book.

The book’s fundamental precepts are not complicated. It is just to defend your country from an invasion and it is unjust to invade a country that has not attacked you—although Walzer does allow for exceptions, mostly for humanitarian causes. It is just to assume certain risks to avoid civilian casualties and it is unjust to bomb towns indiscriminately to compel an enemy to surrender. The justice of going to war—what philosophers call jus ad bellum—is separate from jus in bello, the justice of how a war is fought. Soldiers on either side of a war have the same rights and the same obligations, regardless of which side is more broadly in the right.

Most of these precepts were already commonplace in international law, but they were often ignored in practice, and it was becoming conventional wisdom in some intellectual and political circles that war was a matter of naked self-interest. In this regard, many realists and pacifists were united: neither side believed that a war could be fought morally. In the book, Walzer draws on history as well as philosophy, quoting letters and memoirs from soldiers alongside speeches by politicians and by military leaders, illustrating how people attempt to fight in a moral fashion, and revealing that they usually make imperfect and uncomfortable decisions. Shortly after the book’s publication, a group of officers at West Point adopted it as a textbook, making it required reading for second-year cadets. It is still taught at military schools.

At the kitchen table, that May, we talked about the war in Ukraine, which was then a little more than a year old. It was, by Walzer’s lights, a morally straightforward war: Russia had invaded unjustly and Ukraine was defending itself. But war is never simple. “You know there’s now a version of just-war theory called jus ex bello?” he asked me. “Jus ex bello means ‘When is it just, or obligatory, to stop fighting.’ ” Just then his wife arrived, carrying shopping bags, and we paused to help put things away. Returning to the table, Walzer said that a legal scholar had recently used the jus ex bello doctrine in an op-ed to argue that the war had become so costly to Ukrainians that their government was now obliged to make concessions to stop the fighting. This was wrong, Walzer insisted—such a principle would encourage aggressors to be as destructive as possible.

In the months that followed, we exchanged e-mails and spoke over Zoom. Walzer wrote multiple pieces about the war in Ukraine, and I asked him about those writings as well as those from his past. That September, I drafted my article. On October 7th, Hamas attacked Israel and Israel retaliated. I asked Walzer for another interview. “Yes,” he replied. “We need to talk.”

We spoke again a few days later, over Zoom. Walzer wore thick glasses, and his hair was dishevelled; he offered a polite but fading smile. In the seventies, Walzer and his wife began making annual visits to Israel; by now, they had as many friends there as they did in the U.S. I asked him about an article he’d written years before titled “The Four Wars of Israel/Palestine.” He argued then that though some Israelis were justly defending their security within the country’s 1967 borders, others were unjustly fighting for a Greater Israel that included settlements and occupied territories. And though some Palestinians were justly fighting to end the occupation of the West Bank and Gaza and to create an independent state, others were waging an unjust war to destroy Israel altogether. Peace would only come, he wrote, when those fighting the just wars defeated those, on their own side, fighting the unjust ones.

Did he still see it this way? Yes, he said. And, unfortunately, he added, those fighting the just wars had not been running things on either side. Israel, he said, now had “to find a way to respond to the Hamas attack.” He went on, “It’s a principle of just-war theory that when there is a just cause, there has to be a way of responding.” Did the initial response by the Israeli government constitute a war crime? “I think a prolonged siege of Gaza, yes, is unjust,” he said. Judaism’s greatest philosopher would agree, he noted. “Maimonides wrote, memorably, one of my favorite sentences: ‘When one surrounds a city to lay siege to it, it is prohibited to surround it from four sides; only three sides are permissible.’ You have to allow civilians to leave. Well, Gaza is besieged on all sides.” Then he added, somewhat coldly, “It’s also stupid, because people will start dying, and it’s not Hamas that will be blamed for their deaths.”

I went to see him in person a few weeks later. It was Halloween and, in the streets, people were dressed as monsters and pumpkins. I found Walzer in the hall of his building, and we went up to his apartment together. He was moving more slowly than he had in May; he’d been experiencing back problems. At his door, I noticed a mezuzah. He has long been secular, and I asked him why he’d put it up. “So the antisemites know which door to knock on,” he said.

We took seats in a tiny corner of the apartment. A copy of the left-wing magazine Jacobin sat on a desk. Walzer looked less anxious than he had just after the attack, and he seemed sharper; I could tell that he’d discussed the subjects I was raising many times. “Every dead civilian is a human tragedy and a political asset for Hamas,” he said. “So, I assume, if there are any smart Israelis left, they are trying to minimize civilian casualties. But how committed they are to that I just don’t know.”

Walzer believed that Israel’s war was just but that the Army was waging it, at times, unjustly. Still, he rejected the idea of a ceasefire. If Israel accepted one, he said, “Hamas will claim victory and Israelis will live in fear.” He hoped that an Israeli victory would lead to the creation of two separate states, though he acknowledged that neither Hamas nor the Israeli government seemed to want this. Eventually, he said, there would be a different government in Israel, and he believed that there could be public pressure for such a solution. To my surprise, he was also open to the idea of a confederation, “which would be something less than sovereignty for each side.” When I noted that this was a development in his thinking, he said, “I just want to see some movement away from where we are.”

In 1988, Walzer wrote that terrorism might only be justified “if the oppression to which the terrorists claimed to be responding was genocidal in character.” I mentioned this idea in the context of Hamas’s attack. “That’s certainly not Hamas’s circumstance, because if they give up the project of annihilating Israel, and the border opens, investment comes in,” he said. “It may not be what they want, but the prospect of genocide isn’t there.” I noted that the most extreme members of Netanyahu’s Cabinet had been implying that erasing Gaza’s entire population was not out of the question. Walzer acknowledged that some people in the Israeli government “probably hope for something like that.” But the war cabinet included people with different views, he said. Perhaps there was a steadier hand on the Israeli response.

The following January, Walzer was scheduled to give a talk in Barcelona, near my home, to discuss “Thick and Thin,” which had now been published in Catalan. The book argues that our “thin” values of universalism and international solidarity grow out of the “thick” values that we learn in the communities we are born into; the thin values then help us to overcome the violence that can arise when we clash with other people’s thicknesses, so to speak. I was asked to be his interlocutor at the event, which was held in a four-hundred-seat basement hall beneath the historic Pati de les Dones at Barcelona’s Centre de Cultura Contemporània. Walzer’s back was still bothering him, and so he joined remotely, on a giant screen that loomed over the audience. It felt as though we were speaking in a bomb shelter.

Freedom and self-determination, Walzer said early on in the conversation, are universal values, and the task is to coexist with the differences they produce. Then, as if reading the room—which was packed with college students, progressive intellectuals, and the kind of engaged citizens who worry about the world and feel at home with someone who speaks in subordinate clauses—he interrupted himself, mid-reflection. “I have defended wars of humanitarian intervention because they are enforcing the universal value of life,” he said. “And I have opposed wars for regime change, which are motivated by some conception, some understanding of our own way of life, which we believe should be your way of life.”

The conversation continued for another half hour, drawing closer and closer to the war in Gaza until I asked him about it directly. Some things that seemed unjust, in his view, were not. Hamas, he said, had “a kind of military embeddedness in the civilian population” that “makes it virtually impossible to fight at all without killing civilians, and maybe even without killing large numbers of civilians.” Israeli officials had said that one-third of the Palestinian casualties were Hamas fighters, he noted. “If that’s true—if the ratio is two to one—given the crowdedness of Gaza and given the embeddedness of Hamas, I think that’s a respectable ratio,” he said. Some in the audience gasped. Then we took questions. A young man said he was offended by what Walzer had said about Gaza and that he would never read any of his books. Someone else asked him if the “thickness” of his Jewish upbringing thwarted his ability to see the “thin” injustice of the war. Walzer said he had been “a very strong opponent of some of Israel’s wars in the past,” but “not, as it turns out, of this one.”

There is a special difficulty in applying just-war theory to wars between a powerful military and an insurgent group. It’s a problem that “Just and Unjust Wars” does not address at length and that Walzer has thought about a great deal since. Two months after the event in Barcelona, he delivered a lecture on the topic at West Point, focussed on the war in Gaza. He has visited West Point multiple times since “Just and Unjust Wars” was published, and he said that the officers who taught there had told him that they “felt a special closeness with the IDF—the army, they said, most like ours.” In the lecture, he described the conflict as an “unprecedented” asymmetric war. “I don’t believe that there has ever been a war fought against an underground city,” he said, referring to the network of tunnels in Gaza. Hamas, he argued, had designed the fighting above ground “to produce civilian casualties.” The United States, he added, had sent Israel two-thousand-pound bombs, “not right for urban warfare,” to crush Hamas’s tunnels: “So we are complicit in the damage they did up above and in the civilian deaths they caused.” In May, he gave a longer version of the lecture, over Zoom, to the Minerva Center for Human Rights, in Jerusalem. There, Walzer noted that the powerful armies generally do most of the killing in these asymmetrical wars, but that such conflicts are also political matters, and that the more powerful side often loses. “The French in Algeria, the French lost,” he said. “The Americans in Vietnam and Afghanistan: we clearly lost. And in Iraq: we didn’t win. The Israelis in Lebanon and, a number of times before October 7th, in Gaza: too many cases of not winning.”

I asked Walzer about this the next time we spoke, over Zoom. If Hamas’s strategy had made it impossible for Israel to win the war without killing even more civilians than they already were—and if, in that case, Israel could not win the war at all, on account of public outrage and political pressure—wouldn’t the just thing be to stop fighting? Walzer told me that he had heard that argument from people on the Israeli left, and that, of course, it was important to prepare for negotiations and to hope for a diplomatic solution. But he regarded Israel’s situation as precarious. Israel could not have avoided this war, he said.

I thought of an article he had written years before, with the Israeli philosopher Avishai Margalit, titled “Israel: Civilians & Combatants” and published first in Haaretz and later in The New York Review of Books. “This is the guideline we advocate: Conduct your war in the presence of noncombatants on the other side with the same care as if your citizens were the noncombatants,” they wrote. Had Walzer changed his mind on this? No, he said. Then he posed a hypothetical involving a house partly occupied by Hamas, with rockets stored in the basement and civilians on other floors. “Let’s imagine the civilians in different floors were Israelis,” he said. What should Israel do about this? “I think that is the right way to ask the question,” he went on. “I am not sure what the answer is. I suspect the answer is, you bomb the apartment.”

That September, thousands of pagers and walkie-talkies, booby-trapped with explosives by Israeli operatives, were detonated in Lebanon and Syria, killing dozens, among them two children, and maiming or injuring thousands. Walzer wrote an essay for the Times calling the explosions “terrorist attacks” and “very likely war crimes.” The column constituted his harshest criticism of Israel to date, a fact he acknowledged the next time we spoke, a few months later. He had come to agree with the former Israeli Defense Minister Moshe Ya’alon, whose statements made him think of the conflict as two wars: a legitimate defense against Hamas’s attack and an unjust war of ethnic cleansing in northern Gaza. (The Israeli military denied Ya’alon’s accusation of ethnic cleansing, insisting that it acted “in accordance with international law.”) Walzer confessed, too, that he was beginning to wonder whether a war might be waged so wantonly that the justness of its cause is undermined. “I’ve always tried to keep those two judgments separate,” he said. “But I’ve been challenged by a number of people.”

It was January, 2025. The departing Biden Administration had brokered a ceasefire that allowed for the release of hostages taken by Hamas in exchange for Palestinian prisoners; the ceasefire held until March 18th, when Israel launched a surprise attack, with the approval of the Trump Administration, claiming that Hamas was not negotiating in good faith to release the remaining hostages. In one of the deadliest days of the war, the Israeli military killed more than four hundred people—mostly women and children, according to the Hamas-led Gaza Health Ministry. The next day, Israeli troops began a ground operation in territory that the Israeli Defense Forces had withdrawn from weeks earlier.

Walzer had just turned ninety years old. The Van Leer Jerusalem Institute and the Israel Academy of Sciences and Humanities organized a conference in his honor, to be held that June, and invited him to give a talk. He told me that he would speak about “Just and Unjust Wars” and the war in Gaza, but that he didn’t know what he would say. In May, Israel expanded its offensive in Gaza. In June, Israel bombed Iran, and Iran retaliated. The conference was postponed to the fall.

In October, I called Walzer again, and we discussed a so-called peace plan that had been announced by Trump, which included international administration for Gaza. Its terms were not far from Biden’s, but Walzer was not optimistic. Trump, he said, “wants to be seen signing an important document that he can wave in front of the Nobel Prize committee next year.” It was plain to him now that the Israeli government intended to occupy Gaza the way it has occupied the West Bank. “So I could say the war became unjust when it became clear that the end of the Israeli government was unjust,” he mused. Then he paused. “I don’t want to take that as a way out. I do think that the commitment to the siege was an act that was clearly unjust by in bello terms—and I think it required an opposition to the war itself, not just to the conduct of the war.” He offered a wry smile. “I’m not sure, at age ninety, that I’m going to rewrite the theory. But if I write on this issue, I’ll have to express that anxiety—that gross misconduct, war crimes, could reach a point where we are obligated to oppose the war itself.”

A month later, I flew to Israel to attend the conference. It was my first visit to the country since the war began. On the streets of Jerusalem I saw hip-looking young Israelis carrying assault rifles, some of them with military packs, presumably coming and going from their conscription duties. On the columns of train stations, stickers bore the smiling faces of soldiers in their twenties alongside motivational quotes and the dates of their births and deaths.

The evening of Walzer’s talk felt unusually warm. The lecture hall was packed; I took a seat in the back row. I’d spent the day listening to commentary on his work, much of it laudatory but some of it quite critical. The starkest assessment was delivered by Khaled Furani, a professor of anthropology, who called Walzer’s work a “lullaby” and a “chatty” narrative designed to soothe the Israeli conscience while masking the reality of Palestinian suffering. Furani deemed the current state of affairs a natural consequence of Zionist nationalism, and invited Walzer to flee the sinking ship of Zionism and board the ark of Islam and Palestinian liberation.

When Walzer took the stage, he affirmed his commitment to the safety of Israel and described his fury at the attacks of October 7th before turning to Israel’s conduct in the war. The recent raids in Gaza, he said, seemed intended to make it “uninhabitable, at least by its present inhabitants.” He added that “Israel had managed the siege in a way that meant, and seemed from far away intended to mean, that there would be no end to the suffering, no sustained relief, until a final, a total, victory, a victory that was never morally possible, probably not strategically possible, and that no one really believed in.” What’s more, “once there was a political way to defeat Hamas and to free the hostages, the war became unjust. The effort to win it, when there was no way to win without destroying Gaza and driving out all or most of its people” was, he said, a “criminal effort.”

The hall, by this point, was quiet. Walzer said that he did not trust the international reckoning regarding Israel’s actions that was sure to come. He wanted Israelis to do the reckoning themselves. “Please get it right,” he said.

The applause that followed was intense but sad. There were two more speakers: the Palestinian jurist and philosopher Raef Zreik, who argued that Walzer’s attempt to apply just-war theory to Israel and Palestine was rendered moot by his fundamental misreading of the founding of Israel, and a professor of American history named Yael Sternhell, who compared the situation of Palestinians in Israel to that of African Americans in the South after the Civil War. When they were finished, members of the audience went to where Walzer was sitting, surrounded by family, and offered congratulations. I joined them. He looked more curved by age than he had when I first interviewed him, two and a half years before; he held a cane. I asked if he was happy. That wasn’t the word, he said. Honored, rather. There had been some strong criticism of his work, I noted. Walzer smiled and gestured to a hearing aid. “I could not hear all of it, but I’ll read them,” he said. “Let us talk about it soon over Zoom.”

We spoke a week and a half later. Walzer responded thoughtfully to each of the critiques, but without backing down. He may change his mind on things, but he does so slowly, and, once he moves in one direction, he does not retreat.

This made his one notable shift during our nearly three years of conversation all the more striking: the wall between jus ad bellum and jus in bello, which had sustained his theory for almost half a century, was no longer sturdy. Walzer thought the matter through aloud with me, at one point letting out a small chuckle at his intellectual perplexity. A few minutes later, he looked down, leaning toward the screen and speaking with resignation. “There is a sense in which the carnage of Gaza, the ongoing carnage, simply made the war unjust,” he said. “Did the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki? I thought they were violations of jus in bello, but you could say they made the war unjust. It went beyond a war crime. I don’t know how to explain it.”

I suggested an analogy with what, in his earlier work, he calls supreme emergencies: moments when you must do whatever it takes to preserve the “ongoingness” of your community. In those cases, your cause is so just that you are warranted to be immoral in protecting it. Perhaps, I said, on the other end of the spectrum, if your way of fighting surpasses the threshold of extreme carnage, the justice of your cause vanishes. “That sounds right to me. That sounds very good,” Walzer replied. “Most wars don’t reach anything like the carnage of Gaza,” he added. “Even the firebombings of German cities, which I thought was a war crime, I wonder—if it had gone on and on, if there had been five Dresdens, would that have made me think, No, this isn’t right anymore?”

We spoke again in June. The Trump Administration had launched military operations in both Venezuela and Iran and did not seem terribly bothered about demonstrating any kind of right to do so. “I don’t think our ‘Secretary of War,’ as he calls himself, is a believer in even the language of just-war theory,” Walzer said. The opposition to these operations was still often couched in such language, though—and the Vice-President, J. D. Vance, a convert to Catholicism, invoked just-war theory as a defense after the Pope criticized American warmongering. I asked Walzer if he thought that American foreign policy would ever be guided by such principles again, or if we lived in a permanently hardened world. “We’ll get a sense of that in November,” he said. “I hope this is a temporary aberration in American political history, but I can’t tell you that that’s what it is.”

On a more personal level, I was curious if the war in Gaza had changed Walzer’s relationship with the left—if it had altered his sense of where he belonged. He told me that he felt “radically alienated from Dissent,” his old magazine, but not only on account of Gaza; he also disagreed with its coverage of Ukraine and a “whole range of things,” he said. “The old Dissent always believed in the necessity of fighting on two fronts: against the authoritarian left and against the capitalist right, always,” he said. “And what’s happened with the American left right now is that they’re fighting only on one front.”

But for him the real conflict was not with others, he said. It was internal. He mentioned an old friend who had written to him, early in 2024, to tell him that he was pulling his punches, that he was not writing about Israel’s war in Gaza the way he had written about Vietnam. Was the friend right? “Well, look,” Walzer replied. “I wrote about Vietnam years after the war was over.” He had condemned the siege of Gaza from the first days of the war, but the bombing, for him, was different. Israeli friends had kept assuring him that the I.D.F. was choosing its targets carefully, he said. “Maybe I was late in concluding that that wasn’t entirely true—that there was a policy for parts of Gaza to be made uninhabitable,” he said. A moment later, he added, “I was certainly slow to see that.”

I heard regret in his reply, but also, I thought, still, self-assurance—a belief that he had done his best, whatever his faults, and that he had remained faithful to his underlying convictions, which had not changed. At one point, he reminded me that his critique of pacifism was formed when he was ten years old. I had asked Walzer, in an earlier conversation, what he hoped his legacy might be, now that he was ninety. He waited a long time before answering, and I got the sense that what he told me was what he would have said years, even decades, before, and that it was what he would say to me for as long as he was able. “I wanted to make it possible for people to talk about war in a way that would facilitate ending wars,” he said. “I wanted people to talk about politics in a way that would encourage egalitarianism. And I wanted to help, in the ways available to me, which were small ways, to make Jewish life more secure.” ♦

In early August, election workers north of Spokane, Washington, went across the street to the post office to check for late mail-in ballots. It was three days after the primary and they were expecting no more than a few dozen.

Instead, their mailbox was brimming with envelopes — more than 500 of them, each a vote that wouldn’t count. The sight was “jarring,” said Auditor Lori Larsen, a Republican who oversees voting in Stevens County.

Across the region, other election officials were getting similar jolts. In the 11 predominately rural counties in eastern Washington, nearly 2,000 ballots came in late, compared to about 600 in 2022, the previous midterm primary.

In recent weeks, concerns about mail-in voting disruptions have focused almost entirely on President Donald Trump’s now-shelved plan to sharply restrict who can vote by mail.

Look up whether your ZIP code is affected by U.S. Postal Service changes that can slow mail delivery.

But a ProPublica data analysis shows that delays resulting from the U.S. Postal Service’s cost-cutting measures have endangered the validity of ballots cast in rural areas, not only in Washington, but across the country.

Over the past three years, as mail volume continued to plummet and annual deficits ballooned, the Postal Service started consolidating the processing of first-class mail at larger regional plants instead of at smaller hubs closer to local post offices. Then, in a little-noted switch that mostly took effect after the 2024 election, the agency cut the frequency of mail pickups in half at many post offices 50 miles or more from these processing plants, from twice daily to once.

The changes often added a day or more to delivery time and, most importantly for ballots, pushed back postmarking, which doesn’t happen until mail reaches the regional centers.

In this year’s primary, the effects of the cuts were clearest in five states that make detailed data on mail-in ballots available: Washington, California, Ohio, North Carolina and Oregon. All have or recently had grace periods allowing ballots to count if they come in after Election Day as long as they’re postmarked by the deadline.

In these states, the number of ballots rejected for arriving late soared in counties affected by the service changes compared to 2022, ProPublica’s analysis showed.

Rejection rates in counties where services were cut doubled or tripled from what they were four years ago, dwarfing any increases in counties where postal pickups remained as they were, our analysis showed. In urban Spokane, for example, the number of ballots rejected for being late actually dropped this year.

Across the five states, the delays have had the most substantial effect in areas that voted for Trump in 2024, ProPublica’s analysis shows. In Washington, Secretary of State Steve Hobbs, a Democrat, said he sees the service changes directed by Republican appointees leading the Postal Service as a way to undermine mail-in voting.

“I don’t think they realized how much it was going to affect their voter base,” he said, adding that Washington has “a lot of rural Republican voters.”

Postal Service leaders say such delays are less likely in the Nov. 3 general election because of “extraordinary measures” the agency puts into place for these votes, when there’s typically far more turnout. They have circulated a memo to the nation’s more than 30,000 post offices saying that for the 10 days before the election, managers should separate completed ballots from other first-class mail and deliver those intended for local election offices the day they come in.

At an Oct. 15 news conference, Postal Service leaders emphasized the agency’s longstanding recommendation that voters mail ballots back at least a week before their states’ deadlines, while defending the agency’s ability to fast-track ballots through the system.

“We work to identify and address issues quickly,” said Amber McReynolds, chair of the Postal Service board of governors, “so ballots can continue to move swiftly from election offices to voters and from voters back to election offices.”

Election consultants, researchers and former Postal Service officials told ProPublica that the guidance for the upcoming vote gave more detailed directions on how to expedite ballots. They said they hoped the agency’s special measures would limit delays, but some said mistakes were likely.

“I don’t think we can say with any kind of conviction that there will be no ballots rejected,” said Tammy Patrick, chief programs officer of the National Association of Election Officials. “It isn’t a question of ‘if,’ but a question of ‘how many.'”

A pilot program instituting the delivery changes in 18 regions before the 2024 election ran into problems. An audit by the Postal Service’s inspector general found that agency staff in these areas sometimes didn’t handle ballots correctly, mainly because instructions weren’t clearly communicated. (A Postal Service spokesperson said “learnings from the pilot” have been applied as the program has expanded.)

After this year’s primary, reports by media outlets noted that mail-in ballot rejection rates were higher than in the past, but ProPublica’s analysis shows these increases were directly connected to Postal Service changes. Research published in September by the Brookings Institution also found this connection, projecting that if the pattern held in the general election, tens of thousands of mail-in ballots could be rejected.

At least seven closely contested congressional districts — including Washington’s 3rd, California’s 22nd and North Carolina’s 11th — are in areas that were affected by service changes in the primary, ProPublica found.

Elena Patel, who conducted the Brookings analysis, said service changes may still imperil mail-in votes in the general election.

“I think there remain risks to ballots,” she said, adding that the Postal Service’s plan for the general election “depends on flawless execution.”

Mail-in voting has become more popular over the last decade, with many states broadening access to it during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Nearly 1 in 3 Americans voted this way in 2022 and 2024. Trump himself frequently votes by mail, and in recent weeks has started to encourage his supporters to do so as well, though he has claimed without evidence that the process is rife with fraud.

The Postal Service has mostly ensured the timely delivery of hundreds of millions of ballots, even as mail volume overall has dropped 50% from its high in 2006.

When Louis DeJoy was appointed postmaster general in spring 2020, he inherited an $81 billion operating deficit and rapidly took steps such as eliminating overtime to reduce costs, under pressure from Trump and others in his administration. (DeJoy did not respond to questions from ProPublica.)

The NAACP sued that August, alleging the measures could affect mail-in votes in that year’s general election. Courts ordered DeJoy to reverse some of the cuts and the Postal Service agreed to implement special procedures to ensure ballots weren’t delayed. The Postal Service has continued following those procedures since.

Still, DeJoy went forward with a 10-year restructuring plan designed to save the agency billions. It ended evening pickups from some post offices, often in rural areas. Trucks serving these places travel long distances and at times were mostly empty. This part of the plan — called regional transportation optimization, or RTO — was projected to eventually save the Postal Service $4 billion a year.

The agency announced the national rollout of the RTO plan in 2024, but DeJoy agreed to pause it leading up to that year’s November election, leaving only about 4,000 ZIP codes in the pilot project affected by the service changes.

A pilot program that reduced mail pickups to save money was paused in the lead-up to the 2024 election. The rollout has since resumed, and the changes have been made in five times as many ZIP codes for the 2026 midterms.

In January 2025, the Postal Regulatory Commission, which oversees the Postal Service, warned that moving forward with the plan would be disruptive to rural communities and might not save as much money as expected. The Postal Service rolled out the reductions anyway, extending the changes to almost 20,000 ZIP codes to date. Officials said they expect the system to be fully implemented within two years.

This January, the agency put out a statement saying the transportation cuts would mean it took longer for mail, including ballots, to be postmarked and delivered. But the Postal Service didn’t specify which voters would be affected by the slowdowns and which wouldn’t.

Though the delay from RTO is often only a day, mail can be delayed longer if it’s deposited in a mailbox after the daily pickup time, or just before Sunday or a holiday.

A Postal Service spokesperson said the agency told election officials that RTO would change schedules and reemphasized the need for voters to get their ballots in the mail at least a week ahead of time. Election officials in Washington said the Postal Service wasn’t direct enough about the fact that the changes would lead to delays in delivering and postmarking ballots.

“What they said was, ‘We are going to change the way we do postmarks,'” said Hobbs, Washington’s secretary of state. “That was it.”

Patel, the Brookings analyst and a former Postal Regulatory Commission employee, said that more direct communication to voters could have helped change their behavior. “That’s the part that I think has not been super transparent about the whole process,” she said.

Many of the areas that saw a spike in late ballots were in Western states, which were the earliest and most enthusiastic adopters of expanded mail-in voting.

In 2004, Washington became the second state to start conducting all-mail elections, in which every eligible voter automatically receives a mail-in ballot. Most residents vote by putting their ballots in drop boxes. Still, in 2024, about 33% put them in the mail.

In the 30 mostly rural Washington counties most affected by RTO, the percentage of ballots postmarked late more than tripled compared to the 2022 primary, ProPublica found.

If the frequency of mail pickups has been reduced in your area, mail may take longer to send and receive, and the date on the postmark could be later than the day you dropped it off, which may matter for mail like ballots that is subject to a deadline.

In Garfield County, in southeastern Washington, 19 ballots — nearly 1 in 10 cast — had late postmarks this year. In 2022, only a single ballot was postmarked late. In Pend Oreille County, in northeast Washington along the Idaho panhandle, 6% of ballots were rejected for arriving late, compared to 1% in the 2022 primary.

“It was pretty awful telling people we couldn’t count their votes,” said Marianne Nichols, Pend Oreille’s longtime auditor. “They were quite upset because they felt like, ‘Why? This was in the mailbox on election day, the post office picked it up and had it.’ They felt like it should have counted.”

In Stevens County, the percentage of ballots rejected for having a late postmark jumped to 8%, from 1% in 2022.

Steve Hutkins, a retired professor who analyzes the effect of Postal Service changes on Savethepostoffice.com, also found that places in Washington with RTO had more ballot rejections during local elections earlier this year for school board and other measures. The patterns “set off the alarm bells,” he said.

Stuart Holmes, the state’s director of elections, said he reached out to Postal Service officials to find out what had happened, but got few answers and no specifics on delays related to RTO, which were a “total surprise,” he said.

Election officials in California and Oregon also reported thousands more ballots rejected for arriving late during their primaries. Counties affected by RTO, including Kern County, California, had substantially higher rates of ballots rejected for mail-related reasons, ProPublica found.

Kern County’s assistant registrar of voters, Laura Cantu, said election officials are urging rural voters to use drop boxes to cast their ballots in the general election.

The expansion of RTO had a similar effect in North Carolina and Ohio, where the rates of late ballots were about twice as high in counties affected by the restructuring as rates in counties that weren’t. Since 2022, these states have also eliminated grace periods in which ballots postmarked by Election Day could count even if they arrived later. Voters unused to the tighter deadlines also could have contributed to an increase in late ballots.

ProPublica didn’t find any association between RTO and higher rates of late ballots in Wisconsin, even though clerks reported delays involving mail-in ballots cast in its state election, held this spring. (Other Postal Service changes may have contributed to the delays.)

Wisconsin’s data does not distinguish ballots sent by mail from those cast in drop boxes or in person, making it impossible to definitively measure rejection rates just for ballots returned by mail. Election researchers said Wisconsin’s deadline for mail ballots, which must come into election offices by 8 p.m. on Election Day, also probably had an effect.

“If you know that you don’t have a grace period, it probably affects voter behavior,” said Patel, the Brookings researcher.

Recent Postal Service audits have found that RTO-related changes had saved the agency about $2.8 billion by the end of fiscal year 2025 — less money than expected — and are slowing down all first-class mail, not just ballots. Residents of rural areas are waiting longer for everything from prescriptions to bills, a report published in September by the Postal Service’s inspector general said.

A Postal Service spokesperson still defended the plan, both for financial reasons and to adjust to the changing marketplace. “These savings are both significant and necessary,” he said.

In the lead-up to Nov. 3, the agency’s plan is to route ballots meant for local election offices to a nearby postal facility or directly to their destination. They shouldn’t go to regional plants, which the Postal Service says should minimize delays.

Larsen, the Stevens County auditor, is encouraging Washington voters to deposit their ballots in drop boxes rather than relying on the mail. Since the primary, she’s increased the number of red, white and blue drop boxes that sit outside government buildings from seven to 12. Near each box, there’s a large sign encouraging people to take selfies when they vote and send the photos to Larsen.

In prior years, Larsen said, she avoided telling voters how or when to vote, concerned they might regret choices they made if critical information later emerged about the candidates. That’s no longer uppermost in her mind.

“Now we are more concerned that we get your ballot,” she said.

But a ProPublica data analysis shows that delays resulting from the U.S. Postal Service’s cost-cutting measures have endangered the validity of ballots cast in rural areas, not only in Washington, but across the country.

Over the past three years, as mail volume continued to plummet and annual deficits ballooned, the Postal Service started consolidating the processing of first-class mail at larger regional plants instead of at smaller hubs closer to local post offices. Then, in a little-noted switch that mostly took effect after the 2024 election, the agency cut the frequency of mail pickups in half at many post offices 50 miles or more from these processing plants, from twice daily to once.

The changes often added a day or more to delivery time and, most importantly for ballots, pushed back postmarking, which doesn’t happen until mail reaches the regional centers.

In this year’s primary, the effects of the cuts were clearest in five states that make detailed data on mail-in ballots available: Washington, California, Ohio, North Carolina and Oregon. All have or recently had grace periods allowing ballots to count if they come in after Election Day as long as they’re postmarked by the deadline.

In these states, the number of ballots rejected for arriving late soared in counties affected by the service changes compared to 2022, ProPublica’s analysis showed.

Rejection rates in counties where services were cut doubled or tripled from what they were four years ago, dwarfing any increases in counties where postal pickups remained as they were, our analysis showed. In urban Spokane, for example, the number of ballots rejected for being late actually dropped this year.

Across the five states, the delays have had the most substantial effect in areas that voted for Trump in 2024, ProPublica’s analysis shows. In Washington, Secretary of State Steve Hobbs, a Democrat, said he sees the service changes directed by Republican appointees leading the Postal Service as a way to undermine mail-in voting.

“I don’t think they realized how much it was going to affect their voter base,” he said, adding that Washington has “a lot of rural Republican voters.”

Postal Service leaders say such delays are less likely in the Nov. 3 general election because of “extraordinary measures” the agency puts into place for these votes, when there’s typically far more turnout. They have circulated a memo to the nation’s more than 30,000 post offices saying that for the 10 days before the election, managers should separate completed ballots from other first-class mail and deliver those intended for local election offices the day they come in.

At an Oct. 15 news conference, Postal Service leaders emphasized the agency’s longstanding recommendation that voters mail ballots back at least a week before their states’ deadlines, while defending the agency’s ability to fast-track ballots through the system.

“We work to identify and address issues quickly,” said Amber McReynolds, chair of the Postal Service board of governors, “so ballots can continue to move swiftly from election offices to voters and from voters back to election offices.”

Election consultants, researchers and former Postal Service officials told ProPublica that the guidance for the upcoming vote gave more detailed directions on how to expedite ballots. They said they hoped the agency’s special measures would limit delays, but some said mistakes were likely.

“I don’t think we can say with any kind of conviction that there will be no ballots rejected,” said Tammy Patrick, chief programs officer of the National Association of Election Officials. “It isn’t a question of ‘if,’ but a question of ‘how many.'”

A pilot program instituting the delivery changes in 18 regions before the 2024 election ran into problems. An audit by the Postal Service’s inspector general found that agency staff in these areas sometimes didn’t handle ballots correctly, mainly because instructions weren’t clearly communicated. (A Postal Service spokesperson said “learnings from the pilot” have been applied as the program has expanded.)

After this year’s primary, reports by media outlets noted that mail-in ballot rejection rates were higher than in the past, but ProPublica’s analysis shows these increases were directly connected to Postal Service changes. Research published in September by the Brookings Institution also found this connection, projecting that if the pattern held in the general election, tens of thousands of mail-in ballots could be rejected.

At least seven closely contested congressional districts — including Washington’s 3rd, California’s 22nd and North Carolina’s 11th — are in areas that were affected by service changes in the primary, ProPublica found.

Elena Patel, who conducted the Brookings analysis, said service changes may still imperil mail-in votes in the general election.

“I think there remain risks to ballots,” she said, adding that the Postal Service’s plan for the general election “depends on flawless execution.”

Mail-in voting has become more popular over the last decade, with many states broadening access to it during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Nearly 1 in 3 Americans voted this way in 2022 and 2024. Trump himself frequently votes by mail, and in recent weeks has started to encourage his supporters to do so as well, though he has claimed without evidence that the process is rife with fraud.

The Postal Service has mostly ensured the timely delivery of hundreds of millions of ballots, even as mail volume overall has dropped 50% from its high in 2006.

When Louis DeJoy was appointed postmaster general in spring 2020, he inherited an $81 billion operating deficit and rapidly took steps such as eliminating overtime to reduce costs, under pressure from Trump and others in his administration. (DeJoy did not respond to questions from ProPublica.)

The NAACP sued that August, alleging the measures could affect mail-in votes in that year’s general election. Courts ordered DeJoy to reverse some of the cuts and the Postal Service agreed to implement special procedures to ensure ballots weren’t delayed. The Postal Service has continued following those procedures since.

Still, DeJoy went forward with a 10-year restructuring plan designed to save the agency billions. It ended evening pickups from some post offices, often in rural areas. Trucks serving these places travel long distances and at times were mostly empty. This part of the plan — called regional transportation optimization, or RTO — was projected to eventually save the Postal Service $4 billion a year.

The agency announced the national rollout of the RTO plan in 2024, but DeJoy agreed to pause it leading up to that year’s November election, leaving only about 4,000 ZIP codes in the pilot project affected by the service changes.

In January 2025, the Postal Regulatory Commission, which oversees the Postal Service, warned that moving forward with the plan would be disruptive to rural communities and might not save as much money as expected. The Postal Service rolled out the reductions anyway, extending the changes to almost 20,000 ZIP codes to date. Officials said they expect the system to be fully implemented within two years.

This January, the agency put out a statement saying the transportation cuts would mean it took longer for mail, including ballots, to be postmarked and delivered. But the Postal Service didn’t specify which voters would be affected by the slowdowns and which wouldn’t.

Though the delay from RTO is often only a day, mail can be delayed longer if it’s deposited in a mailbox after the daily pickup time, or just before Sunday or a holiday.

A Postal Service spokesperson said the agency told election officials that RTO would change schedules and reemphasized the need for voters to get their ballots in the mail at least a week ahead of time. Election officials in Washington said the Postal Service wasn’t direct enough about the fact that the changes would lead to delays in delivering and postmarking ballots.

“What they said was, ‘We are going to change the way we do postmarks,'” said Hobbs, Washington’s secretary of state. “That was it.”

Patel, the Brookings analyst and a former Postal Regulatory Commission employee, said that more direct communication to voters could have helped change their behavior. “That’s the part that I think has not been super transparent about the whole process,” she said.

Many of the areas that saw a spike in late ballots were in Western states, which were the earliest and most enthusiastic adopters of expanded mail-in voting.

In 2004, Washington became the second state to start conducting all-mail elections, in which every eligible voter automatically receives a mail-in ballot. Most residents vote by putting their ballots in drop boxes. Still, in 2024, about 33% put them in the mail.

In the 30 mostly rural Washington counties most affected by RTO, the percentage of ballots postmarked late more than tripled compared to the 2022 primary, ProPublica found.

In Garfield County, in southeastern Washington, 19 ballots — nearly 1 in 10 cast — had late postmarks this year. In 2022, only a single ballot was postmarked late. In Pend Oreille County, in northeast Washington along the Idaho panhandle, 6% of ballots were rejected for arriving late, compared to 1% in the 2022 primary.

“It was pretty awful telling people we couldn’t count their votes,” said Marianne Nichols, Pend Oreille’s longtime auditor. “They were quite upset because they felt like, ‘Why? This was in the mailbox on election day, the post office picked it up and had it.’ They felt like it should have counted.”

In Stevens County, the percentage of ballots rejected for having a late postmark jumped to 8%, from 1% in 2022.

Steve Hutkins, a retired professor who analyzes the effect of Postal Service changes on Savethepostoffice.com, also found that places in Washington with RTO had more ballot rejections during local elections earlier this year for school board and other measures. The patterns “set off the alarm bells,” he said.

Stuart Holmes, the state’s director of elections, said he reached out to Postal Service officials to find out what had happened, but got few answers and no specifics on delays related to RTO, which were a “total surprise,” he said.

Election officials in California and Oregon also reported thousands more ballots rejected for arriving late during their primaries. Counties affected by RTO, including Kern County, California, had substantially higher rates of ballots rejected for mail-related reasons, ProPublica found.

Kern County’s assistant registrar of voters, Laura Cantu, said election officials are urging rural voters to use drop boxes to cast their ballots in the general election.

The expansion of RTO had a similar effect in North Carolina and Ohio, where the rates of late ballots were about twice as high in counties affected by the restructuring as rates in counties that weren’t. Since 2022, these states have also eliminated grace periods in which ballots postmarked by Election Day could count even if they arrived later. Voters unused to the tighter deadlines also could have contributed to an increase in late ballots.

ProPublica didn’t find any association between RTO and higher rates of late ballots in Wisconsin, even though clerks reported delays involving mail-in ballots cast in its state election, held this spring. (Other Postal Service changes may have contributed to the delays.)

Wisconsin’s data does not distinguish ballots sent by mail from those cast in drop boxes or in person, making it impossible to definitively measure rejection rates just for ballots returned by mail. Election researchers said Wisconsin’s deadline for mail ballots, which must come into election offices by 8 p.m. on Election Day, also probably had an effect.

“If you know that you don’t have a grace period, it probably affects voter behavior,” said Patel, the Brookings researcher.

Recent Postal Service audits have found that RTO-related changes had saved the agency about $2.8 billion by the end of fiscal year 2025 — less money than expected — and are slowing down all first-class mail, not just ballots. Residents of rural areas are waiting longer for everything from prescriptions to bills, a report published in September by the Postal Service’s inspector general said.

A Postal Service spokesperson still defended the plan, both for financial reasons and to adjust to the changing marketplace. “These savings are both significant and necessary,” he said.

In the lead-up to Nov. 3, the agency’s plan is to route ballots meant for local election offices to a nearby postal facility or directly to their destination. They shouldn’t go to regional plants, which the Postal Service says should minimize delays.

Larsen, the Stevens County auditor, is encouraging Washington voters to deposit their ballots in drop boxes rather than relying on the mail. Since the primary, she’s increased the number of red, white and blue drop boxes that sit outside government buildings from seven to 12. Near each box, there’s a large sign encouraging people to take selfies when they vote and send the photos to Larsen.

In prior years, Larsen said, she avoided telling voters how or when to vote, concerned they might regret choices they made if critical information later emerged about the candidates. That’s no longer uppermost in her mind.

“Now we are more concerned that we get your ballot,” she said.
