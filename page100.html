Israelis have changed. Will they change their leadership?

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Conflict in the Middle East has been escalating. These stories provide context for current developments and the history that led up to them.

A road sign stands in front of a large billboard featuring Israel's Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu (right) of the Likud party and his main center-right opponent, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, in Ramat Gan, Israel. Israel heads to the polls on Oct. 27. Jack Guez/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

TEL AVIV, Israel — Israelis are convinced that elections scheduled for Tuesday are the most pivotal in their nation's history.

Will they vote out Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu — and his coalition of far-right, ultranationalist and religious political parties — because he failed to prevent the Hamas attacks on Oct. 7, 2023, the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust?

Or will they reelect him to continue his campaign of retaliation, waging wars that aim to reshape the Middle East? His main challenger, former military chief of staff Gadi Eisenkot, leads a center-right opposition promising more diplomacy and less extremism.

It is Israel's first vote after these years of battle and bloodshed. Israelis are so concerned about their country's future that tens of thousands of expats are expected to fly back to cast ballots, since Israel does not allow absentee voting.

"We almost could have been destroyed on Oct. 7," said Israeli lawyer Shoula Romano-Horing, who flew in from Kansas City, Mo., to vote. "This is why I'm voting for Netanyahu, to make Israel so secure and so safe."

In the opposite corner are those Israelis distressed by the global backlash to the wars Netanyahu's government has waged.

"The actions of this government cause Israelis in general and Jews in general to be tagged as, you know, mass killers," said Yochai Ben-Haim, who works in renewable energy in California and is flying home to Israel for 36 hours just to vote. "This government is led by extremists that do not represent who we are, and we need to change it."

In conversations with dozens of Israelis over the last few weeks, a portrait has emerged of who Israelis are today.

A man stands in a destroyed building in the Jabalia camp for Palestinian refugees in the northern Gaza Strip on Oct. 20, 2026. Bashar Taleb/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

They are a people defiant, but also weary after three years of incessant war. They are enraged at their enemies, but also at their leaders.

Many are impervious to the pain of their Palestinian neighbors, but traumatized from the war they have wrought. They are resistant to Palestinian independence, but also revolted by the Israeli settlers pushing Palestinians off their land.

They are fed up with the world's rebuke over the Gaza war, but also fearful of their growing global isolation. They are a people in a tug-of-war with themselves.

"Israelis want to be feared and to be loved," said Israeli author Micah Goodman. "The problem is that you have two reputations you need to protect, but you can't."

Meir and Kati Zohar stand together at the memorial marker dedicated to their daughter, Bar Zohar, at the Nova festival memorial site in southern Israel, on Sept. 24, 2026. Their daughter was among the victims killed on Oct. 7, 2023. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

As elections near, Israelis are learning more about what led up to Oct. 7, 2023.

Throughout that year, as Israelis poured into the streets to protest Netanyahu's far-right government and its plan to weaken the judiciary, Netanyahu's security chiefs warned that regional enemies could take advantage of the turmoil to attack.

Behind the scenes, Israel received direct warnings that Hamas was planning something big. Israel and Hamas had been holding secret talks for more than a year, Palestinian mediator Sufian Abu Zaida said. When the talks stalled, Hamas' leader in Gaza had Abu Zaida relay a message threatening an "earthquake."

What kind of earthquake was unclear, said Abu Zaida, but he insists he relayed the warning to Israeli intelligence. The leader of the United Arab Emirates and Egypt's intelligence chief relayed similar warnings, according to reporting by NPR and other media outlets. "All what happened in 7 of October is needless," Abu Zaida said.

Burnt out vehicles in Ashkelon, southern Israel, are pictured following a rocket attack from the Gaza Strip on Oct. 7, 2023. Ahmad Gharabli/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Nearly 1,200 people were killed when Hamas attacked army bases, residential communities and an outdoor music rave — and 251 people were taken hostage back to Gaza. Netanyahu denies he was warned and has never admitted any kind of responsibility for the security failure.

"Within days, his PR people were trying to shape the public narrative about events," said Amos Harel, a defense analyst for the Israeli newspaper Haaretz and author of 06:29 – Anatomy of a Failure, which details the Israeli failures to prevent the Oct. 7 attack. (The attack began that day at 6:29 a.m.) Netanyahu's strategy, he said, was "to shift the blame" to the security services and the military.

Amos Harel, military and defense analyst for the Israeli daily Haaretz, is pictured inside the newspaper's archival library in Tel Aviv. Harel has extensively reported on Israeli defense strategy, military operations, and Middle East security for decades. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

For much of the Gaza war, Israeli protesters flooded the streets demanding that Netanyahu strike a deal with Hamas to release Israeli hostages and end the fighting. It would take two years, Israel's longest ever war, before a hostage deal was reached. Netanyahu said he was seeking the total defeat of Hamas, though former officials who worked with Netanyahu allege he prolonged the war for political reasons — a charge that Netanyahu has denied.

"I'm absolutely convinced that this is what happened," Harel said. "He felt that if this would move on too fast, then there will be more of a significant public demand for a national commission of inquiry."

There is a great demand among Israelis for an investigation into the attack, like the 9/11 Commission in the United States. Netanyahu has blocked one in Israel.

So Israelis grappling with Oct. 7 go to the spot of the day's deadliest attack, where hundreds of people were killed when Hamas attacked the Nova music festival. The memorial there is a dusty field of plaques and thin poles bearing the pictures of smiling young people who were partying when they came under attack.

Meir and Kati Zohar hold hands as they pose for a portrait beneath a tree at the Nova music festival memorial site in southern Israel on Sept. 24, 2026. Their daughter, Bar Zohar, was killed during the Hamas-led assault on the festival on Oct. 7, 2023. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

Meir Zohar points to documentary photographs at the Nova festival memorial site. The center photograph shows his wounded daughter, Bar Zohar, receiving emergency treatment in the final minutes of her life on Oct. 7, 2023. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

A memorial marker displaying the photo and biography of Bar Zohar stands among hundreds of tributes at the Nova music festival site on Sept. 24, 2026. Hundreds were killed at the site during the Hamas-led attack on Oct. 7, 2023. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

Meir and Kati Zohar display matching memorial tattoos featuring the birth year and zodiac sign of their daughter, Bar Zohar. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

Israeli soldiers visit a memorial commemorating security personnel who died defending those who came under attack at the Nova festival site. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

Kati and Meir Zohar, whose 23-year-old daughter Bar was killed there, helped build the memorial with other families. The Zohars say they received no government funding for the effort and no phone call from Netanyahu expressing his condolences.

She believes Netanyahu bears the responsibility for what happened to her daughter on Oct. 7 and, like other Israelis, hopes Netanyahu faces a reckoning for it on election day.

"It's a hope that something will change," Zohar said. "I hope that someone will come and take his place and be the one who [is] trying to keep this country together."

The Oct. 7 attack left Israelis in a state of shock.

"I think the general line of thinking — not only among decision-makers, not only among ministers of the government, but also among commanders in the field — was that something biblical has happened to us and that something biblical needed to happen in return," Harel said.

What happened in return was an Israeli military onslaught that has killed more than 74,000 Palestinians and left most of Gaza destroyed. Today, Israel is fighting back against charges of genocide. Netanyahu faces an international arrest warrant for alleged war crimes. Israeli soldiers who fought in the war risk arrest in some countries.

"I'm proud of what I did and I know what I did," said Lior, 34, who was one of those soldiers. "I don't mind to be in front of a judge."

Lior, 34, an Israeli reservist soldier who commanded a sniper unit during wars in Gaza and Lebanon, poses for a portrait at a farm in Udim, Israel where he plans to launch a horse therapy program for veterans with post traumatic stress disorder. Maya Levin for NPR hide caption

Lior spoke on the condition that his last name be withheld to protect his family overseas from harassment because of the nature of his role: he commanded an Israeli sniper unit in Gaza and Lebanon, serving over 500 days in uniform.

" When you're inside Gaza, your life is under danger 24/7. There is shooting, there is booming… sometimes you hear about Hezbollah, Hamas, what they are doing. So your body, your mind is under survival tension 24/7," Lior said.

He is among the nearly 19,000 Israeli soldiers who have been newly diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder or related illnesses. It touches every part of his life: He imagines snipers on the streets. He sometimes breaks into tears riding the train. He struggles in his sex life. He and his fiancé broke up.

Lior said he often returns to one memory from Gaza: his order to shoot and kill a man he described as a Hamas terrorist, standing next to his child in a place where he shouldn't have been.

"Unfortunately, yeah, the kid saw his father dying in front of him," Lior said.

He does not regret any of his decisions to open fire.

"To be honest, in my opinion, I don't have any mercy of people in Gaza. I know what's going on there. I know of the poverty, I know about the lack of water, electricity. I don't have any mercy on them," Lior said. "When you see things with your own eyes and you remember what happened on the 7th of October, I'm not forgiving for that."

"You know, my Jewish identity (is) saying that I'm sure there is, but…I don't see it that way. That's what I told you about the kid," Lior said. "If he have the chance, he will kill me."

He is among the nearly 300,000 young Israeli military veterans ordered to leave their civilian lives and serve as reservist soldiers after Oct. 7.

Anat Saragusti, an Israeli journalist who reported from Gaza in the 1990s and early 2000s for Israeli Channel 12 TV, poses for a portrait in her home in Tel Aviv. Maya Levin for NPR hide caption

More than 67 percent of Israelis in a July 2025 poll said they were not very troubled about the suffering of people in Gaza. More than 60 percent of Israelis in an August 2025 poll said they believed there were no innocent people in Gaza. That view has been reinforced by mainstream Israeli media.

"There are no uninvolved civilians in Gaza," said Israeli reporter Almog Boker early last year on leading news network Channel 12.

This was not always the way Israeli media covered Palestinians in Gaza. Anat Saragusti, a veteran journalist, used to report from Gaza in the 1990s and early 2000s for Channel 12.

"I tried to cover Gaza as if I was living in a foreign country, to show the complexity of life, to show different stories," Saragusti said from her Tel Aviv apartment. "To show the Israeli audience that Gaza is not what they thought and think now, you know, a black hole of terrorists."

Twenty years ago, after Hamas took an Israeli soldier captive in Gaza, Israel practically sealed Gaza shut to Israeli journalists and to all Israelis. Then, Hamas took control of Gaza, Israel imposed a blockade, there were years of Hamas rocket fire into Israel and Israeli retaliation.

Tents sheltering people displaced by war stand between the rubble of destroyed buildings in the Jabalia camp for Palestinian refugees in the northern Gaza Strip on Oct. 20, 2026. Bashar Taleb/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Since Oct. 7, most Israeli media chose not to show what Palestinian journalists were reporting day after day from Gaza: the tens of thousands of men, women and children killed; the forced displacement; the starvation; the misery of trying to survive.

"The majority of the Israeli journalists didn't do one story, you know, not even one story was done on Gaza… to see whether what these Palestinian journalists are claiming is true," said Saragusti. "They were afraid of the pushback by the audience if they dare say something about Gaza."

Amos Harel, defense analyst for the Israeli daily Haaretz, stands inside the newspaper's archival library in Tel Aviv. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

Harel, who writes a regular column for the Haaretz newspaper, says the nation's continuous war trauma also afflicted Israeli media.

" We were so busy with our pain and our suffering and worrying about the fate of the hostages — hundreds of hostages were held at that time by Hamas — that we didn't focus on what we did to the other side," he said.

Rubble and the remains of demolished Palestinian homes and agricultural structures lie scattered across a hillside in Masafer Yatta, in the Israeli-occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

There is one place where even veterans of Israel's military and security establishment say what Palestinians are enduring has gone too far: the Israeli-occupied West Bank.

Since Oct. 7, Israeli settler violence toward Palestinians has skyrocketed. Settlers have seized more and more land there, with the support of the military and Netanyahu's pro-settler government, which has approved more than 100 new Jewish settlements in the West Bank, a record number.

Retired Israeli defense and government officials toured Palestinian communities affected by Israeli settler violence in the occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. "This is evil," said Ari Shuali (second from left), a former senior Israeli intelligence official. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

A delegation of retired Israeli military and government officials meet with Eid Salem (right), a local community leader, in the Palestinian village of Umm al-Kheir in the Israeli-occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

Rubble and concrete debris from the demolished home of Yaser Abu Sabha lie scattered beside a water tank trailer in Masafer Yatta, in the Israeli-occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

"We are making a historic revolution," cabinet minister Bezalel Smotrich said this July as he announced the 104th new settlement approved by Netanyahu's current government. He said it "would prevent the establishment of a terror state in the heart of Israel."

A retired Israeli military general, Yaakov "Mendy" Or, leads groups of former Israeli military and security officials to Palestinian communities in the West Bank to show them the radical changes that have taken place over the last three years.

Maj. Gen. (Ret.) Yaakov "Mendy" Or poses for a portrait during a visit to Masafer Yatta in the occupied West Bank on Sept. 24, 2026. He leads independent groups of retired Israeli military, defense, and government officials visiting Palestinian areas affected by Israeli settler violence. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

A remote Palestinian pastoral hamlet with stone dwellings and shelters nestled into the arid hills of Masafer Yatta, in the occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

On a recent tour, he brought them to Umm al-Kheir, a Palestinian village surrounded by an expanse of desert hills, where Israeli settlers have set up trailer homes right outside a Palestinian family's property.

Palestinian activist Eid Hatlin said barbed wire in the yard was placed by settlers. The settlers have blocked the family's outdoor bathroom and the Israeli army has blocked the path to their sheep, he said.

An Israeli settler drives an all-terrain vehicle along Route 317 in the South Hebron Hills, near Masafer Yatta in the occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

Retired Israeli military and government officials tour the Masafer Yatta region of the Israeli-occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

An Israeli flag erected by settlers is seen through the window of the home of Ikhlas al-Hathaleen in the Palestinian village of Umm al-Kheir, in the Masafer Yatta region of the Israeli-occupied West Bank, on Sept. 24, 2026. Israeli military closure orders have restricted the Palestinian family from accessing their courtyard toilet and sheep pen. Erik Marmor for NPR hide caption

"This is nasty. This is evil," said Ari Shuali, a former senior Israeli intelligence official who was part of the visiting group. "I don't believe that we as a people became such kind of unhuman behavior. I'm an Israeli. I'm a proud Israeli and I'm ashamed to be an Israeli [at] the same time."

It's one reason why some Israelis — like him — want to vote Netanyahu out.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu stands amid security personnel as he meets with voters at a mall in Ramle, Israel on Oct. 19, 2026. Ilia Yefimovich/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Netanyahu has downplayed Israeli settler violence in the West Bank and his allies are campaigning on a pledge to expel Palestinians — what they call encouraging emigration.

Netanyahu is campaigning on what he's done to fight back against external threats — with the wars in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran as examples of how he's making Israel safer since Oct. 7.

"On that darkest of days, our enemies expected us to collapse. Well, we didn't collapse. Instead, we rose and fought like lions!" Netanyahu said at the United Nations last month.

Over the last three years, Israel has killed the leaders of Hamas, Hezbollah and Iran. It has sent soldiers to capture new territory in the West Bank, Gaza, Lebanon and Syria. It has fought Houthis in Yemen and militias in Iraq. Netanyahu vows to destroy Iran's nuclear capabilities if he is reelected.

"We're entangled in endless wars and this was done on purpose," Harel said. "It's not a bug, it's a feature. The whole idea is for Israel to keep fighting, because endless wars serve Netanyahu to maintain… a constant state of emergency."

Netanyahu has called them wars of "no choice," alongside a "war for truth" waged in international media and social media.

Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party and candidate for prime minister, speaks during an election campaign conference. GIl Cohen-Magen/picture alliance via Getty Images hide caption

At a recent stump speech, Gadi Eisenkot took the stage. He is short with cropped white hair and he was wearing all black. He was speaking to a crowd in Kiryat Gat, a city near the Gaza border — historically a conservative Netanyahu stronghold.

Eisenkot is the former military chief of staff, appointed by Netanyahu a decade ago, who came back into the public spotlight in the days after Oct. 7 when he joined Netanyahu's war cabinet. Soon after, Eisenkot's son was killed as a soldier in the Gaza war. The very next day, his nephew, too, was killed there.

Eisenkot told the crowd that he began to disagree with Netanyahu's prosecution of the war three months in. "I saw a deep dysfunction in the decision-making process," Eisenkot said. "I saw that political and personal considerations were put above everything else."

He has pledged, if he wins, to launch an official national investigation into the security failure of Oct. 7 — something Netanyahu has refused to do.

"He's straight," said Yair Amar, a longtime Netanyahu voter, who said he has switched allegiances to Eisenkot.

"Many Israelis wish for a saner future," said Harel, the Haaretz columnist. "For somebody who perhaps is not as charismatic and doesn't look the part of an American senator, but is one of us and is seen as somebody who sacrificed a lot."

Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, visits the Western Wall in the Old City of Jerusalem on Oct. 22, 2026. Eisenkot is a retired chief of staff of the Israeli military. Erik Marmor/Getty Images Europe hide caption

The race is tight. Most polls show Eisenkot's camp ahead of Netanyahu's, but those same polls show neither has a clear path to victory. To win, parties must form a coalition to control a majority in Israel's parliament. The election could tip on a small amount of votes or it could end in stalemate, forcing a repeat election next year. If that were to happen, Netanyahu would stay in power with full authority to make fateful decisions, like going to war. He strongly suggests he wants to strike Iran again.

If Netanyahu loses, Eisenkot vows a different approach. He has spoken of cracking down on settler expansion and violence against Palestinians in the West Bank and of reaching diplomatic agreements in the wars Netanyahu has kept open, but cautioned he would not withdraw troops from Lebanon and Gaza anytime soon.

"Truth be told," Eisenkot told the crowd, "it will take a lot of time."

On the biggest question of all — how to solve Israel's conflict with the Palestinians — Eisenkot boasted that he "never said the solution was two states for two peoples." Meaning the creation of a Palestinian state alongside Israel — the vision that the U.S. and the majority of the world have advocated for decades. Eisenkot thinks Israel should preserve possibilities for a solution in the future. After Oct. 7, Israelis do not want to discuss a Palestinian state.

"It's not something that the Israeli public can swallow at this time," Harel said. "More and more people suspect that the Arabs, the Palestinians, are out to kill us and that the world is out to get us. And there's less support for any kind of concessions because people keep bringing up Oct. 7."

This is not a race of conservatives versus liberals, right versus left. Eisenkot is leading a group of politicians who are largely right-wing, focused on matters of security and campaigning to enlist religious ultra-Orthodox Jews to the military, which Netanyahu has resisted in order to placate his ultra-Orthodox political allies.

"I'm trusting him on the military level, but is it enough?" said Israeli high school theater teacher Anat Ben-Ari, who came to hear Eisenkot speak. She was almost nervous voicing an unpopular opinion out loud: she wants him to forge peace agreements.

"The only way to end war — all the history says that — is by agreements, is by people talking to people, even enemies. Mostly enemies," Ben-Ari said. "I think we should also pay the price for peace in order for less people to die here and not always live on our swords."

Lior, the soldier, is resigned to a future of war.

"There will always be an enemy, so I think we will always be in a war. We [will] always live on our sword," Lior said. "It will always be."

A worker uses a loupe to examine ballots at a printing factory, ahead of the Israeli legislative elections in Kiryat Gat, southern Israel, on Oct. 8, 2026. Jack Guez/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Israeli voters now consider two different visions for their country. Should Israel fight to regain the world's acceptance or to increase their neighbors' deterrence?

Israelis yearn for both says author Micah Goodman, who has written about the Israeli-Palestinian conflict and previously served as an unofficial advisor to Israeli prime ministers.

"In the Middle East, you wanna have a certain kind of reputation. You want to be seen as unpredictable and a little crazy. Like, don't mess with us… and in the West, we want to be seen as a Norwegian, nice, sweet country playing by the rules, predictable."

"After Oct. 7, the Middle East didn't fear us and you have to restore fear. But everything we did to restore fear eroded the love."

Erst als zweiter Deutscher: Schindler sensationell im EM-Halbfinale, Superstar Littler scheitert krachend

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Dartsprofi Martin Schindler hat bei der Heim-EM das Halbfinale erreicht, Luke Littler ist auf seiner Major-Mission in Dortmund dagegen krachend gescheitert. Die deutsche Nummer eins stürmte beim 10:1 in der Runde der letzten acht über den Weltranglistenfünften Jonny Clayton aus Wales hinweg und feierte den größten Erfolg seiner Karriere.

Ein Halbfinale bei einem Einzel-Major hatte Schindler vorher noch nie erreicht. Dort trifft der Strausberger am Abend (19.10 Uhr/Sport1 und DAZN) nicht auf Weltmeister Littler, sondern auf Krzysztof Ratajski. Der Pole besiegte den englischen Dominator sensationell mit 10:6. Littler muss damit mindestens ein weiteres Jahr warten, um seine Majortitel-Sammlung zu komplettieren. Dem 19-Jährigen fehlt nur noch der EM-Titel - sowie die im kommenden Jahr erstmals ausgetragenen European Open in Salzburg.

Vom Titel darf nun Schindler träumen. Der 30-Jährige ist nach Max Hopp 2018 der erst zweite Deutsche, der bei der European Championship in die Runde der letzten vier vordringt. "Ich bin wirklich glücklich mit meiner Performance. Das Scoring und auch die Doppel haben super funktioniert", sagte Schindler. In einem Major-Finale hat noch nie ein Deutscher gestanden.

Angetrieben von den Zuschauern in der stimmungsvollen Westfalenhalle leistete sich Schindler kaum Fehler. Clayton, der in der ersten Runde den zweiten deutschen Teilnehmer Niko Springer mit 6:3 aus dem Turnier geworfen hatte, gewann nur ein einziges Leg. Durch das Erreichen des Semifinals hat Schindler bereits 50.000 Pfund Preisgeld sicher und wird nach dem Turnier wieder unter den Top 20 der Weltrangliste geführt.

Littler, der am Samstag im Achtelfinale gegen Nathan Aspinall den zweithöchsten Average der Turniergeschichte gespielt hatte, zeigte ungewohnte Schwächen, traf aber auch auf einen hervorragend aufgelegten Gegner. So an Alaskan intends to vote for one candidate, but is confused because of a second candidate with exactly the same name who has voted Democrat his entire life, who donated money to the other Democrat opponent, is running.”

Bash pressed Blanche on how it is illegal for two candidates with the same name to be on a ballot at the same time.

"It is an obligation of the states to avoid confusion, among other things, confusion in the minds of voters in the election process,” Blanche replied. “So the question is whether Alaska, by allowing this to happen, is violating the law and the federal law and the constitution by allowing confusion in the ballot."

Sullivan Jr's name.

Regardless, in the suit filed Thursday, DOJ argued that Dan J. Sullivan Jr.’s name on the ballot constitutes a purposeful effort to mislead voters.

Still, it’s unclear how much the court can do about the Justice Department’s lawsuit. With ballots secret, it is virtually impossible for the Justice Department to identify specific voters and clarify which Sullivan candidate they meant to vote for.

Blanche, though, seemed unconcerned about that hurdle.

“There are a number of scenarios where it could be obvious that a voter was confused by the way they wrote down candidates,” said Blanche.

Republican groups have worked to differentiate between the two Sullivans.

President Donald Trump on Friday said the legal challenge was his idea, though Blanche on Sunday declined to state whether the president had directed him to file the suit.

"I will never talk about any communications I have with the president of the United States,” he said.

Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R-Alaska), who is not up for reelection this year, said Friday that the lawsuit is unnecessary.

“Alaskans don’t need any help voting for their preferred candidates, and the State of Alaska has already handled this by clearly distinguishing the candidates on this year’s ballot,” Murkowski said in a post on X. “The Department of Justice should never have filed this lawsuit.”

Her comments drew the ire of Trump, who on Saturday called Murkowski “Republicans’ Worst Senator, by far,” in a post to Truth Social.

Trump said that Alaska’s ranked choice voting system is “a TOTAL DEMOCRAT SCAM meant to confuse the Voter, so that Senator Dan Sullivan will lose Votes to a man who has never participated in Politics before, named Daniel J. Sullivan, Jr.”

“He is only there because the Democrats want to steal the Election, and Murkowski probably wants that to happen, which is why she is against the DOJ filing a Lawsuit in order to stop this impending crime and tragedy,” Trump added. “I have delivered for Alaska at Historic Levels, nobody has ever seen anything like it, and all I’m asking for is a FAIR AND HONEST SENATORIAL ELECTION! Lisa Murkowski is a DISGRACE to Alaska and our Country. You just don’t get any worse!"

Democratic leaders have also widely condemned the lawsuit, arguing the president is attempting to lay the foundation to overturn the midterm election results if their candidates win critical elections — something Bash asked Blanche about on Sunday.
