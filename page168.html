Turtle Beach Vulcan II XP Review: Clicky Compromise

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Turtle Beach is no stranger to competitive-grade gaming keyboards. In recent years, it has focused on its mid- to high-tier Vulcan range, with the Vulcan II XP as its newest entry.

It has a lot going for it—Titan HS mechanical switches, easy modification and customization options, and a suite of pro-tier gaming features available through its support software. However, while it arrives as a full-size keyboard (NumPad included!), a series of trade-offs and compromises ultimately make it feel very middle-of-the-pack.

Measuring 462 x 153 x 32 millimeters, it looks undeniably sharp thanks to its low profile and floating-key approach, all sitting atop a premium brushed-aluminum top plate. Unfortunately, the first compromise is the disappointing underside: a cheap-feeling basic plastic shell basin. Thankfully, the legs feel sturdy when popped up, adding around 10 mm in height and a comfortable angle to the board.

I also like the included soft foam wrist rest, which snaps to the front underside and adds nice support when gaming or typing. It's not memory foam, though, so I fear it might lose some of that cushion over time. The Vulcan also has integrated media controls on the top right with back, play/pause, and forward buttons, plus a media wheel with a push-to-mute feature.

It’s a $150 wired-only device (Turtle Beach is launching a wireless version, the Vulcan II XP Air for an extra $50) that connects via a detachable 1.8-meter USB-C to USB-A cord. A physical connection means zero input latency, which competitive gamers will benefit from.

The corded approach does make the Vulcan II XP a breeze to set up. Plug it into a Windows PC, and that's it. But you’ll need Turtle Beach’s Swarm II app to get the most out of it, especially given that the default RGB lighting is a blinding rainbow cascade.

Swarm II brings out the best in this keyboard. Visually, it offers 10 built-in customizable light effects—you can adjust brightness, pattern speed, and colors for some templates. They're pretty gimmicky—especially the Snake one—but it makes for a fun showcase. What's cooler is that you can adjust every LED, perfect for the 11th “custom” slot where you can choose colors, speed, and one of six patterns. You can turn the LEDs off entirely, but only through the app.

Beyond the eye candy, Turtle Beach packs in an impressive slate of features. You can remap inputs down to the individual key and store those configurations as one of five onboard profiles—perfect if you want layouts tailored to specific games, or a more work-oriented profile.

Critical to the keyboard’s gaming credentials, it includes a macro manager that lets you create custom inputs and command combos, down to how many milliseconds of input it should take before activating a keypress. It supports dozens of popular games by default, from competitive favorites like Counter-Strike and Overwatch to single-player epics like Skyrim, which have popular modding scenes and can benefit from bespoke controls. It even has presets for office apps and Photoshop, adding to the Vulcan’s overall versatility. You can easily import new layouts, too.

I’ve been testing the version with linear switches—Turtle Beach also offers a model with tactile switches, both available in black or white. If you’re new to mechanical keyboards, the main difference is that linear keys press all the way down smoothly, while tactile ones have a little “bump” of resistance as the internal switch activates, which you can feel as the keystroke registers. It’s ultimately personal preference.

For the most part, this is a joy to type on, with precise spacing between keys, and the keys themselves having a satisfying “clackiness” when fully pressed. The only small issue is that the factory-lubed linear keys are so smooth and silent until you get that clack as they hit the base, that it’s easy to accidentally long-press a key. Until you’re used to that, it can catch you off-guard—not an issue when typing, where the worst is an errant stretch of letters whennnnnnnn pausing to think mid-sentence, but it tripped me up a couple of times in games until I was used to it.

Unfortunately, unlike Turtle Beach’s earlier Vulcan II TKL Pro, the keys here aren’t Hall effect, but they are hot-swappable—another trade-off, exchanging long-term reliability and accuracy for the ability to replace or upgrade keys easily. The board supports three-pin or five-pin mechanical switch keys and makes swapping them easy. The flat keycaps also pop off without trouble, which makes cleaning simple. Turtle Beach claims the switches last 50 million keystrokes, which should be plenty, but only a third of the TKL Pro's 150 million.

In gaming, the Vulcan II XP offers a 1,000-Hz polling rate for a 1-millisecond response time, which is competition-grade but falls short of the higher-end 8,000-Hz keyboards on the market. Hopping back to Swarm II, you can activate its more advanced features, like Easy-Shift, allowing for key duplication—basically putting commonly used features within easier reach—and ReacTap, assigning two commands to the same key, useful for games that require very swift inputs. (This might be banned in some competitive scenes under automation rules, though.)

These features can be a little fiddly to set up and map, but with a little experimentation, you can build layouts and input fields that work exactly as you want or need. I tailored a bunch of controls for Borderlands 3 and was dropping Psychos like never before. A minor annoyance, though: Reverting a specific input while trying to create a custom setup would sometimes revert the entire profile, which meant I had to redo everything.

While the Vulcan II XP offers great performance for the price, I do have concerns about the overall build quality. On the unit I received, the media wheel was inverted by default—turning it counter-clockwise toward the minus symbol turned the volume up. This was even after I updated the board’s firmware through the Swarm II app, and I had to remap the inputs for it to work as intended. It’s good that the Vulcan II XP’s customization is specific and nuanced enough to remap even the dial direction, but I shouldn’t have had to.

I also found that the LEDs below the “0” and “.” keys on the NumPad don’t seem to work properly, glowing a stubborn, glaring white no matter what color or pattern is assigned. I even tried turning off every other key’s light and manually assigning colors to just those—to no avail.

It’s another reason the board feels middle-of-the-road. Like the shiny metallic top plate hiding the cheaper plastic base, the Vulcan II XP dazzles at a glance, but the closer you look, the more flaws and small annoyances are revealed.

For the price, some of those annoyances are forgivable. For anyone who hasn't upgraded to a mechanical keyboard, this is a decent entry without breaking the bank, delivering a lovely typing experience backed up by enough pro-tier gaming features to feel like a real upgrade—but more demanding or professional players will want to aim higher.

MIT scientists build a tiny robot that swims using living muscle cells

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A swimming robot does not necessarily need a bulky motor or powerful mechanical parts to move through water. MIT researchers have demonstrated that a single, carefully engineered layer of living muscle cells can generate enough force to propel a tiny robot forward.

The engineers developed an extremely thin swimming robot that uses living muscle tissue to move its fins. Described in a new study published in Advanced Functional Materials, the device has a flexible gel framework roughly the length and width of a stick of gum. The gel forms two fins, each coated with a layer of muscle cells far thinner than a human hair. These cells have been genetically modified to contract when exposed to light.

By directing light onto either fin, the researchers can trigger muscle contractions that make the fin flap and push the robot through water. Adjusting which fin receives light and how frequently it is illuminated allows the team to control both its speed and direction.

In experiments, the remarkably thin robot successfully swam and turned through a simple underwater maze. At maximum speed, it covered approximately four times its body length per minute. Although that is much slower than an Olympic swimmer, who can travel up to 65 body lengths per minute, it is comparable to the swimming pace of a cow shark moving leisurely through the ocean.

"It takes a lot of force to move through water versus air," says study author Ritu Raman, associate professor of mechanical engineering at MIT. "The robot's quite strong, given its size. "

A New Generation of Robots Powered by Living Muscle

The device represents the first demonstration of a very thin, two-dimensional robot that can move from place to place using living muscle tissue.

Most existing biohybrid robots rely on relatively large, three-dimensional structures made from laboratory-grown skeletal muscle. Producing these structures typically requires millions of cells. Raman explains that thinner designs could reduce manufacturing costs while potentially improving movement efficiency.

"Currently, biohybrid robots from our group and others' are built from bulky, 3D chunks of lab-grown skeletal muscle that require millions of cells to fabricate," says Raman, who notes that thinner, less bulky designs such as the team's new bot could be cheaper to build and could move more efficiently. "We believe that biohybrid robots powered by living muscle could one day perform delicate jobs like exploring environments too fragile or unpredictable for conventional hardware, because living tissue is soft, responsive to its surroundings, and can heal itself."

The MIT research team also included first author Maheera Bawa, along with Arielle Berman, Laura Schwendeman, Ferdows Afghah, and Seanbiron Johnson.

From Artificial Muscle to a Swimming Robot

The new swimming robot builds on an earlier achievement by Raman's laboratory. Last year, the researchers created an artificial muscle disk inspired by the human iris, demonstrating how a thin layer of living cells could produce precisely controlled movements.

For that project, the engineers patterned a gel disk with grooves extending outward from its center and forming concentric circles. They then placed living muscle cells on the surface, where the cells grew into a thin layer following the patterned channels.

When illuminated, the muscle tissue contracted in different directions, causing the disk to stretch and compress. Its movements resembled the way the iris of the human eye changes shape as the pupil expands or narrows.

The experiment established that researchers could grow extremely thin muscle layers in intricate arrangements and use light to control their movements along multiple directions.

"People hadn't seen this muscle architecture engineered from scratch before," Raman says. "And the cells were moving in multiple directions. But they only moved about 100 microns. From a robotics perspective, their movements were tiny."

For the latest project, the researchers wanted to dramatically increase the amount of movement and force generated by the muscle cells. Their goal was to produce contractions powerful enough to propel a robot through water.

They determined that achieving this would require a better foundation for the living muscle tissue.

The earlier artificial iris had used fibrin, an exceptionally soft gel, as its underlying structure. However, the team found that fibrin could quickly shrink under the forces exerted by contracting muscle cells, limiting the effectiveness of the resulting movement.

To overcome this problem, the engineers investigated three characteristics of the gel foundation: its chemical composition, its stiffness, and the dimensions and geometry of the grooves used to organize muscle growth.

"For engineering any type of tissue, it's known that these are knobs you can tune," Raman says. "And we wanted to optimize all these parameters to support live muscle cells."

Engineering a Stronger Muscle-Powered Robot

Finding the right supporting material required testing gels with different formulations, stiffness levels, and surface patterns.

The researchers compared several groove shapes, including narrow channels with square profiles and longer channels with curved bottoms. They discovered that muscle cells aligned more effectively inside square-shaped grooves.

This alignment was important because muscle cells that grow in the same direction are more likely to merge into fibers. Those fibers can then form stronger muscle tissue that contracts in a coordinated way. The square grooves therefore provided a more effective structure for producing force.

The team also replaced fibrin with gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), a material commonly used to grow and engineer biological tissue.

By adjusting the formulation of GelMA, the researchers produced supporting gels with varying degrees of stiffness. They then examined how muscle cells developed on each surface and measured their ability to generate force.

Stiffer gels produced the best results. On these firmer surfaces, muscle cells grew in better alignment and generated stronger contractions.

The engineers next investigated how thick the gel should be. They determined that a film measuring approximately half a millimeter offered an effective balance. It was sturdy enough to support a single layer of muscle cells while remaining sufficiently lightweight to prevent the contracting cells from detaching from the surface.

The team further improved muscle performance through a form of exercise. Repeated pulses of light stimulated the cells to contract, strengthening the muscle tissue before it was incorporated into the swimming robot.

With the gel structure optimized and the muscle cells strengthened, the researchers assembled their swimming device.

The finished robot consisted of a thin gel framework divided into two fins. Both sides of the gel were patterned with square-bottomed grooves and covered with living muscle cells. As the cells developed, they fused into organized muscle fibers capable of contracting together.

Each fin could be activated independently using light, giving the researchers a simple way to control the robot's movements.

"You can think of the robot as having two independent muscles," Raman says. "If we shine a light on just one, only that muscle moves. If shining on both, they both flap."

To test the device, the researchers placed it inside a large petri dish filled with water. They then moved a light source by hand, illuminating the fins to guide the robot through a maze positioned inside the dish.

As the muscles contracted, the fins flapped and propelled the robot through the water. By changing the light stimulation, the engineers successfully controlled its movement and helped it navigate the obstacles.

Although the robot demonstrated the ability to swim and steer, its current shape remains relatively simple. The primary objective of the experiment was to establish that an exceptionally thin layer of engineered muscle tissue could generate enough force for swimming.

The researchers now plan to refine the robot's body to improve its speed. Beyond demonstrating a new approach to biohybrid robotics, the technology could eventually be useful for monitoring aquatic environments or operating in locations where conventional rigid machines might cause damage.

"Our next goal is to optimize the body design to enable faster swimming," Raman says. "But even at slow swim speeds, one could imagine a muscle-powered swimmer being used for purposes like environmental monitoring in aquatic environments."

The Office of Naval Research provided partial funding for this research.

Boom in weight-loss drugs could lead to fall in beer sales, Heineken warns UK pubs

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

World’s second largest brewer says use of drugs such as Wegovy can reduce desire to drink alcohol

Heineken has warned British bars and pubs that the boom in popularity of weight-loss drugs could lead to a fall in sales of beer and cider.

The world’s second-largest brewer sent a presentation to British hospitality customers earlier this month that said the use of GLP-1 medication – such as Ozempic and Wegovy – could reduce the desire to drink alcohol. This in turn could result in “fullness and fewer serves” of pints in pubs and bars, it added.

The document, revealed by Reuters, said the GLP-1 era could lead to the tradition of the multi-pint drinking session becoming a less frequent occurrence.

The Netherlands-headquartered company offered advice including promoting half pints, low- and zero-alcohol options and pairing drinks with small plates of food, or risk declines in customer visits and spending.

“We see some changes in consumption behaviour among (GLP-1) users and will continue to monitor how these behaviours evolve in 2027,” said a Heineken spokesperson. “Consumer behaviour is evolving in response to a range of broader trends. GLP-1 is one of the developments we are monitoring.”

GLP-1 use has soared as consumers seek a quick solution to weight loss, with 10 million people turning to medication in the US last year, according to research by JP Morgan.

Heineken said the presentation was shared with customers as part of its regular engagement with UK businesses to help them understand how consumer habits were evolving.

Trends the brewer has identified in GLP-1 users include opting for smaller serves, lower alcohol drinks and more premium products.

“Our latest consumer research suggests GLP-1 users are not simply replacing alcohol,” said the spokesperson. “They are becoming more purposeful in their choices and broadening their preferences. These behaviours are not unique to GLP-1 users. They reflect broader consumer trends towards health and wellbeing, moderation, choice and more intentional consumption that we are seeing across our markets.”

As well as its eponymous lager, Heineken also owns brands such as Amstel, the Mexican lager Sol and Strongbow cider.

For four decades, China’s rise was built on an extraordinary bargain with the world. China offered labor, scale and, eventually, an industrial ecosystem of unparalleled depth. The world supplied technology, capital and markets. China became richer. The rest of the world got cheaper and increasingly better goods. By the middle of this century, that bargain will have to change. Not because China’s manufacturing model has failed, but because it has become too successful.

Over the past few years, the United States has imposed tariffs and export controls. Multinational companies have adopted “China plus one” strategies. Supply chain resilience has become a boardroom obsession. Yet China’s exports have continued to grow, helping to cushion its weakening domestic economy. Its goods trade surplus reached a record $1.2 trillion in 2025. But it would be a mistake to assume that this can carry on indefinitely. Increasingly, what is economically efficient is no longer politically tenable.

China’s manufacturing dominance is generating resistance. And not only in America. Europe worries about the erosion of its industrial base. Developing countries welcome Chinese investment and admire aspects of China’s development experience, but still ask a reasonable question: where, in this model, is our development?

Most countries do not simply want to buy Chinese electric vehicles, batteries, solar panels, machinery and robots. They want factories of their own. They want their workers to acquire new skills and their suppliers to move up the value chain. They want technology and know-how. For years, Western companies complained that access to China’s enormous market came with demands for technology transfers. Now, China finds itself on the other side of the table, being asked to share its own expertise.

China cannot indefinitely remain the world’s manufacturer indefinitely. Its great economic challenge over the next few decades will be to remain deeply embedded in a global economy increasingly shaped by protectionism and national security. The answer may lie in a new role: a shift from being the factory of the world to an exporter of industrial and technological capabilities.

China’s real advantage lies no longer in cheap labor or subsidies, but in the accumulated learning embedded in perhaps the densest industrial ecosystem the world has ever seen. What China can now export is not just what it makes, but what it has learnt about how to make it.

The industrial ecosystem that China built around smartphones accumulated expertise in batteries, sensors, precision manufacturing and power electronics. Those capabilities then migrated into electric vehicles. EVs, in turn, spurred further advances in batteries, chips and automation. Automation fed into robotics. Knowledge compounds as it moves between industries, suppliers, engineers and firms. Each industry creates capabilities that make the next one easier to build. This is China’s innovation flywheel.

The important question now is whether that learning can travel. Neither Brazil nor Indonesia can recreate Shenzhen by decree. But they need not retrace the 30 years China spent building it. They can build on what China has already learnt. Chinese companies can bring machinery, engineers, production processes and supply-chain expertise with them. Local workers learn;  local suppliers improve.

Knowledge that took decades to accumulate in China can thus accelerate development elsewhere. That would be a very different model of Chinese globalization: not simply exporting the products of China’s industrial ecosystem, but giving other countries access to the knowledge embedded within it.

Such a shift is already under way. In Brazil, BYD is moving from importing cars to manufacturing them locally and developing local suppliers. In Hungary, it is pairing its first European passenger-car factory with a research and development center that will work with Hungarian universities and companies.

In Indonesia, Chinese investment is helping to build an integrated battery industry stretching from nickel processing to battery production and recycling. These early examples point to a different relationship with the world: one based less on exporting what China makes than on exporting the capability to make it.

At first glance, this may sound like a loss for China. Why give up production at home? Why train tomorrow’s competitors? Because the alternative may be worse. Countries increasingly want options. They do not want their economic future to depend overwhelmingly on any single supplier, Chinese or American.

The greater the dependence, the greater the political pressure to unwind it. A similar logic applies to artificial intelligence. The competition between the United States and China over AI is usually described as a race: who has the most powerful chips, the most computing power and the best frontier models? But another contest may matter just as much: whose AI is used most widely?

America retains formidable advantages at the technological frontier. China’s constraints have pushed its ecosystem in a somewhat different direction: toward efficiency, lower-cost deployment, open models and rapid downstream application. Chinese developers have strong incentives to spread their models widely, allow others to adapt them and capture value from the ecosystem that grows around them. The world’s most downloaded family of open AI models is Chinese: Alibaba’s Qwen.

Technologies that transform economies are not always the most advanced ones. Adoption and the intensity of use matter as much as proximity to the technological frontier. For much of the world, the AI that would matter most may not be the most intelligent model ever built. It may instead be one model that is cheap enough, open enough and adaptable enough to be used everywhere.

Openness is not simply an act of generosity. It can also be an economic strategy. Open systems surrender some rents at the core while creating larger markets for applications, integration and services. If China cannot monopolize the technological frontier, it can compete by making technology cheaper, more adaptable and more ubiquitous.

China itself offers the greatest modern demonstration of what technological diffusion can accomplish. After it began reforming and opening up its economy in the 1980s, foreign companies brought capital, manufacturing methods, management expertise and technology. Chinese workers and engineers learnt from them. Domestic suppliers emerged. Chinese companies adapted foreign technologies and eventually became innovators in their own right.  themselves.

China has now reached the other side of that development process. By 2050, the most important measure of China’s economic success will not be how much it sells to the rest of the world, or how much of the world’s manufacturing remains within Chinese supply chains or whether a Chinese model tops every AI benchmark. It will likely be whether China’s success can help generate success for other countries.

Speaking in Shanghai in July, Xi Jinping argued that AI technologies should be “shared” so that countries could benefit from them more equitably. He also called for greater efforts to build AI capabilities in the Global South. The instinct is the right one. Dominance breeds resistance. Diffusion gives a reason for others to remain connected. For four decades, China benefited enormously from a world willing to share capital, technology, knowledge and markets.

The question for the next few decades is whether China can do the same for others.

TDM Neo Hybrid Headphones Review: Two-in-One Woes

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Unveiled at CES 2026, the TDM Neo is a pair of on-ear headphones that transform into a Bluetooth speaker. It caught the imagination of the attending media, won a handful of awards, and inspired countless column inches.

Given the current state of the portable audio industry, it's easy to understand why. Most designs seem to revolve around incremental updates and new colorways rather than genuine technical innovation. Neo is an exciting alternative, and it’s little wonder the startup brand went on to smash its Kickstarter targets.

I’ve spent the past couple of weeks listening to the headphones and Bluetooth speaker, and have lost count of the number of times I’ve showed off the clever twist-and-fold mechanism to friends. But has TDM managed to produce a genuine tech twofer, or designed a novelty product that gives you the worst of both worlds?

The twist-and-fold mechanism on the Neo is clever and addictive. Rotate the ear cups around the cushioned but flexible headband, bring them together, and strong magnets snap everything firmly in place. Once connected, the headphone drivers disable, and the outward-facing Bluetooth speakers kick in. Twist them apart and you've got your headphones back.

It’s innovative, and the engineering is impressive. Once in speaker mode, the two rubber TDM logos sit closely together and form a protective base, while the asymmetrical twisted headband cocoons the ear cups together. It works well—it never once popped open accidentally. The action is smooth, the connection reassuringly solid, and switching between modes works exactly as intended.

The Neo has four 40-mm drivers—two for the headphones and two for the speakers. The outward-facing speakers are each rated at 5 watts (for a total of 10). Ten watts is not a lot for a modern Bluetooth speaker, but wattage doesn't automatically tell you how loud or good a speaker will sound, especially in the current age of digital signal processing. The highly regarded $55 JBL Go 5 has just 4.5 watts of output, for instance.

Battery life is quoted at more than 200 hours for headphones and 16 hours in speaker mode, which is pretty impressive, and a quick five-minute charge gives you an extra eight hours.

On-device control is basic but effective, with rubber power and volume buttons together, and a separate one for Bluetooth. One quirk of the twist design is that in speaker mode the volume button is inverted, and on first use I instinctively turned the volume down instead of up. The power and 3.5-mm AUX input are also left facing up, rather than being hidden from view. This also means the USB-C cable sticks out the top—not the most aesthetic solution.

The TDM companion app allows you to customize what happens when the headphones are twisted. You can switch between headphone and speaker playback, pause the music, or power off. You can also lock them in speaker mode to listen with the Neo around your neck, but please, if you have any love for your fellow commuters, don’t do this on the train. There’s also basic EQ settings for both sets of speakers and Find My–style device tracking too.

The TDM are on-ear headphones with no active noise canceling (ANC). That’s something of a rarity these days, with brands eschewing this style in favor of larger, plusher over-ear designs with powerful noise cancellation. See Sony, Bose, Apple, Nothing—you get the idea …

While the TDM don’t have ANC, they do have relatively firm earpads and a tight fit that blocks out noise the old-fashioned way: with noise isolation. Unfortunately, maybe for this very reason, the earpads are not as plush as I’d like, and the whole thing feels tight on my admittedly large skull and less than dainty ears. It’s not eerily quiet like using the best noise-canceling headphones, and I can’t imagine that’s a compromise many users are willing to give up easily.

At 11.9-ounces, the Neo are also heavy for on-ear headphones. It’s no surprise, given that they’ve got four 40-mm drivers inside, but it is noticeable. For comparison, they’re 3 ounces (or 34 percent) heavier than the Sony WH-1000XM6, 1.9 ounces heavier than the more affordable Soundcore Space One Pro, and a full 4.25 ounces heavier than the on-ear Beats Solo 4.

The Neo sound detailed and pleasant, with clear bass and appreciably distinct vocals, but the presentation feels quite boxed in. Music lacks the scale and openness I expect from great headphones, and individual instruments are crowded for space. If they were comfier to wear, I think I’d enjoy the audio a bit more, but I found it all quite tiring, and certainly not on a par with similarly priced alternatives.

They are not awful, but they do feel compromised, and given the price, they also present a bit of a quandary for travel. You gain a Bluetooth speaker at your destination but may spend the journey wishing you'd packed more comfortable headphones with proper ANC.

Just to reiterate, I really like the engineering and execution of the Neo, and I love how small the headphones can fold away. The Bluetooth speaker looks cool too, and it gives the headphones a second function. Sadly, the TDM can’t compete with the best Bluetooth speakers I’ve tested.

There's enough volume for casual listening, but the sound is thin and underwhelming. It keeps pace with the tiny-but-mighty JBL Go 5, but given that it has twice the power and double the drivers, it really should sound better. I also played it alongside my favorite current portable speaker, the $120 JBL Flip 7, and the difference between the two was like racing a speedboat and a paddleboard.

Having a Bluetooth speaker on hand whenever you have your headphones is convenient, and the ability to share a song instantly might appeal, but compact Bluetooth speakers have become so good that the TDM’s subpar audio quality is hard to ignore.

The obvious benefit to the Neo is having just one device to carry and charge. But is that enough to justify the compromises?

The $200 Soundcore Space One Pro are impressive, midrange ANC over-ear headphones that came out on top in WIRED's blind headphone listening test. They're comfortable, adjustable, and foldable, with excellent sound. They’re frequently on sale for $130 or $140, too. Pair them with the $150 but-often-on-sale-for-less JBL Flip 7, and you can get top-tier headphones and a top-tier portable speaker for nearly the same price as the TDM Neo.

Admittedly, there's more to carry, and you lose the instant gratification of twisting your headphones into a speaker. But the Soundcore sound better, are significantly more comfortable, and fold impressively small, while the 1.23-pound JBL Flip 7 is hardly going to weigh you down.

I admire what TDM has achieved. The Neo are clever, entertaining, and a refreshing reminder that innovation doesn't—and shouldn’t—rely solely on stuffing in AI features, adding a fresh colorway, or creating another smartphone app. The mechanism works beautifully, and taking one audio product on the go instead of two has genuine appeal. I just wish I enjoyed listening to them as much as I enjoyed twisting them.

Erneute Wahlverschiebung in Guinea-Bissau: Politisches Chaos hält an

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Die für den 6. Dezember angekündigten Wahlen in Guinea-Bissau sind auf unbestimmte Zeit verschoben. Ein neuer Termin steht nicht fest. Die Rückkehr zu einer zivilen Regierung bleibt ungewiss.

Die für den 6. Dezember 2026 angekündigten Parlaments- und Präsidentschaftswahlen in Guinea-Bissau finden nicht wie geplant statt. Aly Silva, Sprecher des Übergangs-Premierministers Ilídio Vieira Té, bestätigte der DW, dass die Abstimmung bis auf Weiteres verschoben wird. Ein neuer Termin steht nicht fest. Zunächst wolle sich die Regierung im Kabinett beraten und anschließend öffentlich mitteilen, wann gewählt werden soll. Ob die Wahlen 2027 oder erst 2028 stattfinden, ist offen.

Die politische Krise geht auf den 26. November 2025 zurück. Drei Tage zuvor hatten die Menschen in Guinea-Bissau ein neues Parlament und einen Präsidenten gewählt. Noch bevor die offiziellen Ergebnisse bekannt gegeben wurden, übernahm das Militär die Macht und setzte den Wahlprozess außer Kraft. Sowohl der damalige Präsident Umaro Sissoco Embaló als auch sein Herausforderer Fernando Dias da Costa hatten den Wahlsieg für sich beansprucht.

Die Militärführung hat eine Rückkehr zur verfassungsmäßigen Ordnung versprochen. Doch die angekündigten Neuwahlen rücken erneut in die Ferne. Für den Juristen und politischen Analysten Cabi Sanhá kommt die Verschiebung nicht überraschend. Nach dem Putsch sei kaum etwas unternommen worden, um die Voraussetzungen für glaubwürdige Wahlen zu schaffen.

"Die Verschiebung war absehbar", sagt Sanhá im DW-Interview. Zwar sei der 6. Dezember als neuer Wahltermin festgelegt worden. "Aber es wurde nichts getan, damit diese Wahlen tatsächlich stattfinden können."

Die Übergangsregierung begründet die Verschiebung mit den fehlenden organisatorischen und politischen Voraussetzungen. Die Frist für die Einreichung der Kandidaturen lief bereits am 6. Oktober ab. Die Parteien und möglichen Kandidaten hatten ihre Bewerbungen nicht eingereicht. Nach Angaben des Premierministers hatten die Parteien zuvor auch keine Kongresse abgehalten, um ihre Kandidaten zu bestimmen.

Sanhá sieht darin nicht allein ein organisatorisches Problem. Er verweist auf die Einschränkungen politischer Aktivitäten und den fehlenden Dialog mit den zentralen Akteuren des Wahlprozesses. Parteien und Zivilgesellschaft seien nicht ausreichend einbezogen worden. Auch die Vermittlungsbemühungen der Westafrikanischen Wirtschaftsgemeinschaft ECOWAS und Senegals hätten bislang keine Lösung gebracht.

"Freie, transparente und inklusive Wahlen sind nur möglich, wenn man zuvor die Voraussetzungen dafür schafft", sagt der Jurist. Dass Parteien ihre Kandidaten nicht aufgestellt haben, betrachtet er deshalb auch als Folge der politischen Rahmenbedingungen. "Wie sollen Parteien Kandidaten aufstellen, wenn politische Aktivitäten im Land eingeschränkt sind? Sie können weder ihre Parteitage abhalten noch Versammlungen organisieren."

Führende Oppositionspolitiker halten sich zudem im Ausland auf. Domingos Simões Pereira, Vorsitzender der größten Oppositionspartei PAIGC, durfte im Juli 2026 aus der Haft nach Portugal reisen, um sich medizinisch behandeln zu lassen. Er war zuvor im Zusammenhang mit Ermittlungen zu einem mutmaßlichen Putschversuch in Untersuchungshaft genommen worden. Deine Verteidigung bestreitet die Vorwürfe und kritisiert das Verfahren als "politisch motiviert".

Die Abwesenheit wichtiger Oppositionsvertreter und die eingeschränkten Möglichkeiten politischer Organisation erschweren einen Wahlprozess, der eigentlich den Übergang zu einer zivilen Regierung ermöglichen sollte.

Wie es nun weitergeht, ist unklar. Nach Angaben des Regierungsmitarbeiters Aly Silva will sich die Übergangsregierung zunächst im Kabinett beraten. Erst anschließend soll ein neuer Wahltermin öffentlich bekannt gegeben werden. Einen konkreten Zeitplan gibt es bislang nicht.

Damit bleibt auch offen, wann Parteien ihre internen Verfahren nachholen, Kandidaten nominieren und den Wahlkampf aufnehmen können. Für Sanhá ist das ein Zeichen dafür, dass der politische Wille zur Rückkehr zur verfassungsmäßigen Ordnung bislang nicht ausreichend erkennbar sei.

"Ich glaube nicht, dass es in erster Linie an logistischen Problemen liegt. Vielmehr fehlt es offenbar am politischen Willen, den Wahlprozess voranzubringen", sagt er.

Die politische Krise wird durch eine umstrittene Verfassungsreform verschärft. Bei einem Referendum am 30. August stimmte eine Mehrheit für Verfassungsänderungen, die die Befugnisse des Präsidenten erweitern und das Parlament von 102 auf 65 Sitze verkleinern. Kritiker bezweifeln die Legitimität des Verfahrens, weil die Reform unter einer Militärführung und ohne ein regulär gewähltes Parlament vorbereitet wurde. Die Opposition hatte zum Boykott der Abstimmung aufgerufen.

Fernando Vaz, Sprecher des Nationalen Übergangsrats, verteidigt die Änderungen. Es habe sich nicht um eine umfassende Neufassung, sondern um eine punktuelle Überarbeitung der Verfassung gehandelt. Die Reform solle helfen, die politische Instabilität des Landes zu überwinden.

"Die Verfassung muss eines der Instrumente sein, die unser Land zu mehr Stabilität führen", sagte Vaz im DW-Interview. Die Übergangszeit habe eine Gelegenheit geboten, Regelungen zu ändern, die aus Sicht der Militärführung die politische Entwicklung behinderten. Vaz weist zudem die Kritik zurück, die Reform übertrage dem Präsidenten zusätzliche Macht auf Kosten des Premierministers. "Der Präsident der Republik ist Staatsoberhaupt, der Premierminister ist Regierungschef", betont er.

Kritiker sehen das anders - und verweisen darauf, dass die neue Verfassungsordnung die Stellung des Präsidenten stärke: Er soll den Premierminister und die Regierung ernennen und entlassen sowie unter bestimmten Voraussetzungen das Parlament auflösen können. Zugleich wird die Zahl der Parlamentssitze reduziert. Damit bleibt umstritten, ob die Reform tatsächlich zur Stabilisierung beiträgt oder die Macht stärker bei der Präsidentschaft konzentriert.

Die Übergangsregierung hat angekündigt, 2027 Kommunalwahlen abzuhalten. Sanhá begrüßt das grundsätzlich: Seit der Unabhängigkeit habe Guinea-Bissau noch keine solchen Wahlen durchgeführt. Doch sie dürften die ausstehenden nationalen Wahlen nicht ersetzen, mahnt er: "Zuerst müssen die Parlaments- und Präsidentschaftswahlen stattfinden, damit das Land zur verfassungsmäßigen Ordnung zurückkehren kann. Danach können die Kommunalwahlen folgen."

Die lokale Ebene könne die fehlende demokratische Legitimation auf nationaler Ebene nicht ausgleichen. Zudem müssten politische Freiheiten wiederhergestellt werden, damit Parteien ihre Kandidaten bestimmen könnten und die Bevölkerung tatsächlich zwischen politischen Alternativen wählen könne.

Guinea-Bissau blickt auf Jahrzehnte politischer Instabilität zurück, geprägt von Militärputschen, institutionellen Krisen und wiederholten Unterbrechungen demokratischer Prozesse. Der jüngste Putsch setzt diese Entwicklung fort.

Die erneute Verschiebung der Wahlen zeigt, wie weit das Land noch von einer Rückkehr zu einer verfassungsmäßigen Ordnung entfernt ist. Für Jurist Cabi Sanhá ist klar: Ein neuer Wahltermin allein wird nicht genügen. Dafür, so Sanhá, müssten politische Aktivitäten wieder zugelassen, die Parteien in den Prozess einbezogen und verlässliche Rahmenbedingungen geschaffen werden.
