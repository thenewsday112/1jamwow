Free labour movement not part of new Canada-EU transatlantic pact, ambassador says

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Canada’s EU ambassador Jonathan Wilkinson says the labour discussions are focusing on 'specialized areas' where employers from both places can benefit from access to a shared labour pool.Justin Tang/The Canadian Press

Negotiations toward a new transatlantic pact between Canada and the European Union don’t include discussions for a full-scale free labour movement, but something more focused, Canada’s EU ambassador said ahead of the Canada-EU summit later this week in Montreal.

Canada and the EU are expected to use the summit to announce a new agreement that could touch on a number of issues including labour, critical minerals and energy.

Jonathan Wilkinson, who took the Canadian ambassadorship in Brussels earlier this year after spending more than five years in the Liberal cabinet, told The Canadian Press in an interview the labour discussions are focusing on “specialized areas” where employers from both places can benefit from access to a shared labour pool.

He said any measures that would make it easier for workers and students to move between the two jurisdictions need to be approached in a “thoughtful way.”

“We’re not talking about free movement of labour between Europe and Canada. But what we are talking about is in some specialized areas, it may be actually interesting and useful for both countries to have the ability to tap into labour pools,” Wilkinson said.

The ambassador did not cite specific sectors where labour mobility between Canada and the EU could be eased. The EU and Canada have been discussing increased collaboration on critical minerals, technology, defence and clean energy.

Wilkinson pointed to work already done by Canada and Europe on mutual accreditation for architects and said any future decisions about labour mobility would be focused on areas of “mutual benefit.”

Canada has also discussed joining Erasmus+, the European Union’s student exchange program, which offers study and training opportunities in member nations.

The exact nature of the new pact between Canada and the EU has been murky even from the leaders themselves.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said in September a new alliance could make Canada an “associate” member of the 27-member bloc.

But “associate membership” does not actually exist in the EU and Canadian officials have downplayed the use of that term.

Prime Minister Mark Carney used the term “alliance of the future” to describe the deal, which was first made public when he spoke to the EU Parliament in Strasbourg, France, last month.

This week’s summit will be happening on the 10th anniversary of the signing of the Canada-Europe Union Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement, or CETA.

Nine EU members – France, Italy, Belgium, Ireland, Poland, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Greece and Hungary – have not yet ratified that trade deal.

Wilkinson said he’s spoken with all 27 of his EU member nation counterparts since he moved to Brussels and there is momentum in Europe to get more countries behind CETA. He did not name specific nations.

“I think there are a number of countries that will be moving in that direction over the coming months. So that’s a process that will go along with the rest of this deepening of the relationship,” he said.

One of the EU’s leading nations may have a new government soon. Marine Le Pen, leader of the far-right National Rally, is the current polling favourite to succeed Emmanuel Macron as president this spring.

Le Pen delivered a nearly hour-long speech to supporters Sunday that focused on her party’s nationalist, anti-immigrant positions.

Wilkinson said politics is always dynamic and Europeans and Canadians will go on sharing values regardless of which party is in power in a particular nation.

“I think in France, it’s a very important debate that they are having. And I think that if in fact the government were to change, people will have to take stock of that,” he said.

“But I do think that the European Union is a much bigger project than any one government in any one country. And I have a lot of confidence that Europeans will find their way through this and be just fine.”

Wilkinson said energy and critical minerals – including natural gas, hydrogen and nuclear power – will play a crucial role in the enhanced partnership between Canada and the EU.

“We’re looking at … how can we, for example, encourage significant additional European investment in Canadian minerals projects. We’re talking about how can we think about things like stockpiling together so that we actually can help to stabilize the market,” Wilkinson said.

The European Investment Bank applied for an exemption from Canadian foreign banking rules earlier this month that would allow the state investor to operate in Canada as part of a critical minerals agreement.

Wilkinson said Canada and the European Union are both looking for ways to build up more infrastructure to refine and ship critical minerals and reduce China’s market dominance.

“That’s not a dependency that’s good for Canada. That’s not a dependency good for the European Union. So we are looking at that,” he said.

Ontario’s delayed inspections of two career colleges was after funding approval, records show

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People take part in a rally over OSAP cuts outside Queen's Park in Toronto on Wednesday March 4, 2026. THE CANADIAN PRESS/Allison JonesAllison Jones/The Canadian Press

Ontario delayed inspections of two career colleges that ultimately turned up serious concerns about student financial aid use, which meant the schools received a “fail” only after they had been approved for funding for another year, court records show.

Critics say it points to severe mismanagement of public money and the government has made the wrong people pay the price.

The Ministry of Colleges and Universities sent Academy of Learning locations in Toronto on Bay Street and in Brampton East notices of inspection on Oct. 1, 2024, according to court records, but did not start the in-person inspections until July and August of 2025, respectively.

“I apologize for the delay, which was due to transitions in roles and the implementation of new inspection procedures,” a ministry official wrote in an e-mail to the Brampton East location, looking to schedule an on-site inspection.

That was four days after the schools were sent ministry notices approving their ability to accept Ontario Student Assistance Program-funded tuition for another year.

Correspondence between the schools and the ministry is among hundreds of pages of documents filed in court as Academy of Learning seeks judicial review of the ministry’s ultimate decision in 2026 to bar them from OSAP eligibility for a year, a ban that has now been extended to 2030.

Ontario extends OSAP ban for three career colleges until at least 2030

Concerns about OSAP activity at the schools have been raised by various groups and governments, but no misuse has been proven in court. The schools deny allegations that anything improper occurred and have implemented a long list of corrective actions.

Their OSAP approval in 2025 came despite warnings months earlier from both the federal government and the industry’s own association, Career Colleges Ontario. That organization wrote a letter to the ministry in January 2025, flagging “irregularities” in OSAP usage at 10 schools, including the two Academy of Learning locations.

Growth in OSAP applications at most career colleges was flat between 2022 and 2024, Career College Ontario wrote, but the Bay location saw growth of 642 per cent, and Brampton East saw 527 per cent.

In May of 2025, the federal government told the province about serious concerns with those schools. As the federal government detailed to the schools a few months later, those concerns included unusually rapid growth and “evidence of systematic irregularities.”

The schools have said that instead of being viewed as an indicator of risk, growth represents “a reflection of program relevance, responsiveness to work force needs, and public confidence in the institution’s delivery of quality education.”

Court records show the provincial inspections ultimately found, in September for the Bay location and in March, 2026 for the Brampton East location, evidence suggesting third parties were completing the coursework – sometimes entire courses in one day – and a few students who couldn’t name their school or program.

Students may not have responded to the federal government’s verification attempts because they believed them to be scam calls, and other students having difficulty answering questions in interviews “can be reasonably attributed to situational and linguistic factors,” the school wrote in response to the government.

The Academy of Learning also suggests the government is interpreting IP address data incorrectly. Unusually rapid course completion warrants review, they have said.

Editorial: Students are paying the price for Ontario’s neglect of private colleges

Successful OSAP applications at those two schools, which share a parent company, totalled $173-million in grants alone in 2024-25, according to data previously obtained by The Canadian Press through a freedom-of-information request. The number rises to $219-million when a North York location that is a branch of Brampton East is added.

In comparison, students at all publicly funded colleges across the province that year received $385-million in provincial grants.

Colleges and Universities Minister Nolan Quinn drastically reduced the amount of OSAP grants that students at universities and publicly funded colleges could receive in the name of “sustainability” as the program was facing rising costs. Those costs were later revealed by The Canadian Press to largely be due to activity at a small number of career colleges.

Quinn has also now paused OSAP payments to students at all career colleges and other private postsecondary institutions as his government and an outside firm conduct a broader review of the issue.

Liberal colleges and universities critic Tyler Watt said those actions make Quinn’s delayed action on potential bad actors all the more baffling.

“Their recent actions, where they’ve completely pulled the rug out from every single student in the private sector, goes to show you that they are willing to take drastic action,” he said.

“It’s not OK at all what they’ve done to students and the people that are following the rules, but there’s no reasonable explanation why they let this go on and wasted this much of taxpayers’ money for as long as they did.”

Green Party Leader Mike Schreiner said the government has failed students and the public.

“This amounts to severe mismanagement of public money by the Ford government,” he wrote in a statement. “For years, this government has been aware of private career colleges breaking the rules and has allowed them to continue to benefit from OSAP.”

Ontario’s OSAP pause affects students at ballet and divinity schools, among others

NDP critic Peggy Sattler said the government should have acted earlier on the numerous warnings it received.

“The government still dragged its heels on conducting the investigation that it is obligated to do and then blames students for misusing OSAP grants and punishes the entire sector for its own incompetence,” she said.

A spokesperson for Quinn said the inspection process can be long and in-depth.

“As we have stated, these inspections are multifaceted, and include reviewing data within the ministry’s possession, data requested from the institution that is provided to the ministry Inspectors, and in-person inspections,” Bianca Giacoboni wrote in a statement.

“Due to the technical nature of this process, inspections and subsequent investigations can take over a year. The ministry cannot presume the outcome of these investigations until all steps have been completed.”

OSAP applications at those schools actually went down in 2025-26, the province said in court filings. In the case of the Brampton East location, the number of OSAP recipients dropped by 34 per cent from the previous year, compared to growth of 1,347 per cent from 2021-22 to 2024-25.

The decline is timed around the start of the ministry’s in-person inspections, the government says in a document filed in court.

The Academy of Learning says no single factor explains that decline.

“We reject any suggestion that the decline reflects wrongdoing,” the schools wrote in a statement.

“Enrolment fell during a period of significant uncertainty, as students received repeated communications questioning their OSAP funding and our eligibility. Many chose to withdraw, postpone or not start their studies. In the same period, we ended some community partner arrangements and added verification measures.”

In one of the night’s biggest upsets, Alvin Tedjo pulled off a victory in Mississauga.

Ottawa Mayor Mark Sutcliffe in 2023.Spencer Colby/The Canadian Press

Mark Sutcliffe cruised to re-election in Ottawa.

Sutcliffe’s main challenge came from Jeff Leiper, a longtime councillor who ran as the progressive alternative to the incumbent mayor.

But voters stuck with Sutcliffe, who pledged to keep property taxes low, maintain a hiring freeze on non-front-line workers and create free trade with neighbouring Gatineau.

Mayor Patrick Brown in 2024.Andrew Lahodynskyj/The Canadian Press

Brown had secured a majority of the votes with most polls reporting Monday night.

Fred Francis in 2024.Dax Melmer/The Globe and Mail

The city councillor put an end to incumbent Drew Dilkens’ 12-year tenure as mayor, in an election that took place amid the U.S.-Canada trade war.

Francis, the younger brother of former mayor Eddie Francis, pledged to hold the line on taxes, make city hall more transparent, eliminate strong mayor powers and cap transit fees.

Olivia Chow’s mayoral win was not a vote for Toronto’s status quo

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Toronto mayor-elect Olivia Chow gives her victory speech on stage at her camp's election night headquarters in Toronto on Monday.Nathan Denette/The Canadian Press

A wind of change is blowing in Canadian politics.

In British Columbia, the mayor of Vancouver and then the premier of the province were toppled in spectacular fashion, the first beaten by a Green Party upstart, the second by the leader of a party, the Conservatives, that had been in complete disarray. In Quebec, the separatist Parti Québécois returned to power after a dozen years in the wilderness, reducing the ruling Coalition Avenir Québec to precisely zero seats in the National Assembly.

Olivia Chow managed to buck the trend on Monday evening, beating back a serious challenge from an energetic city councillor, Brad Bradford, and winning a second term as mayor of Toronto. It was quite an achievement for the former city councillor and NDP MP, who turns 70 next year. She arrived in Canada from Hong Kong with her parents at the age of 13, moving into a sprawling public housing complex. She won election to the school board at just 28, beginning a political career that has spanned four decades.

Olivia Chow re-elected mayor of Toronto, staving off challenger Brad Bradford

That career seemed to be over in 2015, after she lost a bid for mayor, then a by-election that would have given her back a seat in the House of Commons. But when former mayor John Tory resigned amid a personal scandal in 2023, Ms. Chow took another stab and won the mayor’s chair in a special by-election. If all goes well, she will hold it until 2030.

In this moment of triumph, she is bound to see a vindication of the record she has established at city hall and the program she had laid out for the future. She is entitled to that view.

Unlike most government leaders, mayors are elected directly. With parties prohibited at the city level, voters mark their ballots for the candidate alone. Armed with that legitimacy – along with the “strong mayor” powers the provincial government has granted to cities – Ms. Chow has a solid mandate and considerable power.

In her first term, she managed to create a school snack program, open all libraries seven days a week and strike a cost-sharing deal with Premier Doug Ford worth billions of dollars to the city. In this term, she plans to fight for tenants’ rights, create more affordable rental housing, keep transit fares frozen and invest in youth programs.

But it would be a mistake to interpret Monday’s vote as a rubber stamp for the status quo. Mr. Bradford gave Ms. Chow a real run for her money. He told voters that Toronto was crying out for change, and that message clearly struck a chord, bringing him close to even with a well-known sitting mayor in late-campaign opinion polls.

Voters will not be content with a steady-as-she-goes approach to governing Canada’s leading metropolis. Traffic congestion is a genuine problem, frustrating to locals and mind-boggling for visitors. It costs Torontonians countless hours of wasted time and the city many billions of dollars in lost productivity.

Though Toronto is safe by North American standards, with crime rates down in several categories, the plague of mental illness, drug addiction and homelessness that afflicts so many cities is painfully visible.
