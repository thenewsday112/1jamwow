El sector de la automoción y la movilidad afronta la recta final de 2026 con unas perspectivas mayoritariamente optimistas, aunque marcadas por una profunda brecha según la actividad y el tipo de empresa. La primera edición del Barómetro Movilidad+Auto 2026, una iniciativa del nuevo canal vertical de elDiario.es, indica que el 50,8% de las empresas que operan en España prevé una mejora del negocio este año en un contexto de tensiones geopolíticas con la descarbonización como telón de fondo. Los resultados del sondeo se presentaron en un encuentro con los líderes del sector celebrado en Casa Seat de Barcelona. La revista con los resultados y los análisis del Barómetro se puede descargar en este enlace.

El Barómetro Movilidad+Auto de elDiario.es lleva a cabo una foto transversal y de 360 grados del sector en un momento de profunda transformación. Este instrumento de información y prospectiva cuenta con la colaboración de las principales organizaciones sectoriales: Anfac, Sernauto, Faconauto, Ganvam, Aedive, Anesdor, ATUC, AER, Empresas por la Movilidad Sostenible, Fecavem, Aemes Smart, CIAC, UGT y CCOO.

En el ámbito laboral, el empleo mantiene su senda expansiva aunque perdiendo dinamismo: el 39,3% prevé aumentar su plantilla en 2026 —mismo porcentaje que en 2025—, impulsado por la demanda de nuevos perfiles profesionales, especialmente en el ámbito de los componentes y servicios (56,3%).

La transformación tecnológica acelera el esfuerzo inversor en España, en contraste con los recortes que se registran en otros países. El 59% de las compañías aumentará sus inversiones globales en 2026, lideradas por las grandes empresas (66,7%). En el campo de la nueva movilidad, un 42,6% incrementará sus recursos, con el vehículo eléctrico como principal foco de atracción de capital (49,2%), seguido del híbrido (39,3%) y la infraestructura de recarga (21,3%).

El contexto internacional introduce importantes elementos de fricción. El conflicto en Irán y el bloqueo del Estrecho de Ormuz son percibidos como la mayor amenaza externa (2,84 puntos sobre 5), castigando con mayor dureza a la red comercial y de talleres (3,38 puntos). Por su parte, los aranceles de EE. UU. (2,20) y de la UE a China (2,54) afectan de forma más acusada a las grandes corporaciones.

Paralelamente, la creciente penetración de las marcas chinas es vista como una oportunidad por el 54,1% del sector, apoyada por empresas de servicios y componentes. Sin embargo, representa una amenaza directa para el 62,5% de los fabricantes tradicionales y concesionarios.

Ante la incertidumbre y el retroceso de algunos mercados automovilísticos, el aumento del gasto en defensa y seguridad abre una vía clara de diversificación para el 54% de las empresas, que ve una oportunidad de buscar una actividad alternativa en ese sector. El interés está encabezado por los proveedores de componentes (62,5%).

El 19 de septiembre de 2021, Eva tuvo que marcharse de su casa, porque a pocos kilómetros montaña arriba había erupcionado un volcán. Cinco años después, aunque su casa finalmente se librase de la lava, sigue viviendo en un contenedor. ¿Qué ha fallado en La Palma para que 110 familias no hayan podido reconstruir su vida?

Rayco, que vivía en el centro de Todoque, ha comprado una finca con un pajero para reformar en Mazo, a 50 minutos de su antigua casa. “Cada vez que quiero socializar tengo que atravesar media isla. Si viviese en el Oeste me sentiría más acogido, allí es donde me crie, pero está todo carísimo”.

Vicente entraba en su casa cada semana para barrer ceniza. No barría el suelo, barría el tejado: su vivienda había quedado totalmente sepultada por la lluvia de piroclasto. Limpiaba la ceniza, sabiendo que tras marcharse, su casa volvería a quedar tapada. “Llegué un día y me di cuenta de que ya no había casa. Tenía que descubrirla yo. Tenía que desenterrarla”. Vicente dejaba unos palos sobresaliendo de la chimenea para saber, al regresar, donde comenzar a excavar de nuevo y no perder de vista la casa bajo un manto de ceniza.

El volcán Tajogaite cumplió el pasado 19 de septiembre cinco años, pero no todas las familias de la isla han podido recuperar su hogar. En total, la lava se llevó 1.600 casas y dos barrios enteros -El Paraíso y Todoque- que han quedado bajo un manto de roca negra.

Por vivienda afectada, el Gobierno central ha concedido una ayuda de 60.000 euros, el Gobierno canario, 30.000 más, y el Cabildo otros 10.000. Quién tuviera la vivienda asegurada, ha podido recibir del Consorcio el valor de su casa. Pero para recibir todo esto, lo esencial es tener una casa. Y sobre el papel, muchos afectados no la tenían. ¿Cómo es posible?

En La Palma, donde el salario mínimo es regla y la mayor parte del territorio no es urbanizable -por lo tanto, el que hay es caro- muchos vecinos levantan sus casas poco a poco, a lo largo de los años, partiendo de pequeños cuartos de apero en una finca o en un terreno rústico. Y nunca llegan a regularizar esa vivienda. Hay quién diría que es picardía. Para muchos es necesidad.

En este contexto, mucha gente no ha podido siquiera demostrar que han sido afectados por un volcán porque no vivían oficialmente en la zona.

En La Palma, el volcán no solo se lleva cuatro paredes. Se lleva el esfuerzo de años de levantar una casa, piedra a piedra, y se lleva a los vecinos que han ayudado a levantarla. Porque cuando la mano de obra es cara, en La Palma las “gallofas” ganan terreno y es la comunidad quién ayuda en la obra. Hace cinco años, el volcán se llevó las paredes, pero también separó a los vecinos.

No son casos aislados, sino que se trata de uno de los principales problemas de esta erupción, y da respuesta a por qué, cinco años después, 110 familias siguen viviendo en los “barracones”, que se construyeron como medidas supuestamente temporales.

La vida en estos contenedores no es sencilla: quién vive ahí ahora, antes del volcán solía vivir en una casa terrera, con su huerta y animales. Los contenedores tienen unos 50 metros cuadrados y están situados en un parking. En la localidad de Los Llanos, los vecinos se quejan de la falta de insonorización y de aislamiento, tanto del frío como del calor. Eva cuenta que no es capaz de sentir el contenedor como un hogar porque “no estoy en mi ambiente, porque no puedo moverme. De aquí al sofá, del sofá a la cama, de la cama al sofá. Salir no salgo, porque todo el mundo ahí afuera está con la oreja pegada”.

Otra dificultad por la que están pasando los afectados es la adaptación a un formato de vida no acorde con sus costumbres. “Vivíamos entre familias y nos llevábamos muy bien con todos los vecinos”, cuentan dos hermanas de La Laguna, barrio que se perdió parcialmente. En los lugares afectados, lo más habitual era vivir en lugares con huerto o jardín, lo que contrasta con el reducido espacio del contenedor. “Árboles frutales, naranjos, orquídeas. Teníamos de todo. Nosotras estábamos acostumbradas a vivir en casas terreras. Y eso está debajo del volcán”.

Eva concluye: “El container no está para vivir personas. Esto es de chapa. La chapa está pensada para meter muebles y animales, y nosotros no somos eso”.

Los vecinos damnificados han podido construir de manera legal en terreno rústico. Pero, ¿quién se considera damnificado del volcán? Ahí radica otro de los grandes problemas.

Rayco no se considera damnificado a efectos legales, ya que su casa estaba a nombre de sus padres. Por tanto, no ha podido acogerse a las ayudas y facilidades para construir que ofrecen las Administraciones. Lo mismo le sucede a aquellos que, tras el fallecimiento del propietario original, no actualizaron el registro de la propiedad. Si el propietario de la vivienda está muerto, sus herederos -hijos, sobre todo- no han podido recibir las ayudas por la pérdida de la casa, aun estando empadronados en ella.

Vicente ha tardado tres años en recibir la consideración de afectado. Su casa sigue en pie, aunque quedase sepultada bajo la ceniza. “Se ha pagado el valor de lo perdido bajo la lava. Pero ¿qué ocurre con estas viviendas que están deterioradas, con grietas, no hay paredes, no hay servicios...? Para esto no ha habido ninguna dotación”. Vicente no puede habitar su casa, porque hasta su barrio en Las Manchas todavía no llega ninguna carretera, ni agua o luz. Es un barrio todavía sepultado por la ceniza. Tan solo algunas viviendas como la suya sobresalen de entre el desierto negro.

Muchos vecinos denuncian violencia burocrática. “ Vas al Ayuntamiento y todo son problemas. Como si no fueras afectada, ¿sabes?”, cuenta Esther, que perdió la casa el último día de erupción y quiso reconstruir sobre la lava, en su propiedad. “ Para construir, tuve que pedir que me quitaran de la zona de exclusión. Me quitaron, después me volvieron a poner, sin dar explicaciones. Pedíamos permiso para aplanar, para quitar la lava. Te cobran hasta por los permisos para aplanar el terreno. Encima que pagas al palista, te cobra el ayuntamiento. Todo son problemas”.

Es difícil sentir un hogar allí donde es difícil construir un proyecto de vida. La incertidumbre ha inundado las vidas de sus vecinos, y preguntas como “¿hasta cuando podré quedarme en el contenedor?”, o “¿cuándo podré empezar reconstruir mi casa?” se arremolinan en la mente de los vecinos.

“Vecinos que nacieron ahí, se criaron ahí, y de pronto toda tu raíz… ¿qué pasa con toda esa parte que desaparece y no queda rastro?”, relata Belqui, cuya casa en el barrio de Tajuya se salvó por muy poco.

El volcán se ha llevado, en tan sólo 85 días, lo que para muchos vecinos es el esfuerzo de 30 años de trabajo. Y les ha quitado, además del esfuerzo, y en contraposición, el descanso. Porque un hogar también es poder llegar a casa y tumbarse en el sofá, sabiendo que nadie ni nada interrumpirá ese momento.

Durante cinco años, los vecinos damnificados han pasado por muchas ubicaciones, pero ninguna a largo plazo, y ninguna donde poder descansar plenamente. Habitaciones en albergues, contenedores, casas de familiares o alquileres temporales han compuesto algunas de las soluciones aportadas a los vecinos. Pero llevando siempre una maleta a cuestas, es difícil construir un hogar.

Scientists Are Teaching Birds to Sing Lost Songs

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

This article was originally published by The Guardian and is reproduced here as part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

A rufous-collared sparrow. Charles J. Sharp via Wikipedia

When bird populations decline, fledglings may struggle to learn the songs they need to attract mates. But new research suggests it is possible to teach birds lost songs.

Listening to birdsong is one of life’s great joys, but when researchers heard a particular tune emerging from a tree in a Buenos Aires park, they were especially jubilant. This arrangement was among those chirped by the local rufous-collared sparrows in the 1960s, but had since disappeared from the area.

That was until scientists created a computer-generated rendition, curious to find out if birds might consider it realistic enough to imitate. Less than three months later, a local avian resident began to copy parts of the song.

There is a growing recognition of birdsong’s importance to conservation, and of the need to better understand how birds learn to sing. If an individual sings well, and in a familiar “dialect,” they are better placed to find a mate, defend their territory, and ultimately survive.

If a species’ population declines, however, there are fewer opportunities for fledglings to become good singers. Birds can begin to tweet abnormally or mistakenly learn the songs of other species, jeopardizing their chances of breeding.

Male birds are in a better position to attract a mate if they can sing well — which, broadly speaking, means their song is complex, sung consistently, and well performed. “If your song requires a lot of energy, you might have to be in quite a good condition to sing it,” says Rebecca Lewis, a conservation scientist at Chester Zoo.

“When people talk about biodiversity, we’re always talking about genes, about genetic material,” says Gabriel B. Mindlin, professor of physics at the University of Buenos Aires, who coauthored the study on rufous-collared sparrows, and whose work is featured in a Guardian-produced documentary, Dream of Birds.

But animals’ learned behavior or culture, such as birdsong, is also an essential part of their population, says Mindlin.

The rufous-collared sparrow is not considered vulnerable globally, but researchers hope that synthetic recordings could help teach birdsong to other species whose populations are dwindling, allowing conservationists to tutor individuals where real-life recordings do not exist.

About 4,000 of the world’s 10,000 bird species are songbirds (or oscine passerines), such as finches, sparrows, and blackbirds, which generally have complex songs that are at least partly learned from other individuals. Their songs are hugely diverse, though separate research on the wider passerine group of birds, which incorporates songbirds, has identified common “motifs” or building blocks: three types of trill, three types of whistle, chaotic notes, and harmonic notes.

The way that birds use these motifs reflects the environment in which the birds evolved. Flat whistles are more likely to be sung by birds in tropical rainforests where sound must travel through dense vegetation. Complex motifs, such as ultrafast trills, are more common in temperate regions where birds tend to communicate at closer range.

The song studied by Mindlin was recreated based on hand-drawn annotations of rufous-collared sparrow songs that were made in the 1960s. Each song consists of a trill, which is consistent among individuals in the same region, and a theme, which varies. Some of the themes recorded decades ago are still sung by the local birds, but others have died out.

It is not clear why the song studied by Mindlin had disappeared, though he notes the population of birds in the park had declined.

Birds’ songs can change naturally over time — for example, birds might make errors when learning songs, which then can become imprinted in future renditions. Human encroachment and population declines can also cause dramatic changes.

Songbirds learn from other members of their species — such as a father or neighbors — which results in local groups developing their own dialects. The window for learning varies from a few weeks to a lifetime. Some species also have an innate level of knowledge.

“There’s such a diversity in songbirds in how they learn,” says Sarah Collins, associate professor of animal behavior at the University of Plymouth, who studied a group of cirl buntings that were translocated from Devon to Cornwall as part of a conservation project.

Initially, the birds produced abnormal songs that were squeaky and slow, and far less diverse than those of the source populations in Devon. “They didn’t have the richness of sound,” says Collins. Eight years later, however, their songs had returned to normal.

A few of the chicks were played recordings of cirl sounds, though not all. The study suggests that recovery of song is possible, though it is unclear if other species would bounce back in the same way. Cirl buntings are born with knowledge of the elements that make up the “notes” used in their songs, says Collins. “What they learn is how to put those elements together,” she says.

Some songbird species have a similar genetic underpinning and so may also be able to recover their songs, but others do not. Either way, Collins recommends a tutor to help other birds that are being translocated.

A separate study in Australia tracked regent honeyeaters that were bred in zoos to boost a critically endangered wild population that had fallen to about 250 individuals. Interacting with just two wild-born male “tutors” helped the zoo-bred birds to learn to sing the wild songs. Thereafter, zoo-bred males that had learned the traditional songs went on to tutor the next generation.

“Sometimes birds don’t learn very well from CDs or just playbacks. They need some sort of interaction,” says Collins.

Bringing wild birds into captivity to teach hand-reared chicks could cause huge distress and bring disease, however, risking the health of individuals from an already endangered population. It is possible that A.I. could help provide live tutoring in the future.

Dialect is also important. Past translocation projects have shown that birds are slower to match if they do not sing in the same dialect. “Birds with mismatched songs don’t pair quite as quickly or do quite as well once they’re paired,” adds Lewis. Familiarizing birds with songs of their destination can help to mitigate this.

A huge volume of birdsong is now being recorded and uploaded into databases, including by a growing number of hobbyists interested in birdwatching, says Mindlin. “One day we’ll be grateful to have all those records,” he says. “I don’t think we still have an idea that really we have to register dialects and communication systematically.”

In Buenos Aires, the song from the 1960s can still be heard. “It’s still being sung by just a few individuals, pretty close to where we inserted it,” he says, though only time will tell how long it persists.

Can you extinguish a flame without touching it? This fun classic science activity is always a crowd pleaser. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air, so when it’s released near a candle, it replaces the air (which contains the oxygen needed for fire to burn) and puts out the flames!

WARNING – This activity involves fire and should be supervised or performed by adults

There are two easy ways to create enough CO2 to put out a small flame. You can create it with a baking soda and vinegar reaction or collect CO2 in a balloon placed on top of a bottle of fizzy water.

Simply place a balloon quickly on a bottle of fizzy water when it’s first opened. If you swirl and gently shake the bottle, the CO2 will collect in the balloon and inflate it.

An unopened bottle of fizzy water ( club soda or sparkling water )

Place the tea light on a heatproof surface and light it.

Remove the lid from the carbonated water bottle and quickly place the balloon over the opening.

Gently swirl and shake the bottle to release the trapped gas. You’ll see the bubbles rising, and the balloon will start to inflate.

Once the balloon stops inflating, carefully remove it from the bottle, pinching the end so no gas escapes.

Hold the balloon over the tea light and slowly release the carbon dioxide. The tea light should go out.

Carbon dioxide is released when vinegar and bicarbonate of soda react.

Place the tea light on a heatproof surface and light it.

Pour the vinegar into the jug and then add the bicarbonate of soda. You should see bubbles of gas (carbon dioxide) straight away.

Carefully tilt the jug over the tea light so the CO2 is near the flame. It should go out!

CO2 fire extinguishers are used for electrical fires. Fire needs oxygen to burn, but when carbon dioxide is sprayed over the fire, it covers the flames in a blanket of carbon dioxide, which starves the fire of oxygen and puts it out.

One advantage of using CO2 is that it doesn’t damage expensive equipment like water or foam.

When CO2 gas shoots out of a fire extinguisher, it’s so cold that it can turn into ice crystals.

They were victims, not abusers. That’s what they told police officers, attorneys, visitors and anyone else who would listen. It didn’t matter. The women were all taken to a sprawling immigration detention center in the woods of west Michigan and handed thin jumpsuits — red, to mark them as a high security risk.

Inside the North Lake Processing Center, they talked about the men whose calls to police landed them there. The boyfriends or husbands who threatened to get them deported and cut them off from their children. The men, or sometimes women, who hit them, pulled their hair or choked them, bruising their throats.

They didn’t know where to go for help before, but now, in the confines of a cold cellblock in a former prison, the women helped one another. If one spoke to somebody on the outside who could connect them with advice, legal aid or money for the commissary, she’d pass around a tablet used for calls so others could tell their stories.

Migladys Bermudez, an immigration attorney in southeast Michigan, remembers a call in May from a Peruvian woman detained at North Lake. Her name was Pilar, and she’d heard about Bermudez from a fellow detainee who had been a client. Pilar told Bermudez about the boyfriend she tried to escape before her arrest, when she used makeup to cover the marks she said he left on her face. But he had accused her of being the abuser, resulting in her arrest.

Pilar handed the tablet to a Cuban woman named Leya. Over the echoes of the other detainees’ chatter, she described how her boyfriend insulted her, isolated her and gave her a black eye. Then, after grabbing him by the arm during an argument, she was the one behind bars, separated from their nursing daughter. On video calls, the girl didn’t seem to recognize her mother anymore. She stopped calling her “Mamá.”

Bermudez was taken aback. All three women in that chain — the former client, Pilar and Leya — said they were trying to get out of abusive relationships when they were arrested by local police on domestic violence allegations. In all three cases, the charges were dropped or never filed at all. The U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained the first woman during a routine check-in a few months after her charges were dropped. The others were handed over to ICE by local officials.

It’s long been intimidating for immigrants who suffered abuse to call 911, regardless of who was in the White House. They knew — and their abusers knew — that their immigration status made them vulnerable. But previous administrations treated victims as just that, people who needed help, not as targets for deportation. Bipartisan coalitions in Congress have long supported the Violence Against Women Act, which creates a pathway toward legal status for immigrants who are victims of domestic violence or other serious crimes.

Now, under President Donald Trump, all bets are off. On inauguration day, his administration rescinded guidelines that largely prevented immigration agents from making arrests at sensitive locations — including domestic violence shelters and hospitals. About a week later, the administration eliminated key protections granted to VAWA applicants and other crime victims. For the first time in decades, immigration authorities could disregard those protections and more freely arrest, detain and deport immigrant victims.

Life became more harrowing in other ways for immigrants who were victims of domestic violence. Federal immigration agents made arrests outside specialized domestic violence courts. And the administration persuaded more and more local police and jails to carry out the president’s mass deportation campaign, sweeping still more immigrants into custody.

Even communities without these so-called 287(g) agreements are helping the government round people up. In west Michigan’s Kent County, where Pilar and Leya were arrested, the county jail turned over more immigrants to ICE in the first six months of 2026 than it did during the entire Biden administration.

Advocates across the country say they’re seeing signs of an alarming increase in the numbers of domestic violence victims who are detained and deported. And many immigrants are staying in abusive relationships, fearful of calling for help or even stepping outside their homes.

“It’s just probably the most dangerous time for immigrant women right now who are victims of domestic violence,” said Bermudez, who took on Leya’s immigration case.

The arrest and detention of domestic violence victims is, in part, an unanticipated consequence of an effort meant to keep them safe. In much of the country, law enforcement officers are encouraged — or even required — to make an arrest when they have probable cause to believe that a domestic violence assault has taken place. Sometimes victims are mistaken as the abuser and taken into custody. Then, if they’re immigrants, they can be turned over to ICE — even if they were never criminally charged.

There’s no comprehensive way to track how often this happens or how many domestic violence victims have been detained by ICE or deported. But ProPublica spoke with a half-dozen alleged victims who were locked up together in a single facility, North Lake, and reviewed police records about others who were there, too. Their stories offer a picture of why Congress created programs like VAWA in the first place, and what’s at stake when those guardrails are taken away.

A coalition of immigrant survivors and advocates have sued the Trump administration, saying changes to how immigrant victims are treated not only undermined public safety, but also broke promises enshrined in federal law and policy.

“We were seeing our clients be more scared, be systematically targeted, be arrested, and deported very quickly,” said Kathleen Rivas, supervising attorney of the survivors team at Public Counsel, a law firm that’s involved in the litigation.

In May, a federal judge ordered the government to restore protections while the case played out. The Trump administration has appealed the order.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said in a statement that ICE is targeting the “worst of the worst.” The real story, she added, is the “American women whose lives have been taken by illegal alien criminals.” A spokesperson for the Department of Homeland Security said in a statement that “we will never apologize for deporting illegal aliens, because that’s how we save lives.”

But Leya, Pilar and other immigrants we interviewed have no criminal records. And advocates said communities are less safe now because immigrants are afraid to call police when their partners hurt them. This gives more ammunition to the threats of their abusers, said Casey Carter Swegman, director of public policy at the Tahirih Justice Center, a national nonprofit that advocates for immigrant survivors of domestic violence, sexual assault and human trafficking.

“Certainly more immigrant survivors are feeling like the violence and abuse they experience behind the closed door is somehow more tolerable than the violence and abuse they could experience if they tried to leave,” she said.

The first time her boyfriend’s fist slammed into her face was in July 2025, Leya recalled. They were living in Florida with his relatives. Only a month before, Leya gave birth to their daughter.

One night, she said, he heard her laugh in the kitchen with his mother and a man his mother was dating. Leya’s boyfriend flew into a jealous rage, she said, and punched her a few feet from their sleeping baby.

Leya called 911 but hung up when the operator answered. She was afraid of what might happen. She was 26; her boyfriend was a decade her senior. Leya, a recent immigrant from Cuba, had applied for status and was in active immigration proceedings. She said he was a permanent resident, with permission to work in the U.S. and better command of English. She felt trapped.

For help in English or Spanish, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 800-799-7233 or text “start” to 88788.

Sheriff’s deputies arrived and spoke with Leya and her boyfriend. They took photos of her puffy and bruised face, with the white of her left eye bright red. Her boyfriend said the injury occurred during an “argument,” according to the report, but he didn’t specify how. He was arrested on suspicion of domestic battery.

Leya said her boyfriend’s mother threw her out that night. Unsure of where to go, she and her daughter moved in with another one of his relatives. Pursuing a criminal case felt daunting. She said his relatives urged her to drop the charges and reconcile with him. Eventually, she asked prosecutors to drop the charge because the battery took place in “a moment of impulse that he feels very sorry for,” records show. Her boyfriend didn’t enter a plea before the case was dismissed.

Like other immigrants interviewed for this story, Leya asked not to be fully identified because of her pending immigration case, and because she worries about repercussions for her and her daughter.

She and Pilar also asked that ProPublica not interview their alleged abusers because they’re afraid the men will interfere with their cases, hurt them or take away their children. To report their stories and get their partners’ perspective, we reviewed records from law enforcement, immigration court proceedings and habeas corpus petitions seeking their release from detention. We also interviewed attorneys, family and others who interacted with them.

Soon after the Florida arrest, Leya and her boyfriend moved to the city of Wyoming, Michigan, a suburb of Grand Rapids that’s a magnet for immigrants who come for jobs in manufacturing and food processing. The unpretentious city is dotted with temporary staffing agencies, money-wiring businesses and Mexican restaurants.

Leya said their relationship worsened. Her boyfriend pushed her, pulled her hair and broke her phone, she said. He made her delete her social media accounts and isolated her from friends and family. They fought about money and talked about breaking up. One night in April, during an argument, he called the police.

“I’m having problems with my wife,” he said in Spanish, according to a recording of the call. The operator asked if there had been any violence. “No, no, no. None,” he said. “Just a discussion.”

He told officers who came to their apartment complex that Leya got angry because he had talked to an ex-girlfriend, according to body camera footage and a police report. When he tried to leave, he said, Leya pushed him and grabbed his arm. Then she attempted to hang up his phone when he dialed 911.

He said he wasn’t injured, though he accused her of hitting him several times a few days earlier.

Officers interviewed his brother, who mentioned the arrest in Florida, according to bodycam footage. “They had a cycle of problems where she called the police for abuse and he was detained for three days,” he said in Spanish. “My brother is trying to avoid having that happen again.”

The police report doesn’t mention the earlier arrest.

Footage of the interview with Leya was redacted due to state privacy laws, in part because she was interviewed inside their residence, unlike her boyfriend, who was interviewed outside. According to the police report, she “minimized” the argument and said there was no physical contact. She acknowledged trying to hang up the phone.

In an interview with ProPublica, Leya said they argued when she learned he sent money to his ex-girlfriend, who is the mother of his son. At the same time, she said, he had told her he couldn’t afford to buy diapers for their daughter. She said she has never hit him.

Officers arrested Leya on suspicion of domestic assault and interfering with a 911 call. They didn’t arrest her boyfriend or seek charges against him. Leya said she didn’t tell officers she was a victim of abuse because she thought she’d be able to tell a judge.

The Wyoming Police Department’s written policy encourages officers to make an arrest in incidents involving domestic violence “when there is reasonable cause to believe an assault was committed.”

Arrests demonstrate that “violence in the home is recognized as a serious crime,” according to Michigan’s law enforcement training manual on domestic violence. This approach is common across the country. Twenty-four states require an arrest when there’s probable cause of a domestic assault.

But domestic violence scenes can be chaotic, with both partners accusing each other of being the aggressor. It can be difficult for officers to understand what happened, said Erin Ross, a former domestic violence prosecutor who works at the national nonprofit Vera Institute of Justice. Sometimes, the wrong person ends up in handcuffs.

“The complexity of DV situations can thus lead to survivors being criminalized by the very system meant to protect them,” Ross and her co-authors wrote in a 2025 report about the effects of arrests on domestic violence survivors.

The Vera report doesn’t touch on immigration. But Ross and a dozen other domestic violence advocates across the country told ProPublica that immigrants who don’t speak English or understand their rights are, under the Trump administration, especially vulnerable to an arrest that can have irreversible consequences.

ProPublica reviewed hundreds of habeas corpus petitions filed nationwide since January 2025, in which immigrants challenged their ICE detention on constitutional grounds. We found dozens of petitioners who were arrested on suspicion of domestic violence and turned over to ICE, even though they were never charged or their charges were dropped. Using jail and police records from Florida and Michigan, as well as news reports, we identified even more cases.

In their petitions, several immigrants said that officers mistook them for the aggressor. The underlying arrest records show that officers often had to make close calls in messy cases where it was unclear who was the victim and who was the aggressor. Either way, under Trump, there’s little grace for immigrants.

In one case in Florida, a state where sheriff’s offices cooperate with immigration authorities, a deputy came upon a Venezuelan couple arguing in the street in November 2025. In his report, the deputy said the woman pulled at her husband’s pants, necklace and arm, leaving marks on his body. The woman said her husband had taken her keys and phone. She didn’t want to be left stranded, she said, so she ran after him.

She was charged with misdemeanor battery; her husband wasn’t charged. The woman was turned over to ICE while the case was pending. Prosecutors later dropped charges. The sheriff’s report didn’t mention any allegations of prior domestic violence, but the woman later said that, during their marriage, her husband had beaten her and threatened to kill her. In her habeas corpus petition, she said she was “afraid to report the abuse to the police.”

The woman was released in May, but during the five months she was incarcerated, her husband took their daughters out of state, her attorney told ProPublica. She still hasn’t seen the girls in person.

For Leya, it was a shock to find herself handcuffed, sitting in the back of a police cruiser, headed toward the Kent County jail. She was silent during the 15-minute ride, unable to make sense of what had just happened: “I had suffered from domestic violence,” she said. “But the one who ended up in trouble was me.”

The next day, a detective asked the prosecutor’s office to file charges against her. Prosecutors declined to do so, saying there was insufficient evidence, there were no injuries and Leya hadn’t successfully interfered with the phone call.

Leya had no criminal history, apart from this arrest, according to a DHS record. But her fingerprints had been run against state and federal databases, flagging ICE officials who could match them with her prints from when she arrived in the U.S. In addition, Leya said, a jail official asked about her status, and she told him she was waiting for an immigration court hearing.

ICE issued a detainer, essentially asking the jail to hold Leya until agents could pick her up. Kent County officials honored their request. A day later, Leya was at the detention center in the woods.

She cried in despair over her daughter. They’d never been separated. The 80 miles between Wyoming and North Lake seemed infinite. Her breasts ached from the drying milk. Leya wondered whether she’d be deported and if their separation would be permanent.

From a narrow window in her cell, she saw people outside holding signs, protesting ICE. She could hear their chants in English. Though she had trouble understanding them, she felt comforted by the care of strangers.

Inside, she familiarized herself with the routines. She spent most of her time in a large, open room with other women in red. Cameras eyed them as they talked about their children and upcoming court dates. They shared contact information for relatives in their home countries in case one of them got deported and had nowhere to go. To pass the hours, they watched telenovelas, played rounds of Uno and pieced together jigsaw puzzles of forests and farms.

Leya grew close to a few women from Latin America and Eastern Europe who said they, too, had been in abusive relationships. Several had called police for help, records show. A Honduran woman said her partner tried to choke her. A Dominican woman said her husband, a U.S. citizen, hit her, pulled her hair and chased her with a stick.

Then there was Pilar, Leya’s first cellmate. The asylum seeker from Peru had also been arrested on suspicion of domestic violence in the city of Wyoming.

Pilar, her boyfriend and her teenage daughter called 911 five times in the 15 months before her arrest, records show. On one occasion, Pilar’s daughter said she worried that the boyfriend would hurt her mom; according to bodycam footage, Pilar assured officers that everything was fine. On another, the man said he experienced mental abuse and wanted to find a shelter. On yet another, Pilar said she just wanted him to leave.

None of these calls ended in an arrest. Officers ensured that the boyfriend had another place to stay when he sought help, and they told Pilar to call back if he tried to return and hurt her, according to the footage. They advised the couple to go to civil court for future disputes over money and property.

In an interview with ProPublica from North Lake, Pilar, 48, said that her boyfriend took financial advantage of her and physically assaulted her. And in her habeas corpus petition, she described the relationship as one of “physical, mental, psychological, economic abuse.” Her sister took a photo of the bruises around Pilar’s neck and feared the worst. “I would tell her to leave that man,” she said.

Pilar said she was ashamed to go to her job at a food-processing plant with bruises on her face. She wondered what her co-workers would think. She distanced herself from relatives and friends. Pilar said she never told the police the full story because she worried about the possible repercussions — not for herself, but for her boyfriend. She didn’t want him to be deported. Despite everything, she loved him and felt he loved her.

One night in October 2025, her boyfriend drove up to their apartment complex in a rental car. The couple argued in the parking lot. He called 911.

“I need police,” he said over and over in English.

Pilar called moments later: “This man is leaving my house and he’s not giving me my keys,” she screamed in Spanish. “This man has hurt me. He has scratched my whole arm.”

When officers arrived, they separated the couple. The boyfriend said that Pilar slapped his head, grabbed his shirt and tried to pull him out of the car while he was in the driver’s seat, according to bodycam footage and the police report. One side of his T-shirt was torn. He said he never assaulted Pilar and any injuries were her own fault. “I’ve never hit her,” he said in Spanish.

He told officers that he’d become a U.S. permanent resident two months earlier and that Pilar was jealous. “She is making this up because she knows she can get papers,” he said.

Pilar insisted she was the victim. She showed officers the scratch marks on her arm and said her boyfriend was to blame. She begged them to make him return the apartment keys, according to bodycam footage and a police report. “I’ve been trying to kick him out for a while and he won’t leave, and he doesn’t want to help pay the rent,” she said in Spanish.

An officer asked how her boyfriend’s shirt got ripped. Pilar said she grabbed onto him when he started driving away in order to avoid being dragged by the car. The officer asked her several more times about the ripped shirt. Pilar told him she wanted to speak to a lawyer.

In their report, police noted that the boyfriend had called 911 first. His shirt was ripped. Pilar’s wasn’t. If the boyfriend had thrown her down, the officer wrote, then her shirt should have been ripped because he was “much taller and heavier (around 80lbs more).”

Her boyfriend wasn’t arrested. Pilar was.

Wyoming Public Safety Chief Kip Snyder declined to speak about the arrests of Pilar or Leya. In an email to ProPublica, he said officers don’t “participate in enforcing federal immigration laws. We encourage trust and cooperation with all residents and aim to ensure that everyone feels safe reporting crimes or seeking assistance.”

Shortly after Pilar’s arrest, a police detective asked the prosecutor’s office to file domestic violence charges against both her and her boyfriend.

Prosecutors declined to file any charges, citing insufficient evidence. Pilar wasn’t released. Apart from this arrest, she had no criminal history, according to DHS records. She also had a pending asylum case.

Still, ICE put a detainer on her and jail officials turned her over.

A few years earlier, Kent County Sheriff Michelle Lajoye-Young said a handoff like this wouldn’t happen.

In 2019, she announced that deputies would stop automatically honoring detainers and instead require warrants signed by a federal judge or magistrate. The move came after a U.S. citizen — a Marine combat veteran — was transferred from her jail into ICE custody.

“There needed to be a judicial oversight inserted into that process to give us the assurance that somebody wasn’t erroneously detained without due process,” Lajoye-Young said around that time.

Trump called Kent a “sanctuary jurisdiction.” The label haunted the county, which in the 2016 election voted for Trump. A national think tank that aims to reduce immigration put Kent County on its list of sanctuary communities, while Republican state lawmakers tried to compel its cooperation with federal authorities.

Yet it’s unclear whether Lajoye-Young ever delivered on her promise. ProPublica requested copies of all department policies, procedures, memos and instructions on how it handles ICE detainers since 2018. None said a judicial warrant was needed. ProPublica also asked for copies of judicial warrants for Pilar and Leya. The county said no such records exist.

Lajoye-Young, who retired last week, declined interview requests. Sgt. Scott Dietrich, a spokesperson for the sheriff’s office, didn’t directly answer questions about whether the policy was ever implemented. But in a statement he said that detainers are “handled under applicable law and established policy, consistent with our commitment to public safety, due process, and fair and impartial enforcement.”

Gema Lowe, an organizer with the local chapter of an immigrant rights group, said the sheriff had backtracked on her promise and wasn’t being forthright. “I don’t understand why they’re being secretive,” she said.

The shift deepened the mistrust among immigrants in a county with one of the state’s fastest-growing immigrant populations. Lowe, an immigrant herself from Mexico, said she advises victims to think twice before reporting domestic violence to the police because of the risk of arrest and deportation, while pointing them to other local resources like shelters.

She and others said the stakes are especially high now. ICE arrests have skyrocketed under Trump. Nobody is off limits, including immigrants with pending asylum claims and no criminal records.

And more local authorities are helping ICE make arrests. In January 2025, fewer than 150 state and local law enforcement agencies had signed 287(g) agreements with DHS to help carry out the president’s deportation agenda. That number has ballooned to more than 2,600 today.

“Partnerships with law enforcement are critical to having the resources we need to arrest criminal illegal aliens across the country,” a DHS spokesperson said. “We have had tremendous success when local law enforcement works together with us.”

Kent County doesn’t have a 287(g) agreement. Still, the jail hands over the vast majority of people with detainers to ICE, records show. Dozens of people who were turned over to ICE this year had their criminal charges dropped, or, like Pilar and Leya, were never charged in the first place.

Dietrich said the dismissal of a criminal charge “does not, by itself, mean the original arrest lacked probable cause.” The sheriff’s office also doesn’t “determine whether criminal charges are ultimately prosecuted, nor does it determine whether an individual is deported,” he said.

DHS told ProPublica in a statement that both Leya and Pilar “illegally entered the country” in 2022 and that both “will receive due process” in their immigration proceedings.

Kent County Prosecutor Chris Becker said he hadn’t heard of victims getting detained by ICE after calling the police. In his experience, he said, more perpetrators with active cases get deported.

Becker said he tries to get the message out that his team serves all victims, regardless of their immigration status. He’s aware that immigrants are reluctant to seek help. His own staff has told him that victims are afraid to testify or file restraining orders. They don’t want their partners — often the breadwinners and the fathers of their children — to be deported.

“There’s been a chilling impact to women coming forward in domestic violence situations due to what’s going on,” Becker said.

As the months passed at North Lake, Leya watched as women she befriended started to leave.

Some were deported, including at least two Mexican women who were also arrested in Kent County on suspicion of domestic violence. Others were released. New women took their cells. Some told Leya that they, too, had been arrested after 911 calls about domestic violence.

Leya fought for her freedom. She met with volunteers from Kent County, who raised money to hire her lawyers and pay for a required physical exam and fees. Even her ex-boyfriend, the one who she said gave her a black eye, wrote a letter on her behalf to help her case, calling her “a calm person.” He explained that he had called the police after their argument “escalated” and that “problems are situations that can happen in any relationship.”

Leya, he added, “deserves a second opportunity to remain in this country and be close to our daughter, as our baby truly needs her very much.” While Leya was locked up, their little girl celebrated her first birthday, got her first teeth and took her first steps.

At virtual hearings, Leya sat with her arms crossed, her dark, wavy hair neatly braided in cornrows and wearing an oversized coat over her red uniform. She tried to follow the discussion of people who seemed to have her life in their hands.

In late August, a judge granted Leya bond. While her immigration case played out, she could be free.

It was a profound relief. At the same time, it felt awful to leave behind her friends. It seemed like some of them were never getting out. Pilar was approaching a year in detention.

Four months after her arrest, Leya walked out of the old prison. She thought about how she would live on her own now and support her daughter.

She promised herself she wouldn’t go back to the man who hurt her.

Migladys Bermudez, an immigration attorney in southeast Michigan, remembers a call in May from a Peruvian woman detained at North Lake. Her name was Pilar, and she’d heard about Bermudez from a fellow detainee who had been a client. Pilar told Bermudez about the boyfriend she tried to escape before her arrest, when she used makeup to cover the marks she said he left on her face. But he had accused her of being the abuser, resulting in her arrest.

Pilar handed the tablet to a Cuban woman named Leya. Over the echoes of the other detainees’ chatter, she described how her boyfriend insulted her, isolated her and gave her a black eye. Then, after grabbing him by the arm during an argument, she was the one behind bars, separated from their nursing daughter. On video calls, the girl didn’t seem to recognize her mother anymore. She stopped calling her “Mamá.”

Bermudez was taken aback. All three women in that chain — the former client, Pilar and Leya — said they were trying to get out of abusive relationships when they were arrested by local police on domestic violence allegations. In all three cases, the charges were dropped or never filed at all. The U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained the first woman during a routine check-in a few months after her charges were dropped. The others were handed over to ICE by local officials.

It’s long been intimidating for immigrants who suffered abuse to call 911, regardless of who was in the White House. They knew — and their abusers knew — that their immigration status made them vulnerable. But previous administrations treated victims as just that, people who needed help, not as targets for deportation. Bipartisan coalitions in Congress have long supported the Violence Against Women Act, which creates a pathway toward legal status for immigrants who are victims of domestic violence or other serious crimes.

Now, under President Donald Trump, all bets are off. On inauguration day, his administration rescinded guidelines that largely prevented immigration agents from making arrests at sensitive locations — including domestic violence shelters and hospitals. About a week later, the administration eliminated key protections granted to VAWA applicants and other crime victims. For the first time in decades, immigration authorities could disregard those protections and more freely arrest, detain and deport immigrant victims.

Life became more harrowing in other ways for immigrants who were victims of domestic violence. Federal immigration agents made arrests outside specialized domestic violence courts. And the administration persuaded more and more local police and jails to carry out the president’s mass deportation campaign, sweeping still more immigrants into custody.

Even communities without these so-called 287(g) agreements are helping the government round people up. In west Michigan’s Kent County, where Pilar and Leya were arrested, the county jail turned over more immigrants to ICE in the first six months of 2026 than it did during the entire Biden administration.

Advocates across the country say they’re seeing signs of an alarming increase in the numbers of domestic violence victims who are detained and deported. And many immigrants are staying in abusive relationships, fearful of calling for help or even stepping outside their homes.

“It’s just probably the most dangerous time for immigrant women right now who are victims of domestic violence,” said Bermudez, who took on Leya’s immigration case.

The arrest and detention of domestic violence victims is, in part, an unanticipated consequence of an effort meant to keep them safe. In much of the country, law enforcement officers are encouraged — or even required — to make an arrest when they have probable cause to believe that a domestic violence assault has taken place. Sometimes victims are mistaken as the abuser and taken into custody. Then, if they’re immigrants, they can be turned over to ICE — even if they were never criminally charged.

There’s no comprehensive way to track how often this happens or how many domestic violence victims have been detained by ICE or deported. But ProPublica spoke with a half-dozen alleged victims who were locked up together in a single facility, North Lake, and reviewed police records about others who were there, too. Their stories offer a picture of why Congress created programs like VAWA in the first place, and what’s at stake when those guardrails are taken away.

A coalition of immigrant survivors and advocates have sued the Trump administration, saying changes to how immigrant victims are treated not only undermined public safety, but also broke promises enshrined in federal law and policy.

“We were seeing our clients be more scared, be systematically targeted, be arrested, and deported very quickly,” said Kathleen Rivas, supervising attorney of the survivors team at Public Counsel, a law firm that’s involved in the litigation.

In May, a federal judge ordered the government to restore protections while the case played out. The Trump administration has appealed the order.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said in a statement that ICE is targeting the “worst of the worst.” The real story, she added, is the “American women whose lives have been taken by illegal alien criminals.” A spokesperson for the Department of Homeland Security said in a statement that “we will never apologize for deporting illegal aliens, because that’s how we save lives.”

But Leya, Pilar and other immigrants we interviewed have no criminal records. And advocates said communities are less safe now because immigrants are afraid to call police when their partners hurt them. This gives more ammunition to the threats of their abusers, said Casey Carter Swegman, director of public policy at the Tahirih Justice Center, a national nonprofit that advocates for immigrant survivors of domestic violence, sexual assault and human trafficking.

“Certainly more immigrant survivors are feeling like the violence and abuse they experience behind the closed door is somehow more tolerable than the violence and abuse they could experience if they tried to leave,” she said.

The first time her boyfriend’s fist slammed into her face was in July 2025, Leya recalled. They were living in Florida with his relatives. Only a month before, Leya gave birth to their daughter.

One night, she said, he heard her laugh in the kitchen with his mother and a man his mother was dating. Leya’s boyfriend flew into a jealous rage, she said, and punched her a few feet from their sleeping baby.

Leya called 911 but hung up when the operator answered. She was afraid of what might happen. She was 26; her boyfriend was a decade her senior. Leya, a recent immigrant from Cuba, had applied for status and was in active immigration proceedings. She said he was a permanent resident, with permission to work in the U.S. and better command of English. She felt trapped.

Sheriff’s deputies arrived and spoke with Leya and her boyfriend. They took photos of her puffy and bruised face, with the white of her left eye bright red. Her boyfriend said the injury occurred during an “argument,” according to the report, but he didn’t specify how. He was arrested on suspicion of domestic battery.

Leya said her boyfriend’s mother threw her out that night. Unsure of where to go, she and her daughter moved in with another one of his relatives. Pursuing a criminal case felt daunting. She said his relatives urged her to drop the charges and reconcile with him. Eventually, she asked prosecutors to drop the charge because the battery took place in “a moment of impulse that he feels very sorry for,” records show. Her boyfriend didn’t enter a plea before the case was dismissed.

Like other immigrants interviewed for this story, Leya asked not to be fully identified because of her pending immigration case, and because she worries about repercussions for her and her daughter.

She and Pilar also asked that ProPublica not interview their alleged abusers because they’re afraid the men will interfere with their cases, hurt them or take away their children. To report their stories and get their partners’ perspective, we reviewed records from law enforcement, immigration court proceedings and habeas corpus petitions seeking their release from detention. We also interviewed attorneys, family and others who interacted with them.

Soon after the Florida arrest, Leya and her boyfriend moved to the city of Wyoming, Michigan, a suburb of Grand Rapids that’s a magnet for immigrants who come for jobs in manufacturing and food processing. The unpretentious city is dotted with temporary staffing agencies, money-wiring businesses and Mexican restaurants.

Leya said their relationship worsened. Her boyfriend pushed her, pulled her hair and broke her phone, she said. He made her delete her social media accounts and isolated her from friends and family. They fought about money and talked about breaking up. One night in April, during an argument, he called the police.

“I’m having problems with my wife,” he said in Spanish, according to a recording of the call. The operator asked if there had been any violence. “No, no, no. None,” he said. “Just a discussion.”

He told officers who came to their apartment complex that Leya got angry because he had talked to an ex-girlfriend, according to body camera footage and a police report. When he tried to leave, he said, Leya pushed him and grabbed his arm. Then she attempted to hang up his phone when he dialed 911.

He said he wasn’t injured, though he accused her of hitting him several times a few days earlier.

Officers interviewed his brother, who mentioned the arrest in Florida, according to bodycam footage. “They had a cycle of problems where she called the police for abuse and he was detained for three days,” he said in Spanish. “My brother is trying to avoid having that happen again.”

The police report doesn’t mention the earlier arrest.

Footage of the interview with Leya was redacted due to state privacy laws, in part because she was interviewed inside their residence, unlike her boyfriend, who was interviewed outside. According to the police report, she “minimized” the argument and said there was no physical contact. She acknowledged trying to hang up the phone.

In an interview with ProPublica, Leya said they argued when she learned he sent money to his ex-girlfriend, who is the mother of his son. At the same time, she said, he had told her he couldn’t afford to buy diapers for their daughter. She said she has never hit him.

Officers arrested Leya on suspicion of domestic assault and interfering with a 911 call. They didn’t arrest her boyfriend or seek charges against him. Leya said she didn’t tell officers she was a victim of abuse because she thought she’d be able to tell a judge.

The Wyoming Police Department’s written policy encourages officers to make an arrest in incidents involving domestic violence “when there is reasonable cause to believe an assault was committed.”

Arrests demonstrate that “violence in the home is recognized as a serious crime,” according to Michigan’s law enforcement training manual on domestic violence. This approach is common across the country. Twenty-four states require an arrest when there’s probable cause of a domestic assault.

But domestic violence scenes can be chaotic, with both partners accusing each other of being the aggressor. It can be difficult for officers to understand what happened, said Erin Ross, a former domestic violence prosecutor who works at the national nonprofit Vera Institute of Justice. Sometimes, the wrong person ends up in handcuffs.

“The complexity of DV situations can thus lead to survivors being criminalized by the very system meant to protect them,” Ross and her co-authors wrote in a 2025 report about the effects of arrests on domestic violence survivors.

The Vera report doesn’t touch on immigration. But Ross and a dozen other domestic violence advocates across the country told ProPublica that immigrants who don’t speak English or understand their rights are, under the Trump administration, especially vulnerable to an arrest that can have irreversible consequences.

ProPublica reviewed hundreds of habeas corpus petitions filed nationwide since January 2025, in which immigrants challenged their ICE detention on constitutional grounds. We found dozens of petitioners who were arrested on suspicion of domestic violence and turned over to ICE, even though they were never charged or their charges were dropped. Using jail and police records from Florida and Michigan, as well as news reports, we identified even more cases.

In their petitions, several immigrants said that officers mistook them for the aggressor. The underlying arrest records show that officers often had to make close calls in messy cases where it was unclear who was the victim and who was the aggressor. Either way, under Trump, there’s little grace for immigrants.

In one case in Florida, a state where sheriff’s offices cooperate with immigration authorities, a deputy came upon a Venezuelan couple arguing in the street in November 2025. In his report, the deputy said the woman pulled at her husband’s pants, necklace and arm, leaving marks on his body. The woman said her husband had taken her keys and phone. She didn’t want to be left stranded, she said, so she ran after him.

She was charged with misdemeanor battery; her husband wasn’t charged. The woman was turned over to ICE while the case was pending. Prosecutors later dropped charges. The sheriff’s report didn’t mention any allegations of prior domestic violence, but the woman later said that, during their marriage, her husband had beaten her and threatened to kill her. In her habeas corpus petition, she said she was “afraid to report the abuse to the police.”

The woman was released in May, but during the five months she was incarcerated, her husband took their daughters out of state, her attorney told ProPublica. She still hasn’t seen the girls in person.

For Leya, it was a shock to find herself handcuffed, sitting in the back of a police cruiser, headed toward the Kent County jail. She was silent during the 15-minute ride, unable to make sense of what had just happened: “I had suffered from domestic violence,” she said. “But the one who ended up in trouble was me.”

The next day, a detective asked the prosecutor’s office to file charges against her. Prosecutors declined to do so, saying there was insufficient evidence, there were no injuries and Leya hadn’t successfully interfered with the phone call.

Leya had no criminal history, apart from this arrest, according to a DHS record. But her fingerprints had been run against state and federal databases, flagging ICE officials who could match them with her prints from when she arrived in the U.S. In addition, Leya said, a jail official asked about her status, and she told him she was waiting for an immigration court hearing.

ICE issued a detainer, essentially asking the jail to hold Leya until agents could pick her up. Kent County officials honored their request. A day later, Leya was at the detention center in the woods.

She cried in despair over her daughter. They’d never been separated. The 80 miles between Wyoming and North Lake seemed infinite. Her breasts ached from the drying milk. Leya wondered whether she’d be deported and if their separation would be permanent.

From a narrow window in her cell, she saw people outside holding signs, protesting ICE. She could hear their chants in English. Though she had trouble understanding them, she felt comforted by the care of strangers.

Inside, she familiarized herself with the routines. She spent most of her time in a large, open room with other women in red. Cameras eyed them as they talked about their children and upcoming court dates. They shared contact information for relatives in their home countries in case one of them got deported and had nowhere to go. To pass the hours, they watched telenovelas, played rounds of Uno and pieced together jigsaw puzzles of forests and farms.

Leya grew close to a few women from Latin America and Eastern Europe who said they, too, had been in abusive relationships. Several had called police for help, records show. A Honduran woman said her partner tried to choke her. A Dominican woman said her husband, a U.S. citizen, hit her, pulled her hair and chased her with a stick.

Then there was Pilar, Leya’s first cellmate. The asylum seeker from Peru had also been arrested on suspicion of domestic violence in the city of Wyoming.

Pilar, her boyfriend and her teenage daughter called 911 five times in the 15 months before her arrest, records show. On one occasion, Pilar’s daughter said she worried that the boyfriend would hurt her mom; according to bodycam footage, Pilar assured officers that everything was fine. On another, the man said he experienced mental abuse and wanted to find a shelter. On yet another, Pilar said she just wanted him to leave.

None of these calls ended in an arrest. Officers ensured that the boyfriend had another place to stay when he sought help, and they told Pilar to call back if he tried to return and hurt her, according to the footage. They advised the couple to go to civil court for future disputes over money and property.

In an interview with ProPublica from North Lake, Pilar, 48, said that her boyfriend took financial advantage of her and physically assaulted her. And in her habeas corpus petition, she described the relationship as one of “physical, mental, psychological, economic abuse.” Her sister took a photo of the bruises around Pilar’s neck and feared the worst. “I would tell her to leave that man,” she said.

Pilar said she was ashamed to go to her job at a food-processing plant with bruises on her face. She wondered what her co-workers would think. She distanced herself from relatives and friends. Pilar said she never told the police the full story because she worried about the possible repercussions — not for herself, but for her boyfriend. She didn’t want him to be deported. Despite everything, she loved him and felt he loved her.

One night in October 2025, her boyfriend drove up to their apartment complex in a rental car. The couple argued in the parking lot. He called 911.

“I need police,” he said over and over in English.

Pilar called moments later: “This man is leaving my house and he’s not giving me my keys,” she screamed in Spanish. “This man has hurt me. He has scratched my whole arm.”

When officers arrived, they separated the couple. The boyfriend said that Pilar slapped his head, grabbed his shirt and tried to pull him out of the car while he was in the driver’s seat, according to bodycam footage and the police report. One side of his T-shirt was torn. He said he never assaulted Pilar and any injuries were her own fault. “I’ve never hit her,” he said in Spanish.

He told officers that he’d become a U.S. permanent resident two months earlier and that Pilar was jealous. “She is making this up because she knows she can get papers,” he said.

Pilar insisted she was the victim. She showed officers the scratch marks on her arm and said her boyfriend was to blame. She begged them to make him return the apartment keys, according to bodycam footage and a police report. “I’ve been trying to kick him out for a while and he won’t leave, and he doesn’t want to help pay the rent,” she said in Spanish.

An officer asked how her boyfriend’s shirt got ripped. Pilar said she grabbed onto him when he started driving away in order to avoid being dragged by the car. The officer asked her several more times about the ripped shirt. Pilar told him she wanted to speak to a lawyer.

In their report, police noted that the boyfriend had called 911 first. His shirt was ripped. Pilar’s wasn’t. If the boyfriend had thrown her down, the officer wrote, then her shirt should have been ripped because he was “much taller and heavier (around 80lbs more).”

Her boyfriend wasn’t arrested. Pilar was.

Wyoming Public Safety Chief Kip Snyder declined to speak about the arrests of Pilar or Leya. In an email to ProPublica, he said officers don’t “participate in enforcing federal immigration laws. We encourage trust and cooperation with all residents and aim to ensure that everyone feels safe reporting crimes or seeking assistance.”

Shortly after Pilar’s arrest, a police detective asked the prosecutor’s office to file domestic violence charges against both her and her boyfriend.

Prosecutors declined to file any charges, citing insufficient evidence. Pilar wasn’t released. Apart from this arrest, she had no criminal history, according to DHS records. She also had a pending asylum case.

Still, ICE put a detainer on her and jail officials turned her over.

A few years earlier, Kent County Sheriff Michelle Lajoye-Young said a handoff like this wouldn’t happen.

In 2019, she announced that deputies would stop automatically honoring detainers and instead require warrants signed by a federal judge or magistrate. The move came after a U.S. citizen — a Marine combat veteran — was transferred from her jail into ICE custody.

“There needed to be a judicial oversight inserted into that process to give us the assurance that somebody wasn’t erroneously detained without due process,” Lajoye-Young said around that time.

Trump called Kent a “sanctuary jurisdiction.” The label haunted the county, which in the 2016 election voted for Trump. A national think tank that aims to reduce immigration put Kent County on its list of sanctuary communities, while Republican state lawmakers tried to compel its cooperation with federal authorities.

Yet it’s unclear whether Lajoye-Young ever delivered on her promise. ProPublica requested copies of all department policies, procedures, memos and instructions on how it handles ICE detainers since 2018. None said a judicial warrant was needed. ProPublica also asked for copies of judicial warrants for Pilar and Leya. The county said no such records exist.

Lajoye-Young, who retired last week, declined interview requests. Sgt. Scott Dietrich, a spokesperson for the sheriff’s office, didn’t directly answer questions about whether the policy was ever implemented. But in a statement he said that detainers are “handled under applicable law and established policy, consistent with our commitment to public safety, due process, and fair and impartial enforcement.”

Gema Lowe, an organizer with the local chapter of an immigrant rights group, said the sheriff had backtracked on her promise and wasn’t being forthright. “I don’t understand why they’re being secretive,” she said.

The shift deepened the mistrust among immigrants in a county with one of the state’s fastest-growing immigrant populations. Lowe, an immigrant herself from Mexico, said she advises victims to think twice before reporting domestic violence to the police because of the risk of arrest and deportation, while pointing them to other local resources like shelters.

She and others said the stakes are especially high now. ICE arrests have skyrocketed under Trump. Nobody is off limits, including immigrants with pending asylum claims and no criminal records.

And more local authorities are helping ICE make arrests. In January 2025, fewer than 150 state and local law enforcement agencies had signed 287(g) agreements with DHS to help carry out the president’s deportation agenda. That number has ballooned to more than 2,600 today.

“Partnerships with law enforcement are critical to having the resources we need to arrest criminal illegal aliens across the country,” a DHS spokesperson said. “We have had tremendous success when local law enforcement works together with us.”

Kent County doesn’t have a 287(g) agreement. Still, the jail hands over the vast majority of people with detainers to ICE, records show. Dozens of people who were turned over to ICE this year had their criminal charges dropped, or, like Pilar and Leya, were never charged in the first place.

Dietrich said the dismissal of a criminal charge “does not, by itself, mean the original arrest lacked probable cause.” The sheriff’s office also doesn’t “determine whether criminal charges are ultimately prosecuted, nor does it determine whether an individual is deported,” he said.

DHS told ProPublica in a statement that both Leya and Pilar “illegally entered the country” in 2022 and that both “will receive due process” in their immigration proceedings.

Kent County Prosecutor Chris Becker said he hadn’t heard of victims getting detained by ICE after calling the police. In his experience, he said, more perpetrators with active cases get deported.

Becker said he tries to get the message out that his team serves all victims, regardless of their immigration status. He’s aware that immigrants are reluctant to seek help. His own staff has told him that victims are afraid to testify or file restraining orders. They don’t want their partners — often the breadwinners and the fathers of their children — to be deported.

“There’s been a chilling impact to women coming forward in domestic violence situations due to what’s going on,” Becker said.

As the months passed at North Lake, Leya watched as women she befriended started to leave.

Some were deported, including at least two Mexican women who were also arrested in Kent County on suspicion of domestic violence. Others were released. New women took their cells. Some told Leya that they, too, had been arrested after 911 calls about domestic violence.

Leya fought for her freedom. She met with volunteers from Kent County, who raised money to hire her lawyers and pay for a required physical exam and fees. Even her ex-boyfriend, the one who she said gave her a black eye, wrote a letter on her behalf to help her case, calling her “a calm person.” He explained that he had called the police after their argument “escalated” and that “problems are situations that can happen in any relationship.”

Leya, he added, “deserves a second opportunity to remain in this country and be close to our daughter, as our baby truly needs her very much.” While Leya was locked up, their little girl celebrated her first birthday, got her first teeth and took her first steps.

At virtual hearings, Leya sat with her arms crossed, her dark, wavy hair neatly braided in cornrows and wearing an oversized coat over her red uniform. She tried to follow the discussion of people who seemed to have her life in their hands.

In late August, a judge granted Leya bond. While her immigration case played out, she could be free.

It was a profound relief. At the same time, it felt awful to leave behind her friends. It seemed like some of them were never getting out. Pilar was approaching a year in detention.

Four months after her arrest, Leya walked out of the old prison. She thought about how she would live on her own now and support her daughter.

She promised herself she wouldn’t go back to the man who hurt her.

As we talk about the cities we’re familiar with, I realize how much spontaneity is tied to walkability. I’ve always lived in places where you can hoof it on a whim. On a night out, the city colludes in what happens next: Singapore, New York, Berlin.

For all its sweep and chaos, my impression of L.A. is linear, contained: You leave home and get in a car to go somewhere specific. You meet the person you arranged to see. You get back in the car. The only extemporaneous variable is traffic.

“You haven’t decided if you’ll stay, but you’re already complaining about traffic?”

Neither of us has a car, which makes it safe enough for me to joke that he should be my passenger princess.

And yet, perhaps something has already started to shift. I look through the open window, catching the eye of the woman who had, moments earlier, saved me from walking into a date with my fly down. We share a private, conspiratorial smile. My New York neighbors barely said hello; in Singapore, a stranger would probably politely look away rather than risk embarrassing you.

Here, outside Capri, a stranger crossed the distance with ease. For all the urban density I’m used to, this feels strangely intimate.

Jonny orders a martini next. Gin with a twist, I note.

Stop it, I tell myself in the next breath. Why are you already clocking how he likes it?

My Silver Lake sublet has a panther-shaped coffee table and David Bowie records on display, though the player isn’t connected to speakers. I extend my stay so I can keep seeing more of the city and — let’s face it — him, the drinker of Corpse Revivers.

I give myself plenty of time to take the 4 to Chinatown.

This time, I’m early. I’m meeting Andres and Lily, in town from Boston. Andres Sevtsuk leads the City Form Lab at MIT. We met in Singapore when he taught urban planning there; now he’s working with Metro to improve walking experiences in L.A. ahead of the Olympics.

Over the years, Chinatowns have offered me ephemeral homecomings in city after city.

As we drink fresh coconuts from a roadside stand, Andres tells me our social sensory apparatus works best below 10 miles an hour.

“Past that threshold, our ability to perceive detail and read socially meaningful cues falls off fast,” he says as a Mustang whizzes by. We talk about how when the vast majority of movement in a city happens by car, there are fewer occasions and grounds for unplanned encounters to materialize — and how, given the statistical scarcity, that feels all the more precious here.

I tell them about the last time I was in Chinatown, at Café Triste with Jonny’s bandmates. Four of us start at a table inside the bohemian cave à manger by the mirrored bar, sharing an inviting fig, speck, ricotta dish and a tomato stone fruit panzanella. As more people show up, we spill outside with another chilled red.

I drift from the band toward a stylish knot of people who turn out to be dancers. The lady in the asymmetrically-cropped black top that perfectly frames her baby bump has performed with Beyoncé; the androgynous hapa with the paisley silk bandanna actually worked a year in Singapore, where I was born and raised. We bond over Hainanese chicken rice before he tells me his local booker had wanted his waist-length hair shorn: more masculine, more bookable.

I’m apologizing on behalf of my conservative countryman when a dead ringer for Tadanobu Asano in “Ichi the Killer” passes on the street and waves at the dancer, who does a double take.

It’s an ex-colleague from his stint in Tokyo, getting pizza from LaSorted’s. I’d assumed L.A. wasn’t a “bump into your ex at the bodega” or “see your editor on the subway” kind of city, much less a “run into a foreign colleague on the sidewalk” one.

Yet here they are, yapping about omakases. Had we stayed inside, they would have passed within 10 feet of each other without realizing.

Raise a glass to Old Hollywood restaurants. The martinis, tuxedoed waiter and cigarette stains on a mural are a window to history.

The indoor table’s pleasure is intimacy and predictability. A sidewalk’s social contract is leakier.

Outside, a new piece can always wander onto the board.

When I later tell Zach Jarrett, Triste’s co-owner, about the dancers’ sidewalk reunion, he’s delighted but unsurprised.

“Not to be woo-woo, but there’s an energetic resonance to it,” he says. We’re sitting in the alcove overlooking the sidewalk, drinking a funky rosé as ML Buch plays. “Space, food and drinks are great nonverbal transmitters.” The people who end up here, he tells me, may already be responding to some shared frequency, be it indie music, pet-nat or an acoustically-treated room.

“It’s about what opens up when you share a language.” He pauses. “It’s a desirous thing.”

The sidewalk hang has prerequisites. New York has a slim window when your glam eyeliner won’t melt but you can still wear the halter without a jacket. In Singapore, you’ve perspired through your little black dress before the first round arrives.

L.A. has the weather for the linger: dry air, low humidity, late sunsets, long evenings, flattering light.

Outside the permanently chic Bar Seco in Silver Lake, people with killer cheekbones and lightly-tousled hair take pictures of one another between sips of matcha horchatas and bites of beurre blanc scallops — cute cocktails and delectable morsels you can consume without interrupting the conversation, or the camera.

At times, this buzzy stretch of Sunset Boulevard between Hyperion and Lucile resembles a catwalk. It doesn’t pretend to be blasé; it’s here to be seen. Perceiving and being perceived are folded into the pleasure of this scene. A Miu Miu gabardine hat set at a jaunty angle, wraparound Moncler sunglasses at night. Legs crossed, uncrossed; another picture. Maybe they’re models; maybe everyone in L.A. low-key knows how to pose like one.

FWIW, I too partake in the 0.5 flash selfie, so what I’m really getting at is: This is not an Angeleno’s usual experience of a sidewalk. Especially as a lone woman, a sidewalk is an interstitial danger to overcome en route to a destination.

We’re used to walking at a clip. Scanning constantly. Hand closing around the pepper spray in our purse, just in case — but outside these bars, the social instruction flips, tantalizingly: Stay.

But permission to linger isn’t neutral. Geographical coordinates and the price of a cocktail have done some sorting before you ask a stranger for a cigarette.

The sidewalk offers randomness, but the bar has curated the sample.

In Eagle Rock, the crowd outside Walt’s wraps around the building beneath green neon that still says “CONNOR’S PLUMBING.” It’s unclear where the bar ends and the street begins. Here, where you can play pinball for a buck and get a Hamm’s for five, it’s harder to tell apart a real ramshackle drifter and an unwashed nepo baby.

A Ukrainian bottle blond in a Hello Kitty hoodie tells me she used to make seven dollars apiece collecting Lime scooters when she first got to L.A. after Kyiv got bombed. Someone takes a swing at a piñata strung from a lamppost. When it finally gives, cigarette packs and condoms rain onto the pavement.

I still don’t know if I’m staying for sure, but I buy a beat-up 1988 Ford Bronco from a lesbian influencer in Silver Lake, and move out of Silver Lake.

While looking at our starred places in New York on Google Maps, Jonny and I discover we’d frequented the same cafe — Sweetleaf in Greenpoint — and bar — Night of Joy in Williamsburg — for a few months in 2018, when he was there rehearsing for tour, and I was finishing my first novel. With zero social or professional overlap, a Scottish drummer and a Singaporean novelist could easily never have met, then or now.

We decide not to f— it up this time and move in together.

In Highland Park, we find something we both thought L.A. might require us to surrender: pedestrianism!

Figueroa Street has all of it, walkable: grocers, cafes, bars, restos, not to mention some of the best tacos in the entire city, and a Metro station. It isn’t New York or Singapore, and increasingly I don’t need it to be.

Join a walking group, visit a new neighborhood and feel the ground beneath your feet.

Even though we have a truck now, we take the A Line to Chinatown. It’s just three stops; the ride itself is seven minutes. We drop by Café Tondo. It’s a Tuesday — bolero night. Cozier than the weekend, when it’s packed to the gills, but no less vibey.

Abraham Campillo, who co-founded this chilled-out wine bar right by the station where the trains go by overhead, had told me as we smoked his Camel Blues and watched the crowd pop off to the DJ’s vinyl selection: “I just wanted a place where it’s relaxed…. You don’t need a reservation, you can pull up in a big group. We’re tribal beings.”

Tonight, the singer in the open window has a lovely voice and a kewpie doll tattoo. She cheekily informs us that Alvaro on guitars is single. The night is warm; I’m in a vintage lemon yellow Frederick’s of Hollywood slip. How decadent to have a vermouth on the rocks and sweet treats — affogato, flan, a silky strawberry panna cotta — under the half moon slung low in the sky.

The band sings “Las Mañanitas” for a Gen Zer in kitten heels celebrating her birthday on the bougainvillea-fringed patio. Turns out the abuelita in a white knit dress a few stools down shares a birthday — she pulls the granny up to dance.

They’ve got the moves. We clap for them. People call out birthday wishes in Spanish. It’s a beautiful thing to see, two strangers holding hands. I think about Andres and I discussing whether Angelenos are friendlier than East Coasters because they’re compensating for the relative shortage of chance encounters.

As “Bésame Mucho” starts up, the abuelita’s husband, a mustachioed gent in suspenders, asks her for a dance. Without a word, they move — rumba hips, waltz steps — in perfect, knowing sync.

“How long do you think they’ve been together?” I ask Jonny.

The red neon casting a Hopper-esque glow over the parking lot flickers as a train goes by. He smiles as he watches them.

Jonny and I drop by Capri Club on a weekday afternoon. Look, if you know your local’s weekly specials by heart, you’re probably staying awhile. Mondays, lasagna. Today is Pasta Tuesday.

We share a tortelloni, handmade moments ago, each piece lusciously coated with butter and sage. Francesco Allegro, their Puglian chef, lets me peek into the kitchen, where he kneads a ribbon of dry white into the dough. When he’s done, he joins us for an espresso on the sidewalk.

We’re sharing a Chinese clove cigarette, dissecting what a good hang means. “It’s not even about the drink, you know?” says Pete St. Peter, Capri’s beverage director. That’s a lot, coming from the babe who invented the beloved Capri Sour. “It’s about feeling natural and unencumbered.”

“In Italy when you hang out, it doesn’t feel like an appointment,” Francesco chimes in. “You go to the piazza, you don’t even say a time, because you know the rhythm.”

Right then, a guy in a Talking Heads shirt waves at Francesco. “Hihi.” He’s a regular who just vacationed in Rome, wants to tell Francesco about all the pasta he ate there. “You Italians do everything perfect, except burgers.” Francesco belly laughs.

After he leaves, Francesco marvels. “This is just what we were talking about, no?” He pauses. “Something that you couldn’t plan feels like home.”

Cities are made of buildings and roads, zoning and transit. You can do it by rote, or you can let your routines be greased by chance.

Jonny and I move to the corner stools by the bar — our usual spot.

Paradoxically, at this time of the day, the light is more golden on the inside than the outside. A brunette with a Bobby Special — beer and a shot of Ferrari, fernet branca and a house blend of red bitters — reads Virginie Despentes’ “Dear Dickhead” alone at the bar. We run into our pal, Travis, two martinis deep with his manager in a booth. Francesco sends over a delicious amatriciana made with crisp-edged guanciale. Almost behind my back, a collection of chance encounters has been acquiring the density of a new life.

I look at Jonny, and think: I live here now.

I don’t realize I’ve said it aloud until he pulls me closer.

Slowly, and then fast all at once, the sun goes down. Behind the bar, warm orange lights come on. Sixties Jamaican dub plays at a perfect volume; the last bottle of green Chartreuse is getting decanted into shot glasses. One gets passed to me, then Jonny.

Everyone looks everyone else in the eye as we raise the glasses.

It’s L.A.; we’d rather die than risk seven years of bad sex.

We clink glasses, bash them once against the counter. Salud.

The job offer says EUR 600 a month. That’s about PHP 42,300, more than double what a minimum-wage job in Metro Manila pays. It sounds like a lot, until you remember that rent, food and winter clothes in Europe are not priced in pesos.

So how much of that EUR 600 do you actually keep? This guide works through the real cost of living in Serbia for a Filipino worker: the 2027 minimum wage, what employers usually cover, what you will spend on food, phone and transport, and how much is realistically left to send home. Belgrade gets the most detail, with Novi Sad, Nis and Kragujevac for comparison. Every figure is in dinars (RSD) and pesos.

Serbia sets its minimum wage per hour, and as a net figure, after the worker’s tax and contributions. The government decision for 2027 sets it at RSD 405 net per working hour, up 9.2 percent from RSD 371 in 2026 (biznis.rs and Novosti, reporting the decision in August 2026). Skilled jobs such as welding, driving or electrical work can pay more than the minimum, so compare offers. For context, the average net salary in Serbia was RSD 121,346 in July 2026, and the median was RSD 95,036, according to the Statistical Office of the Republic of Serbia (RZS). Microsoft Patches More Than 1,400 Security Flaws

Microsoft today issued updates to fix more than 1,400 security vulnerabilities in its Windows operating systems and other software. Today’s epic patch batch dwarfs September’s record of nearly 1,000 fixes, which was already about ten times the normal monthly patch load from Redmond.

Just one of the weaknesses patched this week is considered a “zero day,” or already exploited in the wild. CVE-2026-96951 is a security feature bypass in the Windows Shell that affects Windows 10 and 11 systems, as well as some Server versions.

Windows 10 users might be wondering if they can still expect security updates after this month. Microsoft had been slated to stop shipping updates for Windows 10 systems in October 2025, but ended up adding another year of free updates after considerable pushback. A few months ago, Microsoft quietly extended that support for another year (until October 2027).

At least 78 of the flaws fixed this month are rated “critical,” meaning they could be exploited with little or no action by the user, and most of those critical bugs allow an attacker to execute code of their choice on the vulnerable system.

While Microsoft said there are no signs anyone is exploiting it yet, a security feature bypass in the .NET Framework (CVE-2026-108398) was publicly disclosed prior to today, potentially giving attackers advance time to work out how to exploit the bug.

Satnam Narang at Tenable said the number of flaws fixed this month far surpasses the previous record-setting year 2020, when Microsoft patched 1,261 CVEs.

“We’ve now crossed the 4,000 CVE mark with two months left to go,” he said.

Microsoft credits large language models for the high volume of bug discovery, and presumably AI gets credit for many of the patches used to fix these flaw. Meanwhile, security experts have been warning that these unusually large patch volleys from Microsoft are placing extra strain on corporate security teams, which often have to test individual updates before deploying them across a network to ensure the fixes don’t cause problems for other software.

Tyler Reguly, associate director of security research and development at Fortra, notes that just 26 Microsoft vulnerabilities appeared in the “Known Exploited Vulnerabilities” (KEV) catalog maintained by the U.S. Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) so far this year, and security teams would do well to prioritize patching those 26 vulnerabilities first.

“Security leaders should focus on exploitable risk, and ask whether their teams have the clarity, capacity and support to act,” Reguly said.

How to tell if a startup has a moat

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

👋 Hey there, I’m Lenny. Each week, I share deeply researched product, growth, and career advice. For more: Lenny’s Jobs | Lenny’s Podcast | Lennybot | How I AI | Become an AI-Native Builder and my other favorite AI/PM courses.

Moats! Does anyone have one anymore? As AI makes it easy to build anything, this is one of the biggest questions on people’s minds right now. Especially people trying to decide where to work. I teamed up with my frequent collaborator, the incredibly smart and insightful Dan Hockenmaier, to answer this question: How do I know if a given startup has a moat? The answer is a lot more interesting, and straightforward, than you’d expect. Let’s get into it.

There is a pervasive belief that startups are a lottery ticket. It’s impossible to pick winners, so it’s just a roll of the dice.

Not only can startups be a great learning experience and a lot of fun, but they can also provide economic rewards comparable to (or greater than) the “safe” route into big tech.

However, for this to be true, you must rigorously evaluate the startup you’re considering joining. Some of this is obvious: Does it have a strong team? Do customers love the product? Is it growing fast? But perhaps because it seems harder to measure, people often skip one of the most important criteria: Does it have a moat?

A company can check all your boxes and look like a rocket ship. If it doesn’t have a moat, though, it’s going to stall out. It won’t be able to keep growing once competition gets fierce, and it’s unlikely to reach a major liquidity event. That can mean wasting years of your life.

Moats are more important than ever in the AI era. Anyone can create a product; they can do it faster than ever before, and everyone is building the same things. But only some of these products will win.

Of course, assessing a startup’s moat isn’t an exact science. The good news is, there’s a simple framework that could meaningfully increase your odds of picking a winner or avoiding a flame-out, and you can apply it in just an hour after receiving an offer. The process will also teach you a lot about the business.

The first step is to figure out what kind of moat a startup could have. This can seem daunting, especially given how frenetic the conversation is on the topic right now. The term “moat” is being used much too loosely, as a stand-in for basically anything that gives a company an edge.

In reality, only a small subset of what people call moats actually drive the vast majority of long-term value.

They’re what I’ll call compounding advantages: not just a temporary edge, but a self-reinforcing flywheel that gets stronger and stronger over time, ultimately making a company very hard to compete with.

There are only four compounding advantages that really matter. Every major tech company has at least one. If you can’t see the potential for any in the startup you’re evaluating, that’s a red flag.

1. Switching costs: The more deeply embedded a product is in the way a customer works, the harder it is for that customer to switch. This increases retention and revenue per customer, which can be reinvested into the product.

2. Network effects: The more users a product has, the better it gets for everyone. This increases engagement and attracts more users.

3. Scale economies: The more volume a company has, the larger and more efficient its operations can be. This lowers unit costs, funding lower prices or a better product at the same price, further driving up volume.

4. Data effects: The more usage a product has, the more data it generates. This data can be used to improve the product and increase usage.

What about all of the other moats people talk about? Can they be compounding advantages as well?

A common one is speed of execution. It’s true that speed is critical for startups. But a company that has to sprint forever is not a good business. Ultimately, it will misstep, slow down, and competition will catch up. Speed gives a company more shots on goal as it tries to build a great product and a great business, but it must eventually find something more durable.

Another favorite is distribution. It does matter more than ever: building products has gotten easier, but getting them to customers has not. But distribution is not a compounding advantage either. It helps create the raw ingredients for one, but it is not one itself.

Finally, we have brand. The idea is that when everyone is building the same thing, the most resonant brand will win. But for startups, brand works more like customer acquisition spend: Put money in, get growth out. It’s not something that will effectively keep others from competing long-term. Brand can become a true compounding advantage (as with Coke or Nike or Apple), but it just takes too long to really matter for most startups.

Speed, distribution, brand, a great founding team, and lots of funding—these are all fuel. They get the car moving in the right direction. The four compounding advantages are what it looks like when the car starts rolling downhill on its own.

Your job is to identify which kind of compounding advantage a startup has the potential to build. It won’t be in full force yet (it takes a long time to develop), but it should be pretty clear which path they are on. That’s because it’s not something you can “bolt on”; it’s not some clever competitive tactic or a tweak you can make to your business model.

A compounding advantage is the core loop at the center of the business through which you add more value for customers. Defensibility emerges as a byproduct of turning the flywheel over and over.

So as you evaluate a company, don’t focus on what they say they are or what their “strategy” is. Focus on what they’re actually doing. What is the team working on every day? What’s on their roadmap? Which teams are large and influential inside the company?

Switching costs are sometimes viewed negatively: as something that keeps customers trapped or a reason clunky legacy software never gets replaced.

But think about what it takes for a product to get into this position in the first place. It first has to become so deeply embedded into the way a customer works, so essential to their business, that they can’t remove it. That requires creating a product that delivers enormous value.

Mike Vernal recently wrote something of a manifesto for anyone building this kind of business today:

If you are building a venture-backed software company, I think the answer is once again “do it all.” Just build the whole thing—your moat is the totality of what you’ve built and completely owning the buying center.

Some have argued that AI is making switching costs irrelevant. AI does make the actual migration faster and cheaper, but that is not where most switching costs actually come from. The bigger cost is what I’ll call the “workflow tax”: all of the time the team spends learning a product and building it into how they work, and how disruptive it is to do that over again. AI also helps teams build products much faster, which could allow them to become even more embedded across a wider range of surfaces.

At the core of these companies is a machine to both deepen and broaden customer engagement. The roadmap is rich with efforts to better serve existing use cases or create ways to serve new ones. They’ll often be focused on integrations and working with other parts of their customers’ stack. The team should be heavily tilted toward product and engineering, with an emphasis on shipping rapidly.

The classic examples are large incumbents with broad, deeply embedded products in SaaS, fintech, and healthcare, like Salesforce, Datadog, ADP, and Epic. But there are many earlier-stage companies that are currently on a path to creating something similar.

Rippling is a great example. Parker Conrad is building what he calls a “compound startup.” They started with employee onboarding and expanded rapidly across HR services, talent, finance, payroll, and business intelligence, all operating on shared infrastructure. This is the kind of product that gets embedded so deeply it rarely gets ripped out.

Network-effect businesses are totally different—so different, in fact, that most product features and the UI itself matter very little. How is it that Craigslist can still get so much traffic when it looks like this, in 2026?

Because what matters most is the network. The value of a network-effect business increases with each additional user. It’s all about having all of the people customers want to interact with, all of the content they want to consume, or all of the products they want to buy.

Network-effect products are common in industries like social (LinkedIn), gaming (Roblox), and marketplaces (Amazon).

The emphasis of a network business is less on building out the surface area or feature set of the product, and more on growing the network and making it easier and easier for that network to interact.

For example, during my years at Faire (a marketplace connecting local retailers and independent brands), the vast majority of our roadmap was focused on just a few things:

Getting more brands and making sure we had all of their products

Improving the search and discovery algorithm to recommend the right products

Reducing risk so retailers could try more products (free shipping, net terms, bigger credit limits)

Every decision gets filtered through one lens: “Does this strengthen the network?”

Businesses with scale as their compounding advantage are all about making it possible to offer the same product at a lower cost or a higher-quality product at the same cost.

These businesses are often highly capital-intensive: They make a big investment, such as in a production facility, warehouse, or LLM training run, and figure out how to get it to pay back. And then they do it again, but this time try to do it bigger and faster.

They’ll often have larger operations, engineering, and finance teams. And they will usually be focused on expansion: into newer, higher-scale operations and into new geographies and categories. Scale businesses are commonly found in industries like manufacturing (SpaceX), retail (Costco), media (Netflix), and now in the frontier labs.

Zipline (the autonomous delivery robot company) is a good example of a startup where scale economies are starting to emerge. They benefit from scale in two ways: by manufacturing more drones to reduce unit costs and by increasing delivery density to improve their utilization rate. The result is a cost per delivery that has fallen from $300 at launch to $12 today. Anyone who wants to compete with them will first have to go through the expensive part of that curve.

The final type of compounding advantage has historically been relatively rare, but AI is making it much more common.

These are businesses with data at the core. It’s the input that makes the product higher-quality, more efficient, or work for more use cases. And by using the product, customers generate even more data, which further compounds this effect.

In these companies, you’ll often see a large effort to collect and sanitize data, and to build algorithms and products on top of it. ML and data science are usually large and influential teams.

One good example is Stripe Radar. They use transaction data across all merchants to get better at detecting fraud for each of them. Stripe has processed over 70 trillion data points and $1.9 trillion in transaction volume. 92% of all transactions use a card it has seen before. The result is a product that a company with access to less data can’t match.

Many AI products have the potential for this compounding advantage because of how much they can be improved with more data. Cursor is a clear example: with each accept or reject from users, the model gets better at understanding what good code looks like. Many vertical AI products have the potential to do the same thing for other domains like law, healthcare, and customer service.

The next step is to assess how powerful that compounding advantage could become. Not all compounding advantages are created equal. For example, every marketplace has some form of a network effect, but many still fail to become good businesses.
