France is in turmoil. More than half a million people hit the streets on October 17, as protests swept across the country. As the administration greeted the protesters with a large deployment of riot police, peaceful demonstrations were engulfed by violence in many places. Arsonists set fire to the Lyon tourist office and burnt down the Limoges City Hall, a historic municipal building. Protesters stormed the townhall in Marseille and attacked the prefect’s residence in Bordeaux. In Paris’s Place de la Republique, protesting youth who clashed with the police torched vehicles and looted shops. Luxury retailers such as Louis Vuitton, Hermes, Dior and Galeries Lafayette suffered millions of dollars in losses as their stores were ransacked. Police arrested or detained more than 6,000 people, including hundreds of minors.

With the police outnumbered and unable to protect public and private property, several cities resorted to full or partial curfews. Government officials have blamed the unrest on “seditionists from far-left fringe groups”. They have accused La France Insoumise, or LFI (France Unbowed), the leftwing political party launched by veteran politician Jean-Luc Melenchon (75), of inciting the protests with the aim of extracting political mileage ahead of the presidential elections in April 2027.

Noah Adams, a longtime host of NPR news program “All Things Considered” and a journalist known for his gentle voice and genuine curiosity, has died. He was 84.

Adams died Oct. 16 at his home in Yellow Springs, Ohio, according to NPR. The cause was complications of a benign brain tumor removed eight years ago, his wife and longtime NPR news producer Neenah Ellis told the New York Times.

A host of the weekday edition of “All Things Considered” for 18 years, Adams helped cover major news events and produced segments from his travels around the country, talking to everyday people, often in small rural towns.

“He never liked talking to people who he knew had preprogrammed things to say,” Melissa Block, a former “All Things Considered” host who’d previously worked as a producer for Adams, said in NPR’s segment announcing his death. “He wanted people who were genuinely speaking from their heart and with conviction and sincerity.”

Susan Stamberg, a ‘founding mother’ of National Public Radio and the first female broadcaster to host a national news program, has died. She was 87.

Born on Sept. 15, 1942, in Ashland, Ky., Adams joined NPR in the mid-1970s after working various jobs including as a DJ and in construction in and around his hometown. A college dropout, Adams was inspired to start working in public radio after encountering “All Things Considered” as a listener.

He was introduced to the program by a colleague shortly after it debuted in 1971. He told The Times in 2002 that he remembered thinking, “That sounds conversational, that they intend for me to be listening, that I’m smart enough to be listening,” when he first heard the show.

After working at the University of Kentucky’s public radio station, he applied for a job at NPR. He was hired as a production assistant on “All Things Considered” in 1975 before becoming a weekend co-host for the show in 1978. He moved to the weekday show in 1982.

Susan Stamberg, Adams’ “All Things Considered” co-host from 1982 to 1987 who died last year, described him as “the most genuine, uncontrived voice in broadcasting” in the foreword of his 1992 book about his work on the program.

“The conviction shines through the words he writes, and the quiet and very powerful way in which he speaks,” she wrote, according to Current. “He is a quiet, careful man, a craftsman in his work, his seriousness softened by a twinkle in his eye, an occasional goofy grin, and the daffodils he’ll bring into the studio on an especially tense or stuffy day.”

Following the announced departure of Ari Shapiro from the flagship radio show ‘All Things Considered,’ NPR has called on Scott Detrow to become the new full-time host.

Adams mentioned the camaraderie with his colleagues and one-on-one interviews as among the things he would miss when he left his job as host of “All Things Considered” for a second and final time in 2002.

These interviews, which often turned into “a conversation that’s so quiet and so slow,” were the “kind of conversation [that] cannot be accomplished on anything but radio,” he told The Times.

Among Adams’ signature audio work is the 1981 radio documentary “Father Cares: The Last of Jonestown,” as well as a series of interviews with Tom DeBaggio, a man living with Alzheimer’s disease, and an interview with Betsy Walter, an 8-year-old girl whose parents had gotten divorced. Adams wrote a number of books, including “Piano Lessons: Music, Love, and True Adventures,” which chronicled Adams’ quest to learn how to play the piano in his 50s, and “The Flyers: In Search of Wilbur and Orville Wright.”

“He has a writer’s eye, and I think you hear that when he’s on the air,” Robert Siegel, Adams’ “All Things Considered” co-host, said in 2002. “And he takes sound very seriously. When you combine writing with sound, you can create extraordinary radio.”

Gabon’s media regulator said access to social media was restored on Saturday after an eight-month suspension that sparked widespread criticism.

The High Communication Authority (HAC) pulled the plug on social networks on 17 February blaming content posted online for stoking conflict and division in society.

The crackdown came as Gabonese President Brice Oligui Nguema faced a wave of social unrest less than a year after his election.

Facebook and TikTok, the most-used platforms in the country, as well as YouTube and Instagram were shut down, while WhatsApp worked intermittently.

Some people still managed to access social networks via a virtual private network (VPN), a secure, encrypted tunnel that hides your IP address and protects your internet traffic.

Provider Proton reporting a 60,000 per cent increase in downloads of its VPN tool the day after the ban came into force.

The HAC says the suspension was aimed at allowing "the government to adapt its legal framework to better regulate social media".

Users are now required to provide formal identification, effectively ending online anonymity.

The authority said it was working with Meta - which owns Facebook and Instagram - as well as TikTok and YouTube to "improve content moderation" on those platforms.

Gabon's opposition had slammed the social media ban as an abuse of power, and it faced a string of legal challenges that were all rejected.

Former prime minister Alain-Claude Bilie-By-Nze, who has been in prison since April over alleged abuse of trust and fraud dating back to 2008, was one of the most vocal critics of the measure.

Activists have cited the ban and Bilie-By-Nze's arrest as examples of the repressive climate in Gabon under Nguema.

The 2023 coup that brought the military general into office ended 55 years of rule by the Bongo family, had sparked hope of democratic change.

Serbia has entered a new political era. The Student List has won the country's parliamentary election, marking a dramatic breakthrough for a movement that emerged from months of student protests and rapidly transformed itself into the country's most important political force.

The result represents far more than a change in the distribution of parliamentary seats. It is a profound rejection of the political model that has dominated Serbia for more than a decade.

The Student List's victory is particularly significant because it was not created as a conventional political party. Its origins lie in the student protests that became a powerful expression of dissatisfaction with the country's political institutions, the government, corruption allegations, media conditions and what many protesters described as a lack of accountability.

What began as a protest movement has therefore achieved something considerably more difficult - it has translated street mobilisation into electoral legitimacy.

The result suggests that the movement managed to attract voters far beyond the university campuses where the protests began. Young people were its most visible supporters, but the electoral coalition appears to have extended to parts of the urban middle class, opposition-minded voters and citizens who had previously been reluctant to support established opposition parties.

For the ruling establishment, the result is a major political setback.

For years, President Aleksandar Vučić and the Serbian Progressive Party dominated Serbian politics through a combination of strong electoral organisation, extensive political influence, control over a large part of the media environment and a highly personalised political strategy centred on Vučić himself.

The election result indicates that this model has reached a critical limit.

The transition from protest movement to government could therefore prove much harder than winning the election for the Student List.

The students will now have to demonstrate that their political legitimacy can be transformed into effective state governance.

First, it represents a generational change in Serbian politics. A movement dominated by young people has succeeded in challenging a political establishment that has shaped the country for years.

Second, it could lead to a major institutional reset. The new government is likely to face strong expectations concerning the independence of institutions, the judiciary, corruption, public media and electoral conditions.

Third, Serbia's foreign policy could enter a new phase. His ability to dominate the political agenda would be significantly reduced, while his party would have to rebuild itself in opposition.

The Student List has won the election. But its real political test begins now.

Winning an election requires mobilisation, a powerful message and the ability to unite voters around the desire for change. Governing requires something different: institutions, experienced personnel, economic policy, compromise and the ability to take responsibility for difficult decisions.

For Serbia, the election therefore represents both a political rupture and a moment of uncertainty.

The central question is no longer whether the students can challenge the established political order.
