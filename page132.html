Storms battered northern Europe on Sunday cutting power to more than 150,000 homes, forcing the cancellation of hundreds of flights and silencing phone networks.

Winds of up to 135 kilometres an hour were recorded in Argyll and Bute in western Scotland and fallen trees and power cables blocked roads and rail lines across the country.

Storm Boelo, the second of two storms to hit the UK this weekend brought widespread travel disruptions and power outages to Scotland, Northern Ireland and northern England on Sunday. The Met Office, the UK's national forecaster, said Storm Boelo will bring a danger to life from flying debris and crashing waves.

Flood warnings are also in place, while airports, ferries, railways and roads have seen disruptions. Power outages have affected some 82,000 homes in Britain, nearly 60,000 in France and at least 10,000 in Germany. Boelo, pronounced “Boo-Loh”, has followed hard on the heels of storm Austen, which battered southern England, in particular, on Friday, before continuing across northern continental Europe

As the storm moved eastward into continental Europe, Amsterdam’s Schiphol Airport warned travelers of cancellations and delays of flights to and from the busy aviation hub caused by strong winds that were battering the Netherlands. Belgium also took a battering - falling trees injured four people in Brussels on Saturday.

Two people were seriously hurt Saturday by scaffolding blown away by storm Austen's winds during an autumn market at the town of Nastaetten near Frankfurt, German police said. Germany's weather agency DWD predicted more powerful winds of up to 100 kilometres an hour on the northern coast on Sunday.

High wind and rain were expected in Denmark, Norway and parts of Sweden. Some ferries in the region were canceled, train services were suspended on some Swedish lines as a precaution and some trailers were barred from Denmark’s Great Belt Bridge.

Venezuelan opposition leader and Nobel Peace Prize winner María Corina Machado is back in her home country following a series of failed attempts to return and nearly a year in exile.

Machado announced her return via a post on social media site X, in which she wrote "I am here. With you".

Machado had covertly left Venezuela in December 2025 to receive her Nobel Peace Prize. Just weeks later, US forces seized the country's longtime leader Nicolás Maduro and flew him to New York to face criminal charges.

Machado has heaped praise on US President Donald Trump for launching the risky operation, though Washington has allowed Maduro's deputy, Delcy Rodríguez, to rule as interim president.

According to her party, Machado attempted to return seven times in the past three months, from the United States, Panama and Curaçao. They say she was repeatedly blocked by Venezuelan authorities.

Many believed Washington played a role in blocking Machado's return, given Trump's repeated expressions of satisfaction with Rodríguez.

That claim has been denied by US Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

However, following her return earlier, Rubio claimed responsibility for the negotiations that allowed her go back.

"The conditions allowing for the safe return of Ms. Machado to Venezuela is the direct result of the US supported reconciliation talks which has already seen hundreds of political prisoners freed and hundreds of political opposition figures return from exile," Rubio said on social media.

Asked by reporters Sunday about the breaking news, Trump said "I don't know anything about it, but I think she's a very nice woman. She gave me her Nobel prize, so how can I say anything else?"

"I hope she's safe. She's a very nice woman. But we're doing very well in Venezuela," he added.

Machado has spent the last few months trying to regain her place on Venezuela's political stage while talks on the country's future moved forward without her being able to take part directly from within.

This past week, she called on Venezuelans to take to the streets and demanded that a date be set for the presidential elections, one of her main demands of Delcy Rodríguez's government.

Trump has repeatedly expressed satisfaction with Rodriguez -- who was Maduro's deputy -- as she has acceded to US demands, particularly on granting access to Venezuelan oil.

Machado had accused Rodríguez's government of being afraid of her return. Now, with her as the opposition leader back in Venezuela, the question is no longer when she will manage to return, but what role she will be able to play in a political landscape shaped by negotiations between Chavismo, different sectors of the opposition and the United States.

Rodríguez did not immediately react to the news of Machado's return.

The flock of 1,250 sheep and 160 goats set off from Casa de Campo before crossing the Puente del Rey and making its way through the historic centre. Bells chimed and animals bleated as crowds of residents and tourists lined the streets to watch, take photographs and follow the procession.

Several shepherds wore traditional dress, while others carried young lambs or offered treats to their animals. Folk musicians added to the festive atmosphere as the flocks filled streets and squares normally dominated by traffic.

The event marks transhumance, the centuries-old practice of moving livestock between summer and winter pastures along vías pecuarias (drovers’ routes). At Plaza de la Villa, officials staged the traditional payment of 50 maravedís per thousand animals, a symbolic ceremony dating from 1418.

This year’s route was altered to avoid Puerta del Sol, where housing campaigners have set up a protest camp following the eviction and subsequent death of an 87-year-old local resident.

Beyond celebrating rural heritage, the festival highlights the environmental value of traditional grazing: by consuming vegetation, the animals help reduce flammable undergrowth and lower wildfire risks.

Welcome back to Off the Record, coming to you from Brussels.

This week, a story made my jaw hit the floor.

Russian President Vladimir Putin has floated a three-way meeting with Donald Trump and China’s Xi Jinping to discuss a way out of the war in Ukraine, which he started. Trump seemed to confirm the pitch, without putting a date on it, suggesting it could be good for the world if it included denuclearisation.

If you’re not alarmed by this, you should be.

Putin wants a Yalta 2.0 moment. One that would restore his international stature and present Russia as a great power, much like the three-way summit that helped shape the post-WWII order 81 years ago in the resort city of Yalta.

In 1945, as victory over Nazi Germany drew closer, Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin gathered in Yalta to divvy up the post-war world. The conference helped lay the groundwork for spheres of influence across Europe, with the Soviets subsequently cementing their control over practically all of Eastern Europe.

Churchill was an imperfect man, but nonetheless the war hero Great Britain needed at the time. He walked away from the whole affair disenchanted.

He knew the Soviets would engulf half of Europe, leaving the continent divided by an Iron Curtain for decades to come. In the midst of it all, Poland would be sacrificed, alongside the Baltic states, much of Central Europe and parts of the Balkans. And he was right. But he still had to accept the deal.

Yalta dictated the terms of a new world order, but also set the stage for the Cold War. Of course, the echoes of history can be cruel: Yalta has been under Russian occupation since 2014, following Moscow’s illegal annexation of Crimea.

If you follow that logic, the modern-day triumvirate of Trump, Xi and Putin would get together and split up the world for the next 100 years. One piece for you, another for me — you get the gist of it.

Presumably, the talks would focus on Ukraine and Taiwan. If Yalta 2.0 works anything like the 1945 conference, the three would cut a backroom deal and the rest of the world would have no choice but to go along with it. Putin would emerge as the winner, which explains why he’s pitching it in the first place.

Russia is a nuclear power, but it is no longer a great power. Putin, who once called the collapse of the Soviet Union “the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the century” for Russia, has long sought to restore Moscow’s lost sphere of influence.

If Trump joins that meeting, he would be handing the Russian leader his biggest strategic victory since the Soviet Union’s collapse.

Along the way, the US President would alienate America’s friends. Trump may not think much of his allies, but they are crucial. Policing the world has brought Washington lucrative deals, enormous influence and military bases that it needs for its own security, whether Trump likes to admit it or not.

A new Yalta without his allies would certify the death of the West as we know it.

The Europeans should be very concerned, as they too are on the chopping board in this dangerous ménage à trois. A backroom deal over Kyiv’s head would also spell the end of the transatlantic relationship and erode NATO’s deterrence.

​Brussels may insist it is in the game, but it’s clear that much is happening behind its back already. The Europeans need their own peace plan and a clear definition of what a good outcome truly entails — for Ukraine and for Europe.

Inevitably, this will force the EU to confront uncomfortable questions it has long avoided, from the territories Ukraine may not get back to the security guarantees it will need to provide in exchange for a US backstop. That means boots on the ground to ensure peace, and it can’t just be France and the UK.

The EU will also need to ring-fence funding for Ukraine as the political cycle turns against continued support. Let’s face it: it is expensive, and it’s becoming unpopular as protests over the cost of living mount.

Our politicians need to do a much better job of explaining that our security is at stake too, with more than 4,000 kilometres of Europe’s eastern flank at play. It is in Europe’s interest to reach a fair deal in Ukraine. The opposite will come back to haunt us.

Europe needs its own peace plan, starting with the appointment of an envoy who can secure a seat at the negotiating table, set out a clear negotiating position and report back. This should have been done a year ago. Enough with the Trump whisperer strategy. It doesn’t work. It is futile as long as Europe is not represented by someone who can speak with a political mandate.

If you’re not at the table, you’re on the menu. Serve it while it’s hot.

Protesters chanted "smells like a dictatorship" overnight into Sunday as police hit desmonstrators with batons in both the capital Lisbon and and the city of Porto, in an operation to dismantle tents set up to protest against skyrocketing housing prices.

In a message posted on X, the police said officers had "intervened early this morning... to put an end to these illegal encampments".

"Once dialogue ended, the operation led to 31 arrests for disobedience," the police added.

A spokeswoman for the protest camp in Porto told the Portuguese news agency Lusa there were 37 minor injuries and two arrests.

"It is the third night in which we are being repressed with a method of total intimidation that one would never expect in a democratic country governed by the rule of law," said Catarina dos Santos, speaking to reporters.

Around 130 tents were erected in Rossio Square in Lisbon.

The operation to remove the tents began at around 1 am in both cities, after protesters started to notice what they called "disproportionate" police reinforcement, with canine units gathering in the squares in both locations.

"There were at least 20 crowd-control dogs, clearly agitated, and and a number of weapons pointed at us, and if this is not intimidating and serious, I don't know what is," lamented Catarina Santos, quoted by Lusa.

Shortly before 2 am on Sunday, the police contingent had both squares cordoned off and began moving in against the demonstrators, who had formed a human chain. Police used batons, while firecrackers were heard causing considerable confusion. In Praça D. João I, bystanders criticised the PSP's conduct and began to sing "Grândola Vila Morena", a song associated with the revolution of the 25 April that ended decades of dictatorship in 1974.

The "clean-up" operation in the squares was swift: the tents were removed immediately by the authorities and the protesters, who say they will return, dispersed to nearby squares.

The Lisbon and Porto camp movements are calling for measures such as the reversal of the tenancy law, an end to evictions of people with no housing alternative, limits on the purchase of property by real-estate funds, the rehabilitation of vacant buildings and a ceiling on rents.

Large crowds had turned out the previous day for peaceful demonstrations, accusing the government of failing to act to tackle Portugal's housing crisis

Eighteen electoral lists, featuring a total of 2,418 candidates, are taking part in the election. The elections were called after President Aleksandar Vučić dissolved parliament on September 9, following a proposal by the Serbian government.

However, the political reasons for the early election go much deeper than the constitutional procedure itself. Serbia has been going through a period of intense political and social polarisation for almost two years, beginning after the tragedy at the railway station in Novi Sad on November 1, 2024.

The collapse of a concrete canopy at the station killed 16 people. Student protests that followed initially focused on demands for accountability and the publication of documents related to the station’s reconstruction but gradually developed into a broader movement against, as they claim, corruption, political responsibility and the way that state institutions function.

During 2025, students organised mass protests, university blockades, blockades of institutions and roads, as well as rallies across Serbia. The movement gradually expanded beyond student issues and became the strongest yet challenge to Aleksandar Vučić’s government, whose rule had not been seriously challenged for a full 14 years. In May 2025, students called for snap parliamentary elections, and during 2026 they decided to participate in the elections with their own list.

This fundamentally changed the character of the election. What was supposed to be another parliamentary contest has become, in many ways, a referendum on Vučić’s political dominance and the political system that has been in place in Serbia for more than a decade.

The largest political bloc is built around the Serbian Progressive Party, running under the electoral list “Aleksandar Vučić – United Serbia.” Although Vučić is no longer president of Serbia, he remains the central figure of the campaign. In late September, he resigned from the presidency in order to lead the SNS (Serbian Progresive Party) campaign and run for the position of prime minister, the position he had already held before becoming President of the Republic. This has further turned the election into a direct vote on his political future.

The list “Ivica Dačić – Stability Factor” is also taking part, bringing together the Socialist Party of Serbia and smaller parties. An important part of the political spectrum consists of right-wing and nationalist-oriented lists, including “Authentic Right – Miloš Jovanović – Boldly Serbian,” and the pro-European opposition centre is represented by the list “European Serbia". Participants in the election include the minorities Alliance of Vojvodina Hungarians, the SDA of Sandžak, Usama Zukorlić’s list, the Albanian Coalition and several smaller parties and movements, which, in the event of a close result, could play an important role in forming the new government.

The most distinctive participant is the Student List – “Students Win.” It is not a conventional political party and does not have a traditional party leader. Candidates were selected through student plenums, and the list includes professors, doctors, artists, activists and other people who have generally not held senior political positions. The list has 250 candidates and represents an attempt to transform the protest movement into a parliamentary political force.

The fundamental problem is no longer simply the relationship between the government and the opposition. At the centre of the conflict is the question of trust in institutions.

For a large part of the protesters, the Novi Sad tragedy became a symbol of a much broader problem: corruption, political accountability, public procurement, institutional control and the relationship between the government and its critics. The authorities, on the other hand, describe the protests as an attempt to destabilise the country and bring to power a movement without sufficient political experience. Moreover, government officials claim that “foreign actors” are actually behind the student movement, while President Vučić has repeatedly stated that foreign intelligence services are financing a “colour revolution” in Serbia.

As a result, two fundamentally different narratives have emerged. The government emphasises stability, economic development, national interests and the danger of foreign interference. The student movement focuses on accountability, the fight against corruption, the rule of law and the independence of institutions from political influence.

The biggest change is the disappearance of the traditional opposition as the main challenger to the ruling party. In the 2023 election, opposition parties grouped around the “Serbia Against Violence” coalition won around 24% of the vote and 65 parliamentary seats. However, subsequent fragmentation and internal disputes weakened their position. In the meantime, the student movement has taken over much of the political energy and voter support that previously belonged to the traditional opposition.

As a result, the main political dividing line is now much simpler: the ruling SNS and Vučić versus the Student List, while the other parties are fighting to enter parliament and secure the role of potential coalition partners. Here, voter turnout becomes a particularly interesting factor, as does which parties will clear the 3% electoral threshold. The election outcome could ultimately hinge on these seemingly minor technicalities.

The most important trend is that Serbia’s political system is undergoing a major transformation. The student movement is the first major social movement in many years to successfully move beyond traditional opposition parties and attract large numbers of people who had previously been politically inactive.

At the same time, the SNS still has a very strong party infrastructure, a large network of local organizations and a stable core of voters. This means that the outcome is far from predetermined. Opinion polls have produced different forecasts, and because of the changing political landscape and the large number of undecided voters, the result is difficult to predict with precision.

Another important trend is the continued polarization of Serbian society. The country is divided not only over the government and the opposition, but also over identity, Serbia’s relationship with the European Union, Russia and the West, national issues and the country’s future foreign-policy orientation.

A third trend concerns European integration. Serbia remains a candidate for EU membership, but its European path has been slowed by problems involving the rule of law, its relations with Russia and its limited alignment with parts of the EU’s common foreign policy, as well as the stalled normalisation process with Kosovo. At the same time, Belgrade continues to maintain close relations with both Russia and China.

If the SNS wins, Vučić will seek to form a new government and stabilise the political system after almost two years of protests. If the Student List wins enough seats to form a government alone or with its allies, Serbia could enter an entirely new political era. And if the result is close or disputed, there is a real possibility that the political crisis will continue even after the polls close.

For all these reasons, this election is not simply an election for a new parliament. It is a test of the survival of the political model that has dominated Serbia for more than a decade — and a test of whether a new, non-party movement can change that model.
