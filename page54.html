Conservative wave hits roadblock in Brazil as Lula holds off Bolsonaro challenge

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Flavio Bolsonaro joined supporters in Santa Barbara D'Oeste to lead a motorcade. Polls in Brazil show a tight presidential race with incumbent Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva. (Video: Reuters.)

Incumbent leftist President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva narrowly defeated conservative Sen. Flavio Bolsonaro in Sunday's presidential runoff, according to The Associated Press. The deeply polarized nation returned to the polls for a second round runoff on Sunday.

Lula’s victory followed chaos at polling places after a Supreme Court justice ordered a last-minute, two-hour voting extension, a move that could face a legal challenge from Bolsonaro.

While the Flavio Bolsonaro campaign had also requested an extension in certain areas impacted by heavy rains, the unusual decision by the court to extend voting hours nationwide by two hours will certainly draw scrutiny.

Brazil's President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, the Workers' Party (PT) candidate for re-election, holds a Brazilian flag as he votes during the presidential runoff election, in Sao Bernardo do Campo, Brazil, Oct.25, 2026.  (Amanda Perobelli/Reuters)

Lula petitioned for the extension, claiming that buses were not circulating to take voters to the polls as expected.

"I've never complained about anything in any election before. Whether I won or lost, I'd just accept it and get ready for the next one. But this time, something abominable is happening," Lula argued.

The Bolsonaro camp is likely to claim that the extension favored Lula.

Bolsonaro narrowly won the first round on Oct. 4, with 47% of the vote, to Lula's 45%, but fell short of the 50% majority required to win the election outright.

The victory marks an unprecedented fourth presidential term for Lula, and a remarkable comeback victory. The result is even more surprising given that Bolsonaro had led, albeit by tight margins, in recent polling, and Brazil had recently elected a conservative congress aligned with the Bolsonaro agenda.

Earlier in the day, Lula voted in Sao Bernardo do Campo, Sao Paulo, while Bolsonaro voted in the north of Rio de Janeiro, the state he represents in the Senate.

The first vote tallies came in from Brazilians living overseas, where Lula won a decisive victory in Germany, and Bolsonaro won an overwhelming victory in Japan.

While Bolsonaro performed well in his southern electoral strongholds, winning by comfortable margins in Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, Paraná, and Rio Grande do Sul, he won those states by smaller margins than in the first round.

His turnout in the south was not enough to overcome Lula's famed Northeastern firewall; a reflection of the Workers Party dominance in the country's populous "Nordeste" region.

Bolsonaro also turned in a disappointing performance in Minas Gerais, which geographically and politically straddles the Brazilian north/south divide. Often referred to as the nation's quintessential bellwether state, it is fair to say that as goes Minas Gerais, so goes the nation.

While Bolsonaro won Minas Gerais by 5% in the first round, in the second round it was essentially tied.

Bolsonaro had received endorsements from other first round candidates: best-selling author Augusto Cury, former Minas Gerais Governor Romeu Zema, and former Goiás Governor Ronaldo Caiado.

However, they were not enough to overcome the much-respected electoral machinery of the Workers Party. Lula has now appeared on the presidential ballot 7 times, winning 4 of those elections.

Heading into the first round, Flavio Bolsonaro was effectively able to make the case to working-class and middle-class voters that the Lula's economic approach was driving up their cost of living.

He painted the Lula administration as bloated, tax-heavy, and given to racking up debt and tied this to inflation and kitchen table economics.

Additionally, his message of hardline law and order appealed to a Brazilian public where voters routinely suggest that violence is the most pressing issue.

Nonetheless, these economic and security concerns were not enough to overcome a generation of loyalty to the Workers Party and its social programs, particularly in the North and Northeast of the country, where Lula won by wide margins.

While Lula will be riding high heading into a historic fourth term in office, he will face a Congress with a center-right and right-wing majority that has been described as one of the most conservative in history.

While Flavio Bolsonaro lost the 2026 election by a narrow margin, the Bolsonaro movement is certainly not going away, and arguably remains an influential force in modern Brazilian politics.

The Lula victory also brings to an end a long string of victories for Trump-backed right-wing candidates across the region. While Trump had not officially endorsed Flavio Bolsonaro, he made no secret of his preference, and enjoyed a close relationship with the Bolsonaro family.

Looking forward to 2030, all eyes will turn to Bolsonaro clan ally Tarcisio de Freitas, who stepped aside this election cycle, in order to clear the way for Flavio Bolsonaro's run. Will the Brazilian right back another Bolsonaro in four years time, or will the popular São Paulo governor also mount a bid?

In addition to the presidential election, voters in five states, as well as the federal district of Brasília, were also selecting governors in runoff elections. Bolsonaro-aligned candidates appeared poised to win most of those races.

Faisal Islam: Healey's Budget needs to sustain the stronger economy through gathering clouds

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Healey's Budget needs to sustain the stronger economy through gathering clouds

The chancellor's aides are using intelligence-style security measures to guard its secrets: the Budget has been split into "compartments" with very few given access to all of them. There are codenames. The IT has been locked down and ministers viewing the plans have to sign a digital promise not to brief media.

Such security is a response to the feeling that speculation around the last Budget, sometimes informed by insiders, was economically destabilising.

As a recent former defence secretary, Chancellor John Healey has embraced such strict procedures, and has approached most of his public engagements and interviews with a sturdy refusal to break the Budget omertÃ , akin to his fellow Yorkshireman Geoffrey Boycott's at times ultra-defensive stance on a cricket pitch.

The economic news has been good - growth has been better than expected and the International Monetary Fund has endorsed the UK for "standing out" among the G7 countries for at least planning to cut borrowing.

John Healey has embraced strict security procedures to keep this year's budget secret

The bond market's reverse beauty contest, in which investors single out the countries they distrust most, has moved on from Britain. Traders I occasionally hear from who were once focused on UK borrowing costs are now more preoccupied with "le spread" - the widening gap between French and German borrowing costs (the former being higher than the latter) - and with America's long-term debt outlook.

And yet there are clouds on the horizon. The inflation rate rose more than forecast to 3.5% in September, and borrowing numbers were also higher than forecast. This means an imminent Bank of England rate rise is very much on the cards, with the resultant rise in mortgage repayments for some.

Credibility with investors requires not just that Budget maths add up on Wednesday, but also belief that growth plans are realistic, and the perception that Downing Street can actually convince MPs to pass its Budget.

The question for John Healey is whether he can deliver a Budget that can sustain the revival in growth and offer the help with the cost of living that his backbenchers demand. At the same time he needs to reassure the markets that his budget will keep a check on borrowing.

"Is it resilient, or is it actually strong?" has been the tone of the debate among economists about the curious case of the UK economy ahead what is a vital Budget.

The UK is the fastest growing G7 country so far this year, at 0.6% in Q1 and 0.5% in Q2. The widespread assumption of stagnation in the second half is being defied with Q3 heading for 0.6 to 0.8%.

Each quarter has seen growth twice as fast as predicted by the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) in March and independent forecasters. This is the fastest growth in the first eight months of a year since 2017, ignoring the post-lockdown re-opening.

It's more resilient than it was in 2022. Back then, the Ukraine war, massive energy bailouts, and Liz Truss' mini budget all culminated in the UK facing the highest borrowing costs in the G7, in what was derided in financial markets as a "moron premium".

Then last year, with constant policy u-turns, UK borrowing costs rose the most of the G7.

This year the gap has narrowed, as uncertainty about the US and France takes centre stage, but all advanced economies are feeling the heat from the Iran war energy shock.

Inflation has risen, but not just in Britain. Reforms to the electricity market have weakened the link between gas and electricity prices, meaning spikes in gas prices have less impact on electricity bills than they used to and helping to keep inflation lower than it otherwise might have been.

The fact that up until now the Iran war has been more of an oil shock than a gas shock (where the UK is more exposed) has been another saving grace. Meanwhile, the significant amount of AI and tech investment in the UK is another boost to the economic numbers.

The net result is that doom-mongering predictions of recession, of being hit worst by the Iran war, and, in some quarters, of imminent national bankruptcy, have been defied.

That economic defiance of the early part of 2026 has persisted in the first three months of the Burnham-Healey tenure in Downing Street, buoyed by a lull in the Iran war and the energy price shock.

Post Liz Truss' mini-budget the UK faced the highest borrowing costs in the G7

Burnham was advised upon entry to Downing Street that if consumers could at least feel more confident, they might save less and spend more and therefore help boost growth.

So far Burnham and Healey appear to be relying on a series of relatively inexpensive "feel-better" policies - such as capped bus fares and a temporary VAT cut on household energy bills - that have improved confidence without significantly increasing spending.

But there isn't a lot of spare money, which limits just how much can be offered for "temporary and targeted" measures. And come January, the clouds on the horizon could be overhead, with typical energy bills predicted to rise by 16%, interest rates potentially up and an enduring fear that the AI bubble could burst.

At a gleaming hi-tech robotics research facility in Coventry last month, John Healey sought to set out the broad contours of his approach to the economy. On a walkabout at the Manufacturing Technology Centre, I asked him if there had been too much doom-mongery.

"I know the global conditions mean that it is hardâ¦ we have huge latent potential in this country. Great talent, and deep capital markets.

"The challenge now is to make sure that we balance the books, we manage and control public spending, and the prime minister and I are in lockstep in our determination to meet the fiscal rules with a buffer," he said.

The giant orange robot arms whizzing around were being trained by AI to use lasers to carve the most intricate metal shapes for prototype parts for the jet engines of the future. The Budget manoeuvres required by Healey may require even more precision.

The essential question is whether he has chosen to impress the markets and explain himself to his MPs, or impress his MPs and explain himself to the markets. Judging by my three interviews with him in recent weeks, it is more the former than the latter.

At Labour conference Healey wore the same tie once worn by Gordon Brown

The answer may not be found in his speech at all, but in the borrowing tables that will reveal how much money the government expects to borrow over the coming years, published in the OBR's forecast released - assuming no repeat of last year's disaster - when Healey sits down in the House of Commons around 13.00.

Just after that, as Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch is delivering the Opposition's response, the real reaction the chancellor's team will be looking for is whether the markets move, particularly when the Debt Management Office publishes the "gilt remit" - the detail of any changes to its plan to sell UK government bonds to investors.

My sense is that every effort is being made to minimise this number, and help guide down medium and long-term borrowing rates for the government and for mortgages. (It was at this moment in 2022 that markets vomited at the prospect of how much extra they thought the government would need to borrow after Liz Truss' mini budget.)

From Healey's first day in office, the prudence message was practically tattooed to his head. He has spent more time working in the Treasury than any recent chancellor. He spent five years from 2002 to 2007 as a junior minister when Gordon Brown was chancellor - five finance bills, five Budgets, pre-Budget reports and two Spending Reviews.

Healey says he will take on some of the challenges Rachel Reeves found tricky to resolve

For his Labour Party conference speech, Healey sported Brown's old conference tie. But the Treasury he returned to this year is a changed place in important ways. When his predecessor wore the tie, the Treasury was running an annual surplus, not a Â£120bn deficit adding to a Â£3 trillion total national debt.

I asked him just afterwards, and just before he was whisked off to a meeting of the government's emergency COBR committee on RAF Fairford, if he was going to have another go at some of the things that proved so difficult for his predecessor, former Chancellor Rachel Reeves.

He replied: "100%. I was very clear to the conference today and the public beyond. There isn't the money that there was in the 1990s. People understand that. The public aren't daft.

"Running through the Budget I deliver must be fiscal discipline and the maintenance of control on the public finances, because otherwise we won't get on top of the debt costs."

One intriguing element of what we may see on Wednesday is that this theoretically more left-leaning Labour government appears to believe they have the political capital to save money on welfare payments.

The aim is to get some of the 1.35 million people currently off work with mental health issues back into jobs.

Some reporting suggests some affected younger people may see benefits payments cut, with the government instead investing more in getting them into jobs and keeping them in them through the offer, for example, of in-work therapy.

Back in Healey's time under Gordon Brown, there was no OBR. Back then it was not unknown for late budget maths problems to be settled with an optimistic tweak of a forecast, a date change to the economic cycle, or choosing a stronger rate of long term growth.

The OBR's verdict is crucial now - to what extent it agrees with projected welfare savings or even the importance it places on stronger GDP figures matters now.

The bigger point here is that the chancellor must be shocked by the contrast between the sheer power of the Brown Treasury to do what it wanted, and the reality of what he currently faces: the constraints of the OBR, manifesto red lines, bond markets, significant debts and an energy shock.

There are reasons to postpone some of the toughest decisions beyond this Budget, such as how to fund desired increases in spending on defence and social care. The economic outlook could improve were the Iran war to settle at some point in the next few months, and after revised statistics the OBR may also have cause to rethink last year's downgrade to long term economic growth.

There is an imaginable pathway to a forecast next year with higher growth, lower inflation and lower debt interest costs, plausibly improving the public finance projections by tens of billions.

This would be a much more agreeable basis upon which to decide the pace of those big ticket spending rises. It also may be a tempting option for anyone thinking about an election. Former Treasury officials lament: "There is always a reason for procrastination."

Expect the Chancellor to laud the resilience and strength of the UK economy and seek to sustain it, and spread growth geographically. His key judgement, however, will be a balance between extra financial and economic support and the peril of still elevated borrowing costs.

He will try to offer breathing space. It may be needed not just for households and families, but for the Exchequer itself.

Brazil's President Lula, 80, is re-elected for a fourth term after going up against son of jailed former president Jair Bolsonaro

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Published: 00:26 GMT, 26 October 2026 | Updated: 01:58 GMT, 26 October 2026

Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva clinched a fourth term in a tight runoff against the son of jailed former president Jair Bolsonaro on Sunday.

The Leftist leader won 51.7 percent of the vote, compared to 48.2 percent for Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, official results showed.

Eighty-year-old Lula pulled off a dramatic turnaround after a shock second-place finish in the first round.

Flavio Bolsonaro had finished first in the election's first round and hoped to revive his father's far-Right movement and pardon him.

The shock result defied polls, precedent and global anti-incumbent sentiment to put the brakes on a wave of Right-wing election victories across Latin America.

At times, Lula's reelection looked like a long shot, as voters soured on him and his Workers' Party after years of corruption scandals and rising prices, while sweeping societal changes left the former metalworker and union leader looking out of touch with an increasingly app-based economy and its growing ranks of informal workers.

But Lula's victory marks another comeback in a career full of them. The 80-year-old was jailed from 2018 to 2019 on corruption charges that were later overturned, paving the way for him to beat Jair Bolsonaro in the 2022 election.

'He is a statesman, a giant,' said Leftist federal lawmaker Erika Hilton, who was with Lula in Sao Paulo during the vote count.

Supporters of the president of Brazil and candidate Luiz Ignacio Lula da Silva gather at Avenida Paulista during general election day on October 25, 2026

Right-wing candidate Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, son of imprisoned former president Jair Bolsonaro, casting his vote early on Sunday morning

Final polls showed the two candidates neck-and-neck. Polling stations were originally due to close at 5pm local time (8pm GMT) on Sunday but the country's Supreme Court extended voting for two hours following complaints that public transport issues had obstructed people from voting, following a request from President Lula.

Speaking after his victory, Lula said Brazil 'will not accept being anyone's back yard,' in reference to the United States.

He added that the level of warmth he experienced on the campaign trail was 'something I never imagined'.

The hotly-contested election became in effect a referendum on whether Brazil remains one of Latin America's few Leftist nations or joins a regional swing to the right championed by US President Donald Trump.

'Brazil can't stand another four years like this, everyone's life has gotten worse,' said Mr Bolsonaro outside a polling booth.

Bolsonaro had said he would hand Brazil to 'the Lord Jesus Christ' and had vowed to crack down on crime and slash government spending if victorious.

But pollsters said better-than-expected turnout had powered Lula to victory. Leonardo Barreto, the co-founder of consultancy Think Policy, said voters had chosen Lula's familiar embrace over Bolsonaro's novelty.

'People today are choosing between change, with some apprehension, and a known path, which guarantees certain rights,' he said.

During the campaign, Lula pointed to a track record of falling poverty and inequality, while unemployment, illiteracy and deforestation hit record lows during his third term.

He warned Bolsonaro would cut welfare spending, backslide on social advances and hand over Brazil's sovereignty to US President Donald Trump, whose State Department led a campaign to get the Bolsonaros back to power.

Lula's win deprives Trump of a major addition to what has been a growing bloc of friendly Right-wing Latin American governments that have embraced Washington's agenda on security and migration.

Celso Amorim, a former Lula foreign minister, told Reuters the Leftist 'would be very important for establishing a point of balance in Latin America. Lula is that balance.'

Lula becomes the first candidate since Brazil's return to democracy in the 1980s to be elected after finishing second in the first round.

Lula, who was president between 2003 and 2011, campaigned on a potent mix of pragmatism and populism that has shaped his career in national politics for half a century, rolling out a slew of reforms aimed at improving the lives of workers and poor Brazilians.

These gained steam with late-campaign pledges that ranged from free nationwide weekend public ​transport, which has to be formally submitted to Congress, to a ‌15% increase in his trademark welfare program that is already being implemented.

Lula also championed a constitutional amendment that became Brazil's biggest labor reform in decades, which passed with wide support in Congress.

But Lula's fourth term could be his most difficult yet, after Brazil elected its most conservative Congress since returning to democracy in the 1980s.

Combined, Bolsonaro's Liberal Party (PL) and allied parties could control over 280 seats in the lower house and up to 50 seats in the Senate, enough for comfortable majorities in both chambers.

'He is likely to be a lame duck from the start,' said Oliver Stuenkel, a professor of international relations at the Getulio Vargas Foundation in São Paulo.

The incoming legislature will likely deepen recent trends of presidents ceding power to Congress, Stuenkel said, predicting Lula will face waning influence over Supreme Court nominations, unwieldy party coalitions, and even the threat of impeachment.

Brazil's Lula wins historic presidential election; Bolsonaro reportedly refuses to concede

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has won an unprecedented fourth term as Brazil's president, overturning a first-round deficit to beat Senator Flávio Bolsonaro in a runoff that split the country almost evenly.

Lula took 51.8% of valid votes to Bolsonaro's 48.2%, according to the Superior Electoral Court, with nearly all ballots counted on Sunday.

The results are also historic as it's the first time since Brazil returned to direct elections in 1989 that a candidate has won the final runoff after losing in the first round.

Bolsonaro refused to concede defeat, saying he would seek the annulment of the outcome, Reuters reported. He also said a ruling by the Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin to extend voting for two hours was "unacceptable," according to the report.

The election process was marred by allegations of voter suppression, with Lula's campaign reporting suspended public transportation in some key areas, prompting the court to extend voting by two hours. Bolsonaro's campaign had also requested an extension, citing heavy rains that shut polling stations in the south, a right-wing stronghold.

The victory of the 80-year-old leftist, who had trailed in the first round, blocks a rightward shift in Latin America that U.S. President Donald Trump has backed.

Trump, who had labeled Bolsonaro's lead in the first round as "a very, very big victory," would have preferred a Brazilian in closer alignment to U.S. foreign policy as Washington makes efforts to project U.S. power across the Americas, according to Daniela Portella Sampaio, senior research fellow for Latin American Security at the U.K. think tank Royal United Services Institute.

Lula, who has publicly sparred with Trump, is set to govern from 2027 to 2030, facing a Congress where Bolsonaro's Liberal Party holds the largest bench in decades, and with a substantial public debt that he would need to stabilize.

Brazil, under Lula's leadership, has grappled with worsening fiscal health and mounting public debt driven by double-digit interest rates, elevated cost of servicing the debt, and rigid mandatory spending. Gross debt reached 82.9% of the country's GDP in August, the highest since March 2021, according to the Central Bank of Brazil.

Investors are bracing for heightened volatility across Brazilian markets on Monday following Lula's victory as the right-wing Bolsonaro was seen as more likely to push for fiscal austerity and deregulation.

The iShares MSCI Brazil ETF (EWZ) plunged nearly 20% in overnight trading following election results.

Analysts had expected a rally in Brazilian assets before Sunday's election results, with Bolsonaro holding a narrow lead earlier this month. Chris Turner, Global Head of Markets and Regional Head of Research for UK & CEE at ING, had forecast both the currency and bond markets to rally if Bolsonaro won.

The new president will need to convince investors that Brazil can stabilize its debt trajectory, said Alejo Czerwonko, a fellow at the Atlantic Council. Lula's administration will likely extend the more "gradual approach," building on the existing fiscal framework while seeking to preserve public investment and social spending, Czerwonko said.

Goldman Sachs analysts expect "modest measures" to contain the government's spending growth, alongside further increases in an already high tax burden, while major structural reforms remain unlikely.

Leaders from across Latin America largely congratulated Lula's historic win.

Peruvian President Keiko Fujimori, in a post on X late Sunday, hailed Lula's victory, calling it a "democratic day" for the Brazilian people. Uruguay President Orsi Yamandu congratulated Lula over a call, calling the Brazilian president a "friend."

Lula's victory came the same day as Serbia's long-serving strongman leader, Aleksandar Vucic, conceded defeat in a parliamentary election to the student-led opposition movement, ending 14 years of leadership.

Lula wins election in stunning comeback – live updates

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, the Brazilian president, has staged a dramatic election fightback, defeating his far-right opponent, Flávio Bolsonaro, to claim a historic fourth term in power.

With 100% of votes counted the 80-year-old leftist received 4.4m votes more than Bolsonaro, with a 51.79% share to his 45-year-old rival’s 48.21%. However, soon after Brazil’s electoral authority confirmed Lula’s victory, Bolsonaro refused to concede defeat and asked for the results to be annulled.

Lula narrowly lost the first round of the election to Bolsonaro in early October, casting his political future into doubt and plunging progressive Brazilians into three weeks of despair. Never, since the return of democracy in the 1980s, has the first round runner-up won the second vote.

But Lula did precisely that on Sunday, prompting joyous scenes on the streets of São Paulo, where he had been following events, and at the hotel where he was watching the count.

Addressing a packed crowd of ecstatic supporters in a São Paulo conference hall, Lula said: “We are going to change the way that this country is governed … This election was a defeat for denialism … I want to thank God and to thank you, Brazilian men and women of this country … it was democracy that made this happen.”

After the result was confirmed, Alexandre Padilha, Lula’s health minister, said: “This is a huge victory for the Brazilian people who in the second round had the chance to think about what President Lula means for the environment, for the defense of our national health service and our education system and the Brazilian constitution. And the risks that the extreme right candidate entailed.”

“It’s a massive victory – not just for Brazil. It’s also an important signal to the rest of Latin America and to the world,” Padilha added.

The 8o-year-old appears to have made up much of the difference through what his allies had identified as his main opportunity: persuading his voters to turn out. After a record abstention rate of 21.08% in the first round, which disproportionately affected Lula voters because they tend to live further from polling stations, the rate fell to 19.21%.

Speaking during Lula’s victory speech, Bolsonaro said it was “impossible to concede the election result” and that his legal team would seek to have the election annulled.

Following in the footsteps of his father, former president Jair Bolsonaro, who four years ago also refused to acknowledge his defeat to Lula – before attempting a coup and being convicted for it – the senator cited a supreme court justice’s decision to extend voting hours as the alleged reason.

His speech made no mention of the fact that his campaign had also asked the electoral court to extend voting hours, but he nevertheless said Justice Cristiano Zanin’s decision to extend voting by two hours to 7pm “was not normal, it was unacceptable” and amounted to “changing the rules of the game while the game was being played”.

For Lula, who turns 81 on Tuesday, the victory represents a sensational final chapter in a storied political career that began when he emerged as a union leader in São Paulo’s industrial suburbs in the late 1970s.

After entering politics, the former factory worker who was born in rural poverty made three unsuccessful presidential bids, in 1989, 1994 and 1998, before finally becoming Brazil’s first elected working-class president in 2002.

Lula stepped down after the maximum two consecutive terms allowed by the constitution but returned to power after winning the 2022 election against Bolsonaro’s father, then the incumbent president Jair Bolsonaro, who was subsequently jailed for plotting a coup.

Lula’s win is bad news for Jair Bolsonaro who is serving a 27-year sentence for that botched power grab and was hoping to be freed and given a role in government had his son prevailed.

It is also a blow for Donald Trump, the Bolsonaro clan’s most important international ally. The US president had been hoping Bolsonaro would become the latest in a series of pro-Trump leaders to take power in Latin America, including Abelardo de la Espriella in Colombia, Daniel Noboa in Ecuador, José Antonio Kast in Chile and Javier Milei in Argentina.

Confirmation of Lula’s victory was greeted with anger and bewilderment in Brasília, where Bolsonaro is living under house arrest and where his son watched the count.

On Sunday night, political leaders applauded Lula. Mexican president Claudia Sheinbaum said: “Long live democracy, the people of Brazil won. Congratulations, ⁠President Lula!”, while Paraguayan president Santiago Peña said “I salute the ⁠Brazilian people for this great democratic journey.”

Lula’s victory came after his supporters staged two massive shows of support on Saturday, with more than a million people flocking to Rio’s Copacabana beach and an even larger number marching on Paulista Avenue.

Many people on the streets of São Paulo were angry at an apparent attempt by pro-Bolsonaro politicians to derail the election by sabotaging public transport in Lula-leaning voting areas across the country.

“I think it’s another coup attempt that the extreme right is trying in Brazil. But we need to really fight so we can win,” said Lucas Lopes, a 32-year-old from São Paulo who works with public relations.

But that alleged attempt to scupper a Lula victory failed.

“I’ll be sincere: in the first round the left failed. But in the second round they woke up,” said Caio Marques, a 40-year-old photographer from Franca, a city in the interior of São Paulo state. “And there is no coup that will take this away from us.”

“I feel so, so happy … There’s not enough room in my chest for all this joy. Lula’s victory represents so much. It means life for those who believe in love and in life,” said Maicon Douglas Bento, a 34-year-old photographer, as he joined tens of thousands of jubilant Brazilians on Paulista Avenue at the heart of São Paulo.

Lula holds off far right with victory in Brazil presidential election

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Leftist president mounts stunning comeback after unexpected first-round loss galvanised supporters of Flavio Bolsonaro.

Leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has defeated right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro in Brazil’s contentious presidential race, pulling off a remarkable comeback after a first-round loss that galvanised the country’s political right.

Shortly after 9pm Brasilia time on Sunday (00:00 GMT), Brazil’s Superior Electoral Court called the race for Lula, who had earned more than 51 percent of the vote. More than 64 million ballots were cast for the incumbent.

Bolsonaro, meanwhile, netted 48 percent, or north of 59 million ballots. More than 4 million votes separated the two candidates, with the final votes still being counted.

Coughing and audibly hoarse, Lula took to a flag-draped podium shortly after to give his victory speech. He acknowledged clashes ahead, including with his legislative agenda.

“I do not choose the Congress,” he said, citing the legislature’s rightward tilt. “It was the people who chose it, and it is with them that I will vote, work and get things approved.”

He also thanked the voters who cast their ballots “to secure democracy in the country”.

With his victory, Lula has made history. No other politician in modern Brazilian history has lost the first round of a presidential election only to win the second.

“A historic turning point for democracy,” Edinho Silva, the head of Lula’s Workers’ Party, posted on social media, along with a video of supporters chanting, “Ole, ole, ole, ole, ola! Lula, Lula!”

But Bolsonaro has so far refused to concede, teeing up a likely court battle over the legality of a Supreme Court decision to extend the voting time by two hours.

“It is impossible to acknowledge the result,” Bolsonaro said in a short speech, accusing the Supreme Court of having “changed the rules of the game mid-game”. He added that he would seek an annulment of the results.

Leaders across the Western Hemisphere, particularly on the left, applauded the result. “Long live democracy, the people of Brazil have won,” Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum wrote in the wake of the results. “Congratulations, President Lula!”

United States Senator Bernie Sanders likewise offered his felicitations, calling Lula’s re-election “extraordinary”.

“Lula has stood up for Brazilian workers & democracy — and will continue to do so in the next four years,” Sanders said in a statement.

Still, the vote process was marred by allegations of voter suppression after reports emerged that public transport in some areas had been suspended.

Lula’s allies accused Bolsonaro’s supporters of reducing bus availability in an attempt to dampen the president’s working-class base.

Heavy rains, meanwhile, affected southern areas considered to be Bolsonaro strongholds.

The campaigns of both Lula and Bolsonaro ultimately petitioned the Supreme Court for a voting extension. Polls across the country were kept open two hours later than expected, until 7pm Brasilia time (22:00 GMT), following a ruling from Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin.

Bolsonaro, however, has argued that some polling stations were not able to reopen in time, harming any additional votes he may have earned.

Sunday’s victory means Lula will retain the presidency of Latin America’s largest country, denying a crucial victory to the region’s right wing.

In recent years, right-wing leaders have swept presidential races in nearby countries like Peru, Colombia, Argentina, Bolivia and Ecuador. Many have ridden a wave of anti-incumbent energy and enjoyed support from US President Donald Trump, who returned to office in January 2025.

A former union leader for metalworkers, Lula – at age 80 – will be sworn in on January 5, 2027, when he will beat his own record as the oldest Brazilian president to take the oath of office since the country’s return to democracy.

His victory has earned him an unprecedented fourth non-consecutive term.

Lula came into Sunday’s run-off as an underdog. While he narrowly led pre-election, the first round of voting on October 4 showed him trailing Bolsonaro.

He earned 45 percent of the first-round vote, compared with the right-wing senator’s 47 percent, both tallies short of the 50 percent needed to avoid a run-off.

But in the weeks since, Lula has rallied supporters around cost-of-living issues. He championed a constitutional amendment passed this month that shortened the Brazilian workweek, and floated the possibility of providing free public transport on weekends.

Having entered politics in the 1980s, during the twilight of the country’s military dictatorship, Lula has run for president seven times.

He won two consecutive terms from 2002 through 2010, as well as a third term in 2022, in what was the tightest race in modern Brazilian history up to that point.

His rival in that election, former far-right President Jair Bolsonaro, is the father of Senator Bolsonaro, Lula’s adversary in this race.

Jair Bolsonaro has since been sentenced to 27 years in prison for his role in plotting a coup following his loss to Lula.

Despite overseeing a decline in poverty and low unemployment during his third term, Lula has faced questions about his advanced age, his ability to combat crime, and the country’s polarised political landscape.

An explosion of household debt has also left many Brazilians feeling their economic circumstances have worsened.

In September, Lula banned online gambling and sport betting apps to address the issue.

While the victory over Bolsonaro cements Lula’s status as one of the most significant and influential figures in Brazil’s modern history, efforts to advance legislative priorities during his fourth term could be stymied by the most right-wing Congress in recent memory.

There are also questions about how Bolsonaro’s right-wing base and the Trump administration might react to Lula’s re-election.

Already, Bolsonaro has suggested he would seek to nullify the election, citing the prolonged voting hours.

“What happened today was not normal. It was, in fact, unacceptable,” Bolsonaro told his supporters.

Trump’s eldest son, Donald Trump Jr, also suggested there had been judicial malpractice as a result of the extension.

“If a conservative government in Brazil did this, everyone in the mainstream press in America would lose their minds, but I guess they’re just choosing to be silent because it’s all for the cause,” Trump Jr wrote on social media.

The Trump White House has been openly supportive of the Bolsonaro family, issuing 50-percent tariffs last year on certain Brazilian products in a push to end the prosecution of Jair Bolsonaro.

Judges involved in the case were also subjected to US sanctions, most notably Supreme Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes.

Lula has framed such manoeuvres as an attack on the sovereignty of Brazil’s judicial system.

One of Trump’s critics, US Representative Ro Khanna, suggested the US president could take further actions against Brazil in response to Sunday’s vote.

“People all across the world are fighting back against extremism. With Lula in Brazil, they just won,” Khanna wrote.

“Trump must respect the results and not undermine Brazil’s elections.”
