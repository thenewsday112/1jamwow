US approves sale of 500 Patriot missiles to Ukraine for $2.1 bln — State Department

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

WASHINGTON, October 31. /TASS/. The United States has approved the sale to Ukraine of 500 PAC-3 missiles for the American-made Patriot PAC-3 air defense system, along with related equipment, for $2.1 billion.

The US Department of State and the Defense Security Cooperation Agency (DSCA), a Pentagon agency responsible for foreign military sales under government-to-government agreements, announced the decision.

The statement noted that the purchase would be "funded entirely by partners and allies." The delivery will include "PAC-3 MSE classified and unclassified spare parts; and other related elements of logistics and program support."

According to Washington, "this proposed sale will support the foreign policy and national security objectives of the United States by improving the security of a partner country that is a force for political and economic stability in Europe."

US authorities said Ukraine would pay for the missiles with funds provided by Canada, Germany and Norway, as well as through an EU loan. "No US funds are involved in this sale," the statement said. The US authorities also said that "the proposed sale will improve Ukraine's capability to meet current and future threats by improving its integrated air and missile defense capability."

The missiles will be supplied to Ukraine by Lockheed Martin, one of the Pentagon’s key contractors. The statement said that implementing the deal "will not require the assignment of any additional US Government or contractor representatives to Ukraine," and that there will be no adverse impact on US defense readiness as a result of this proposed sale.

The US administration has already notified Congress of the decision. Lawmakers now have 30 days to review the agreement and potentially block it.

HOUSTON (AP) — A U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officer accused of intentionally ramming his car into a Mexican construction worker in Houston was indicted by a grand jury Friday on a charge of aggravated assault with a deadly weapon, prosecutors said.

The officer, Mario Alberto Morales, was driving an unmarked SUV on Oct. 10 when he struck and ran over Miguel Parra Hernandez, 48, who was running away during a pursuit through a parking lot, according to the Harris County District Attorney’s Office. Parra Hernandez broke his leg and remains in federal custody at a rehabilitation facility, said District Attorney Sean Teare, who announced the indictment at a news conference that included body camera video of the encounter and a recording of Morales calling 911.

ICE has asserted that Parra Hernandez ran in front of the vehicle, an account that Teare said is contradicted by video.

“The evidence we have in this case is as clear as any that I’ve ever prosecuted or investigated,” Teare said.

Morales, 33, is one of just a few federal officers who have faced criminal charges over their actions on the job during President Donald Trump’s immigration crackdown, although other encounters have come under investigation, including a fatal shooting by an ICE officer this summer in Houston.

Morales’ attorney, David Lindenmuth, said his client did not commit a crime. He and ICE both said Morales did not intentionally strike Parra Hernandez and accused the Democratic district attorney of indicting Morales for political reasons.

“Mr. Morales is truly a good man, a former member of our military, that has given his entire life to public service either for his country or in or adjacent to law enforcement,” Lindenmuth told The Associated Press. “To have him charged for what was so clearly just an accident — the DA’s office, the DA himself, has invented a crime out of thin air.”

Teare hinted at concerns over Morales’ background but would not provide details.

Morales had worked as an ICE Enforcement and Removal Operations officer for a little more than a year, Lindenmuth said.

Morales, who is charged with a second-degree felony, was not in custody Friday and a warrant will be issued for his arrest, according to Teare.

Parra Hernandez has lived in Harris County for more than 30 years and did not have a deportation order or criminal background, according to Teare.

He was in a vehicle that ICE tried to stop he jumped out and was followed by Morales, who was in a Jeep Cherokee. Previously released surveillance video shows the Jeep speed over a median and drive toward Parra Hernandez as he runs away.

Morales’ body camera footage that Teare played Friday shows the officer making a few sharp moves with the steering wheel before the SUV shakes. Morales is heard saying, “He got hit with a car, bro.” It is not clear to whom he is talking.

“I was coming in and I hit him with the car,” Morales says after another ICE officer arrives.

In a 911 audio recording in which Morales requests an ambulance, Parra Hernandez can be heard yelling. Morales tells the operator, “He’s fine. He’s just complaining of pain.”

Parra Hernandez told deputies that he believed a police officer was stopping the vehicle he was in but got scared and fled when the man started putting on a mask.

He repeatedly told deputies that Morales “threw the car at me,” according to a Harris County Sheriff’s Office incident report reviewed by the AP.

Morales told deputies he did not intentionally hit Parra Hernandez. He acknowledged that he did not brake before hitting Parra Hernandez and said he was trying to avoid another truck in the parking lot.

Body camera video indicated that Morales had two other suspects in custody in his backseat, according to the report. But the deputy wrote that Morales told them there was no one else inside.

Other aspects of ICE’s account have also been called into question.

After Parra Hernandez was taken into custody, ICE released a photo of another man, Jose Mendoza-Cervantes, and said he was the target of the operation that day. FIEL, an immigrant rights group in Houston that has been working with the family, said Mendoza-Cervantes’ daughter told them her father has been dead since 2017.

Teare’s office has previously said its investigation has not found that Parra Hernandez and the man ICE identified as Mendoza-Cervantes were in a vehicle together, contrary to ICE’s statement.

At the Third Russia-Africa Summit, Moscow Talked Big. But Who Was Listening?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Every three years, the leaders of some of Africa’s largest nations descend on a major Russian city for a few days of shmoozing, media-ready photo opportunities and closed-door meetings with Kremlin officials.

Now in its third iteration, the Russia-Africa Summit has few surprises to offer. Each time, Moscow makes its pitch as an anti-imperialist bulwark, drawing on historic ties to African independence movements, while African leaders vie for trade deals and prestige.

The result is a mutually beneficial diplomatic concoction. Russia shows that it doesn’t need the West to have reliable allies, and African nations can boast of winning a global power’s favor.

This year’s event, which took place over the course of two jam-packed days this week, presented an altogether more pressing question: Nearly five years into the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, is cooperation with Russia still worth it?

The answer depends on who you ask, analysts told The Moscow Times this week.

The Russia-Africa Summit has become a staple diplomatic initiative for Moscow, one whose key attraction is the opportunity to meet with Russian officials including President Vladimir Putin. This year, delegations from 48 countries were in attendance, including 22 heads of state.

As with other regions where it has longstanding ties, Moscow’s relationship with Africa has been made trickier by the war in Ukraine.

Nowhere is this more visible than in the issue of military recruitment. African countries have been some of the biggest targets of Moscow’s shady efforts to fill a gap in manpower for the war in Ukraine by enlisting foreign citizens to serve, a practice that rights advocates say meets the criteria of human trafficking.

Meanwhile, fighting on land and sea continues to sporadically disrupt supply lines to longstanding buyers of Ukrainian agricultural products, like Kenya, Libya and Tunisia, driving up food costs as importers pivot to other providers.

These issues were present in a nascent form at the last Russia-Africa Summit in 2023. But three years on, the picture of how the war is reverberating across the continent is much clearer, and it doesn’t look reassuring for Moscow.

In countries like Kenya and South Africa, the recruitment of foreign citizens for the war has become a matter of national outrage, drawing attention from government investigators.

This week, human rights watchdog Amnesty International called on African officials to raise the issue with their Russian counterparts, saying it would be “a profound disservice to the people of their countries” to “remain silent.”

At the same time, Moscow continues to take a hands-off approach with managing its vast mercenary network. Rebranded as the Africa Corps and placed under the control of the Defense Ministry after the Wagner Group’s dissolution in 2023, the organization is still trailed by stories of human rights abuses in countries like Mali.

All of this complicates the image that the Russia-Africa Summit puts forward of two places “united by truly strong bonds of friendship and close partnership,” as Putin said during the second day of the summit.

In reality, those words might only apply to a limited number of countries in the region.

Many of the attending nations depend on Russian military or diplomatic support in one way or another, noted Joseph Siegle, a senior research associate at the University of Maryland’s Center for International and Security Studies and expert on African security issues.

“These are the majority of the 22 heads of state that are there,” Siegle told The Moscow Times. “Their rationale for being there is political rather than anything else. There’s a mutual interest in supporting that perception of Russia’s broader ambitions with Africa.”

The spectacle around the Russia-Africa Summit shouldn’t be taken as widespread approval of Moscow’s conduct, he added. Indeed, many countries in Africa are at worst skeptical and at best modestly enthusiastic of Russia’s ability to be a transformative partner.

There’s also fear of the potential reputational hit of engaging with Moscow.

For evidence of that, look no further than the number of participants. The Russia-Africa Summit’s strongest showing was in its first year, in 2019, when 43 heads of state attended. Both iterations since then have taken place during the war in Ukraine, and participation each time shrunk by nearly half.

What’s more, while the summit sells itself on being a venue for dealmaking, there are some basic discrepancies in the macro Russia-Africa economic relationship, Siegle said.

Russia is not in Africa’s top 10 trading partners, what trade that does occur is lopsided, seeing Moscow export about seven times more than it imports, and Russia provides only about 1% of the total foreign direct investment going into the continent.

“From the African perspective, the primary interest for most external partners is what they can do to help with Africa’s economic prospects, and there’s a realization that Russia offers relatively little there,” he said.

Some countries, however, are coming away from this year’s summit with tangible agreements. In what was perhaps the summit’s highest-profile deal, Ethiopia, a country teetering on the brink of civil war, received 12 Russian Su-35 fighter jets. Officials did not say how much the deal would be worth.

Elsewhere, promises were abundant but light on details. Putin said that Russian firms are interested in opening production plants in African countries to circumvent Western sanctions, while officials from Rosatom, the state nuclear agency, announced that they are in talks with multiple countries to build power plants, including some that could float in coastal waters.

African attendees were clear that this is the kind of thing they want more of.

“We invite Russian companies to invest in Mozambique and establish reliable partnerships,” the state-run TASS news agency quoted Mozambique President Daniel Chapo as saying.

If this year is any indication, that’s not lost on the Russian side. While the summit is unavoidably a politicized affair, Russian officials appear to have “refined their pitch to their African counterparts” by this point, at least in their rhetoric, said Ivan Klyszcz, a research fellow at Estonia’s International Center for Defense and Security.

“The themes gesture more consistently at topics that are important for African development, which has always been a big kind of flaw in Russia’s offer to Africa,” Klyszcz said. “What it can offer cannot match what African governments seek.”

The biggest question going forward, then, will be whether Russia can actually deliver on the economic opportunities it promises, especially as it remains preoccupied by the war in Ukraine.

Even after two summits that promised a substantial expansion of the economic relationship, Russia’s trade with Africa is still falling short of targets, and its ties to many African nations remain primarily based on security assistance.

Those kinds of assistance “are important in the way African governments define their foreign policies — but not always,” Klyszcz said. “They’re less focused on these big political issues and more on concrete development.”

Russia Designates Opposition Delegation at PACE as ‘Undesirable’

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Russia’s Justice Ministry has added the Dialogue with Russian Democratic Forces at the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe (PACE) to its list of “undesirable” organizations, a move that renders affiliation with the anti-war group illegal.

The delegation of Russian exiles was set up in January after years of lobbying efforts by leading opposition figures like former businessman Mikhail Khodorkovsky.

Several members of the anti-war group, including dissident Vladimir Kara-Murza and Pussy Riot co-founder Nadya Tolokonnikova, already face criminal prosecution in Russia.

Under Russian law, individuals affiliated with an “undesirable” entity can face up to four years in prison. Organizing the work of an “undesirable” group carries a maximum sentence of six years in prison.

Neither the Prosecutor General’s Office, which ruled to designate the Dialogue with Russian Democratic Forces as “undesirable” on Oct. 21, nor the Justice Ministry, which approved the designation on Thursday, have officially commented on the decision.

Besides the PACE delegation, the Justice Ministry blacklisted another 11 foreign entities on Thursday, including Vidim Books, a publishing house founded in Slovakia in 2023 to print books that are censored or banned inside Russia.

Russia was expelled from the Council of Europe and PACE in March 2022, shortly after launching its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. It had been a member for 26 years.

Introduced in 2015, Russia’s “undesirable” designation has been used to hinder the work of independent media outlets, civil society groups and the political opposition. Hundreds of entities have since been added to the registry, including The Moscow Times.

Putin’s remarks come amid continued disruption to LNG supplies. QatarEnergy has extended the suspension of certain deliveries into December, while India’s LNG import bill rose 23% to $8.5 billion during April-September, despite volumes increasing by just 4.4%.

For import-dependent economies such as India, the question is whether prospective Arctic cooperation could eventually expand supply options, or whether sanctions, pricing and transportation constraints will keep that possibility beyond commercial reach.

Russia’s interest in Arctic cooperation extends beyond developing its vast gas reserves. Following Putin’s October 27 remarks, Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak clarified that discussions with Washington concerned potential joint development of gas fields and LNG liquefaction facilities, including opportunities involving Russian infrastructure.

One possible example has already surfaced. Russia’s Novatek has acknowledged agreements to provide technology for the proposed $25 billion Polar Train LNG project in Alaska. The venture aims to use Russian liquefaction infrastructure for American gas exports, although its implementation remains subject to regulatory approvals.

Russia and the US compete for LNG buyers, yet Arctic development could give their energy companies reasons to work together. Russia could find opportunities to deploy its Arctic expertise and attract investment, while American developers could benefit from technology and infrastructure suited to challenging northern conditions.

Yet commercial interest alone cannot overcome the obstacles facing Arctic LNG development. Washington sanctioned Russia’s Arctic LNG 2 project in November 2023, subsequently extending restrictions to companies and vessels involved in supplying equipment and transporting LNG from the project.

In September, the Novatek-led Arctic LNG 2 project sought approximately $1 billion in damages from South Korea’s Hanwha Ocean in an arbitration dispute over cancelled contracts for specialized ice-class LNG carriers, highlighting the shipping complications facing Russian Arctic gas development.

The regulatory environment remains equally complicated. On October 16, President Donald Trump waived sanctions and tariffs under the Graham Act, but the decision did not automatically remove restrictions imposed separately on Russian LNG projects.

Beyond regulatory approvals, Arctic operations depend on a limited fleet of specialized carriers capable of navigating ice-covered waters. Seasonal conditions further restrict their deployment, making transportation capacity a critical consideration for new projects.

India’s response to recent LNG disruptions offers an indication of how importing economies may approach future supply arrangements. According to Kpler data for the month of October, the US, Oman and Nigeria accounted for 68% of India’s LNG imports between March and September, against 25% a year earlier. Qatar’s share fell sharply before shipments began recovering this month.

India’s changing LNG suppliers expose a distinction between diversifying sources and reducing exposure to international prices. With more than a third of its LNG reportedly sourced through spot purchases, Indian buyers remain exposed to price fluctuations even when physical supplies are available. Adding Russian Arctic LNG to India’s supplier portfolio would not necessarily change that exposure unless the contracts offered a different pricing structure or greater protection against international price swings.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) projects India’s LNG requirements could reach 64 billion cubic metres annually by 2030, with the gap between contracted supplies and projected demand widening after 2028.

That gap could influence how Indian importers negotiate their next generation of LNG contracts. Long-term agreements offer greater supply certainty, while shorter commitments provide flexibility when demand or prices change. The balance between them will become increasingly important as India seeks to expand natural gas consumption.

Transport economics add another dimension. Analysis by Reuters in May estimated that shipping LNG from Russia’s Yamal project to India’s Kochi terminal via the Northern Sea Route could cost approximately $3.80 per million British thermal units (MMBtu), compared with $2.60-$2.70 through the Suez Canal using transshipment facilities at Murmansk.

In August, the Indian authorities announced plans for a joint Russia–India pilot voyage along the Northern Sea Route in 2027, alongside a bilateral cooperation agreement that is being negotiated. In September, Russian First Deputy Prime Minister Denis Manturov confirmed these plans, stating in the future, Russian-Indian shipments along the NSR could reach 5 million tons annually.

The proposed voyage could help establish whether the NSR offers practical advantages for India-bound cargoes. For LNG, however, that assessment would require more than a successful transit, including evidence of year-round operating reliability and the costs of deploying specialised vessels.

China offers an early indication of how Arctic LNG is entering Asian markets despite Western restrictions. According to S&P Global Energy, Chinese imports of Russian LNG increased nearly 25% during January-July 2026, supported partly by discounted Arctic LNG 2 cargoes. Reuters reported in June that Beijing was preparing a second receiving terminal for the sanctioned project’s shipments.

Meanwhile, Kpler estimates cited by Reuters on October 22 suggest Asian LNG arrivals could reach 23.31 million tonnes in October, their highest level in three months despite elevated prices.

Yet stronger imports do not necessarily signal sustained demand growth. The IEA reported that Asian spot LNG prices averaged $17.50/MMBtu in the second quarter, up 45% year-on-year, and projected a 0.5%  decline in regional gas consumption during 2026 as higher prices encouraged fuel switching and reduced industrial demand.

China’s experience contrasts with that of other Asian importers. On October 1, Britain exempted certain Russian LNG shipments to Japan and South Korea from its sanctions restrictions until March 2028, but only for Sakhalin-2 supplies under existing contracts. India, meanwhile, declined an offer of sanctioned Russian LNG in May, according to Reuters, while remaining open to permitted cargoes. These differences show how sanctions compliance and contractual commitments can shape Asian buyers’ access to Russian gas, regardless of the availability of additional supplies.

Prospective US-Russia cooperation could bring additional Arctic gas into international trade, but new production alone would not redraw global LNG supply patterns. For India and other importing economies, the larger question is whether future projects can offer access on terms that improve their existing procurement choices. The Arctic’s next commercial breakthrough may depend as much on reaching buyers as on producing gas.
