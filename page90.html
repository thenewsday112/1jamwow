Board of Deputies of British Jews declares Greens to be racist party

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Green Party of England and Wales has been declared a racist organisation by the Board of Deputies of British Jews.

The Jewish body, which is the largest in the UK, was responding to the "Zionism is racism" policy motion passed at the Green Party's conference earlier this month.

Board of Deputies president Phil Rosenberg said a vote passed by 118 votes to one to declare that the Greens had, "by adopting an antisemitic platform, become a racist party".

A spokesman for the Greens insisted the party does not discriminate against people who hold Zionist views and said it rejected the Board of Deputies' "characterisation of this motion and our party".

They added the decision "will not stop us from speaking out against the genocide in Gaza and pushing for stronger sanctions against the Israeli government and its leaders". Israel has repeatedly denied its actions in Gaza amount to genocide.

The motion passed by the Board of Deputies urged other parties not to do deals with the Greens unless they rejected the policy.

Forming coalition deals with Greens at local or national level would be "failing to take antisemitism with the requisite seriousness", it said, unless the party's local leadership have rejected the policy and committed to overturn it.

Labour has already issued an ultimatum to local Green groups it is currently in coalition with, telling them to disown the motion or lose the party's support.

Rosenberg said: "By adopting an antisemitic platform, the Green Party of England and Wales has become a racist party. We will treat it accordingly.

"No political party has the right to tell Jews that a central part of our identity, rooted in thousands of years of history, faith and peoplehood, is racist.

"The Green Party's policy brands much of our community, including our synagogues and communal organisations, with that slur. It gives prejudice against Jews a political licence.

"The Green Party's leadership must now take responsibility for reversing this policy and confronting the antisemitism it has legitimised."

The motion backed by Green members at its party conference earlier this month said Zionism was an ideology supporting "an ethnonationalist Jewish state in historic Palestine to the exclusion and/ or domination of the non-Jewish population".

It was criticised by Jewish groups, as well as four Green MPs and two former leaders, and opposition political parties.

Responding when the Green motion was passed, the Board of Deputies and Jewish Leadership Council said in a joint statement that "Zionism is anti-racism, creating a refuge for Jews in their ancient homeland after millennia of persecution outside it".

Green Party leader Zack Polanski, who is Jewish, did not take part in the vote on the motion as he left the conference early to campaign in the Holborn and St Pancras by-election which was held days later.

He has since told the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg that, while he would like to make amendments, he would not overturn the motion, which would be party policy until 2028.

Brendon McCullum has vowed England will learn from yet another agonising loss which has left their bid to make next weekend’s tri-series final hanging in the balance.

Three days after Sri Lanka chased a staggering 430 in Rawalpindi, England looked set to break their duck in the tournament when they reduced Pakistan to 154 for nine in their pursuit of 242 in Lahore.

Shadab Khan had other ideas, though, as his maiden international hundred with just number 11 batter Haris Rauf for support sealed an unlikely one-wicket victory and a third defeat in a row for England.

Harry Brook’s side are now all but eliminated from the tri-series and their exit will be confirmed on Tuesday if Pakistan get beaten by Sri Lanka, who then face England on Thursday in the final group game.

McCullum admitted the past few days have been bruising for his team but, with the 50-over World Cup in southern Africa in 12 months’ time in mind, he believes this has been a worthwhile experience.

“We will learn from this,” England’s white-ball head coach said. “The boys are gutted, to be honest. They were gutted after the last game and they’re even more gutted after this one.

“These boys are immensely talented, they’re incredibly driven to perform for England, we know that we’re not the best side that we can be just yet but that’s the challenge that’s in front of us.

“There’s some good lessons and learning opportunities and we will learn from it because we are desperate to get to South Africa and have a realistic chance of challenging for that World Cup.”

Shadab’s 113 not out is the highest score by a number eight in ODIs and he impeccably marshalled the chase as Rauf faced just 19 balls in an unbroken last-wicket stand of 89 off 80 deliveries.

It meant that Ben Duckett’s 105 in England’s 241 all out – a total that looked below-par only for a collective effort from their bowlers to take them to the brink of victory – was in a losing cause.

Asked what they could have differently, McCullum replied: “It’s so fresh. We’ll look at it in the next little while, go through all the footage, the data and analytics and look at some of the options if we’re placed in that situation another time.

“They are the conversations we are having and we need to keep having. When you’ve got an inexperienced line-up and you’ve got a big World Cup in 12 months’ time and you’ve only got 20 games between now and then, you need to have that level of communication and those examples of real time experiences.”

Shadab was given out lbw to Gus Atkinson on 97 and the teams were even shaking hands, only for a hopeful review to show the all-rounder had grazed the ball with his bat.

There was more drama when the umpire failed to signal a wide from Sam Curran’s final ball of an over, prompting Rauf and Will Jacks into a confrontation as tensions rose.

Joe Root had to separate former Yorkshire team-mate Rauf from Jacks and it was the tail-end batter who had the last laugh as Pakistan, clad in pink to raise awareness for breast cancer, sealed victory with a four to leave England feeling blue.

Assessing the coming together between Rauf and Jacks, McCullum said: “It’s just cricket. Teams are playing for their country and it means a lot.

“There are pressure situations and moments where we get a little bit confrontational but it’s just the way it is. There’s no malice in it. I know Haris very well, he’s a good competitor and so is Will Jacks, and you’d expect nothing else from that.”

Beaucoup de tristesse et une forme de rage. Plusieurs centaines de personnes se sont réunies pour rendre hommage à Pengo Bossio Wangi, dimanche 25 octobre, à Aubervilliers, en Seine-Saint-Denis. L'homme âgé de 26 ans a été tué lors de l'attentat terroriste et raciste perpétré à Trappes, dans les Yvelines, vendredi. Son club de MMA, sport de combat, situé à Aubervilliers, a organisé cette marche blanche qui s'est déroulée dans le silence.

La garde à vue du suspect de l'attentat raciste à Trappes "suspendue" le temps "d'une période d'observation psychiatrique susceptible de durer 48 heures", annonce le parquet antiterroriste

"Face à celles et ceux qui veulent nous détester, nous avons une pensée pour Pengo, pour sa famille, que je salue, pour ses proches, pour toutes celles et ceux qui, de près ou de loin, ont croisé son chemin", déclare le maire d'Aubervilliers, Sofiane Karoumi, avant le début du cortège. Une minute de silence a été observée et plusieurs centaines de participants se sont élancés, des personnes de tous âges, de toutes origines et de toutes les couleurs de peau venues pour se mobiliser contre le racisme.

L'émotion est palpable, des larmes coulent et les hommages à Pengo Bossio Wangui se succèdent, notamment de la part de ses proches, parmi lesquels Thibault, membre du même club de MMA que la victime. "C'était quelqu'un de joyeux, souriant, toujours là pour les autres, confie-t-il, ému. On se soutenait mutuellement, on avait des liens très fraternels avec notre groupe. Pengo, c'était le gars que tout le monde aimait bien. Nous, on est là pour lui, vraiment pour lui. En dehors des messages politiques, de ce genre de choses, on veut vraiment honorer sa mémoire."

En tête de cortège se trouvent le maire d'Aubervilliers, ainsi que celui de Saint-Denis, Bally Bagayoko et Stéphane Chaufourier le responsable de la Atch Academy, le club de MMA dans lequel Pengo Bossio Wangui était inscrit. C'est lui d'ailleurs qui est à l'origine de cette marche blanche. Sur une banderole déployée, un dernier hommage à la victime : "Tu resteras à jamais dans nos cœurs".

"Je suis née ici, c'est mon pays, et il n'y a pas de raison qu'on se sente en insécurité."

Au fur et à mesure, des passants s'agrègent à la foule, des anonymes venus en soutien marcher contre le racisme. Nawel, 26 ans, brandit sa pancarte sur laquelle est inscrit "Le racisme tue". "Je suis française, j'ai des origines maghrébines, mais il n'y a pas de raison en fait qu'on vienne comme ça nous tuer. Bien sûr que je suis française, et j'aime la République, et j'aime la France", s'indigne la jeune femme avant de poursuivre : "Et on sait aussi qu'il y a une petite fille de 12 ans qui a été touchée. Pourquoi une enfant ? Pourquoi ? On n'a pas les mots." La marche s'est terminée par une nouvelle minute de silence et sous les applaudissements. D'autres rassemblements contre le racisme sont organisés dans les jours suivants dans toute la France.

Extra nach Berlin! Denn hier findet Kunst statt, die Wellen schlagen will: Milo Rau wagt sich an seinen ersten Wagner und zum ersten Mal explizit an die Holocaust-Israel-Thematik. Was ist ihm gelungen, dem Intendanten der Wiener Festwochen, der sich mit seinem International Institute of Political Murder durchaus nuanciert mit Menschenrechtsverletzungen in Rwanda oder Rumänien befasst hat, sich aber, was Israel im Schatten des 7. Wellen schlägt er wohl mit dieser Regiearbeit. Aber Wagners Oper wird von der politischen Dimension der Regie – in Idee wie Ausführung – besetzt, annektiert und geplättet. There is a turn on the SW3 bus route that has long made me wince, where Sydney Metro’s now-infamous pink replacement bus services are forced to make an unwieldy left turn onto Canterbury Road from Wonga Street, near the Cooks River in Canterbury.

Each morning, I sit among other commuters who brace for impact as some poor driver is forced to swing the bus wide into the outside lane in order to then jam the vehicle leftward into whatever space is available to it on Canterbury Road – all while an audience watches on.

All things considered, I haven’t had it so bad. I moved to Campsie from Sydney’s eastern suburbs late last year and have been held hostage by the chaos lottery these pink buses induce for only the past 12 months. Longtime neighbours of mine have suffered much longer.

The old T3 rail line went dark in September 2024. It was originally meant to be closed for only 12 months. When the south-west metro line opens on Friday morning, I will thankfully be enjoying an early start to my weekend. But for the patient residents scattered between Sydenham and Bankstown, I can only imagine that first commute will trigger ecstasy.

Not just because they’ll get to enjoy the arrival of one of the finest pieces of infrastructure in the country at their doorsteps, but because it’ll mark the end of the pink buses that have put a hex on their commutes for years.

Every time the pink buses come up in conversation, particularly in the early weeks after I moved, I turn straight to Canterbury’s dead man’s turn. My rants are usually met with dead-eyed stares suggesting hysteria. “It can’t be that bad,” I’m told. “Think of how good it will be when the Metro is finally open,” others are quick to console.

But eventually, I’d be vindicated. On the morning of February 11 this year, one driver went into the manoeuvre a little over-confident. The approach was forebodingly fast and sharp. Then, about halfway through the turn, came a yawning metallic groan, so slow and guttural it may well have delivered us all pure catharsis if we weren’t so concerned about the driver beside us.

Once the bus made its way around the corner, its victim came into view: a small yellow Hyundai hatchback, pinned up onto the footpath. The driver, thankfully, was okay. The car, not so much.

This incident, I’ve thought in the months since, can’t be the only one. So my guess is it won’t just be the ecstatic commuters boarding the south-west metro line from 4.31am on Friday who will be breathing sighs of relief.

Drivers unlucky enough to rely on Canterbury and New Canterbury roads to get to and from work during peak hour have been forced to drive alongside these buses with the requisite care and caution of a bomb squad. The buses have made the already clogged section of Canterbury Road between the Cooks River and the RSL so bad during peak hour that my friends and I now call it “the Canterbury crawl”.

Then there are the residents of the tight suburban thoroughfares along these routes who, for the last two years, have been forced to acclimatise to the dreaded hum of heavy vehicles bowling through streets too narrow to accommodate them.

I can’t wait to descend the stairs at Campsie station for the first time, for the way it’ll bring the people who live up and down the south-west line closer together and for the way it promises to make all our lives easier.

But right now, I’m almost more excited to be writing an obituary for the dreaded pink bus. May they never return.

Let's bin the silly arguments over bins

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bins, like fences, are great sources of discord.

I still can't forgive my neighbours who objected to me putting my recycling in "their" yellow top bin. I got into the habit of placing the odd can and bottle into "their" bin when it was out on the kerb early on Mondays. If "their" bin wasn't full, I reasoned, why put two bins out?

But one evening, the two women who live behind me knocked on my door and told me that they had spotted me doing so. And it just wasn't on. Not on.

I pointed out that it wasn't actually their bin but the council's. All the same, my reasonableness cut no ice. It buttered no parsnips.

Since then, lots of people have told me their bin horror stories - or so they perceive these matters of utter triviality to be.

The management committee of an apartment block in which a friend lives has just put in cameras to spot people putting items in the wrong recycling bin. The penalty has not yet been disclosed but presumably the transgressor will be forced to sit in the stocks and get pelted with rotten tomatoes, organic of course.

Some councils say that "dog poo and other pet waste can be bagged and disposed of in the red general waste bin". But there are instances of people objecting when they spot a dog owner putting the waste into "their" bin.

And don't get anyone going on those lads who go round yellow top bins at night to get the cans which earn 10 cents in the excellent recycling centres in NSW.

To my mind, if people want to work really hard to earn ten cents, more power to their elbows. Such enterprise should be encouraged. They are strivers who deserve to get ahead.

My local one in the Woolies car parks gets 34,000 cans a day. On a Saturday morning, streams of enterprising people queue to push the cans and bottles in.

Every time I see them, I admire their energy. It also makes me reflect on how 10 cents is significant money for some people.

But putting your waste in "their" bin is the big issue of our times. The Nine news organisation actually did a poll on it and found that "Australia divided over whether it's OK to put stuff in other people's bins on bin night".

Divided is the right word: 49 per cent said it was OK and 51 per cent said no way.

There is etiquette on this big issue apparently. "The polite thing to do is ask first. If a neighbours bin isn't full and you aren't about to fill it with smelly, inappropriate items, then it is most likely that they wont mind, but you should never assume," according to the Australian Seniors organisation, quoting the director of the Australian School of Etiquette (there is such a thing).

But some people just don't know this etiquette.

In June, the Nine network reported that "what started as a petty dispute over rubbish bins with her neighbours escalated into all-out war, with the mum-of-three flying into a rage". Escalation is the right word: "Danielle's pretty handy with a chainsaw. Even when she's dressed in heels."

Nine's A Current Affair also covered "neighbours at war over wheelie bin placement".

There are wars on this planet but I don't feel that "wheelie bin placement" is one of the more serious ones.
