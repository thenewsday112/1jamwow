Apple iPhone 18 Pro review: big changes for small differences

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Guardian journalism is independent. We earn a commission if you buy something through an affiliate link. Learn more.

Longer battery life, better screen, upgraded Siri and new variable aperture camera in refreshed design from last year’s model

Apple’s smaller Pro iPhone gets the best kind of spec bump for 2026: longer battery life, faster chips and a fancy new variable aperture for the main camera.

The iPhone 18 Pro is one of a pair of slab phones launched alongside Apple’s first folding phone, the iPhone Duo, and is the cheapest of the new phones, with a regular iPhone 18 yet to be announced.

It isn’t exactly low cost, priced from £1,199 (€1,449/$1,199/A$2,099), with a £100 or equivalent rise over previous models courtesy of RAMageddon.

Having fully revamped the design of the body for the iPhone 17 Pro, the new model is a modest refinement. It is 7g heavier and the back glass now matches the colour of the aluminium body, which is available in a handful of new shades.

Apple has shrunk the amount of the screen taken up by the Face ID camera, allowing the dynamic island to show three continuing tasks, up from two on previous devices. It also now shows the 3/4/5G indicator next to the signal level when playing music, which is a most appreciated feature for public transport commuters sorely missed on previous models.

Otherwise, the 6.3in screen is one of the very best on a phone: super bright, crisp and smooth, while being big enough without making the phone enormous. An anti-glare coating makes it much easier to see outside, too.

Screen: 6.3in Super Retina XDR (120Hz OLED) (460ppi)

Camera: 48MP + 48MP UW + 48MP 4x, 18MP front-facing

Connectivity: 5G, wifi 7, NFC, Bluetooth 6, Thread, USB-C, Satellite, UWB and GNSS

Water resistance: IP68 (6 metres for 30 mins)

The 18 Pro has Apple’s new A20 Pro chip, which is about 27% faster than the A19 Pro in last year’s model, with about 45% faster graphics performance. Since the A19 Pro was no slouch, that’s quite the leap and puts it miles ahead of the competition, ready to handle anything.

The phone has a redesigned cooling system for longer peak performance, too, which is especially useful for running games on top settings.

The battery lasts a solid six hours longer than the iPhone 17 Pro, managing to stretch to about two days between charges with more than six hours of active screen use across apps, browsing, messaging and photos. Even with the heaviest of use while out and about, the 18 Pro managed to end the day with 40% left in the tank. Most people will only need to charge it every other day.

The 18 Pro ships running iOS 27, which was released for the rest of the iPhone line on 14 September. It refines the relatively controversial Liquid Glass redesign from last year and speeds up the system, including app launches. But the big new addition is Apple’s revamped Siri, backed by Google’s AI technologies.

Siri AI, as the new system is called, is significantly more capable of understanding and answering questions than previous iterations. It is a chatbot like most other AI assistants, but it is less chatty and sycophantic than others, being more likely to just get the job done. As well as a choice of voices, there are sliders to control how fast it talks and how expressive its responses are. It has its own app where you can review previous interactions, but it is accessible anywhere on the phone via the side button, is built into Spotlight search and can see what’s on your screen, including when using the camera.

It can also offer suggestions, such as adding an event to your calendar from the messages or mail apps, and attempts to display relevant information from your emails in the phone app when you call a business. But Siri can only do so if you use Apple’s apps, so if you use WhatsApp for texting and Gmail for your email, none of it works.

The battery will last more than 1,000 full-charge cycles with at least 80% of its original capacity and can be replaced for £119. Out-of-warranty screen repairs cost £349. The specialists iFixit awarded the phone a seven out of 10 for repairability.

It contains more than 40% recycled material. The company breaks down the phone’s environmental impact in its report. Apple offers trade-in and free recycling schemes, including for non-Apple products.

The 18 Pro has a similar camera system to last year’s 17 Pro with one big addition, a variable aperture for the main camera.

That means it has a very good 18MP centre stage selfie camera on the front and three 48MP cameras on the rear: main, 0.5x ultrawide and 4x telephoto.

The ultrawide is one of the best on a phone, handy for landscapes and group shots. The 4x telephoto is very good too, full of detail and manages lower light settings well. It shoots excellent photos with a crop zoom to reach 8x before digital zoom is required, which is a little behind rivals with 5x/10x telephoto zooms.

The variable aperture on the main camera can be left on automatic or controlled manually. It allows the camera to open wider than previous models for better low light performance, speeding up night mode shots and reducing unwanted motion blur.

But it also enables a number of creative uses, such as making starbursts by narrowing the aperture when shooting lights, which is very fun, or controlling the depth of field in your photo. However, because the iPhone’s camera is tiny compared with an APS-C or full-frame camera, the difference in depth of field – where the foreground and background of the image are either in focus or blown out for a nice bokeh effect – is pretty minor. When shooting something up close, manually closing the aperture to f/4.0 can bring things in the foreground or background into focus, but otherwise the difference for most shots is small.

The 18 Pro’s camera has a number of other useful upgrades. My favourite is AI-powered focus tracking that keeps a subject in focus as it moves about or into and out of the frame, which has been one of the best features of a Pixel camera for years and is extremely handy for fast-moving children or pets.

Apple has also added texture and grain control to its Photographic Styles feature to customise how the camera shoots photos beyond what was introduced with the iPhone 16 Pro, including film simulation. Finally, the new Apple Reference Image feature can sign an image for authenticity, on a per pixel level directly on the sensor, to help later prove a shot is not AI-generated, similar to a digital negative.

The camera shoots some of the very best video on a phone, too. New for this year is the ability to convert a regular video to a cinematic one after the fact, a bit like the excellent automatic portrait mode for photos.

The iPhone 18 Pro costs from £1,199 (€1,449/$1,199/A$2,099).

For comparison, the iPhone 17 costs £899, the iPhone Air costs £1,099, the iPhone 18 Pro Max costs £1,299, the Google Pixel 11 Pro costs £1,079 and the Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra costs £1,359.

The iPhone 18 Pro is an iterative upgrade on an already great device from last year. Inside, it has some quite big changes, but their impact is fairly minor on the whole experience.

The new A20 Pro chip is super fast, but you can’t really notice the difference day to day, as previous iPhone chips have already been very quick. The extended battery life is great, but most people will still have to charge it every other day. The smaller camera cutout in the dynamic island now shows three continuing activities instead of two. Even the variable aperture in the main camera makes only a small difference to image quality unless you’re really trying to get creative.

The screen is fantastic and big enough. The aluminium body feels great to hold. The cameras are some of the best on a phone and even Siri is better now.

All of these things add up. The 18 Pro is without doubt the best iPhone ever made and at this size is one of the very best smaller phones available. It’s just not a transformative change unless you’re upgrading from a much older model and it now costs even more thanks to RAMageddon.

Pros: relatively compact, great screen, better dynamic island, longer battery life, great cameras, variable aperture, USB-C, top performance, long software support, Face ID, better AI.

Cons: small upgrade on last year, expensive, heavier than previous model, AI tools still lag behind top competitors.

"Superintelligenz" statt KI: Trumps neue Sprachregelung

📅 Oct 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Donald Trump hat die US-Behörden angeordnet, statt "Künstliche Intelligenz" künftig den Begriff "Superintelligenz" zu nutzen. Warum der US-Präsident KI für unpassend hält und was Tech-Experten dazu sagen.

Was ist nur los mit Donald Trump und seinem Bedürfnis, alles Mögliche neu zu benennen? Erst wurde der Golf von Mexiko zum Golf von Amerika - zumindest in offiziellen Dokumenten der USA, wenn auch nicht global. Dann kam der Ontariosee, nun nach Willen des US-Präsidenten der Amerikasee. Und nun soll auch die neue Technologie, die unser Zeitalter prägen wird, die künstliche Intelligenz oder KI, einen neuen Namen erhalten.

Anfang September behauptete der US-Präsident auf seiner Plattform Truth Social, der Begriff "künstliche Intelligenz" sei "ungenau" und "unzutreffend". Bessere Alternativen wären "überlegene", "extreme" oder "höchste" Intelligenz.

In einer über drei Tage in den sozialen Medien durchgeführten Umfrage gaben Trump-Fans mit 65 Prozent dem Begriff "Superintelligenz" (SI) den Vorzug über "Überlegene Intelligenz" (35 Prozent). Daraufhin verkündete Trump vergangene Woche vor der Vollversammlung der Vereinten Nationen, dass von nun an in Dokumenten der US-Bundesregierung diese neue Bezeichnung verwendet werde.

Bislang hat die Tech-Branche die Bezeichnung jedoch noch nicht übernommen und Akademiker weisen darauf hin, dass Superintelligenz ein Begriff für die Zukunft sei, wenn die Technologie den Menschen in allen Bereichen überlegen ist. Aktuelle KI-Modelle seien davon noch Jahre entfernt, betonen sie.

"KI ist in den meisten Fällen keine Superintelligenz sondern lediglich eine andere Intelligenz", sagt Pedro Domingos, emeritierter Professor für Informatik und Ingenieurwissenschaften an der University of Washington, zur DW. "KI macht andere Dinge als die Menschen, sie als Superintelligenz zu bezeichnen ist also irreführend."

Computerwissenschaftler und Mathematiker prägten den Begriff "künstliche Intelligenz" 1956 während eines Sommer-Workshops am Dartmouth College in New Hampshire, USA. "Künstlich" hieß dabei einfach "von Menschen gemacht", im Unterschied zur natürlichen menschlichen Intelligenz.

Nicht alle Experten waren mit dieser Bezeichnung einverstanden. In ihrem Buch "Denkmaschinen: Die Geschichte der künstlichen Intelligenz" zitiert Pamela McCorduck den KI-Pionier Arthur Samuel, der der Meinung war, der Begriff lasse einen an "etwas Unechtes" denken.

Doch in den vergangenen 70 Jahren hat sich der Begriff durchgesetzt. Ihn zu ändern wird die vielen Ängste im Zusammenhang mit KI - dass Jobs verloren gehen, der Mensch überflüssig wird oder wir die Kontrolle über die Maschinen verlieren - nicht mindern, sagen Kritiker.

"Die Namensänderung fördert solche Ängste: 'Oh, es handelt sich um Superintelligenz und die wird uns Menschen überflüssig machen, sich gegen uns wenden, unsere Jobs wegnehmen", warnt Domingos. "Wenn du die Gegenbewegung gegen KI maximieren willst, dann nenne sie Überlegene Intelligenz."

Die Ängste der Bevölkerung nehmen rasch zu. In einer Anfang des Monats von Public First für Politico durchgeführten Studie sprach sich etwa die Hälfte der Befragten in sechs Ländern - den USA, Kanada, Frankreich, Spanien, Deutschland und Großbritannien - dafür aus, weitere Entwicklungen im Bereich KI zu pausieren, um das Risiko zu verringern, dass die neue Technologie die Menschheit auslöschen könnte.

Einer weiteren, von SSRS für CNN durchgeführten Studie zufolge sind fast neun von zehn US-Amerikanern eher besorgt als begeistert darüber, dass die KI die menschliche Intelligenz übertreffen könnte.

Diese Bedenken haben auch den US-Kongress erreicht. Gemeinsam mit anderen hat der parteiunabhängige Senator von Vermont, Bernie Sanders, einen Gesetzesentwurf für ein "dauerhaftes Verbot der Entwicklung künstlicher Superintelligenz", die vom Menschen nicht kontrolliert werden kann, eingebracht.

Der Gesetzesentwurf fordert außerdem eine Aussetzung der Entwicklung fortschrittlicher KI-Technologien, bis entsprechende Tests und Kontrollmechanismen auf Bundesebene eingerichtet sind.

Den großen KI-Entwicklern in den USA, die ihre Modelle gerne ins Zentrum der Aufmerksamkeit rücken wollen, könnte die von Trump geforderte neue Bezeichnung natürlich gelegen kommen. Ihre Systeme sind den Menschen bereits in vielen spezialisierten Aufgaben überlegen und waren China bis vor kurzem weit voraus.

Doch sowohl Anthropic, das Unternehmen hinter dem Modell Claude, als auch OpenAI, das ChatGPT entwickelt hat, haben Pläne für einen Börsengang verschoben. Einige Marktbeobachter machen dafür die jüngsten Vorfälle verantwortlich, bei denen KI-Modelle sich in Computersysteme gehackt oder Aufgaben ausgeführt hatten, die sie nicht ausführen sollten.

Während chinesische Modelle den Qualitätsrückstand bei deutlich geringerer Rechenleistung weitgehend aufholen, könnte den US-Entwicklern eine Umbenennung, die den Eindruck bestärkt, dass sie weiterhin führend im Rennen um KI sind, durchaus gelegen kommen. Laut Zahlen von Goldman Sachs sollen die Ausgaben für KI bald die Marke von einer Billion US-Dollar (880 Milliarden Euro) übersteigen.

"Es ist ganz im Interesse der US-Entwickler, von Super-, Hyper-, überlegener oder sonstiger Intelligenz zu sprechen, weil es ihre Fähigkeiten hervorhebt. Es ist schlicht Marketing", sagt Domingos, Autor des 2015 veröffentlichten Buchs "The Master Algorithm".

Die aktuellen KI-Modelle werden immer besser und führen viele Aufgaben bereits eigenständig aus. In den Entwicklungslaboren wird jedoch viel über den nächsten Schritt, KI-Systeme, die sich selbst verbessern, diskutiert. Dies wird als rekursive Selbstverbesserung oder RSI bezeichnet.

Elon Musk hat bereits vorhergesagt, dass die Lernprozesse der KI bis Ende des Jahres oder bis 2027 automatisiert werden könnten, ohne die Notwendigkeit menschlicher Eingaben. Bei OpenAI geht man davon aus, dass es bis dahin ein Jahr länger dauern könnte. Doch selbst dann werden die KI-Systeme nicht vollständig rekursiv sein.

Anfang September argumentierte Jürgen Schmidhuber, ein deutscher Informatiker, der in der Schweiz arbeitet, in einem Blog-Eintrag: "Für eine vollständige RSI ist nicht nur sich selbst verbessernde (KI-)Software, sondern auch sich selbst verbessernde Hardware erforderlich."

Mit anderen Worten, um Superintelligenz oder RSI zu erreichen, müssten die Tech-Unternehmen Chips und humanoide Roboter produzieren und ganze Fabriken bauen, die keinen menschlichen Input benötigen.

Auch wenn die KI die Chipentwicklung beschleunigt, kann sie den Fertigungsprozess noch nicht alleine ausführen. Bis Maschinen sich ohne jede menschliche Beteiligung selbst verbessern können, vergehen vermutlich noch Jahrzehnte.

Doch neben den Wissenschaftlern, die am maschinellen Lernen arbeiten, wird auch die Politik eine große Rolle dabei spielen, ob sich die Bezeichnung "Superintelligenz" durchsetzt. Auch wenn Trump behauptete, dem chinesischen Präsidenten Xi Jinping habe der Begriff gefallen, gibt es noch keine Anzeichen dafür, dass China sich von "KI" verabschiedet.

Auf einem Tech-Gipfel mit führenden Vertretern der Branche unterzeichnete Trump am Dienstag eine Durchführungsverordnung, die US-Behörden und -Agenturen anweist, den Begriff Superintelligenz zu verwenden. Doch auch wenn einige der führenden Köpfe von Big Tech den Begriff übernommen haben, halten Experten es für unwahrscheinlich, dass er sich durchsetzen wird.

"Angesichts der politischen Ausrichtung der meisten Menschen im Bereich KI ist es wahrscheinlicher, dass sie genau im Gegenteil sagen, 'Zur Hölle mit dir. Jetzt sprechen wir erst rechtvon künstlicher Intelligenz", meint Domingos.

Gypsy Gravy: Sometimes the recipe isn’t the real point.

📅 Oct 03, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

I didn’t set out to create a recipe the day I made Gypsy Gravy.

I was having a bad day at work. Nothing dramatic—just one of those days when everything seemed to be piling up. I needed to step away from it for a while, so I went into the kitchen and started cooking.

I had some vegetables on hand, so I started chopping. Onions, garlic, peppers, zucchini and potatoes went into a pan with olive oil. I added some seasoning, poured in crushed tomatoes and let everything simmer. There wasn’t really a plan. And that was kind of the point.

I’ve always loved what happens when I get lost in cooking. It’s therapeutic. It gives my mind something tangible to focus on. There’s a rhythm to it—chop, sauté, season, stir, taste, adjust. You don’t have to think about everything else for those moments. You just have to pay attention to what’s happening in front of you. For a little while, everything else gets quieter. The problems don’t disappear, but my mind has somewhere else to go.

By the time lunch was ready, I felt different.

The problems hadn’t disappeared. My day hadn’t magically gotten better. But I had gotten better.

While enjoying my lunch, I thought about a recent conversation I had with a friend over dinner. We were talking about traditions and family recipes, and she shared about a dish her family would often make. It wasn’t the recipe itself that stuck with me, but the emotion and passion she shared while describing it. I realized that conversation had helped ignite the spark for the dish I had just made.

What I love about Gypsy Gravy is that there really isn’t one way to make it. Add eggplant or beans. Change up the vegetables. I’ve simmered shrimp and scallops in the same tomato base, turning it into something more like a rustic seafood stew. Serve it over pasta or rice, or grab some crusty bread and eat it as a stew.

It’s forgiving. It’s adaptable. You work with what you have.

Maybe that’s another reason I like it so much.

A few weeks later, I had the opportunity to share Gypsy Gravy as part of Cooks Who Care’s Food Is Therapy series. What started as a recipe created on a difficult workday had come full circle. I wasn’t there simply to demonstrate how to make a tomato-based vegetable dish. I was there to talk about what happens when we give ourselves permission to step away from the noise, breathe, and get lost in something we love.

Eight people gathered around the kitchen that day. We cooked, we shared the meal, and we shared stories.

And somewhere between the cooking and the conversation, I was reminded that the recipe was never really the point.

But sometimes it gives us the space we need to come back to ourselves.

That’s what Gypsy Gravy has come to mean to me—a simple dish born out of a not-so-great day that somehow became part of a much bigger story about food, connection, and finding a little room to breathe.

And, of course, it doesn’t hurt that it’s pretty darn good.

The post-war "peace dividend" owed its existence to three factors: industrial capacity, political freedom and pent-up demand.

Political freedom: the war's orgy of capital destruction of the majority of the wealth (and thus the power) of the world's oligarchs;

Demand: Making up for years of neglect and privation during the war required new production of infrastructure and consumption goods.

In Capital in the 21st Century, Thomas Piketty and his students analyzed painstakingly assembled records of 300 years' worth of capital flows, showing that wealth tends to pool in the hands of the already-wealthy. This creates mounting instability, thanks to the misrule of a shrinking class of increasingly powerful hereditary, unaccountable oligarchs whose whims and follies trump the material and political needs of the vast majority:

https://memex.craphound.com/2014/06/24/thomas-pikettys-capital-in-the-21st-century/

That instability eventually reaches a breaking point in which the old order collapses in spectacular fashion, and that collapse destroys vast amounts of capital stock. Since this breaking point arrives as a result of oligarchy, in which nearly all the world's wealth has been hoarded by a tiny number of people, those aristocrats are disproportionately impoverished by the conflagration. If 90% of the wealth is in the hands of 1% of the people, a war or disaster that wipes out most capital will mostly destroy the wealth of the 1%. The poor suffer terribly, but they start with nothing – and end with nothing.

We are clearly steaming into one of these situations. The wealthy squandered two generations preventing the world from tackling the climate emergency, and now the inevitable has arrived. California is on track to see a 12 inch sea-level rise in the next month:

Advice for GLP-1 Users

📅 Oct 02, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Someone asked me what I’d recommend to someone taking GLP drugs. I’ve written posts and newsletters on this before, but there’s one newer bit of advice I haven’t mentioned before: eat more.

The thing with these drugs is that they work incredibly well. Whether it’s semaglutide (Ozempic), tirzepatide (Mounjaro), or the yet-to-be-FDA-approved retatrutide, these compounds are very good at getting you to eat less. They have other effects, too, like slowed gastric emptying, “slower” digestion, and others—some positive, some more negative.

But the one thing almost everyone reports is that you eat far less food on these things.

That can be good if you need it. Most people eat too much. Even if it’s healthy food. I remember being at PrimalCon back in the day and being shocked by the amount of food people could put away. Everything was high quality, cooked in good fats, well-sourced, but people would routinely report that they gained weight at PrimalCon. Food is food. Volume is volume. And that’s why these drugs are having such powerful effects in the overweight and obese. They’re losing weight because they aren’t eating very much and, most importantly, they aren’t hungry doing it.

But what about when you start losing too much? When you start losing muscle, losing energy?

Everyone says the obvious: “make your calories count.” Get your micronutrients. Make it high quality, whole foods. With less volume coming in, the quality of what little volume you do get becomes increasingly important.

And they talk about protein, too, rightfully. A big part of countering the negative effects of GLP drugs, like muscle loss, requires that you stay active. Lift weights, and support that activity by eating protein.

These are all good, but you’ve heard them before.

The one piece of advice you might not have heard, that sounds a bit unconventional, but that becomes useful especially as you start approaching your ideal weight: eat more.

Try to eat a bit more than you feel like.

Many issues we see with these drugs are from undernourishment. You’re running a deficit and, depending on the drug in question, burning more energy. You need to eat a little more than you think.
