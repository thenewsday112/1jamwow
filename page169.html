Drei Schlösser, die in Österreich derzeit zum Verkauf stehen [premium]

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Schlösser haben wohl so viele Zwecke erfüllt und Besitzern gedient, wie kaum eine andere Immobilienart. Einige davon stehen wieder zum Verkauf.

Wenige Immobilien sind so vielseitig wie Schlösser. Viele dienten als Adelssitze, Verteidigungsbastionen und Heeresunterkünfte; wurden zu Krankenlagern oder Verwaltungsgebäuden adaptiert, als Firmensitze oder Internate genutzt.

Geplant wurde ein Großteil als Wohnschlösser, deren Erbauer sich großzügige Grundrisse und eine gewisse Lebensart leisten konnten – die bis heute von einer kleinen, aber feinen Zielgruppe geschätzt wird. Auch wenn in den Jahrhunderten danach ein wenig Zeitgeschichte über die Immobilien gekommen ist. Drei heimische Schlösser, die derzeit zum Verkauf stehen.

Ausgesprochen jung ist allerdings das Plabutscher Schlössl bei Graz, das wieder auf dem Markt ist. 1897 wurde es von Stadtbaumeister Georg Hönel und Bildhauer Hans Brandstätter im Auftrag eines Gutsbesitzers entworfen und gilt heute als Beispiel der Cottage-Bewegung in Kombination mit der Bürgerromantik.

Bereits zehn Jahre später wurde das Gebäude in einer gut vier Hektar großen Parkanlage in eine „Heilanstalt für Epileptiker und geistig beeinträchtigte Menschen“ umgewandelt und hat seither mehrfach den Besitzer gewechselt. Unter anderem findet sich in der „Ahnengalerie“ auch Ex-Tennisprofi Thomas Muster, der es 2018 kaufte.

Seit 2014 stehen die Fassade, der Torbogen und der Brunnen unter Denkmalschutz, in den vergangenen Jahren wurde das Schloss aufwendig renoviert und in den Originalzustand gebracht. In den 14 Zimmern und drei Bädern, die sich hier über drei Etagen und gute 750 Quadratmeter Nutzfläche verteilen, sind etliche historische Details erhalten. Darunter dunkle Holzvertäfelungen, kunstvolle Deckengewölbe, massive Holzdecken – und die tiefroten Wandbespannungen des Roten Salons. Vermittelt wird das Schloss über Dahler Immobilien in Graz, aufgerufen sind dafür 2,9 Millionen Euro.

Um die gleiche Summe ist auch ein saniertes Renaissance-Schloss in Oberösterreich zu haben. Im 13. Jahrhundert als Burg gebaut, um 1600 zu einem Renaissance-Schloss umgebaut und 2004 sockelsaniert, hat auch dieses Anwesen schon seine Vielseitigkeit unter Beweis gestellt. Heute ist es ein Wohnschloss mit rund 800 Quadratmetern Wohnfläche, das sich auf einem fast 20.000 Quadratmeter großen Grund mit Altbaumbestand befindet – der standesgemäß durch ein schmiedeeisernes, barockes Tor betreten wird.

Der Weg führt von dort durch das einstige Tor mit Zugbrücke zum Innenhof des Schlosses, der mit einem Renaissance-Brunnen und Arkadengängen die Geschichte des Hauses weitererzählt. Das Schloss selbst betritt man ebenso standesgemäß durch eine doppelflügelige Tür mit der eingemeißelten Jahreszahl 1605 darüber. Dahinter verbergen sich eine Halle mit Steinplatten und ein Rittersaal mit Stichkappengewölbe und einem auf 1556 datierten Marmorkamin; außerdem 14 Zimmer – darunter acht Schlafzimmer und eine Bibliothek – sowie vier Bäder.

Darüber hinaus gibt es auf dem Gelände neben dem Schlossteich noch eine Kapelle aus der Renaissance mit Kreuzrippengewölbe und Stuckfassung, ein Torwärterhäuschen, in dem heute ein Gästeapartment untergebracht ist, und eine separat zugängliche Wohnung über einer Garage. Der ausgeschilderte Kaufpreis von 2,9 Millionen Euro versteht sich ohne Inventar, vermittelt wird das Schloss über die Makler von Hendrich Real Estate.

Schon viel erlebt hat das Schloss Schwarzenau bei Zwettl (NÖ). Im zwölften Jahrhundert als Burg erbaut, wurde es im Laufe der Jahrhunderte erweitert und verändert. Seine heutige Form mit zwei Ecktürmen und vier Flügeln erhielt es gegen Ende des 16. Jahrhunderts; im 18. Jahrhundert wurden der Süd- und Ostflügel um je ein Stockwerk erhöht und Teile der Repräsentationsräume im Stil des Spätbarocks umgestaltet – ehe sie 1835 ausbrannten. Der Ostflügel wurde beim Wiederaufbau schließlich spätklassizistisch, während der Südflügel lediglich wieder instand gesetzt wurde.

Von 1986 bis 1993 wurden die Burg und die angrenzende Burgschänke dann vollständig renoviert und mit Heizungs-, Sanitär- und Elektroinstallationen ausgestattet, die Holzfachwerk- und Dippelbaumdecken erneuert. Die Türme und das Dach brauchen aber aktuell einiges an Zuwendung.

Erhalten sind daraus eine große Menge an Stuck, Gewölbedecken, Skulpturen und eine Schlosskapelle, das könnte die Nutzung als Hochzeitsort sinnvoll machen. Das Schloss und die Nebengebäude haben 5000 Quadratmeter Nutzfläche, in einem Teil des Erdgeschoßes befinden sich inzwischen ungenutzte Zivilschutzräume. Auf dem knapp zehn Hektar großen Grund gut eineinhalb Fahrstunden von Wien entfernt gibt es neben bewaldeten Flächen auch noch einen großen Teich. Vermittelt wird das Schloss über Raiffeisen Immobilien.

A tiny defect in a crystal reveals a big quantum surprise

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When light strikes a tiny imperfection inside a material such as diamond or silicon carbide, it can transfer energy to electrons and push them into an excited state. These imperfections, known as spin defects, could become valuable building blocks for future quantum technologies, including quantum sensors. Because they can function as stable qubits whose states can be read, scientists are eager to understand exactly how they behave. However, predicting how their energy changes over time has remained a major challenge.

Researchers at the University of Chicago Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering (UChicago PME) have now developed a computational method that provides a more complete picture of what happens when these defects interact with light. Their approach can simulate the entire optical cycle, including a particularly difficult process known as internal conversion.

The findings, published in Physical Review Letters, could help scientists design better materials for quantum technologies. The method may also prove useful for studying materials involved in renewable energy conversion and storage.

"This internal conversion process is not only relevant to spin defects -- it's also related to processes of interest to energy technologies," said Giulia Galli, the Liew Family Professor of Electronic Structure and Simulations in UChicago PME and the Department of Chemistry and the senior author of the study. "That's one of the reasons this work is so exciting."

When a laser excites a spin defect, the defect can return to its original, lower-energy state through several different processes. It might release a photon of light, transition between states with different spins, or transfer its energy into vibrations of the surrounding atoms.

That final pathway, called internal conversion, has been especially difficult for scientists to model accurately. Although the fundamental equations of quantum mechanics can describe the other two processes, calculating how energy is lost through atomic vibrations presents substantial computational obstacles.

"Although the equations are known, it's hard to describe the process accurately because of computational difficulties," said Stefano Villani, a UChicago PME postdoctoral researcher and the study's first author. "You need to account for complex interactions between the electrons and surrounding atoms, and that's challenging in realistic solids represented by models with hundreds or thousands of atoms"

To overcome this problem, Galli, Villani, and Yu Jin combined several computational techniques that their research group had developed over many years. Jin, a former graduate student in Galli's group, is now a postdoctoral scholar at the Flatiron Institute in New York.

The researchers used mathematical descriptions known as many-body wavefunctions to represent the excited states of spin defects. Through exact mathematical derivations, they determined how strongly each excited state interacts with the movements of nearby atoms.

This allowed them to calculate how individual electronic states connect with all the possible vibrational configurations of hundreds of surrounding atoms, rather than having to analyze those configurations individually.

"In the past, a clear mathematical derivation and efficient algorithms were not available," explained Yu Jin. "The new derivation came together after years of work on the optical cycle of spin defects."

The researchers incorporated their new framework into WEST, an open-source simulation program optimized for graphics processing units (GPUs). Galli's group has spent the past decade developing WEST through the Midwest Integrated Center for Computational Materials (MICCoM), which has received funding from the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Basic Energy Sciences since 2015.

A Surprising Discovery Inside Silicon Carbide

To test their approach, the researchers first examined a nitrogen-vacancy center in diamond, a type of crystal defect widely studied for quantum applications.

Their calculations closely matched recent spectroscopy measurements. For triplet states, internal conversion contributed relatively little to energy loss. In singlet states, however, it was the primary mechanism responsible for releasing energy. Both findings agreed with what scientists had previously expected.

The results changed when the team investigated a divacancy defect in silicon carbide.

Their calculations revealed that internal conversion has a much greater influence on the lifetime of the defect's excited triplet state than earlier models had accounted for. This previously underestimated contribution helped explain a long-standing disagreement between theoretical predictions and experimental observations.

"We were expecting to see an effect, but of a smaller magnitude," Galli said. "What we found is that this internal conversion process turns out to be more important than we thought."

Understanding this unexpected energy-loss pathway could help scientists more accurately predict the behavior of silicon carbide defects and improve their potential use as qubits in future quantum devices.

A New Tool for Quantum Computing and Energy Research

With the framework now available through WEST, researchers at other institutions can begin applying it to their own studies. However, Galli noted that the simulations still demand considerable computing power.

The team is exploring ways to reduce those requirements, including the use of artificial intelligence.

"We are working on ways to integrate machine learning into excited states calculations to further improve the efficiency of our method," Villani said.

Meanwhile, the scientists are expanding their approach to investigate spin defects in silicon, as well as materials used in energy technologies where internal conversion influences how energy is transferred or lost.

By addressing one of the most difficult parts of modeling how spin defects respond to light, the researchers have made it possible to predict their behavior more completely and accurately.

"We provided the missing piece of the puzzle needed to completely describe the optical cycle of spin defects," Galli said. "Our work can now be used to make more accurate predictions about many materials of interest for quantum technologies," added Yu Jin.

A Dating App Is Offering ‘Chivalry Mode’ and ‘Marriage Timeline’ to Get Gen Z Married

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Adreanna Pulliam signed up for the Christian dating app Upward over the summer, she was prepared to come across men who identified as hyperconservative.

So when she swiped on a guy’s profile that listed “a progressive donkey or a ‘Trump is King’ elephant” as his relationship deal-breaker, she was shocked. “I hadn’t expected to see a moderate libertarian” on the app, she says.

It only got more bizarre from there. There was the guy advertising his Snapchat with the username “Yaboi Blaze reallysexgod.” Another dater, for reasons she still can’t fathom, included a photo of him at a funeral, standing in front of a poster-size image of the deceased.

While Pulliam was apprehensive about the prospects, she wanted to see how different Upward, one of the lesser-known apps in Match Group’s sprawling portfolio of dating platforms, was from Hinge and Tinder. She hadn’t had much luck with either of them. Many of the profiles she encountered on Upward, she tells WIRED, were “MAGA types of men” who “fit more of that red-pill, trad wife, conservative values” category of dater.

A 25-year-old opera singer in Bloomington, Indiana, for whom marriage has always been “the end goal,” Pulliam is the kind of person Upward is trying to appeal to since relaunching last month.

The relaunch was redesigned around two key data points from Match Group’s 2026 Singles in America study: 72 percent of Gen Zs want to get married, and a quarter of singles aged 18 to 34 “identify with a combination of traditional values around family, faith, and community,” a news release about the survey noted.

The pivot away from an app exclusively for Christian daters was about opening up its doors for people “who share those same goals, but may not lead with a faith-first mindset,” says Lauren DeFord, director of brand marketing at Match, of the app’s new focus on Gen Z singles.

Upward’s expansion is representative of the ongoing change in the dating market. Dwindling engagement and the introduction of AI have pushed companies to reassess user growth. For some, that means abandoning their original mission.

Upward, which runs on a tiered subscription, works similarly to Tinder. You can swipe left or right on profiles, and users are encouraged to include a Faith Statement on their page, explaining what they value most about their relationship to God. Among its new features are Chivalry Mode, which, if you’re familiar with how Bumble used to operate, persuades men to message first, and Marriage Timeline, which lets daters share when they hope to get married with potential suitors.

But while a marriage-focused app like Upward may be tapping into a genuine desire for commitment, that doesn’t mean young people are returning to traditional relationship norms en masse, says Daniel Cox, author of Generation Uncoupled: America’s New Gender Divide and the Crisis of Human Connection. In fact, the opposite is happening.

In his book, Cox argues that Gen Z is entering adulthood with a less clear sense of their “relational priorities,” and a decline in social and civic life has given rise to a “hyperindividualistic culture” that doesn’t always prioritize things like marriage. As a result, fewer Gen Zs are coupling up today. An American Institute for Boys and Men survey found that 44 percent of Gen Z men have had no relationship experience at all during their teen years, double the rate for older men. “This movement makes it a lot harder to ... want to sacrifice and to be there for someone,” he says.

Upward’s relaunch framed the app as the “home of modern courtship,” including video testimonials of singles who’d found love on the app. But aesthetically, there wasn’t anything “modern” about its promotional rollout. In one video, newly married Zach donned a cowboy hat and blue jeans, doing all the things men should presumably do for their significant others: gift them flowers, carry her through a meadow, open the car door. A bold text overlay on the video read “chivalrymaxxing.” According to the company, branding was inspired by an “Americana” theme, and its “Wanted: For Life” campaign draws on Western-style imagery.

Cox, who is the director of the Survey Center on American Life, is convinced that the trad trend is “a reaction to the lack of cultural norms around courtship. People are wanting something over nothing,” he says, adding that daters are desperate for “a script” with direction and defined roles, given how nontraditional approaches to partnership, such as polyamory and relationship anarchy, are increasing among young people. “And something that’s nice about the trad script is it’s very clear. Though I don’t think it’s close to ideal for a lot of young people.”

A 2023 Pew Research survey found that only 23 percent of adults considered being married “extremely” or “very important” to living a fulfilling life, compared with 61 percent who said the same about having close friends. In the last 30 years, the percentage of high school seniors who said they were likely to marry someday had fallen from 80 percent in 1993 to 67 percent in 2023, according to Pew. The decline was driven mostly by changing views among young women who, despite the portion of married adults slightly increasing from 50 percent to 51 percent between 2019 to 2023, remain more likely than men to be unpartnered.

The trend is likely to continue in that direction. “There’s increasingly a belief among young women that marriage makes them less happy,” Cox says. “So it’s not only that they have less of a desire to get married. There’s a concern that if they get married, they won’t be as happy as if they are single. That seems like a view that is potentially not going to change.”

Upward, which declined to share information regarding the number of active users on the platform, is betting that it will.

When setting up her account, Pulliam was baffled to find out that Upward let users list their body type, ranging from “fit” and “curvy” to “chubby,” a feature typically reserved for hookup apps. She was also confused that Upward allows married people on the app. Some of the stranger options for relationship type included “it’s complicated” and “I’m not into labels.” “I’m not knocking open relationships or anything like that, but it was interesting to see such a progressive take from a more of a conservative demographic,” she says.

Tauti, a social work student who lives in California, lasted less than 48 hours on the app, disturbed by the overwhelming conservative majority looking for domesticated partners. Sometimes the hook with mission-specific apps like Upward is that the religious identity associated with the platform can create an assumption of compatibility before users have really gotten to know one another. “I did not know me being Christian and leaning liberal was a big ask,” she said on TikTok. “It’s turned me off from Christian dating altogether.”

Some users believe the recent pivot is diluting the experience for daters who joined seeking out people with purely Christian beliefs. “Why are you on there if you’re agnostic?” Heidi Marais, a teacher and single mom, asked on TikTok in September. “And Upward, why is it an option?”

DeFord, who maintains that Christian singles remain at the core of Upward’s mission despite describing the relaunch as a “brand shift,” pushes back against the assertion that the experience is worsening. “Those users can still interact and match with people who share the same denomination, the same religious views, the same cadence of how they like to practice.”

Since its debut in 2020, Upward has struggled with various hiccups that include everything from functionality issues to user perception. Many reviews in the Apple App Store complain of terrible user support, poor filtering features, and automatic bans, with one user calling the app “janky as hell.”

Cox notes there is ample reason to be wary of Match’s claim that a majority of Gen Z want to get married. Given that Gen Z is younger, it is likely that more people have never tied the knot. “So if they’re comparing Gen Z to millennials or Gen Xers in the survey, it could be that they have already been in relationships, maybe even divorced, so they are not as eager to get married again.” Match’s survey, which the company says is “demographically representative” of the US Census, sampled 5,000 US singles over the age of 18 who were not married, living with a partner, or in a committed relationship. In any case, Cox says, the “norm around what is the ideal age to get married” has vanished.

Pulliam still wants to get hitched but doesn’t consider it a priority right now. She attended a Christian college and recognizes the potential for Upward to be a hit “within communities of Christian private universities,” she says. “But I think the only way that the app works is for maybe someone who has a little bit more of a closed mind.”

President Donald Trump has invoked a Cold War-era law in an effort to boost oil production and lower energy prices.

In a presidential memorandum issued Thursday, Trump determined that petroleum production, refining, and related activities are essential to national defense under the 1950 Defense Production Act. The determination follows Trump’s declaration of a national energy emergency in January 2025.

“Ensuring resilient domestic petroleum production, refining, and logistics capacity is central to United States defense readiness,” the memo reads. “Without immediate Federal action, United States defense capabilities will remain vulnerable to disruption.”

The memo comes as the price of gas and diesel has soared across the country amid supply disruptions caused by the U.S. and Israel’s war against Iran. Brent crude futures rose 1.2% to $114.47 a barrel early Friday, extending gains after Thursday hit a five-month high. U.S. diesel pump prices averaged $6.32 a gallon, slightly higher than Thursday but below the September record, according to AAA. The high energy prices, and related inflation, have become a defining issue for voters in the coming midterm elections.

The memo means that energy production shortfalls are seen as a national defense risk.

The memo invokes Section 303 of the Defense Production Act, a law that allows the federal government to intervene in private industry for national defense. Section 303 allows the President to make purchases, purchase commitments, provide financial support and equipment, and expand production through other measures.

The memorandum also removes certain Section 303 requirements through an emergency waiver, including ordinary congressional notification and authorization constraints.

While the memo does not specify which projects could be funded, Vice President J.D. Vance said last week that the Administration would use the Defense Production Act to expand capacity at a Delek U.S. Holdings-owned refinery in Texas.

Without taking action, the memo said, U.S. industry would be unable to provide sufficient energy services in a “timely manner due to constrained financing, long lead times, permitting and infrastructure bottlenecks, and supply ​chain limitations.”

Enacted during the Korean War, the Defense Production Act was first used to expand domestic industries in critical materials, such as aluminum and titanium, for national defense.

Section 303 of the act was also used during the pandemic to fund domestic production of medicines and vaccines. Former President Joe Biden also invoked the statute to support clean energy manufacturing and supply chains, including minerals used in electric vehicle batteries and the production of of energy-efficient heat pumps and insulation.

In April, more than a month after Iran began disrupting shipping through the Strait of Hormuz in retaliation for U.S.-Israeli Feb. 28 strikes, Trump invoked the act to declare petroleum, natural gas, coal, electricity grid, and other energy infrastructure essential to national defense. Citing April’s natural gas determination, the Department of Energy on Monday committed up to $42 million to advance the proposed Constitution Pipeline linking Pennsylvania and New York. The department also announced up to $50 million last week for transmission upgrades in Virginia and West Virginia, citing April’s grid determination.

Last year, Trump signed legislation providing $1 billion for Defense Production Act activities, including energy projects deemed critical to national security.

Trump’s use of Section 303 of the act for fossil fuel projects has drawn criticisms from environmentalists, with the Center for Biological Diversity calling the April memoranda, which waived congressional notification, “unlawful.” Nevertheless, the statute expressly includes energy production and construction in its definition of national defense.

The Trump Administration has pursued a number of other measures aimed at lowering energy prices since the Iran War began. Earlier this month, Trump signed an executive order temporarily expanding access to tax-exempt dyed diesel, which is typically reserved for off-road farm and construction vehicles. The Group of 7 countries, including the U.S., also agreed to release 100 million barrels of diesel and crude oil reserves to the world over four months.

In August, the Administration extended the Jones Act waiver for another 90 days, allowing foreign-flagged ships to transport oil and certain other commodities between U.S. ports.

Trump also announced an agreement with Putin earlier this month to import diesel from Russia, alongside temporary sanctions relief. The deal has faced significant backlash from lawmakers.

For many of these measures, industry analysts and stakeholders have said the reduction in energy prices is likely to be limited and possibly short-lived. Analysts estimated that the Jones Act waiver was expected to reduce gasoline prices by only pennies per gallon. Capital Economics economist Hamad Hussain said the reserve release could lower diesel prices temporarily, but would not resolve the underlying supply crunch. And analysts warned that the dyed diesel measure would have a limited effect on most consumers since the tax-exempt fuel isn’t widely available at ordinary commercial pumps. Scientists discover a surprising double benefit of exercise for Parkinson’s

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People with Parkinson's disease who are more physically active may experience slower declines in memory, thinking, and movement, according to a new study published October 7, 2026, in Neurology, the official medical journal of the American Academy of Neurology.

The findings suggest that staying active could offer two important advantages for people living with Parkinson's. However, researchers emphasize that the study identified a connection between physical activity and slower symptom progression. It does not establish that exercise directly causes the disease to progress more slowly.

"Parkinson's disease is a brain disorder that causes movement and cognitive problems that gradually worsen leading to disability and a loss of independence," said study author Dong-Woo Ryu, MD, PhD, of the University of Cincinnati in Ohio. "Previous research has shown associations between physical activity and movement symptoms. Our study found physical activity may have dual benefits, with associations with slower declines in movement and cognition."

Tracking Exercise and Parkinson's Symptoms Over Seven Years

The research involved 294 adults with mild to moderate Parkinson's disease. Participants were 66 years old on average, and researchers monitored their health for up to seven years.

To understand their activity habits, participants completed questionnaires estimating how many hours each week they spent being physically active. The questions covered activities performed at work, around the home, and during hobbies or recreation.

Researchers classified activities by their intensity. Moderate activities included brisk walking, doubles tennis, leisurely swimming, and household tasks such as cleaning windows, vacuuming, or mowing the lawn. More demanding activities included running, cycling at higher speeds, participating in team sports, playing singles tennis, shoveling, and carrying heavy objects.

The team examined physical activity in three ways. First, they calculated the total time participants spent doing moderate or vigorous activities. Second, they estimated overall activity volume by combining duration with intensity, giving greater weight to strenuous exercise. Third, they separately measured the amount of time spent doing vigorous activities.

Based on total weekly activity time, participants were placed into three categories. Those in the least active group reported up to four hours per week, the moderately active group reported four to eight hours, and the most active group reported more than eight hours.

Each year, participants also completed assessments designed to track changes in thinking and memory, along with tests evaluating the severity of their Parkinson's symptoms.

More Activity Linked to Slower Memory Decline

One of the most striking differences emerged in cognitive performance. Researchers used a 30-point test of thinking and memory, with lower scores reflecting poorer cognitive function.

Among participants in the least active group, scores fell by an estimated average of 0.478 points each year. The moderately active group experienced a much smaller annual decline of 0.090 points.

For the most active participants, the estimated average yearly decrease was only 0.007 points, indicating that their cognitive scores remained nearly unchanged over time.

The researchers also examined changes in Parkinson's movement symptoms using an assessment scored from zero to 132. Higher numbers represented more severe symptoms, and participants began the study with an average score of 23.

In the least active group, symptom scores increased by an estimated 0.143 points annually, indicating a slight worsening. By contrast, scores decreased by 0.225 points per year in the moderately active group and by 0.783 points in the most active group.

Even after accounting for participants' age, sex, and how long they had been living with Parkinson's, the researchers found that the most active group experienced slower cognitive decline and less worsening of movement symptoms than the least active group.

The connection was not limited to one measure of exercise. Higher total activity time, greater overall activity volume, and more time spent exercising vigorously were each associated with slower deterioration in both thinking abilities and movement symptoms.

Why Consistent Physical Activity May Matter

The results suggest that remaining physically active over time could be especially important for people with Parkinson's disease. Researchers noted that even relatively small differences in the rate of decline might become more significant when they accumulate over several years.

"The possible benefits of physical activity for people with Parkinson's disease may depend on how long they stay active and whether they continue exercising over time," said Ryu. "While our study found small differences between activity levels, these differences may become more meaningful over time. Since participants generally had good thinking and memory skills at the start of the study, our results should be interpreted with caution and further studies are needed."

The researchers also identified an important limitation. Physical activity levels were based on participants' own reports rather than direct measurements, meaning some estimates may have been inaccurate.

Although the findings strengthen the evidence connecting physical activity with better long-term outcomes in Parkinson's disease, additional research will be needed to determine whether exercise itself can slow the progression of symptoms.

Is a job guarantee the solution to mass AI unemployment?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An old idea springs anew as a chorus of influential voices predicts that AI may trigger crises of vast magnitude

In a preface to his book Capitalism and Freedom, the American economist Milton Friedman famously observed that a crisis can propel once-fringe ideas into the political mainstream.

“Only a crisis – actual or perceived – produces real change. When that crisis occurs, the actions that are taken depend on the ideas that are lying around,” he wrote. The job of intellectuals, thinktanks and economists, he argued, is to refine the quality of these ambient ideas, so that good ones are ready when disaster strikes.

A chorus of influential voices is now predicting that AI may trigger crises of vast magnitude. Bill Gates has declared that AI will advance so rapidly that within a decade, humans will not be needed “for most things”. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has warned that the technology could cause widespread unemployment within one to five years, eliminating many positions in law, tech, finance and other white-collar professions. Even if these predictions are wrong, investors who bet heavily on the technology may conclude that AI is a bubble, and this in itself could cause a recession and widespread job loss.

Whether a crisis of mass AI-related job loss turns out to be actual or perceived, one policy idea that is attracting growing attention and support could directly mitigate this problem: a job guarantee.

In its most ambitious form, such a program would ensure that anyone who wants work is guaranteed a meaningful, decently paid job. By creating a public option for employment, its supporters argue, a job guarantee could protect millions of people from the financial and psychological damage of joblessness and mobilize a vast labor force to tackle urgent societal problems, from infrastructure and climate adaptation to elder care and childcare.

The concept of a job guarantee has a long history, both as an ideal and an actual policy. Article 23 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights states: “Everyone has the right to work, to free choice of employment, to just and favourable conditions of work and to protection against unemployment.” During the Great Depression in the 1930s, Franklin D Roosevelt established the Works Progress Administration (WPA), which employed millions of Americans in ambitious public works projects that constructed roads, bridges, sewers, playing fields and much more. More recently, France, India and Argentina are among the countries that have launched versions of a job guarantee.

Though a job guarantee and universal basic income (UBI) are often grouped together, a recent global survey found greater support for job guarantees than UBIs. Polling of Americans by the Democratic strategist David Shor also shows the same pattern, with greater support for the creation of well-paid jobs than for direct income support. This makes sense, the venture capitalist and civic activist Nick Hanauer said, because “jobs represent so much more than income in human societies. They represent connection and dignity and enfranchisement and participation.”

Algernon Austin, director for race and economic justice at the Center for Economic and Policy Research, agreed: “A job guarantee is significantly preferable to UBI because work is more than just income. When you have a job, you gain work experience and you gain skills and you gain a network.”

Calculating the cost of a job guarantee depends on a holistic assessment of its impacts. During a US Senate hearing on affordability in June, John Kennedy, a Republican senator for Louisiana, suggested that a job guarantee would cost trillions of dollars and was impossible. When Dr Julie Margetta Morgan, president of the public policy thinktank the Century Foundation, told Kennedy that she supported a federal job guarantee, he replied: “Do you believe in the Easter bunny?”

Economists who study job guarantees see the calculation of costs and benefits differently. Max Kasy, a professor of economics at Oxford who has studied a recent job guarantee experiment in Austria, said that while the wages a job guarantee pays participants comprise a cost, such programs also save money in multiple ways.

“The cost saving comes from the fact that you don’t have to pay unemployment benefits, or maybe disability benefits or early retirement benefits. Then people who are working pay taxes and make social insurance contributions, so that’s additional revenue for the state. And then they hopefully do something that’s actually useful for society,” he said.

Participants in the job guarantee experiment in Austria also experienced social and psychological benefits. While the monetary value of greater wellbeing may be hard to quantify, such intangibles are crucial, according to Pavlina Tcherneva, a professor of economics at Bard College.

“Providing a good job to a person is absolutely transformative,” she said. Factoring in the costs of health problems that come from precarious unemployment and long-term unemployment, she added, makes the policy even more attractive, saying: “It’s not only affordable, it’s an investment.”

In September, the US representative Ayanna Pressley, a Democrat from Massachusetts, reintroduced a congressional resolution calling for the creation of a federal job guarantee, something she first proposed in 2021. Her proposal would create a federally funded but locally administered job guarantee that pays participants at least $25 an hour, with regular adjustments to reflect the rising cost of living. The jobs would include a range of sectors, such as emergency preparedness, relief and recovery after natural disasters, care for children and seniors, and public art projects modelled on the WPA’s popular Federal Art Project.

Another recent proposal would directly tax AI companies to fund a federal job guarantee. The plan, which is supported by Democratic US representatives Greg Casar of Texas, Valerie Foushee of North Carolina and Sara Jacobs of California, would tax large AI companies on whatever was higher: the value of their “tokens” – units of data used in AI models – or their revenue from AI products.

If the unemployment rate rose, the tax rate would increase. “AI CEOs are openly predicting their technology could put millions of Americans out of work, and our government is doing nothing to save jobs and protect working people,” Casar said in a written statement.

In Cleveland, Ohio, a non-profit called Universal Basic Employment (UBE) is launching a small pilot study with a cohort of five workers who drive vans at a transportation company. Rather than create new jobs from scratch, the program subsidizes the wages of existing private-sector jobs in a way that benefits both workers and employers, according to Devin Cotten, founder and CEO of UBE.

Cotten and his team wanted workers to make what they call an autonomous wage – the pay level at which people are no longer eligible for most social safety net benefits. In Cleveland, that was just over $27 per hour. At a cost of almost half a million dollars for one year, UBE’s pilot will split that $27 figure with employers. The drivers currently make about $15 an hour, so the employer will save money, and the workers will get a sizable raise.

The program is not meant to replace other forms of assistance such as housing vouchers, Cotten said. But workers receiving more money as wages, rather than as a siloed benefit, can build their credit, perhaps qualify for a mortgage and also keep dollars circulating in the local economy, helping to revitalize poor neighborhoods.

Such a model may even attract a broader base of political support. “Work is a shared value across political lines,” Cotten said. “I think crafting a solution based on work allows us to be a bipartisan solution.”

Alaska’s Senate Race Could Be Decided by Salmon

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Perhaps the most important thing to know about Alaska’s Senate race, a close contest between the Republican incumbent, Dan Sullivan, and the former congresswoman Mary Peltola, is that salmon are pretty amazing fish. There’s the swimming upstream, the spawning, and the dying. Bears love salmon. Orcas love salmon. People love salmon. Without salmon, the first Alaskans, crossing the Bering land bridge some fifteen thousand years ago, could not have survived. On a recent trip to Alaska, I was, at various points, fed salmon for dinner, offered salmon as a gift, and given salmon for barter. Directly or indirectly, salmon feeds almost everything in the state, including its politics. Alaska’s bid for statehood, in the late nineteen-fifties, was in part a matter of keeping the hands of Seattle businessmen off local salmon stocks. Business deals are hammered out at fishing retreats. In 2010, a seaplane carrying Ted Stevens, likely the most powerful politician ever to come out of Alaska, crashed en route to a salmon lodge near Bristol Bay. He died wearing his waders.

People in the Lower Forty-eight care a lot about Alaska’s Senate race. This cycle, it has been twice as expensive, per capita, as Michigan’s, and eight times Texas’s. The attention is unusual. In federal elections, Alaska reliably votes Republican; every time Donald Trump has been on the ballot, Alaska has voted for him by ten points or more. But, amid historically low approval ratings for the President, the Democrats are hoping to flip the seat, an essential part of any attempt to wrest back control of both houses of Congress. Trump, meanwhile, is clearly nervous about holding the seat. He recently headlined a rally for Sullivan in Wasilla, a small city north of Anchorage. “This is worth the eight hours on a plane,” Trump said. “And don’t forget, I got eight hours going back, too.”

In January, Chuck Schumer recruited Peltola to run, calling her the “last piece to the puzzle” of his midterms plan. Peltola, the state’s former at-large House member, launched her campaign with the slogan “Fish, Family, Freedom.” To most outsiders, this read as folksy branding for a race that would inevitably be dominated by Donald Trump. But Peltola really wants to talk about fish. Recently, I saw her speak at the elementary school in Utqiagvik, an Arctic town at the state’s northern tip. It was a very unusual stump speech. She apologized for the “super negative, toxic ads” flooding the state, including her own. She mentioned her opponent just once: a flattering anecdote about his advocacy for an oil project she supports. She didn’t mention Trump at all. And even though, compared with the rest of the state, salmon is not so big in Utqiagvik, Peltola still mostly talked about fish.

This piscine primacy is relatively new in Alaska’s politics. In past election cycles, fish have always been one issue among many. But Alaska’s fish are in crisis. In recent years, runs of Chinook, or king salmon, the biggest and most revered species on the Yukon River, have been down as much as eighty per cent from average levels, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Chum salmon, a more ordinary variety, have had an O.K. year, but they’ve been down as much as ninety per cent. Some species and some areas are worse off than others, but the general trend is bad, including for the state’s other two iconic seafood catches, halibut and crab. As I travelled around the state, I was surprised to find that virtually everyone I talked to was upset about the fish situation. One political consultant explained, “A lot of people come into the state, and they’re, like, ‘Why the hell is Mary Peltola “fish, family, freedom”?’ I’m a white guy who lives in Anchorage, and my freezer is currently full of rockfish, halibut, and salmon that I’ve caught because that’s just what we all do.”

The crisis is worst up rivers such as the Yukon and the Kuskokwim. For many thousands of years, entire Athabascan and Yup’ik villages have subsisted on salmon. Indigenous residents have been delivering emotional testimony at meetings of the North Pacific Fishery Management Council (N.P.F.M.C.), which was created by Congress to oversee the region’s federal fisheries. “I got nine grandkids. My two youngest ones haven’t even tasted fish,” Robert Albert, a local from Tanana, said at a meeting in February. Life and social structures in remote river villages are rooted in summer fish camps, where the community catches, processes, and preserves the salmon that feeds them through the winter. But stocks have got so low that, in 2024, the Alaska government, in conjunction with Canada, shut down fishing for king salmon on the Yukon River and its Canadian tributaries for seven years—one complete king-salmon life cycle. The fish camps stand empty. “I wish I could express to you what it feels like for a man who provided for multiple families to become a beggar,” Carrie Stevens, who testified to the N.P.F.M.C. as a representative of the Council of Athabascan Tribal Governments, told me. Janessa Newman, from Rampart, told the council, “My immediate concern is whether I can morally bring my own children into this world without fishing on the Yukon River.”

Research scientists with the N.P.F.M.C. and N.O.A.A. attribute the die-off, primarily, to climate change. But activists in Alaska have mostly focussed their ire on a type of fishing known as trawling. In the Bering Sea and the Gulf of Alaska, the main target of commercial fishing boats is pollock, a medium-sized, mild fish that swims in fantastically vast schools. Trawlers catch pollock by dragging enormous nets; some open wide enough to swallow a thousand M.T.A. buses. Any net you put in the water will incidentally catch other, non-targeted fish. This is known as bycatch. Every year, the Alaskan pollock industry brings up around twenty thousand king salmon and around a quarter million pounds of halibut as bycatch. Recently, anger at this collateral damage, heightened by the fish shortage, has evolved into a political movement.

On Facebook, the group Stop Alaskan Trawler Bycatch, launched in early 2020, has close to sixty thousand members, almost a tenth the population of Alaska itself. It calls for a ban on trawling. “You know how ‘Tiger King’ came out during lockdown and got big? The Facebook group was the same thing,” David Bayes, who moderates it, told me. Peltola, who is Yup’ik and grew up along the Kuskokwim, joined the group in its early days. At the time, she was the head of the Kuskokwim River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission, a nonprofit that co-manages fishing resources with the government. Two years later, in a special election in 2022, she emerged out of a crowded top-four primary to defeat Sarah Palin, becoming the first Democrat since 1970 to win Alaska’s sole House seat. That campaign’s defining issue was trawl bycatch. This year, she is campaigning on a call to ban out-of-state factory trawlers. This anti-trawl sentiment has caught on, politically, around the state. It has become a marquee issue in races for governor, the state legislature, and even in municipal elections. “There was one guy running for school board talking about trawl,” Bayes told me.

I’d assumed that pollock people—businesspeople—would lean Republican, and the salmon people—the conservation-oriented—would lean Democratic, but that’s not really the case. “There’s nothing partisan about it,” Peltola told me recently. “When you ask folks who are MAGA why they’re splitting the ticket and voting for me, it’s largely because of trawling.” (The campaign later pointed out that a lot of those voters are also animated by high fuel prices.) Politics in Alaska are idiosyncratic. They rarely map onto a national partisan model. Republicans outnumber Democrats two to one, but independent voters outnumber both. “Partisan politics doesn’t work that well for a candidate,” Tony Knowles, the last Democrat to serve as governor, told me. A government staffer in a remote region put it to me, “Locally, no one gives a shit about the parties.”

One oddity of Alaska politics is that, though only twelve per cent of voters identify as Democrats, both the State Senate and the State House of Representatives are currently controlled by coalitions made up mostly of Democrats and independents, with some Republicans. Coalition governments are not uncommon in the state, where politicians are fond of saying that pragmatism trumps partisanship. Sometimes it’s more about vibes. “Somebody is a magnificent pain in the ass, and we don’t want them in our caucus so we build a caucus to exclude them,” Joelle Hall, a longtime union leader, told me. Imagine a congressional coalition formed by kicking out the lawmakers, on either side, who are annoying—the Ted Cruzes and the John Fettermans. Peltola tried to explain this unique approach by telling me that the current coalition “took the hardest people to work with and just put them to the side, and then everybody else joined as a majority to move forward and solve problems. And that is a model I am hoping we can export to every state in the nation.”

Peltola knows that relationships can be just as important as parties. She has been friendly with Palin since they ran against each other in 2022. At one debate during that race, they wore matching earrings; Peltola had given them to Palin as a gift. (“It’s an Alaska-chick thing,” Palin said.) Peltola is even closer with Lisa Murkowski, Alaska’s other incumbent senator, a Republican who, despite supporting Sullivan in this race, had endorsed Peltola during her previous campaigns. After Peltola won her first election, Murkowski offered to let her stay at her place in Washington. “That’s the thing about Alaska: there’s only seven hundred thirty-five thousand of us,” Peltola told me. “We’re all stuck with each other until we die.”

“Alaskans consider themselves more Alaskan than Republican, and more Alaskan, quite honestly, than they are American,” Scott Kendall, an attorney and longtime G.O.P. political consultant in the state, told me. “We like each other. To me, it’s kind of a beautiful thing. And I’ve been back to D.C. and elsewhere enough to know how good we’ve got it.” I told him that many of my political assumptions from the Lower Forty-eight didn’t quite translate to the state. “Most of my Democratic friends have guns,” Kendall said. “A lot of my conservative Republican friends grow weed.” Many political ads in the current race—the ones Peltola had apologized for—have resorted to partisan rage bait (trans fearmongering, Epstein) or personal attacks. These tend to be produced by out-of-state super PACs not connected to either campaign. “I actually don’t think they’re very effective,” Kendall said. “We all know each other. People see Mary or Dan in the airport all the time.”

The fact that subsistence fishing has become important to the campaign is good for Peltola, who once wrote of herself, “I am made of fish,” and less good for Sullivan, who is originally from Ohio and not a notable angler. On a walk in Anchorage, I saw a sidewalk chalked with the words “DAN EATS FARMED FISH WITH KETCHUP.” But he, too, has been forced to weigh in on the issue. In June, back in D.C., he introduced a trawling bill that offered wonkish, incremental changes to fishing practices in Alaska. The bill never made it off the Senate floor, but it did give him something to run on. In Anchorage, Arthur Hackney, who runs a Sullivan-aligned PAC called We Stand with Dan, showed me an ad he’d recently cut. On the screen, the text read “This Election is About Fish, Family, Freedom,” Peltola’s exact campaign motto. Then it said, “On all of these, Senator Dan Sullivan actually delivers results.” Hackney explained, “People don’t understand how easy it is to just take the other person’s thing and you claim it for your own.” A strategist working with the Democrats told me it was surreal to see Sullivan trying to co-opt fishing. “He’s a marine, good-looking guy. There’s a lot of good things you could say about him. He’s not a commercial fisherman.”

As I spoke with Peltola voters across Alaska, I noticed a split between those who think she is talking too much about fishing, when a stronger message would be the war in Iran or gas prices, and those who worry that the outside strategists urging the Peltola campaign to focus on national topics will drown out her winning message. Alaska’s core constituency is composed of Natives, fishermen, rural legislators, and unions, with some chamber-of-commerce types mixed in. Peltola is a gun-owning, fish-catching Alaska-bush native, who has worked for mines and supports oil drilling, and is allied with the state’s largest labor unions. “You’ve got a once-in-a-generation candidate who is all the pieces of that coalition as one person,” the strategist told me. “You worry that the federal PACs will come in and fuck all that up. What’s actually somewhat surprising to me is that we’re here in October, and the dominant issue of the race is still trawl bycatch and local fisheries, which says they didn’t fuck it up that bad.”

Kodiak Island, in the Gulf of Alaska, is home to a fishing community with a large mom-and-pop fleet and a lot of big trawlers. The evening after I arrived, around sunset, I saw a mother Kodiak bear and her cub scavenging along the shore. Nearby, I noticed a convocation of bald eagles. They were all feasting on pink salmon carcasses, which had floated downriver after spawning days earlier. A local fisherman pointed out that you can find salmon DNA everywhere on the island, even atop the big mountains that run along its interior—bear scat, bird poop. “Salmon is fertilizing the entire goddam system,” he told me. In the Karluk River, on the island’s southwest, king salmon used to be everywhere. By 2024, scientists counted only seventy-six in the entire run.

The next day, I met Jake Everich and Mike Helligso, trawling captains who’d docked their boat, a seventy-three-footer called the Alaskan, next to a cannery. They were in town to deliver a load of pollock for processing. They pointed out to me that death threats had been posted on the anti-trawling Facebook group. (“I bet sinking their boats would work real good.”) Everich added that a trawler friend in town had recently had his tires slashed. He suspected it was anti-trawlers. They invited me aboard, where there was a wall of screens: radar and sonar that help monitor which fish are going into the net. “I spend as much time avoiding the fish we don’t want to catch as catching the fish I do want to catch,” Everich told me. “You make mistakes. But sometimes it’s pretty damn clean.” Alaskan pollock trawlers catch more than three billion pounds of fish annually. Ninety-seven per cent of that is pollock. The majority of the industry’s bycatch is jellyfish, pacific cod, and squid. Sam Murphy, the spokesperson for the Alaska Pollock Fishery Alliance, said that the twenty thousand king salmon they catch annually has averaged .0086 per cent of the total bycatch by weight over the past five years. The N.P.F.M.C. also conducts genetic testing on the salmon bycatch, which shows that most of it consists of not Alaskan salmon but salmon from hatcheries in Russia and Asia. “We’re not even catching that fish that you’re trying to catch,” Helligso said. “That’s, like, a whole different fish.”

Everich’s boat was holding three hundred thousand pounds of pollock, the legal limit for a twenty-four hour period. He did it with a single drag, which he said took about ninety minutes. At the dock, a fish pump, which works like a giant vacuum tube, was sucking the fish out of the hold. We could see a small amount of bycatch, which, as far as I could tell, was all rockfish. Helligso grabbed one to show me. He held it by hooking his thumb underneath an eyeball, which was protruding out of its head. Trawling can be brutal. Fish die by being crushed, by asphyxiation, or by barotrauma—when fish surface quickly, things sometimes go pop. Helligso also showed me a pollock. It weighed two pounds or so and looked unremarkable. Pollock generally becomes fish sticks or processed fish products—Filet-O-Fish fish. No one’s putting pollock on a postage stamp. But it’s good, cheap food. This season, pollock sells to canneries for sixteen cents a pound. “There’s the thought, ‘It’s a garbage fish,’ ” Everich said. “It’s a stable source of protein that the majority of the world can eat.”

Trawling is a wedge issue in Kodiak. Many people rely on trawling, directly or indirectly, for work, but small seiners, long-liners, and crab fishermen have major issues with it. (“There’s five different fisheries groups, and they all basically think the others are stealing fish from them,” Hackney, the G.O.P. political consultant, told me.) Kodiak went for Trump by nineteen points in 2024. In the open primary, in August, Peltola won Kodiak by ten points. “This is the only reason a lot of the sons of bitches will vote against Trump,” one left-leaning fisherman told me. Last year, a poll found that almost three-quarters of Alaskans supported a trawl ban. There was virtually no difference in the level of support between Democrats, Republicans, and independents. “They’re making decisions based on emotion and something that sounds good when in fact it’s not true,” Bruce Schactler, a local fisherman who worked for Alaska Seafood Marketing Institute for two decades, told me. “I can’t stand it. Look, it’s a big deal. You have the crash of the king salmon. But it’s everywhere. And it has nothing to do with fishing.” We were at the brewery in town with the mayor, Terry Haines, who is also a retired fisherman, and the host of the Alaska Fisheries Report on KMXT, the local radio station. (He also d.j.s a half-hour spot of soul music every week.) “Bycatch is not the driver of our stock crashes,” he said. “It is simply the only lever that we have to pull. So that makes it a valid reason to look at pulling that lever. But you have to realize that you can pull that lever all the way down to the floor, and you’re still not going to change the environment that’s causing the fishery collapses.”

Peltola insists on what is called “rural preference”: in times of shortage, subsistence fishing must come first. Carrie Stevens, the representative for the Council of Athabascan Tribes, told me, “If people on the rivers have a moratorium to stand down, why is it so obscene to ask others to bear any brunt of conservation?” The tribes contend that the government’s scientists are underestimating the effect of trawling. One argument is that it’s not just the bycatch but the catch itself. Salmon eat juvenile pollock—or would, if they didn’t become fish sticks. Jim Balsiger, who retired as NOAA’s top fisheries official in Alaska in 2021, said recently, “Removing three to four billion pounds of fish from the Bering Sea every year for four decades is not a benign activity.”

One morning, I visited a local fisherman named Dave Kubiak in the wheelhouse of his boat, the Lara Lee. He catches salmon, halibut, and crab. He was joined by his dog, Corky, whom he fed bits of muffin and occasionally bantered with. (“You spilled it on the floor, buddy. That’s why I don’t want to give you any more, you dirty little hound dog.”) Kubiak and his peers argue that the trawlers diminish their fish stocks and also cause broader damage to the seafloor. Some trawlers, such as those targeting arrowtooth flounder, are bottom draggers. They crush crabs and tear up vegetation. Pollock trawling is supposed to be midwater. But the nets get so big and heavy that they sometimes drag. “We’re taking huge bites out of the universe without any knowledge of what the hell that means,” Kubiak said.

Kubiak taught high-school English for many years. (“Half of the adults in town had him as a teacher,” Haines, the mayor, told me.) In the fall and winter, he’d teach Thoreau and Emerson. In the summer, he’d be on the water. “It completely renews you,” he told me. Most fishermen I talked to told me that they loved their jobs. It pays well. It’s performed outside. There’s a satisfaction in the work: you catch it, people eat it. “Fishing ruins you for an actual job,” Haines told me.

Fishing jobs like these are essential to Alaskan identity and have been declining across the state. Gunnar Knapp, a professor emeritus at the University of Alaska Anchorage and a prominent fishery economist, told me that the trawling issue has become about more than just fishing regulations. “A lot of this is symbolic. There is a vision that the world economy has been moving inexorably toward this dystopia controlled by large corporations, capitalism, and global enterprises, where the individual is marginalized and means nothing,” Knapp said. “If you’ve ever seen a trawl net when it comes in, it seems so appalling. You think, ‘You won’t have any fish left in the ocean!’ Well, it actually is sustainable, for pollock. But it’s not noble like a guy holding up one subsistence salmon.” The choice for Alaskans is difficult: crack down significantly on trawl, and you’ll limit global food supply and inflict significant damage to the state economy. In return, you’ll possibly get small gains upriver. “It’s less economic than moral or ethical,” Knapp said. “What costs are worth it for even a marginal improvement to the salmon runs? I think some people would say anything is worth it.” As a friend in Anchorage put it, “Trawling is our version of data centers.”

The problem in Alaska isn’t finding persuadable voters. It’s reaching them. Campaign staffers pack sleeping bags and pads, in case they get socked in at a remote village. This year, in the election precinct of Nuiqsut, an Iñupiat Eskimo area, only ten people voted in the primary election. It’s about three times the size of New Jersey. On St. George Island, way out in the Bering Sea, Peltola won the primary by a ratio of two to one—two votes total, to one.

This election season, candidates have hit the campaign trail in bush planes, boats, and dog sleds. (When Peltola’s mother was pregnant with her, she was a volunteer on a congressional campaign; she canvassed for votes on a snowmobile.) During Peltola’s recent Arctic swing, she took flights with the sole purpose of visiting towns with total populations of four hundred and forty-seven, eight hundred and nine, and six hundred and ninety-four.

After the stump speech at the elementary school in Utquagvik, which is roughly as far from Anchorage as New York City is from Charleston, and following a celebration featuring Iñupiaq drumming and dancing, I met with Peltola. So many of her positions—on guns, on oil—are either centrist or defy partisan categorization, as with the fish. I’d asked one political consultant to name some issues where Peltola differed from, say, Lisa Murkowski, who is a moderate, pro-choice Republican. He thought for a while and said, “I’m struggling to think of a good example.” Murkowski is admired by independents and some Democrats in Alaska, while being largely scorned by the MAGA right. It’s not difficult to foresee an inverse dynamic with Peltola and the national left. Peltola’s father was a Republican, and her mother was a supporter of the longtime Republican congressman Don Young. I asked her what made her a Democrat. “The region that I’m from, they’re almost all Democrats,” she told me. “It’s a curiosity to me because the region is very conservative, extremely, extremely conservative. We are very much interested in preserving our Second Amendment rights. We’re into local control. A lot of the things that are part of the Republican platform. But I don’t think anybody’s reading platforms.”

Everywhere I visited around the state, I was surprised by how trawling manifested politically. Everich, the trawling captain, said that he used to be “a very strong Republican,” but fish politics had scrambled everything, and he was presently undecided. Although Sullivan is widely viewed by trawlers as less antagonistic, the industry has also been squeezed by rising costs from tariffs, insurance, and fuel. I encountered some MAGA flags along the docks, but this year, Everich said, “Trump supporters have changed their perspectives. To pay seven dollars a gallon for diesel because of a foreign war and then be, like, ‘Go Trump’? That would be difficult.” Kubiak, the retired English teacher and fisherman, was voting for Peltola, but he actually didn’t like her trawling ban. “Too easy,” he said. “She’s painting with a broad brush, and it’s really dangerous to do that.”

I asked Peltola if she saw any parallels between the fish battles and fights elsewhere: against climate change, A.I. monopolization, or against the bankers after the Great Recession. (Kubiak told me that he was worried the pollock industry had become “too big to fail.”) She said no. Salmon is uniquely important. “I have not figured out a way to teach my kids Yup’ik values unless we’re hunting and fishing,” she said. As a political issue, its utility is that everyone likes salmon. Peltola told me she has some connections to Utqiagvik, where the big food is muktuk—bowhead-whale blubber and skin. Her late husband liked it. He had a friend in town, and, every year, the man would give him some muktuk. In return, he gave the man some salmon. ♦

Inside Meta’s Efforts to Prevent School Shootings

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Larry Baimbridge spent thirty-one years with the Houston Police Department, including stints as a homicide detective and as commander of the SWAT team. In 2023, he took a job as chief of police for the Spring Branch Independent School District, most of which is within the city. The job was more low-key—no more shootouts with snipers—but it came with its own challenges. After the 2022 shooting in Uvalde, Texas, which left nineteen elementary-school students and two teachers dead, the state legislature had neglected to pass any meaningful restrictions on guns; instead, the state required that every school campus have an armed security officer. “Of course, they didn’t provide enough funding for it, but that was the mandate,” Baimbridge said.

The Spring Branch district served thirty-one thousand students across forty-odd campuses—“a very wealthy area on one side of the freeway and a very poor area on the other side,” Baimbridge said. Late in the school year, on February 18, 2025, he got a phone call from Brian Ennesser, a supervisory special agent at the F.B.I.’s Houston field office. The two men had met the year before, when Ennesser formed a threat-mitigation task force, a group that specializes in disrupting targeted mass violence, including at schools. Baimbridge knew right away that something was up; Ennesser usually texted or e-mailed. “Hey, man, we’ve got a problem,” Ennesser said.

The LedeReporting and commentary on what you need to know today.

The F.B.I. had received a tip about a potential threat to a local school. It involved two teen-agers, one a student at Spring Branch, the other at a school district north of Houston. Both were girls. “Once we identified it being two females, we were double-checking our work,” Ennesser later told me.

Baimbridge got to the Spring Branch school quickly and briefed its administrators on the threat. As they walked down a hallway, a young woman came out of the bathroom. The assistant principal’s eyes widened—it was the girl they were looking for. Baimbridge stopped her and asked her to come with them. “And she just kind of put her head down, which, from my years in homicide, I knew if I got a head slump I’ve got them,” he told me. “She didn’t ask why.”

Baimbridge later learned that the two teen-agers had met in a therapy group; when their parents didn’t want them to meet up outside of treatment, they kept in touch online. At some point, they allegedly discussed plans for a violent attack. That day, as Ennesser and a handful of other F.B.I. agents spoke with one of the girls in a conference room, Baimbridge sat outside with her mother. With the woman’s permission, he pulled a binder from the girl’s bag and began to flip through it. He was struck by the skill of her drawings. Then he stopped on a page that appeared to be a jumble of names, places, and dates connected by lines, like some kind of confounding word map. “You know that movie ‘A Beautiful Mind’?” he asked me. In the middle of the page were two names: Eric Harris and Dylan Klebold, the perpetrators of the Columbine High School murders. (Both girls pleaded guilty, one to conspiracy to commit murder and the other to making terrorist threats; they will remain on probation until age eighteen, and one will remain under continuous monitoring and receive psychiatric care, according to the F.B.I.)

Mass murderers have long attracted macabre groupies, but, like many ugly human tendencies, the phenomenon has been accelerated and amplified by the internet. According to law enforcement, the Texas girls were participants in the T.C.C., short for True Crime Community, an online fandom that celebrates perpetrators of mass violence. In the past two years, at least nine attacks and sixteen disrupted plots have been linked to the T.C.C., according to the Institute for Strategic Dialogue, a nonprofit think tank with a focus on hate and extremism.

In the aftermath of the arrests, law enforcement announced that the girls’ plot had been discovered through their online activity. “Everyone was, like, ‘Oh, so the F.B.I. really is spying on us?’ And, no, it’s not the F.B.I.,” Baimbridge said. The initial tip had come from a little-known team inside Meta that has spent the past decade honing a method to identify potential school shooters. Algorithmic social media, which has helped create the conditions for the spread of online content that might inspire such shootings, is reckoning with how it can become part of the solution.

Before Emily Vacher was recruited by Meta, in 2011, she was an F.B.I. agent specializing in crimes against children. At Meta, Vacher began working with the law-enforcement-outreach team, which she now helps lead. In the immediate aftermath of high-profile incidents, such as the Sandy Hook school shooting, in Newtown, Connecticut, the team would make contact with law enforcement to offer assistance. “You know, are there any co-conspirators? Is there any other impending danger they need to know about?” Vacher said.

As an agent, Vacher had spent time working with the behavioral-analysis unit in Quantico, the heavily mythologized home of the Bureau’s criminal profilers. There, she studied the work of Mary Ellen O’Toole, a longtime profiler with a specialty in school shooters. O’Toole had come to realize that many people who commit mass violence telegraph their intentions in advance, a phenomenon she referred to as “leakage.” School shooters, it turned out, were particularly prone to it: an F.B.I. study of active-shooter attacks between 2000 and 2013 found that nearly nine out of ten shooters under the age of eighteen had leaked their intent.

If would-be perpetrators were dropping hints about imminent violence online, Vacher thought, it should be possible to detect and stop them—“to think about disrupting attacks, not just investigating them,” she said. In 2018, she helped assemble a group of people with expertise in school shootings, including former journalists and police investigators, to study the online footprints of past attackers and to identify behavioral patterns. They worked with a team of engineers to build systems that use A.I. tools in order to scan for signals. Content flagged by the automated systems is passed along to a human for further review of the user’s online behavior: the posts they make and share, their interactions with others, the prompts they feed into A.I. chatbots. When there is evidence of “substantial steps, overt acts,” as Vacher put it—a list of targets or a manifesto, say—the investigations team provides relevant information to law enforcement. Last year, Meta did so around eight hundred times worldwide.

The process can unfold rapidly. According to prosecutors, last fall, a student at Benjamin N. Cardozo High School, in Queens, posted a photograph on Instagram of schoolwork with a text overlay that read “boutta shoot the school up.” The post was flagged by Meta, which passed the user’s information along to the F.B.I., which in turn notified the N.Y.P.D. After law enforcement matched the I.P. address associated with the post to a phone number and a name, they searched the student’s backpack and found a loaded pistol. Just over two hours after posting the picture, the student was taken into custody. (The student was charged with gun possession and making a terroristic threat.)

But few cases are as straightforward as that one. Publicly announcing an attack in advance is “part of the whole lore in this community,” Vacher said, “but they can’t be so overt that they just immediately get caught.” (Meta’s community standards ban threats of violence, in addition to content that “glorifies, supports or represents . . . multiple-victim violence.”) Young people, adept at evading content bans, know how to speak in a densely coded language that often outpaces moderation tools built to target more explicit forms of radicalization. “The swastika is easy to understand,” Katherine Keneally, the senior director of threat analysis and prevention at the Institute for Strategic Dialogue U.S., said. “The Atomwaffen Division logo, that’s obvious. But if there’s, like, an A.I.-generated version of a person who committed a shooting in the Philippines doing a TikTok dance with a number behind them that signifies the number of people killed in the attack—that’s a lot more difficult to understand.” Keneally said that many social-media companies have downsized their trust-and-safety teams in recent years, allowing more T.C.C. posts to slip through the cracks.

Instead of using perpetrators’ names, T.C.C. posters may invoke Arlene (Eric Harris’s nickname for the shotgun he used in the Columbine attack) or Smiggles (a screen name used by Adam Lanza, the Sandy Hook shooter). When people began to catch on that Meta’s moderation tools were flagging profiles that used widely known images of shooters, they began using childhood photos instead. “You have to have someone who can recognize that, O.K., this is not the normal Adam Lanza picture we’re used to seeing—this is Adam Lanza much younger,” a member of Meta’s investigations team told me. As with any fan culture, the lore and language are self-referential and constantly evolving. “Any time there is an actualized attack, the imagery, the iconography, anything associated with the attacker—the music they listened to, the clothes they wore, the specific weapons they used, the things they wrote—tends to get recycled back into the canon,” the team member said.

The details of past attacks become a template for future violence: in the moments before a fifteen-year-old girl in Wisconsin killed a classmate, a teacher, and herself, in December, 2024, she posted a photo of someone in a bathroom stall, flashing a hand signal associated with white supremacy. A month later, a seventeen-year-old boy in Tennessee posted a similar image before killing a classmate and himself. An act of violence doesn’t need to be high-profile to make an impact. An incel-motivated stabbing at a school in Mexico City last year barely made the news in the U.S., yet it quickly became absorbed into the T.C.C. symbology.

The vast majority of young people who post in T.C.C. communities won’t commit violence, of course. In 2012, I wrote about the Columbiners, a kind of precursor to the T.C.C. The Columbiners congregated on Tumblr, where they posted swoony, heart-covered memes about Harris and Klebold. When I spoke with Columbiners, I was more worried about them than about anything they might do; their memes struck me as a clumsy and self-consciously edgy way of expressing their rage and pain and feelings of alienation. “The experience of engaging with TCC narratives is often deeply emotional for online users,” a report from the I.S.D. notes. One worrying sign is when someone moves from romanticizing or idolizing school shooters to identifying with them, according to Vacher: “They’re not just saying, ‘Oh, this guy’s amazing.’ They’re buying the clothes that that shooter wore, or they’re finding the gun that the shooter used, or they’re getting floor plans for the school, or buying tactical gear.”

Social-media companies must weigh competing claims of privacy, liability, and caution when deciding what to report to law enforcement. Overreporting runs the risk of shunting troubled kids into the criminal-justice system merely for expressing morbid thoughts. But the consequences of underreporting can be dire. Last June, the automated-review system at OpenAI flagged an account used by Jesse Van Rootselaar, a teen-ager whose conversations with ChatGPT featured repeated discussions of gun violence, including mass-shooting scenarios. A team reviewed Van Rootselaar’s activity but decided against reporting it to law enforcement, evidently concluding that it did not constitute an immediate or credible threat. OpenAI shut down the account, but Van Rootselaar soon created another one, on which the discussions continued, albeit in a slightly more coded way: the planned shooting was hypothetical, Van Rootselaar now said. The chatbot gave advice about how not to get flagged again. “You can still be twisted. Just be clever about it,” it advised. During the following eight months, Van Rootselaar “discussed building and using firearms and homemade explosives, fantasized about becoming a notorious mass killer, and asked about the timing of previous real-world school shootings,” according to reporting by Mother Jones. In February, after killing her mother and brother, Van Rootselaar opened fire at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School, in British Columbia, killing six people, and then herself. The province and multiple victims’ families have filed lawsuits against OpenAI. “I am deeply sorry that we did not alert law enforcement,” Sam Altman, the C.E.O., wrote in an open letter.

Meta’s investigations team is just “one small piece” of the work of preventing school shootings, Vacher told me. “This is very much a societal problem. We need schools and parents and mental-health professionals and teachers and law enforcement.” But, after speaking with members of the company’s team—sharp, sincere people doing potentially lifesaving work—I couldn’t help feeling that a kind of cognitive dissonance underlay the whole project. “It’s all well and good to report specific cases to law enforcement, but at the end of the day the platforms are also playing a significant role in the spread of this content that is radicalizing people to engage in violence,” Keneally, of the I.S.D., said—namely, by creating algorithmic feeds that amplify extreme content while also decreasing funding for trust-and-safety programs. (TikTok, she noted, is the mainstream platform on which T.C.C. content seems to spread the fastest and farthest. When reached for comment, a TikTok U.S. spokesperson said that the platform removes content that violates its community guidelines, permanently bans accounts dedicated to glorifying attackers, and takes action against additional accounts that may be operated by the banned user.)

The more time I spent in this world, with its strange mixture of coy menace and clout-driven nihilism, the more I thought about Columbine. Twenty-seven years later, the attack on a school in the Denver suburbs retains an archetypal mystique for generations of troubled young people. Harris and Klebold, early to catch on to the self-mythologizing possibilities of new technology, obsessively documented themselves and their fascination with violence, both online and off. It’s as if they were native speakers of a language that hadn’t quite been invented yet. ♦

Political Skirmishes in “Other Desert Cities” and “A Bright Room Called Day”

On the night that I attended the current Broadway revival of Jon Robin Baitz’s 2010 play, “Other Desert Cities,” there was a mad clapper at large, a mercenary determined to let us know whose side he was on. It was clear which actor he favored: Joe Keery, who shot to fame on the Netflix hit “Stranger Things” as the moody, mulleted Steve Harrington, a small-town doofus drafted into a teen militia. In “Other Desert Cities,” Keery plays a different sort of conscript, the quietly perceptive reality-TV producer Trip Wyeth, a louche charmer negotiating a ceasefire between his neocon parents and his sister, Brooke, who is threatening to spill the family’s secrets in the pages of this very magazine. I have no idea whether the clapper was a Keery superfan or just agreed with Trip’s critique of his family, but he wasn’t wrong on either count, with the actor delivering a career-making performance: loose, bright, and unpredictable. After his best speech—a witty stem-winder that ends with the declaration “I am California! And California is not happy!”—I was tempted to clap, too.

Unfortunately, the starry ensemble surrounding Keery felt unevenly weighted and off-kilter, like billiard balls only occasionally landing in the pocket. Baitz’s story is set in Palm Springs, in December, 2004, at the home of Polly and Lyman Wyeth, an opulent sandstone aerie with meditative views of the desert, a massive Christmas tree, and portraits of eminences like Barry Goldwater. The Wyeths are Republican grandees and L.A. royalty: Polly (Julia Louis-Dreyfus, all sparkle and venom) is a retired screenwriter, Lyman (a craggy, mournful Ed Harris) a former Hollywood cowboy. Polly, a verbal sharpshooter in a family of clay pigeons, is a dab hand at witticisms like “Sarcasm is the purview of teen-agers and homosexuals,” but if she’s a casual bigot she’s also a reinventor, a steerer of fates: a Jewish go-getter from Texas, she co-wrote frothy commercial films with her sister, then married into Wasp aristocracy with Lyman, who became a government ambassador after their pal “Ronnie” got elected President. By the time we meet the couple, however, that’s ancient history: the Iraq War is raging, and they rarely leave Palm Springs, where they’re in a right-wing bubble, barely tolerating Polly’s now estranged sister, Silda, a lefty dipsomaniac fresh out of rehab, who is played with leggy relish by Allison Janney. The plot revolves, as it inevitably does in shows like this, around a collective trauma. Here, it’s the loss of a third sibling, a radical son who, in the chaos of the seventies, blew up his life and warped the family trajectory. That’s the dark story Brooke is ready to tell the world in a manuscript she’s brought from Sag Harbor—the world’s least welcome Christmas present.

This material should feel resonant, given how many families are split right now—over MAGA, over Israel—and how much of a default confessionalism has become online. (These days, Brooke could post an incendiary tweet storm and skip the airfare.) The script is full of pungent, funny debates about power, with observations that still land. “Families get terrorized by their weakest member,” Polly argues. And Trip tells Brooke, “Isn’t it revenge enough that everyone worries about you all the time?” If the exposition creaks, the cast is sprightly, and, for a long time, it’s a blast to watch them spar, particularly Louis-Dreyfus, who scores laughs off the smallest phrases—a disgusted “amongst the Basques,” say. Together, she and Harris capture something formidable about the slice of Old Hollywood the Wyeths represent—sybaritic and hypocritical, brined in self-pity but with a fierce, glamorous quality.

The primary problem is, unfortunately, Lily Rabe, who plays Brooke in a different, blunter register than everyone else onstage. I’ve seen Rabe give beautiful, varied performances, from her dignified Hermione in this past summer’s “The Winter’s Tale” to her demon-possessed nun in “American Horror Story,” gyrating saucily to “You Don’t Own Me.” In “Other Desert Cities,” she’s all sulk and grimace. To be fair, it’s a hard role: Brooke is a solipsistic bummer, a frustrated writer who spends months in a mental hospital being tended to by her mother, then ambushes her with a score-settling tell-all. Instead of seeming righteous but flawed, Rabe’s Brooke validates Polly’s harsh judgment that she’s a reckless brat convinced her truth is the only truth. When, in the final act, a twist reframes the Wyeths’ behavior as, if not fully sympathetic, more understandable, it just made me furrow my brow. By then, it was hard to imagine Brooke’s parents opening up and easier to imagine them manipulating her—defusing, by any means necessary, their daughter’s incendiary literary device.

Even so, I had fun. I loved every costume and considered trying to dress more like a Palm Springs Republican, as Polly would want me to. And there was even, perversely, a touch of escapism to the experience. “Other Desert Cities,” so topical in 2010, now feels as if it were written in the Paleolithic era. If the Wyeths are monsters, they’re also dinosaurs, eclipsed by the nihilists who followed. There’s a wonderful moment when Harris puts on a show, mimicking the death scenes he specialized in onscreen: he buckles at phantom gunshots, then mimes shock, his torso atilt. It was his one true skill, Lyman says, modestly, with a Reaganite twinkle—he always knew how to make an exit.

Two nights later, I went to Crown Heights to catch a revival of a play that felt much more timely, despite having débuted in 1987 and being set in the nineteen-thirties. A tiny, passionate production of Tony Kushner’s “A Bright Room Called Day,” directed by Rachel Gita Karp, was being staged in a town house in the heart of Brooklyn’s so-called Commie Corridor, which helped steer Mayor Zohran Mamdani to victory. As I reclined on a sofa, sipping rosé, a cadre of Berlin bohemians—Reds, fellow- travellers, anarchists, and homosexuals; actors, socialist-realist painters, and writers—began celebrating New Year’s Eve, 1932, at a kitchen table just a few feet away. Lifting glasses of champagne, they toasted, giddily, to “health, happiness, and relative safety on this fierce and splendid night and for many years to come.” It flashed me back to nights of political hope in recent decades, and I felt sick.

“Day” is Kushner’s début—his early problem play, which premièred, in San Francisco, four years before “Angels in America.” At the time, the project felt hyperbolic, largely because of the finger-wagging interventions of Zillah, a modern-day punk anarchist who ranted about Reagan as a new Hitler. (With “Angels,” Kushner found a more fruitful comparison: Roy Cohn and the Red Scare.) Zillah’s speeches have since been reworked, most notably in a 2019 production in which a Kushner-y character called Xillah debated her, framing the story with an anxious meta-narrative. By then, the Hitler comparisons had a different feel: if Kushner had once been the boy who cried wolf, the wolf was at the door.

In 2026, the gemlike Brooklyn venue lent Kushner’s project a magical quality, turning the audience into ghosts from the future, eavesdropping on the fall of the Weimar Republic but unable to intervene. There was no meta frame, and although Zillah still chimed in, it was in smaller doses—and the truth is, we no longer needed her guidance. As the characters playfully debated the rise of Fascism, the dialogue often felt transcribed from some Brooklyn polycule. Is class the best lens for understanding Hitler’s appeal, or is daddy worship? Can anything be learned from sleeping with Nazis? When, ultimately, is it time to flee? The cast was excellent, particularly a sad, sexy Molly Rose Heller as Agnes, a movie actress clinging to her perfect Berlin rental, Jonathan Bock as her Trotskyite beau, a poignant Portal Rose as a sexual adventurer, and Aja Downing as a morally malleable starlet toying with working for the Reich.

Still, the real star was Kushner’s text, a message in a bottle. Witty, bitter, flamboyant, and emotionally grounded, the dialogue swings, delightfully, between intimate exchanges and grandiloquent soliloquies, capturing the vulnerability of idealists in the face of evil. When the Devil himself appeared, in a puff of smoke, the town-house alarm went off and the play paused. For a moment, it all seemed, surreally, to be part of the production: yet another alarm to ignore so that the show could go on. ♦

The Fragile Unions of “Possible Love”

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The South Korean writer and director Lee Chang-dong makes beautiful films about terrible things. The question that haunts his stories, giving them a pulsing undertow of dread, is whether those things have already happened or not. In “Secret Sunshine” (2010), a young widow moves to a new town with her son; tragedy has scarred them both, but the film, relentless in its ability to surprise, has even graver ills in store. “Peppermint Candy,” which had its première in 1999, unspools a nightmare in reverse: it opens with a man’s suicide, then rewinds gradually, through two decades of accumulated agonies, to reveal how he got there. In Lee’s movies, time tends to flow inexorably in a single direction, sans flashbacks (or flash-forwards). We see the characters’ histories in their anxious posture, their stricken faces, their frustrated yearning for a glimmer of transcendence.

Time works a bit differently in “Possible Love,” Lee’s first feature since the masterly psychological drama “Burning” (2018). The pain of this story lies squarely in the past, and Lee, who wrote the script with Oh Jung-mi, proves more willing than usual to bridge the gap between then and now. The outset is linear enough. We are in an unnamed town where, two years earlier, a manufacturer named SP Heavy Industries laid off forty per cent of its workforce. The workers organized a strike, but their efforts were brutally suppressed. Among the terminated is a welder, Choi Ho-seok (Sul Kyung-gu), who wears a frown so implacable that it might have been etched in steel. He has since found another manufacturing job, and his sharp-witted, warm-spirited wife, Mi-ok (Jeon Do-yeon), works at a protective-face-mask factory. They have a young son, Cheol, an ordinary kid with a telltale core of solemnity. The Chois can’t shake their memories of more prosperous times, or of the profit-motivated culling that brought them to an end.

They are not alone in their trauma. We meet the Chois on their way to a memorial gathering for a former colleague of Ho-seok’s who took his own life. The grief of those in attendance goes beyond one death; they seem zombified by their anguish. One woman, though, stands calmly apart. Her name is Lee Ye-ji (Cho Yeo-jeong), and she’s shooting a documentary about laid-off workers. Ye-ji is an amateur but she has a muckraker’s instinct for drama. When Mi-ok whisks a cup of soju away from Ho-seok—he’s an alcoholic, and has promised never to drink in public—Yeji, anticipating an outburst, thrusts her lens into the fray. “Do we amuse you?” Ho-seok growls at her. She apologetically lowers her camera, though not for long. She knows there’s a story here, and Lee does, too.

Despite this inauspicious start, Mi-ok and Ye-ji become fast friends. So enlivened are they in each other’s company—and so gracefully does Jeon’s performance harmonize with Cho’s—that you’re tempted to forget Ye-ji’s ulterior motives. But Mi-ok is never blind to them, or to another complicating fact: her new friend is very wealthy. At one point, she calmly calls her out for wearing a pricey-looking shirt while interviewing people who’ve been laid off. Ye-ji has a hunky hedge-fund-type husband, Sang-woo (Zo In-sung), who condescendingly tolerates, but doesn’t finance, his wife’s cinematic caprices. Seeing her attempt to empathize with her subjects, Sang-woo extends the most backhanded of compliments. “You’re different from those people. But you yourself believe that you’re the same,” he says. “That’s great.”

Ye-ji blanches at that jab but only tightens her grip on her project: the Chois are to be the battered working-class foundation of her vérité masterpiece. Ho-seok wants no part of it. Inarticulate with self-loathing, he can scarcely drag himself to his ex-colleagues’ regular group-therapy sessions, let alone unburden himself on camera. But Mi-ok is game to be interviewed, and her words spring to life in wistfully lovely flashbacks. Here she is as a young girl, witnessing, for the first time, the depths of her mother’s working-class shame. And here she is as a teen-ager, catching the eye of a brashly confident Ho-seok, and sensing, with a thrill, that her sheltered world has finally burst open. The first time I saw “Possible Love,” these forays into the past, for all their dewy poignancy, felt like missteps—a rare concession to the backstory gods. Only on a subsequent viewing did I see the craftiness in Lee’s design: Are these Mi-ok’s tearily recalled flashbacks? Or are they being imagined by Ye-ji, eager to transform her subject’s every reverie into a reënactment?

All of which is to say that “Possible Love” is a multitasking marvel: a drama about the fragility of unions, in every sense, wrapped in a seminar on documentary ethics, but one with implications for the fiction realm, too. In an interview, Lee has said that it’s no coincidence he and Ye-ji share a surname, however common. He employs a lightly jostling handheld camera that insidiously mimics the formal syntax of Ye-ji’s documentary—and, in so doing, leaves the mere idea of authorial objectivity by the wayside. (The cinematographer is Kim Ji-yong.) Lee seems to be acknowledging his own potential for dramatic exploitation, if not of his characters, then of the events that inspired the story—an automaker’s restructuring in Korea in 2009 that threatened thousands of layoffs, leading to both a strike and a police crackdown. Lee knows that the liberties of fiction do not absolve him of a responsibility to tell the truth.

No wonder some of the film’s most piercing moments are on the periphery. One woman in a group-counselling session recalls the joyous early days of the strike, with their almost sacred spirit of community: “In all my life, I’ve never felt such happiness.” Another speaks of dreading the elevator in her building, lest she run into neighbors who still work at SP Heavy. Lee’s films have often zeroed in on misfits and outcasts, cut off from society’s embrace. What he evokes here is a different kind of alienation, suffused with bitterness and loss: a mass- casualty event that once brought people together must now be suffered in isolation. The boldest declarations of solidarity, Lee knows, often precede the quietest and cruellest of betrayals.

After the film’s première, at the Venice International Film Festival (where it won the Silver Lion Grand Jury Prize), more than a few observers invoked another class-conscious, double-decker marriage story from Korea: Bong Joon Ho’s “Parasite” (2019). (In both films, Cho Yeo-jeong, evidently cursed with resting rich face, plays the wealthier of two wives.) Lee’s contemplative realism could hardly be further removed from Bong’s blood-slicked flamboyance, but let’s indulge the reductive comparison. Like “Parasite,” “Possible Love” leads us into an enviable piece of real estate—Sang-woo’s family vacation home, a beachside paradise that was built for knotty third acts. But Lee, who has been known to gut his characters in the final stretch, doesn’t raise the body count to Bongian proportions. There is violence here, but it comes at us obliquely, with no inflection or exaggeration—in a sex scene that shivers with menace, or through a grimly mediated screen of documentary footage. Physical force has a hundred ways to shock and linger, but, crucially, it isn’t granted the last word.

The title asks whether love can survive what life throws at it: unemployment, depression, addiction, disappointment, poverty. A superficial glance would conclude that Ye-ji and Sang-woo are a dream couple, yet the more we see of them, the more glaring their lack of emotional capital. Miraculously, though, whenever the camera shifts to Ho-seok and Mi-ok—whether they’re bickering on their way home or scarfing down late-night snacks on the kitchen floor—love feels not just possible but plausible. Sul and Jeon have admittedly had plenty of practice; this is the third movie, after “I Wish I Had a Wife” and “Birthday,” in which they’ve played a couple. Both actors have also worked with Lee before, to tremendous effect. Sul was the suicidal protagonist of “Peppermint Candy,” and Jeon was the grieving heroine of “Secret Sunshine”—two performances that, for sustained emotional force, have few rivals in the acting annals of this century. They, too, are part of the past that haunts “Possible Love.”

Faced with such a high-stakes reunion, the actors, wisely, rise to the challenge with exacting understatement. You keep waiting for Sul to explode, but Ho-seok’s sadness lies beyond histrionics, and we realize that he isn’t angry or distant by nature; his decency cost him almost everything, and he now wields detachment as a shield against further harm. Without his wife to chide and console him, where would he be? Mi-ok feels her family’s losses acutely, but her impishly defiant spirit—watch as she tells her boss she’s starting a union, almost on a whim, like a goofball Norma Rae—has armored her against despair. And Jeon, in the most intricately detailed and emotionally liberated performance I’ve seen this year, gives flesh and definition to the character’s sardonic sense of mischief, her sudden spasms of grief, and her deep understanding that the most romantic gestures can be the most pragmatic. Hence the gorgeous moment when Mi-ok joins Ho-seok for a nightcap under the stars—secret moonshine—and affirms that, in the face of insurmountable problems, enduring them together is their only solution. “Drinking without you, it’s so bitter,” Ho-seok says. Solidarity forever. ♦
