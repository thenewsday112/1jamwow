Some terminal frustrations

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A few weeks ago I ran a terminal survey (you can read the results here) and at the end I asked:

What’s the most frustrating thing about using the terminal for you?

1600 people answered, and I decided to spend a few days categorizing all the responses. Along the way I learned that classifying qualitative data is not easy but I gave it my best shot. I ended up building a custom tool to make it faster to categorize everything.

As with all of my surveys the methodology isn’t particularly scientific. I just posted the survey to Mastodon and Twitter, ran it for a couple of days, and got answers from whoever happened to see it and felt like responding.

Here are the top categories of frustrations!

I think it’s worth keeping in mind while reading these comments that

  • 40% of people answering this survey have been using the terminal for 21+ years
  • 95% of people answering the survey have been using the terminal for at least 4 years

These comments aren’t coming from total beginners.

Here are the categories of frustrations! The number in brackets is the number of people with that frustration. I’m mostly writing this up for myself because I’m trying to write a zine about the terminal and I wanted to get a sense for what people are having trouble with.

remembering syntax (115)

People talked about struggles remembering:

  • the syntax for CLI tools like awk, jq, sed, etc
  • the syntax for redirects
  • keyboard shortcuts for tmux, text editing, etc

One example comment:

There are just so many little “trivia” details to remember for full functionality. Even after all these years I’ll sometimes forget where it’s 2 or 1 for stderr, or forget which is which for > and >>.

switching terminals is hard (91)

People talked about struggling with switching systems (for example home/work computer or when SSHing) and running into:

  • OS differences in keyboard shortcuts (like Linux vs Mac)
  • systems which don’t have their preferred text editor (“no vim” or “only vim”)
  • different versions of the same command (like Mac OS grep vs GNU grep)
  • no tab completion
  • a shell they aren’t used to (“the subtle differences between zsh and bash”)

as well as differences inside the same system like pagers being not consistent with each other (git diff pagers, other pagers).

One example comment:

I got used to fish and vi mode which are not available when I ssh into servers, containers.

color (85)

Lots of problems with color, like:

  • programs setting colors that are unreadable with a light background color
  • finding a colorscheme they like (and getting it to work consistently across different apps)
  • color not working inside several layers of SSH/tmux/etc
  • not liking the defaults
  • not wanting color at all and struggling to turn it off

This comment felt relatable to me:

Getting my terminal theme configured in a reasonable way between the terminal emulator and fish (I did this years ago and remember it being tedious and fiddly and now feel like I’m locked into my current theme because it works and I dread touching any of that configuration ever again).

keyboard shortcuts (84)

Half of the comments on keyboard shortcuts were about how on Linux/Windows, the keyboard shortcut to copy/paste in the terminal is different from in the rest of the OS.

Some other issues with keyboard shortcuts other than copy/paste:

  • using Ctrl-W in a browser-based terminal and closing the window
  • the terminal only supports a limited set of keyboard shortcuts (no Ctrl-Shift-, no Super, no Hyper, lots of ctrl- shortcuts aren’t possible like Ctrl-,)
  • the OS stopping you from using a terminal keyboard shortcut (like by default Mac OS uses Ctrl+left arrow for something else)
  • issues using emacs in the terminal
  • backspace not working (2)

other copy and paste issues (75)

Aside from “the keyboard shortcut for copy and paste is different”, there were a lot of OTHER issues with copy and paste, like:

  • copying over SSH
  • how tmux and the terminal emulator both do copy/paste in different ways
  • dealing with many different clipboards (system clipboard, vim clipboard, the “middle click” clipboard on Linux, tmux’s clipboard, etc) and potentially synchronizing them
  • random spaces added when copying from the terminal
  • pasting multiline commands which automatically get run in a terrifying way
  • wanting a way to copy text without using the mouse

discoverability (55)

There were lots of comments about this, which all came down to the same basic complaint – it’s hard to discover useful tools or features! This comment kind of summed it all up:

How difficult it is to learn independently. Most of what I know is an assorted collection of stuff I’ve been told by random people over the years.

steep learning curve (44)

A lot of comments about it generally having a steep learning curve. A couple of example comments:

After 15 years of using it, I’m not much faster than using it than I was 5 or maybe even 10 years ago.

and

That I know I could make my life easier by learning more about the shortcuts and commands and configuring the terminal but I don’t spend the time because it feels overwhelming.

history (42)

Some issues with shell history:

  • history not being shared between terminal tabs (16)
  • limits that are too short (4)
  • history not being restored when terminal tabs are restored
  • losing history because the terminal crashed
  • not knowing how to search history

One example comment:

It wasted a lot of time until I figured it out and still annoys me that “history” on zsh has such a small buffer; I have to type “history 0” to get any useful length of history.

bad documentation (37)

People talked about:

  • documentation being generally opaque
  • lack of examples in man pages
  • programs which don’t have man pages

Here’s a representative comment:

Finding good examples and docs. Man pages often not enough, have to wade through stack overflow

scrollback (36)

A few issues with scrollback:

  • programs printing out too much data making you lose scrollback history
  • resizing the terminal messes up the scrollback
  • lack of timestamps
  • GUI programs that you start in the background printing stuff out that gets in the way of other programs’ outputs

One example comment:

When resizing the terminal (in particular: making it narrower) leads to broken rewrapping of the scrollback content because the commands formatted their output based on the terminal window width.

“it feels outdated” (33)

Lots of comments about how the terminal feels hampered by legacy decisions and how users often end up needing to learn implementation details that feel very esoteric. One example comment:

Most of the legacy cruft, it would be great to have a green field implementation of the CLI interface.

shell scripting (32)

Lots of complaints about POSIX shell scripting. There’s a general feeling that shell scripting is difficult but also that switching to a different less standard scripting language (fish, nushell, etc) brings its own problems.

Shell scripting. My tolerance to ditch a shell script and go to a scripting language is pretty low. It’s just too messy and powerful. Screwing up can be costly so I don’t even bother.

more issues

Some more issues that were mentioned at least 10 times:

  • (31) inconsistent command line arguments: is it -h or help or –help?
  • (24) keeping dotfiles in sync across different systems
  • (23) performance (e.g. “my shell takes too long to start”)
  • (20) window management (potentially with some combination of tmux tabs, terminal tabs, and multiple terminal windows. Where did that shell session go?)
  • (17) generally feeling scared/uneasy (“The debilitating fear that I’m going to do some mysterious Bad Thing with a command and I will have absolutely no idea how to fix or undo it or even really figure out what happened”)
  • (16) terminfo issues (“Having to learn about terminfo if/when I try a new terminal emulator and ssh elsewhere.”)
  • (16) lack of image support (sixel etc)
  • (15) SSH issues (like having to start over when you lose the SSH connection)
  • (15) various tmux/screen issues (for example lack of integration between tmux and the terminal emulator)
  • (15) typos & slow typing
  • (13) the terminal getting messed up for various reasons (pressing Ctrl-S, cating a binary, etc)
  • (12) quoting/escaping in the shell
  • (11) various Windows/PowerShell issues

n/a (122)

There were also 122 answers to the effect of “nothing really” or “only that I can’t do EVERYTHING in the terminal”

One example comment:

Think I’ve found work arounds for most/all frustrations

that’s all!

I’m not going to make a lot of commentary on these results, but here are a couple of categories that feel related to me:

  • remembering syntax & history (often the thing you need to remember is something you’ve run before!)
  • discoverability & the learning curve (the lack of discoverability is definitely a big part of what makes it hard to learn)
  • “switching systems is hard” & “it feels outdated” (tools that haven’t really changed in 30 or 40 years have many problems but they do tend to be always there no matter what system you’re on, which is very useful and makes them hard to stop using)

Trying to categorize all these results in a reasonable way really gave me an appreciation for social science researchers’ skills.

Weakened US fuel-efficiency rules and promises of cheaper cars could see drivers pay higher running costs while also increasing carbon emissions.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Weakened US fuel-efficiency rules and promises of cheaper cars could yet see drivers pay higher running costs while increasing planet-heating emissions.

As part of its "Freedom Means More Affordable Cars" initiative, the US Department of Transportation this week rolled back fuel efficiency rules under the guise of lowering vehicle prices.

The Trump administration's transport agency says US taxpayers will save $138 billion (€122 billion) over the next five years — new vehicles will be $1,300 cheaper on average — since automakers will no longer have to invest in more fuel-efficient fleets.

But this does not account for soaring gas prices as Middle East conflicts choke global supply.

"Any savings on the front end get eaten up and spat out by excess costs of fuel… Fuel economy standards have saved drivers hundreds of billions of dollars," said Democratic Senator Sheldon Whitehouse from Rhode Island in a social media post.

Under the revised Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) rules, vehicle fleets only require an average fuel efficiency of about 35 miles per gallon (14.7 kilometers per liter) by 2031, down around one-third from 50 miles per gallon. Implemented by then-President Joe Biden in 2024, the updated efficiency rules aimed to incentivize the production of low emission electric vehicles and hybrids. The US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) estimates the standards would have saved 70 billion gallons (265 billion litres) of fuel over 25 years.

Meanwhile, nonprofit advocacy group the Union of Concerned Scientists (UCS) says an earlier version of CAFE regulations has saved US consumers about $321 billion at the gas pump since 2010.

As the world's biggest petrol consumer, the US burns through 20 million barrels of oil per day, which is 20% of global consumption, notes Dan Becker, director of the Safe Climate Transport Campaign at the US-based nonprofit environmental group Center for Biological Diversity.

"Transportation is the biggest consumer of that oil, making strong auto standards the most effective way to cut pollution and oil use," Becker said in a statement.

The rules that have been diluted this week would have prevented more than 710 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions by 2050 — the equivalent of running 165 million gas-powered passenger vehicles for a year.

"With transportation representing nearly 28% of US greenhouse emissions, rolling back fuel economy standards is a dangerous setback for near-term climate goals," said Ben Scott, head of Energy Demand at UK-based climate think tank Carbon Tracker. "It risks keeping inefficient combustion engines on the road much longer than climate science demands," he told DW.

But following President Donald Trump's elimination of greenhouse gas emissions standards for light- and heavy-duty vehicles last year, Dave Cooke, senior vehicles analyst for the UCS Clean Transportation Program, said the rollbacks merely compound "a much more significant step backwards."

He said the trend sends "a specific signal to automakers," adding that "the United States is seeking to maximize oil usage to increase oil company profits."

This will be to the detriment of climate targets, with high-polluting oil contributing one-third of global fossil fuel emissions that continue to rise — along with temperatures.

The world just had the hottest August on record as severe heatwaves in Europe caused more than 16,000 excess deaths in Germany alone.

Sales of low emission EVs are down in the US in 2026, especially after Donald Trump scrapped the $7,500 tax credit for new electric vehicles.

American automakers also continue to push back against regulations that would incentivize a switch to EVs, arguing there is a lack of consumer demand.

"It's highly unlikely that the United States will suddenly become awash in electric options when neither the auto industry nor regulators seem interested in providing such choices for consumers," said Cooke.

But Ben Scott believes that while softening US fuel economy standards "will create temporary headwinds", it won't stall an inevitable transition to electric vehicles.

"With EVs delivering vastly lower running costs, electrification becomes a clear-cut economic choice," he said of cars running on "cheaper, domestically secure energy that will drive the transition forward, regardless of short-term politics."

Nobel Prize in chemistry goes to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai won the Nobel Prize in chemistry for their groundbreaking discoveries related to the origin of chirality and life's building blocks.

The Nobel Prize in chemistry on Wednesday was awarded to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis."

Kagan, who is 95 years old and was born in France, is affiliated with France’s Université Paris-Sud, and Soai, who is 76 years old and was born in Japan, is based at the Tokyo University of Science.

The scientists share the Nobel Prize for answering longstanding questions in chemistry about life's origins.

Specifically, the duo solved an age-old mystery that has long puzzled chemists: some molecules, like amino acids, exist as two variants that are mirror images of one another, but just one of the variants out of the two is found in living organisms.

The two answered questions about why life chooses one variant over another.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the winner in Stockholm.

In chemistry, life's building blocks are what scientists call homochiral, after the Greek words for "same" and "hand."

The Nobel Committee said the chemistry prize recognizes discoveries that have enabled chemists to drive chemical reactions that lead to homochirality.

"Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously," Heiner Linke, chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, said in a news release. "The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular."

The duo's discoveries, the news release said, "have been decisive for chemists who design reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals."

Scientists overcome a hidden obstacle to more powerful quantum computers

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Silicon spin qubits could play a major role in the future of quantum computing because they can be produced using the same semiconductor manufacturing methods that make today's computer chips. However, researchers have faced a significant challenge. When multiple silicon qubits are controlled at the same time, their operations tend to become less accurate. Building practical, large quantum computers will require scientists to control many qubits simultaneously while keeping errors to a minimum.

Researchers at the RIKEN Center for Emergent Matter Science and their collaborators have now made important progress toward solving this problem. In a study published in Nature Physics, the team demonstrated highly accurate control of several silicon spin qubits operating simultaneously. Their experiments used an array of five qubits connected to a shared microwave control line. By identifying a major cause of errors during simultaneous operation, the scientists developed a technique to reduce those errors and maintain precision.

Leon Camenzind, a RIKEN research scientist and co-first author who supervised the experiments, explains the significance of the results: "This shows that high-fidelity control can be maintained even when several qubits are operated at the same time and represents an important step toward scaling up silicon-based quantum processors."

Scientists Identify a Hidden Source of Quantum Computing Errors

Unlike conventional computers, which store information as bits representing either 0 or 1, quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, that can exist in combinations of both states. Silicon spin qubits store this information using the spin of individual electrons confined within silicon. Performing reliable quantum calculations requires extremely precise control over these electron spins.

Microwave signals provide that control, but operating multiple qubits simultaneously can create unwanted effects that interfere with their accuracy. The researchers traced much of this problem to a phenomenon called the AC Stark shift.

This effect occurs when microwave control signals alter a qubit's effective energy levels, producing changes in the phase of its quantum state. Those phase shifts can disrupt the intended operation of individual qubits, particularly when several are being controlled at once.

To counteract the problem, the team created a calibration method that compensates for these unwanted shifts. Rather than separately calibrating every possible combination of qubits, the technique relies on calibrations performed between pairs of qubits. This makes the approach more practical for systems containing larger numbers of quantum bits.

"Rather than requiring an entirely separate calibration procedure for every possible combination of qubits, our method uses calibrations between pairs of qubits to compensate for the unwanted phase shifts," explains Yi-Hsien Wu, the first author who carried out the experiments as part of his PhD research.

Quantum Qubits Achieve Accuracy Above 99.99%

The researchers combined their calibration technique with an improved device design and carefully adjusted microwave control pulses. Their results showed that exceptionally high accuracy could be maintained even when multiple qubits were operating simultaneously.

When the five qubits were tested individually, each achieved single-qubit gate fidelity well above 99.99%, with the highest approaching 99.999%. Fidelity describes how closely a quantum operation matches its intended result.

The team then tested how well the system performed when several qubits were active together. With as many as three qubits operating simultaneously, fidelity remained above 99.99%. Even when all five qubits were controlled at once, accuracy stayed at approximately 99.9%, approaching the levels needed for practical fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Although the calibration method greatly reduced errors, it did not eliminate them entirely. The researchers found that the remaining loss of accuracy was linked to drive-induced decoherence, an effect caused by the greater microwave power required to operate multiple qubits simultaneously.

Unlike the unwanted phase shifts corrected by the new technique, decoherence involves the loss of the delicate quantum coherence needed for reliable operations.

According to Camenzind, "This is a limitation, but further improvements to device design could help mitigate this source of error."

A New Path Toward Larger Quantum Processors

The findings show that controlling several silicon spin qubits at the same time does not necessarily require a major sacrifice in accuracy. By correcting a key source of errors, the researchers have demonstrated a promising approach for operating more qubits through shared microwave control lines.

This is especially important for the development of large quantum processors. Giving every qubit its own independent control system would make increasingly complex devices difficult to manage. Shared control lines could offer a more practical way to expand these systems while maintaining the precision needed for quantum calculations.

"By addressing a key source of control errors through pairwise calibration," says Camenzind. "Our study supports the use of shared control lines as a practical approach to scaling up quantum processors. Combined with silicon's compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing, we believe that this advance could help pave the way toward larger quantum processors capable of performing increasingly complex computations."

The broader objective is to develop quantum computers that can reliably perform calculations despite the errors and disturbances that affect quantum systems. Such machines, known as fault-tolerant quantum computers, will require precise, dependable control over large numbers of qubits.

"Our long-term goal is to build a fault-tolerant quantum computer based on silicon spin qubits, and reliable parallel control is an essential step toward that goal," adds Seigo Tarucha, project manager of a silicon quantum computing project under JST's Moonshot Goal 6.

Earth’s Magnetic Shield Is Rippling Unevenly – Scientists Have Uncovered the Cause

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The solar wind can weaken the very waves it creates along Earth’s magnetic shield, simulations show, helping explain why some regions are more active than others.

Earth’s magnetic shield protects the planet, yet particles and energy from the Sun can still find their way through. Along its outer edge, a constant stream of solar material brushes past, stirring up waves where the two environments meet. A new study led by researchers at the University of Oulu explains why those waves grow stronger in some places than in others, revealing how uneven this boundary can be.

The protective region created by Earth’s magnetic field is called the magnetosphere, while the stream arriving from the Sun is known as the solar wind. Their interactions help drive space weather. On Earth, the effects can appear as auroras, while strong solar storms can disrupt satellites, positioning systems, radio communications, and power grids.

The boundary waves are known as Kelvin–Helmholtz waves. Similar patterns sometimes appear in clouds, and related phenomena occur in the Sun’s atmosphere, along the edges of other planets’ magnetic fields, and in interstellar space. Around Earth, however, satellites rarely pass through the most active regions.

Those limited encounters have made it difficult to establish how the waves behave under different solar wind conditions, so the researchers used computer models of the entire magnetosphere to examine their formation.

“This finding grew out of a long process of thinking through what I was seeing. In 2020, when I was visiting NASA as a postdoctoral researcher, I noticed unusual simulation images of the magnetosphere. I later realized that they showed Kelvin–Helmholtz waves at the edge of the magnetosphere, and that the different behavior of the waves on different sides was part of the same phenomenon,” says Academy Research Fellow Lauri Holappa from the University of Oulu.

The models traced the uneven wave activity to the way the solar wind’s magnetic field meets Earth’s field. Rather than wrapping evenly around the magnetosphere, the solar wind’s field drapes asymmetrically around it. This draped field can dampen the waves, but its damping effect varies from place to place, allowing stronger wave activity in some regions than in others.

“Asymmetries in the wave motion at the edge of the magnetic field have been observed before, but now we were able to show how they arise,” Holappa says.

The strongest activity appeared in the winter hemisphere, which faces away from the Sun. Within that hemisphere, the direction of the solar wind’s magnetic field determined whether the waves were concentrated on the dawn side or the dusk side.

Scientists have long known that the Southern Ocean plays a central role in regulating atmospheric CO2. Changes in its circulation and sea ice can increase the amount of carbon stored in the deep ocean, contributing to the cooling associated with ice ages. The growth of massive Northern Hemisphere ice sheets begins with changes in Earth’s orbit that alter how solar energy is distributed across the planet. According to the new study, published in the Journal of Climate, these expanding ice sheets may set off a much larger chain of events by cooling nearby North Atlantic waters.

As those colder waters move through the global ocean circulation system, they can eventually reach the Southern Ocean. If the northern cooling is strong enough, it could alter circulation around Antarctica and promote conditions that allow more CO2 to remain stored in the ocean rather than the atmosphere.

This proposed connection provides a physical explanation for how ice sheet growth in one hemisphere could help amplify cooling across the planet.

To test the hypothesis, researchers used computer simulations that cooled only the ocean’s northernmost latitudes (mimicking the effect of a large ice sheet). They then examined whether the temperature changes could travel south and alter Southern Ocean conditions in ways that reduce atmospheric CO2.

Ice age research has traditionally emphasized processes occurring around Antarctica, where ocean circulation strongly influences the exchange of carbon between the atmosphere and deep ocean. The new findings suggest that temperature changes in distant northern waters may also be an important part of that system.

Past ice ages provide natural examples of how carbon moves between Earth’s atmosphere and oceans over long periods. Understanding these processes can help scientists improve climate models and investigate how the global carbon cycle responds to changes in atmospheric CO2, including the additional carbon dioxide released by human activities.

Satellite observations demonstrate the existence of a ‘grounding zone,’ sometimes kilometers wide, where warm ocean water is reaching deep underneath the ice sheets and causing vigorous melting.”

Synthetic aperture radar readings from the Finnish ICEYE satellite constellation allowed the researchers to detect kilometer-scale fluctuations in this zone along the main trunk of Thwaites. The data indicate that seawater penetrates several kilometers beneath grounded ice and lifts it during tidal cycles. This flushes the underside of the ice with pressurized, warm seawater, which currents altered by climate change push toward the Antarctic coastline.

That contact with the ocean affects how firmly the glacier remains anchored to ridges on the seafloor. According to Poinelli, a NASA JPL research affiliate who is now a project scientist at UCLA, the study showed that grounding zone processes accelerate the glacier’s detachment from these stabilizing features, allowing faster ice flow and deterioration.

“The Thwaites main trunk is being temporarily stabilized by the Mouginot Ridge,” he said. “Once that ridge is unpinned, the glacier will continually retreat across a predominantly retrograde seafloor toward its next topographic high, the South Ridge.”

A retrograde seafloor slopes downward farther inland, so retreat would carry the glacier into deeper water before it reaches the next rise in the terrain.

The researchers calibrated their model against more than 20 years of observations. By including a grounding zone instead of a grounding line, they reconstructed changes in the glacier from the 1990s to the present that were four times as pronounced as those represented in traditional models.

Simulations incorporating a grounding zone 4 to 6 kilometers wide, or about 2.5 to 3.7 miles, predict a 60 percent probability that the glacier will lose its anchoring contact with Mouginot Ridge by the end of the century, Poinelli said. “When you factor in key physical processes and calibrate against historical observations, you gain a reliable pathway for narrowing uncertainty in projections,” he said.

“We have documented and quantified the impacts of climate change on our planet’s sensitive ice sheets for decades, but a mounting realization gained in recent years of what is happening on the undersides of glaciers like Thwaites is leading to an even greater sense of alarm,” said co-author Eric Rignot, UC Irvine professor of Earth system science. “Most projections up-to-present have struggled to match the rapidity of the mass loss because of missing physics; our new model with more complete physics is able to match the record and then produces projections that are twice larger than before. These results predict greater risk of and exposure for coastal flooding than anticipated in the coming decades.”

Souvankham Thammavongsa on the Gears That Turn Underneath a Story

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

This week’s story, “Wall Cano,” opens: “Mom left. Dad didn’t tell us anything.” The narrator of the story is a ten-year-old girl who has been living with her parents and her younger brother in a small basement apartment until one morning, before school, her mother’s not there. Why did you want to start the story in such a direct way?

I was thinking of writing a poem—just a list of my father’s smiles. But then I began to build in things around the list of smiles I had. A window. Cardboard. I really played around with the arrangement of the story. I felt like I could begin the story anywhere, put it out of order, and no matter how I arranged the details the story remained the same. Mom left. So I began with that.

The narrator’s father is a stylish man. He’s short, she says, but he has suits made up by a tailor that fit him perfectly. It’s clear that her mother is attractive, too. Other women are always trying to get the father’s attention and other men the mother’s. Does the narrator feel as though it was her job to keep them together?

I think she just thinks of it as normal. This keeping them together. It isn’t any different to her than being told to tie her shoe or eat breakfast.

At one point, the narrator’s father tells her that he never got to go to school. In Laos, where he grew up, “the war came, and that was it.” The narrator is left to figure things out by herself, and she often gets it wrong. The concept of homework, for example, is something she can’t understand. If school is the equivalent of work, then surely time at home is for relaxing after work. How hard is it for her to puzzle her way through a life her parents never experienced?

When I write, I know that the room reads me—or the voice on the page reads me—as someone who will tell a sad story. It’s a real surprise for people to discover that the way they have read my sentences in their head is not the same as experiencing my sentences live—in my own voice. I think of my stories as being built around humor. Maybe not laugh-out-loud humor, though that’s there, but other forms of humor like wit, deadpan, understatement, exaggeration, recognition, irony. We know what homework is, but the way the story asks us to suspend our understanding of homework for a bit to live in hers is funny. Doing it this way doesn’t diminish or judge or humiliate her lived experience. I love who I come from and where I come from. When I tell a story, I make sure a reader does that, too.

She does know that she’s supposed to make something for a science project, but she’s forgotten about it in the weeks after her mother left. Her father helps her make something, pulling down some of the cardboard that they use on the windows in place of curtains. What he makes—a model of a house on stilts, like one he’d lived in in Laos—is beautiful, but when she gets to school, she realizes that it’s completely wrong. Does she feel worse for herself or for her father?

I think she thinks everyone else is wrong. It’s a lot like that song Dolly Parton wrote, “Coat of Many Colors”: everyone else at school might think it’s a coat made of rags, but to her it is still a coat and it was made by her mother with what they had around the house. To build a house and make it stand is a science project, too. It might not win anything, but you don’t have to win anything to know that what you made deserves to live in this world.

The project that wins—and that gives the story its title—is a model of a volcano, a word she hears as “wall cano.” It’s ugly, like “a pile of shit,” she thinks, but impressive when smoke comes out of a hole. As in other of your stories, such as “How to Pronounce Knife,” the title story of your 2020 collection, English is the language your characters must use to navigate the world outside the home, but any mistakes, like pronouncing “knife” with an audible “k,” convey a kind of glorious defiance, too. Would the narrator ever think of the “wall cano” as a volcano?

I write about language. I ask us to pretend the English language right in front of us is not there. This is very easy to understand when we read a work and are told it is a translation. We are made to understand our distance from the English language. I write that distance into the English language in front of us with the English language. We all know we don’t pronounce the “k” in “knife.” It’s something we have learned—to see a letter there and agree that even though it is right there in front of us we will not give it a sound. And yet, in the telling, we are made to wonder, When did we agree to this and why did we agree to this? We understand why someone new to the language would question this. Instead of focussing on the “mistake” someone new to the language makes, we don’t see it as a mistake at all, but see that it makes absolute sense. I do a similar thing here, but with a different word. This time, though, I never give the correct word. We remain isolated with the word “wall cano,” and remain in her perspective and point of view and understanding of the language. We live and move through the story the way her father knows the English language. He says, “I just poo-poo paa-paa my way around until something makes sense to them. That’s how English works.” We do what he says of the English language—we, the readers, are given the responsibility of carrying the meaning and understanding of these words.

At the end of the story, the time frame shifts, and the narrator is now a thirty-seven-year-old woman, visiting a friend and watching as she checks her son’s homework. Why did you want to explicitly switch into this adult perspective?

My stories come across as really simple and tiny. Breathe and the story is over already. Blink and it’s done. There are all these gears turning underneath the story that make it tick. I can’t draw attention to all that in the telling, because it would betray the telling of it. I could have certainly done this a different way. I could have had the child go over to another child’s house and discover that their parents helped with their homework, too. Take a reader into a scene where she discovers she doesn’t have what other people have. I could have had this moment come out in a discussion between her and her brother. But I don’t think she could really know how significant the story she just told is. It also feels too easy and cheap and sentimental to keep her that age and have her realize hers is a different experience. I needed a lot of life to happen for her. And there’s a lot of life between that science project and her friend’s house. And I need that life to happen in order to give significant weight to the story that has just been told. The refusal, even as an adult, to say the word “volcano.” That restraint, that absence there in the last scene, is so important to the story.

Do you think of this as a story about education? What’s more valuable to your narrator? What she learns from watching her father, her mother, and her brother, or what she learns in school?

I think of this as a story about love. People leave or they change, and no one is coming to make things better. You will have to learn to love yourself even when there is no one there to tell you that you’re worth loving. In a way, that is what an education is. You learn how to teach people how to love something until they can do that on their own without you. ♦

“Wall Cano,” by Souvankham Thammavongsa

Mom left. Dad didn’t tell us anything. He just got up, and dressed, and told us to eat something. I saw some bananas on the kitchen counter with a few freckles on them and tore one from the bunch. Dug a nail through the end and peeled its yellow skin off. It came away in strips, three thick strips. I looked for bruises. I can’t stand eating a banana with bruises and was glad to see there were none. I peeled another one for my brother. “We don’t have much time,” my dad said, hurrying us out the door.

Read an interview with the author for the story behind the story.

He had a great smile, a mouth full of even teeth, and a dimple in each cheek. He was wearing his work clothes, not his nice ones. He wore nice clothes no matter the occasion, except when he went to work. None of the wool suits at the shops around here fit him, so he would buy the fabric at a store and take it to the one tailor he knew. That way, the shoulder stitching wouldn’t hang below his shoulder and his pants wouldn’t drag on the ground. You couldn’t tell he was short.

The tailor worked out of his home, not far from where we lived. My dad’s pieces were side projects the tailor took on for extra cash. He worked on them when he had time. There was never a finish date set, and my dad wasn’t pushy about it. My dad wasn’t tall, but he was handsome, especially when he wore his suits. He wore them to weddings, funerals, any kind of party or place with a dance floor. And he loved to dance. With Mom, especially. They were the first ones on the dance floor at a wedding. My dad always made sure that anyone who wanted to dance would have a chance to with him.

Whenever my dad went to drop off fabric at the tailor’s or received a call letting him know that the pieces were finished, we’d go with him, to give Mom a break. We’d wait in the car outside the tailor’s. It wasn’t long. Twenty minutes, maybe. He said, “That man doesn’t like kids.” Even if my father was angry or disagreed with someone, he smiled. Just smiled through it all.

My father worked somewhere that had a lot of cardboard boxes. We had a lot of cardboard around our place. We lived in a basement with two bedrooms. My father taped cardboard to all our windows so that no one could see us from the outside, but that also meant that we couldn’t see out from the inside. Still, he kept the cardboard taped to the windows because, he said, “It’s cheaper than curtains. More privacy, too.” My father also cut out a small square of cardboard for us to put our winter boots on. He said, “This way we won’t ruin the floor in the entryway.” I don’t know why he worried about that. It wasn’t like he owned the place. “Doesn’t matter. What if we move out, and someone else comes to live here? We don’t want them to think we’re pigs,” he said.

My brother and I shared one bedroom. He took the top bunk, and I took the bottom. He was two years younger than me, but that difference wasn’t a big deal. We talked about all our friends at school, and their parents, and our parents. “Did you see her dad? I thought he was her grandfather because he was sooooo oooold!” We were proud of how young our parents looked. They were often mistaken for teen-agers at the high school just down the hill from our school. And the guys in sixth grade would say to our mom, “What’s uuup, girl? You new here?” She’d smile, and me or my brother would say, “That’s our mom!” They’d apologize and ask, “Why’s she young?”

At night, there was always some noise outside that I couldn’t recognize, and it would take me a while to make up something to explain it away before I could calm down and go to sleep. I was a child who lived in my own mind and scared myself, and had a hard time talking myself out of it. Once, I thought I heard gunshots outside, but it was firecrackers going off. It was the Fourth of July. I thought there was a fire outside when what I was smelling was a car’s exhaust. A woman screaming at night turned out to be the blaring siren of an ambulance. For the longest time, there was this pretty, ethereal sound, as if a fairy were about to appear before me, but the fairy never appeared. I couldn’t sleep. When we walked to school, my brother showed me what it was. “Look, it’s that fairy music you hear,” he said, pointing to a porch with a small wind chime hanging from the ceiling.

My brother was really sensitive and noticed things that I didn’t. “Did you see Ryan at school today? He didn’t have anything to eat, and his mom doesn’t pick him up anymore. It’s a woman, but it’s not his mom. And Miss Stevens got a perm,” he said. “Someone called her Miss Curly in class, and everyone laughed. It must be hard to be a teacher. To go to school for so many years and know all that stuff and then come teach us so we know it, too.”

Our dad said that he never got to go to school like us. In Laos, the war came, and that was it. At least he knew how to read people, he said. “I just poo-poo paa-paa my way around until something makes sense to them. That’s how English works.” He smiled, like someone who was about to be hit but thought he could stop the blow if he shrank into himself and made like he was so tiny he wasn’t worth anything. Our mom didn’t know how to read and would tell us to take our questions to our dad. When she was still living with us, she’d grab the side of her head and tell us it hurt, and then she would go into their bedroom and close the door. Because of the cardboard, our place was always so dark.

When I think about it, other people were always trying to break up our parents. There was the woman on the sixth floor, who often forgot her keys and would press our buzzer to get Dad to go out and open the door for her. She knew that our intercom didn’t work properly and that the superintendent hadn’t come around to fix it. So she’d buzz, and Dad would have to go out there and let her into the building, and they’d talk in the hallway. I don’t know what they talked about, but Dad would come back and Mom would be so angry. He’d do his three smiles for her. He’d start with a smile that made his dimples sink in, then his lips would turn tight, as if he were pretending to smile, and then he’d flash a more relaxed smile where the wrinkles in his forehead didn’t show. He’d say he was ugly and no one wanted him, let alone wanted him enough to steal him away. “What for, to be a pet?”

It wasn’t the same when it came to Mom. Any man Mom talked to, our dad said, was a man trying to steal her. So it was our job to watch out for these men and make sure they didn’t come near our mom. “She’s married,” I would say if a man sat next to her on the bus. Me and my brother would squeeze in between to let him know she had children.

One time, at a park on a hot summer day, an old man came out of nowhere and sat on the blanket with our mother. He had hair the color of my pee, and there were so few hairs on his head I could count them all. I’d never seen him before, and I didn’t know who he was. He was the type of person who called my mom a lotus flower or yelled “Ten dollars! Ten dollars!” at her. People like that were always telling us to go home. I don’t know why. It was just a basement. We didn’t even have a balcony.

I tapped my brother on the shoulder and showed him that I had some stones in my hand, and I turned my chin toward the man with my mom. My brother understood. We crept close and hid behind a tree stump that was half poking out of the ground, and we threw the stones at him. We were nice about it at first, using small ones that mostly hit him on the arm. He laughed and kept on trying to talk to her. After that, we knew we had to do greater damage.

I signalled like a soldier to my brother to try a spot on his face. He said, “I don’t want to. He’s just a man.” I did it myself—picked up some more stones, heavier ones, and threw them. They whizzed by the man’s head. Then I picked up another one, and it hit the man on the mouth. My mom looked at us, and then back at the man, and laughed. The man got up off the blanket and shouted at me, “Fucking kids!” I stared back at him and took my index finger and slid it across my neck like it was a knife. I yelled “Fucking old man!” and stuck out my flat chest. My brother slunk away and hid inside an orange tube slide.

We didn’t tell our dad. He wouldn’t have been able to get past the fact that some man had come to talk to our mother, so I couldn’t even tell him how I’d protected her. Some things you just do and you know you did right. You don’t have to wait for someone to tell you.

I didn’t know what homework was. And since my parents had never gone to school themselves, they never asked me about it. I thought that because we spent all day at school sitting at a desk—working—when I got home my evening was supposed to be like Dad’s. It was supposed to be for rest, for watching television as long as my eyes would stay open. I brought schoolwork home with me, but I just left it in a plastic bag by the door. The science project, though, was a big thing. Our teacher had talked about it since the first day, and we were supposed to work on it all year. I’d forgotten about it after Mom left, and now there were only three weeks until this big show. There would be prizes. I’d never won anything before, and my brother said, “Dad can make something easy. First place, too, I bet,” so I told Dad and we scrambled something together two nights before it was due.

He took a piece of cardboard from the corner of our kitchen window and asked me to cut it into small strips, the size of Popsicle sticks. We needed more sticks, he said. The more cardboard we took from the window, the more light we let in, and the more we let people see us. But once we’d got about a hundred of those sticks he began building something. It looked like a house, but not like the houses here. It was on stilts. He said he’d lived in a house like that before we came here. “Now I’m a refugee,” he said. I didn’t know what a refugee was, and neither did my brother, but I thought that since my dad had said he was one that must mean that we were, too, like the way we carry our last name. Whether we wanted that name or not, it was just there, telling everyone where we came from and that we were related.

The thing my father made was tiny and beautiful and so delicate and tender. If it weren’t for the glue, I was pretty sure the whole thing would have fallen apart real easy. I didn’t want to take it to school and have it on display. It was so obvious that I hadn’t built that thing. This was an adult’s hand at work. All the same, I was proud of my dad and what he’d made, and I wanted everyone to see it.

When I brought my dad’s little house into the classroom and placed it on top of my desk, a crowd formed quickly. Everyone was impressed. There were doubts, even accusations already that I hadn’t made it. I didn’t defend myself. I just stayed quiet. I looked around at the other projects. A bowl of water. If you dropped ice cubes into it and then dropped a thread with a bit of salt in, it would melt the ice and the thread would sink into it. A kid held up a thread, and an ice cube dangled from the end of it. Someone else had made an eyeball. It had labels, explaining what each part was called and how it all worked—what happened when a part got damaged and a person couldn’t see. What it means when we wear glasses. I thought that was interesting. Then, there was this brown thing that looked like a pile of shit. I really did not know what it was. It was gigantic and took up a whole table all by itself. Smoke came out of a hole. When someone poured liquid into that hole, you could hear something rumbling and foaming. It was impressive. The moment I saw that thing, I knew that the competition was over and it would win. By the time school ended for the day, the prizes had been awarded. First, second, third, and honorable mention.

I walked home with the thing my dad had made and left it in the living room. I should have just trashed it, but I didn’t have the heart. I knew it was something you didn’t destroy. So I just brought it home.

When my dad came home from work, he came running toward me, excited, and he asked me what we’d won. He was so certain and confident.

I told him that what he’d made didn’t win anything.

I don’t know why it meant so much to him, but my dad began to cry then. His shoulders dropped, and his arms hung by his side. He looked like he was smiling, but he was crying. Tears wet his whole face, even though his eyes were closed. My brother cried, too. He cries at just about everything, and the sight of my father crying—well, it didn’t matter why. He was done for.

I didn’t know what to do for my father then. It didn’t seem right to tell him that things would be fine. It didn’t seem right to tell him there was always next year. It was just that he’d never been to school. He didn’t know you could work so hard and no matter what you did, it wouldn’t be good enough. He didn’t know that once you began to understand something, something else would come along and you’d have to learn to understand it all over again.

He turned to me after he was done crying, wiping his nose and tears. Who won? he asked. He wanted to know. I told him that I couldn’t remember. And it really didn’t matter. What mattered were the hours he’d put into making his house. Making something beautiful that no one had ever seen before. And I don’t know why I said this or where it came from or what I meant exactly, but I said, “Sometimes the winner doesn’t win.”

I didn’t want my father to know that something called a “wall cano” that looked like a pile of shit with foam coming out of its hole had won first place. Or that an eyeball thing had won second. Or that a stupid trick with ice cubes had won third. I just knew not to bring my schoolwork home and have my dad help me ever again for as long as I lived. I would go it alone. The man just wasn’t cut out for that stuff.

Not long after this, I thought I saw my mom across the street from my school. I felt something bright draw my eyes, and when I looked a woman was standing there, but I wasn’t sure if it was her. She had messy hair, and her clothes looked too big. I told my dad that night. He smiled at me without showing any of his teeth and pretended that I hadn’t said anything, and told me to finish my dinner. I tried to talk to my brother about it when we were alone in our room. I thought maybe he could tell me if it was her, and we’d talk about where she was or might be. But he also got quiet, tried a few words, and then disappeared into the dark. He pretended that I hadn’t said anything, too. People become who they are, and I think that’s a moment when my brother became who he is now.

I am not ten years old anymore, but I still have the same feelings at thirty-seven. Once, I was visiting a friend when she put up a finger like she’d heard a sound from the ceiling, but that was her way of telling me to pause our conversation. She leaned back in her chair and called out to her ten-year-old to remind him about his book report. He said he’d done it already, and he brought the report over for her to read, to look for spelling mistakes, to check for clarity. She reminded him what to aim for. A clear beginning, a middle, and an end. My friend had gone all the way in school. She’d finished and taken all the degrees. When I was ten, I did not have her, someone like her. I had to learn all that by myself. Out of order, probably. Middle, beginning, end. End, beginning, middle. End. What is an end, and when you think you have one, where does it go, exactly, when there is no one to tell you? ♦

Ein Winter als Ei, Raupe, Puppe oder Falter: Alle Entwicklungsstufen haben Strategien gegen Kälte, Eis und Nahrungsmangel.

Scientists discover a hidden flaw that challenges 40 years of superconductor science

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists at the University of Warwick have uncovered a surprising secret inside a superconductor that researchers have studied for 40 years. Long believed to have a uniform crystal structure, the material actually contains a complex patchwork of different atomic arrangements. Using an advanced 3D imaging technique, the team was able to see deep inside the crystal for the first time, revealing hidden features that challenge decades of scientific assumptions.

High-temperature superconductivity ranks among the most important scientific breakthroughs of the past four decades. Superconductors can carry electrical current without any resistance, eliminating the energy losses that normally occur as electricity travels through conventional materials. They also have the potential to support extraordinarily powerful magnets, making them promising for future power grids, medical scanners, and quantum computers.

The new findings, published in Physical Review Letters, come from researchers in Warwick's Department of Chemistry working with the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in France. Their investigation focused on a class of copper-based superconductors known as cuprates, which can conduct electricity without resistance at unusually high (though still very cold) temperatures.

Advanced 3D Imaging Reveals a Hidden Crystal Structure

For four decades, scientists have generally built their theories of cuprate superconductivity around the assumption that these materials have the same crystal structure throughout their interiors. The new research shows that this fundamental assumption does not always hold.

To investigate, the team used scanning 3D X-ray diffraction, a sophisticated imaging method that reveals how atoms are arranged deep within a crystal. The approach is similar in principle to a medical CT scan, except that it maps atomic structures rather than tissues and organs.

This allowed the researchers to construct a detailed three-dimensional picture of the superconductor's interior, exposing structural variations that conventional techniques had not revealed.

Instead of finding a uniform crystal, they discovered a patchwork of regions containing two slightly different atomic arrangements. Even more surprising were the boundaries separating these regions, which were hundreds of times wider than expected.

These unusually broad transition zones were not simply narrow dividing lines between neighboring structures. They were extensive enough to behave almost like distinct structures themselves, potentially influencing how electricity moves through the material.

A 40-Year Assumption About Superconductors Overturned

Professor Mark Senn, who led the study at the University of Warwick's Department of Chemistry, explained why the findings could have major implications for superconductivity research.

"For forty years, the working assumption has been that these materials are the same all the way through, and nearly all the theory is built on that picture. We've shown it doesn't hold. The crystal is fundamentally patchworked and textured, with unusually wide boundary regions that likely work against superconductivity rather than just sitting alongside it.

"This might explain why some materials perform better than others and means some existing bulk measurements will need to be reinterpreted and future theoretical models built that incorporate this structural complexity."

The discovery suggests that the internal boundaries may actively interfere with superconductivity rather than simply coexist with it. If that is the case, the hidden structural differences could help explain why certain superconducting materials perform better than others.

It also means that some previous experiments, particularly those measuring the overall properties of these materials, may need to be reconsidered. Future theories will likely need to account for the complex structures inside real crystals rather than treating them as perfectly uniform.

What the Discovery Means for Future Superconductors

The researchers suspect that similar hidden structural patterns may be widespread among other cuprate superconductors. They could also occur in related materials being investigated for their ability to become superconducting under extreme pressure.

Beyond challenging existing theories, the study demonstrates the power of a new generation of imaging technology.

The advanced measurements were made possible by a recent 150 million euro upgrade to the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, which provides extremely intense X-ray beams for studying materials in extraordinary detail.

This technology now allows scientists to examine the three-dimensional interiors of many different materials with a level of precision that was previously unavailable. By revealing how hidden atomic structures affect a material's behavior, the technique could provide valuable insights across materials science.

For superconductivity research, the implications are particularly important. Understanding how internal crystal structures influence electrical performance could eventually help scientists identify ways to improve superconducting materials, bringing more advanced energy systems, powerful magnets, medical imaging devices, and quantum technologies closer to reality.

Physicists Use Light To Unlock a Crystal’s Hidden Electrical Powers

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A crystal’s laser-induced electrical order lasts just a trillionth of a second, giving researchers a way to explore ultrafast approaches to processing and storing information.

Inside a crystal, tiny electrical regions can point in different directions or line up in an organized pattern. Cooling helps bring them into alignment, but physicists at Auburn University have now shown that brief flashes of laser light can also produce order at warmer temperatures, where those regions would otherwise remain disorganized.

The team worked with single crystals of a modified form of strontium titanate. The material allows electrons to move through it while also containing tiny regions with distinct positive and negative sides, an unusual combination of electrical properties. These regions are known as polar nanoregions.

For decades, physicists have debated whether atoms shift together to create the material’s polarized state or whether its polar regions already exist and become organized as it cools. Telling those explanations apart has been difficult because the regions are so small and their signals so faint.

Wencan Jin, the Thomas and Jean Walter Endowed Associate Professor in Auburn’s Department of Physics, led the research with graduate student Mohamed Kandil. The project began at the 2023 American Physical Society March Meeting, where Jin connected with researchers in Japan who could grow the crystals. Collaborators at Japan’s National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology grew the samples used in the experiments.

The Auburn researchers built a highly sensitive laser-based measurement system, then spent nearly a year improving the equipment before detecting the regions’ faint optical signal. By measuring how the crystal changed incoming light, they could track the regions’ alignment through repeated rounds of warming and cooling. The measurements showed disordered directions at higher temperatures giving way to preferred orientations as the crystal cooled. This provided strong evidence that the polar regions were already present before they lined up.

The team still had to capture the fleeting moment when laser illumination brought the regions into alignment. “The breakthrough came in 2025, when Mohamed finally observed the disordered polar regions becoming aligned under laser illumination,” Jin said. “Seeing that signal emerge was a thrilling moment for the team.”

The light-induced alignment lasted only a picosecond, or one trillionth of a second (10⁻¹² second), but an optical measurement could still detect the resulting pattern. Being able to create and read that brief arrangement with light gives researchers a way to investigate extremely fast approaches to processing and storing information. It also provides a tool for studying electrical behavior that is otherwise difficult to observe.

“This experiment has truly been Mohamed’s odyssey,” Jin said. “It took him three years of dedication to complete the work and achieve his first publication.”

Researchers Find That Arthritis May Leave a Hidden Memory in Your Joints

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Arthritis may leave a memory in the joints, and mouse experiments suggest stress can reawaken it with help from the body’s heat-producing fat.

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) can flare in previously affected joints even after the disease has gone into remission. Psychological stress has long been associated with worsening symptoms, but how it might help inflammation return to those joints has remained unclear.

A study in mice now points to a chain of signals linking the nervous system, heat-producing fat, and cells in the joints. Blocking parts of that chain reduced stress-triggered arthritis in the animals, suggesting a possible direction for future treatments.

The study was led by Assistant Professor Shigeru Tanaka and Professor Hiroshi Nakajima at Chiba University in Japan, in collaboration with two Japanese hospitals. The findings were published in Science Advances on October 16, 2026.

The researchers used a mouse model in which arthritis entered a remission-like state before the animals were exposed to restraint stress. Joint inflammation returned after that exposure. To trace the signals responsible, the team analyzed tissues, profiled immune cells, and measured molecular changes.

Stress activated the sympathetic nervous system, which helps coordinate the body’s stress response, and brown adipose tissue (BAT), a type of fat that produces heat. This activation prompted the brown fat to release interleukin-6 (IL-6), an inflammatory signaling protein. Circulating IL-6 then acted on fibroblast-like synoviocytes, specialized cells in the synovial tissue surrounding the joints.

In response, these joint cells produced CXCL1, a chemokine that attracts immune cells. CXCL1 drew in neutrophils, a type of white blood cell involved in inflammation, contributing to the return of arthritis.

“Our findings suggest that the effects of psychological stress on arthritis extend beyond the immune cells traditionally associated with autoimmune disease. Instead, stress can activate processes within other tissues that contribute to the return of joint inflammation,” Tanaka explains.

The team tested whether interrupting this sequence could reduce the flares by blocking key receptors and monitoring inflammatory markers, joint swelling, and immune-cell accumulation. Treating the mice with a β3-adrenergic receptor antagonist, a substance that blocks that receptor, reduced IL-6 levels in blood plasma, joint swelling, CXCL1 expression, and the accumulation of neutrophils in the joints. Blocking CXCR2, the receptor that responds to CXCL1, also significantly reduced stress-triggered arthritis in the mouse model.

The findings suggest that previously affected joint tissue may retain a form of inflammatory memory that stress can reactivate independently of lymphocytes, a group of immune cells. This offers a possible biological explanation for how inflammation can return to joints after arthritis has subsided.

To examine whether the pathway might also be relevant to people, the researchers compared perceived stress and disease activity in patients with RA. Patients carrying Trp64Arg, a specific variant of the ADRB3 gene that makes cells less responsive to stress-related hormones, showed a weaker relationship between perceived stress and arthritis activity than patients without the variant. The human analysis was observational, however, so it does not establish that stress directly caused changes in disease activity.

Understanding this pathway could eventually help researchers develop strategies to improve disease management and quality of life for people with RA. Its potential as a treatment target remains to be explored.

“Our findings suggest that targeting the stress-responsive pathways may offer a future therapeutic strategy that acts without broadly suppressing the immune system,” Nakajima notes.

6 ways bats are surprisingly human

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 24, 2026 1:30 PM EDT

Bat Week officially begins today. It’s the one week where Earth’s only winged mammals get top billing. Often associated with disease, vampires, and other generally unpleasant things, bats are actually important pollinators and play critical roles in our ecosystem.

“They’re just so cool and so interesting. During first forays into bat studies, it was just one question after another,” Rachel Page, an evolutionary biologist at the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute in Panama, tells Popular Science. “The more I sort of scratched the surface, the more interesting it became—which is the fun part.”

One reason bats are so interesting to scientists like Page is that they are surprisingly similar to humans. Here’s how.

Sharing a meal with good company brings immense joy for us humans. For many vampire bat species (subfamily Desmodontinae), sharing food is a matter of survival. They survive exclusively on blood and can only go about two nights without a blood meal before they die. To get around this, they rely on their social networks.

“They have the equivalent of friends. Basically, if a bat comes back from a night of foraging and has not been successful, other members of that social group will share food,” Page explains. “They’ll regurgitate blood for the bat that came back hungry.”

If that donor bat has difficulty getting food later, the one recipient will often become the donor. “So there’s this very reciprocal relationship,” says Page.

According to Page, bats will have their distinctive personalities just like people. It’s present across species, but also individual bats as well.

“You have some species that are very feisty, and they’ll go out of their way to pick a fight, and then other ones that are much more passive, calm, and very gentle,” she says.

At the edge of Panama’s Soberanía National Park, tiny, fruit-eating bats will take over the roost of larger, carnivorous bats. The Seba’s short-tailed bat (Carollia perspicillata) weighs roughly the same as a strawberry. By comparison, the frog-eating, fringed-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) weighs roughly as much as an apricot and twice as much as the short-tailed bat.

Page and her colleagues in Panama have witnessed fights between the two species from roosts set up throughout the forest.

“Basically, it was the male Seba’s short-tailed bat who was instigating the fight. He was coming over and was kind of boxing and threatening much larger bats with wide open mouths and lots of teeth,” Page recalls. “And he just kept at it, and then finally, this group of fringed-lipped bats left.”

Even in the thick jungles of Panama, bats can find motionless insects on leaves. Initially, scientists believed that the thick tree cover would give small insects an “acoustic camouflage” that would confuse a bat’s echolocation system. But they were wrong.

The bats can see through this acoustic camouflage by changing the angle at which they approach the still insect.

“If you approach a flat leaf at just the right angle, you can actually get an echolocation signature,” Page explains. “You can get an echolocation signal that jumps back at you, but only if you direct your echolocation call at just the right angle.”

That’s exactly what the bats were doing in order to find insects who tried to hide in leaves.

When it’s time for the babies to wean or leave the roost, mother tent-making bats (Uroderma bilobatum) will physically prod their young with their forearms.

“There is this interesting tug that you see across so many different species. For birds, when they’re ready to fledge, there’s this sort of uncertainty.”

When sick, humans and even non-human primates change their behavior. We isolate so that the disease does not spread, and will look to plants and other sources of medicine for relief. Bats appear to change their diet to less nutritious leaves. While this may sound backwards, it’s in the hope that they have medicinal properties.

In an ongoing study with a team from the Ukrainian Bat Rehabilitation Center, the Smithsonian team is looking at the bats from Barro Colorado Island in Panama.

“We have fecal samples from these bats, so we were able to see exactly what they are eating,” says Page.

In addition to changing their diets, vampire bats will self-isolate when they are sick and others will act in a way familiar to any human parent—continuing to care for our young, even when sick.

“You don’t do as many of the normal behaviors unless it’s taking care of your babies,” says Page. “And again, it’s like a human relationship. If your little kid has a fever, you’re not going to shut them in a room and leave them. You’re not going to desert them.”

To find dinner, the fringe-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) will listen in on their prey’s mating calls. These frog-eating bats eavesdrop on the mating calls that the male frogs use to attract females. They then use the calls to assess if the frog is edible or poisonous before going in for the kill.

“They’ve really cracked this communication of another entire group,” says Page.

According to Page, it’s similar to being in a quiet room and hearing someone open up a bag of potato chips and is loudly eating. The noise makes everyone pay attention. “Bats attend to those acoustic signals of other bats, and that attention then helps them learn,” Page says. “They can learn those distinctions of palatable versus poisonous, and they can learn that socially as well from observing the other behaviors with bats.”

NASA’s asteroid Bennu samples reveal a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Samples collected from asteroid Bennu by NASA are revealing surprising new details about how our Solar System formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago. A new chemical analysis challenges earlier theories about the asteroid's birthplace and suggests that Jupiter played an important role in shaping the materials from which Bennu and other ancient objects formed.

Bennu completes one orbit around the Sun every 1.2 years and makes a relatively close approach to Earth about every six years, coming within roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles). NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission traveled to the asteroid, collected material from its surface, and successfully delivered the samples to Earth in 2023.

On September 23, 2023, a capsule carrying approximately 120 grams of asteroid material landed in the Utah desert. Scientists around the world subsequently received small portions of the precious cargo for detailed study.

Among them was Maria Schönbächler, Professor of Isotope Geochemistry at ETH Zurich, whose laboratory received half a gram of Bennu material. Her team immediately began examining the samples for clues about the asteroid's history.

The research, now published in Science Advances, has uncovered a distinctive chemical signature that not only helps identify Bennu's origins but also provides new evidence about the processes that shaped the early Solar System.

Bennu's Chemical Fingerprint Reveals Its Cosmic Relatives

To investigate Bennu's origins, the ETH Zurich researchers examined isotopes of three elements: iron, titanium, and chromium.

Isotopes are different forms of the same chemical element that contain varying numbers of neutrons, giving them slightly different masses. By measuring the relative amounts of these isotopes, scientists can identify a material's chemical fingerprint. These patterns provide clues about where an asteroid's ingredients originated and, in some cases, when they formed.

The analysis revealed that iron and titanium are distributed remarkably evenly throughout Bennu's material. It also uncovered a close chemical relationship between Bennu, the asteroid Ryugu, and a rare group of primitive meteorites known as CI meteorites.

CI meteorites are carbon-rich remnants of the early Solar System that have undergone relatively little alteration over billions of years. They are exceptionally uncommon among meteorites recovered on Earth.

Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites share strikingly similar isotopic fingerprints, suggesting that their original materials came from the same reservoir of ancient cosmic dust. Their chemical signatures also differ substantially from those of other known asteroids, meteorite groups, and planets.

These similarities offer an important clue about where these objects formed and what conditions existed during the Solar System's earliest stages.

Scientists Discover Bennu's Unexpected Birthplace

For years, researchers generally believed that primitive asteroids like Bennu originated in the outer Solar System, perhaps in the same distant regions where comets formed. They also suspected that Bennu's original material came together relatively late in the Solar System's development.

The new findings challenge both assumptions.

Instead, the researchers propose that the material that became Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites accumulated near a boundary known as the water-ice line.

This boundary marks the region in the disk surrounding the young Sun where temperatures were low enough for water vapor to freeze into ice. Around 4.5 billion years ago, it may have been a particularly important meeting place for material traveling from different parts of the developing Solar System.

Dust originating in both the inner and outer regions mixed near this boundary. Water ice helped the smallest particles stick together, allowing them to gradually accumulate into larger bodies.

"Bennu is a hybrid: the material does not clearly match either the inner or the outer Solar System," says Schönbächler.

Its unusual chemical composition suggests that Bennu formed from a combination of materials originating in both regions rather than from a single, isolated source.

Jupiter May Have Played a Crucial Role in Bennu's Formation

The researchers also developed a scenario explaining how this mixing could have occurred, with Jupiter emerging as a key player.

Jupiter began forming extraordinarily early in Solar System history, within approximately one million years of the Sun's birth. At that time, the young Sun was surrounded by a rotating disk of gas, dust, and ice.

Within this disk, countless small particles gradually collided and stuck together, building larger objects through a process called accretion. Over time, some of these growing bodies became asteroids and planets.

As Jupiter rapidly increased in mass, its immense gravitational influence began reshaping the distribution of material throughout the disk.

The researchers suggest that the growing gas giant acted somewhat like a structural barrier, preventing most larger particles from crossing its orbital region while still allowing finer dust to move around it.

This fine material, originating from different parts of the disk, could then mix extensively near the water-ice line.

According to the proposed scenario, the parent bodies of Bennu and Ryugu, along with the objects that produced CI meteorites, formed from this thoroughly mixed material.

Jupiter may therefore have helped determine not only where these ancient asteroids formed but also which ingredients became incorporated into them.

Ancient Dust and Ice Reveal the Ingredients That Built Earth

The proposed formation scenario could also explain two of Bennu's most intriguing characteristics: its abundance of water-bearing material and its chemical resemblance to the Sun.

Near the water-ice boundary, ice could evaporate as temperatures changed, releasing water vapor that later condensed again in cooler regions. This process would have helped concentrate water in the material from which Bennu formed.

Meanwhile, Jupiter's influence may have limited the movement of larger particles, leaving the asteroid's parent material dominated by fine dust.

Because these tiny particles could circulate and mix efficiently throughout the disk, they preserved a chemical composition that closely resembles the overall elemental mixture found in the Sun.

The process is somewhat similar to the way fine household dust spreads and settles across many different surfaces rather than remaining in one location.

This extensive mixing could explain why Bennu's material retains such a representative sample of the ingredients present during the Solar System's earliest history.

"Bennu may offer our best glimpse of the original mix of chemical elements from which the terrestrial planets were ultimately built," as Schönbächler states.

Bennu is considered an exceptionally primitive asteroid because its material formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and has experienced relatively little chemical alteration since then.

That makes its samples particularly valuable for reconstructing the conditions that existed before Earth and the other rocky planets finished forming.

By studying the asteroid's chemistry, scientists can better understand how the Solar System developed and which processes determined the composition of its planets.

Bennu's water-bearing minerals and organic compounds also make it important for investigating how early Earth obtained some of the chemical ingredients necessary for life.

Although these findings do not establish exactly how those materials reached our planet, they provide valuable evidence about where such ingredients originated and how they were distributed throughout the young Solar System.

A New Mission to Mars' Moon Could Reveal More

Despite the new discoveries, several questions remain unanswered.

"We are now wondering whether other asteroids have the same isotopic signature as Bennu and Ryugu," Schönbächler goes on to state.

Researchers also want to determine more precisely how Jupiter influenced the movement of dust and whether its gravitational effects were responsible for favoring the accumulation of particularly fine particles.

Additional asteroid samples could help scientists test the proposed formation scenario and establish whether Bennu's chemical signature is shared by other ancient bodies.

With the Bennu analysis now complete, Schönbächler is looking ahead to another opportunity to examine material from the early Solar System.

Japan's space agency, JAXA, is preparing a sample-return mission to Phobos, one of Mars' two moons. The mission is scheduled to launch at the end of October 2026, with the goal of collecting material from the Martian moon and bringing it back to Earth.

"It would be exciting to also obtain this material to analyse it in my laboratory," she says.

Schönbächler plans to apply to JAXA for access to the samples. However, the scientific community will need to be patient. The capsule carrying material from Phobos is not expected to return to Earth until 2031.

Those future samples could provide another important piece of the puzzle, helping researchers determine how different regions of the early Solar System exchanged material and ultimately produced the planets, moons, and asteroids we observe today.

What Did Denisovans Look Like? Scientists Have Their Best Answer Yet

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Ancient proteins are helping scientists put a face to a long-lost branch of humanity.

For years, we’ve mostly known this mysterious human lineage from fossil bits and pieces. Scientists have found fossilized bones, skull parts, they’ve even found traces of their genes in people alive today. But what did the Denisovans look like?

Now, a new study is finally bringing their appearance into focus. By analyzing ancient proteins preserved in fossils from China, researchers have identified more Denisovan remains and pieced together a remarkably detailed picture of their features including massive brow ridges, sloping foreheads, and broad, relatively flat faces.

The findings also suggest something surprising: several ancient human groups that scientists thought were distinct may have been Denisovans all along.

The Denisovan story began in a cave in Siberia. In 2010, scientists analyzed DNA recovered from a tiny finger bone and discovered that it belonged to a previously unknown group of ancient humans. They named them after Denisova Cave, where the bone was found.

Denisovans were close relatives of Neanderthals. They had also interbred with our species, leaving traces of their ancestry in people alive today.

Unlike Neanderthals, whose skeletons have been found at numerous sites, Denisovans were initially known from only a handful of fragments. Scientists could read their genetic history more easily than they could picture their faces.

That began to change in 2025, when researchers showed that a nearly complete skull from Harbin, northeastern China, belonged to a Denisovan. The fossil, nicknamed “Dragon Man” and previously described as Homo longi, offered a rare look at the shape of a Denisovan head.

The latest discovery adds another crucial piece.

At Xujiayao in northern China, archaeologists excavated 20 human fossil specimens between 1976 and 1979. The roughly 199,000–138,000-year-old remains included skull and jaw fragments whose place on the human family tree remained disputed.

The DNA didn’t survive. But the new team, led by researchers in China, turned to something else: proteins preserved inside bone.

They recovered 18 ancient proteins from two fossils. In both specimens, they found a distinctive version of a collagen protein previously identified in confirmed Denisovans, but not in Neanderthals or modern humans. A separate analysis of the protein sequences also grouped the Xujiayao fossils with Denisovans.

Taken together, the Xujiayao remains and the Harbin skull suggest a few traits. Denisovans had long, low skulls, heavy brow ridges and foreheads that sloped backward. Their faces were relatively broad and flat through the cheeks, with little forward projection around the mouth. Their nasal openings were broad, and their skull bones thick.

So, they weren’t just Neanderthals living farther east. They shared some features with Neanderthals but differed in the back of the skull and parts of the jaw. Some features also resembled the much older Homo erectus, while others looked surprisingly modern.

Of course, these facial reconstructions can never be perfect; there’s a lot of information you can’t derive from bones, DNA, and proteins. For example, we can only infer their skin tone, hairstyle or exact facial appearance. Any lifelike image necessarily involves artistic interpretation. But it’s the first time we have a more complete idea of what Denisovans looked like.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia, for Nature.

The bones now reveal far more than scientists could have inferred from the original Siberian finger bone. And they may explain another longstanding mystery.

For decades, researchers encountered ancient human fossils across China that seemed to fit nowhere neatly. Some were classified as early modern humans. Others were treated as Neanderthal relatives or members of separate species.

The Xujiayao fossils themselves were recently placed in a proposed species called Homo juluensis, partly because of their enormous estimated braincase capacity of about 1,700 cubic centimeters. Meanwhile, the Harbin skull had been assigned to Homo longi.

The new study argues that differences such as skull size may not justify dividing these fossils into separate evolutionary branches. After comparing the bones from these regions, the scientists suggest we’re simply dealing with a varied Denisovan lineage.

This is still far from a settled issue, and the conclusion is stronger for the specimens identified through proteins or DNA than for those linked only by resemblance. But this would explain why scientists just weren’t finding any Denisovan remains; they were, but they didn’t realize they’re Denisovans.

The accumulating discoveries also suggest that Denisovans were remarkably adaptable.

Fossils and molecular evidence now place them across a huge swath of Asia, from Siberia to the Tibetan Plateau, northeastern China and as far south as the Taiwan Strait. The authors suggest they may have occupied parts of East Asia over hundreds of thousands of years, across radically different environments.

They weren’t a small group confined to a Siberian cave. They were a widespread part of humanity’s history.

And their story didn’t end entirely with extinction. Ancient interbreeding left Denisovan DNA in modern humans, particularly among populations in Oceania and parts of Asia. One inherited Denisovan-related genetic variant even helps some Tibetans cope with oxygen-poor air at high altitudes.

We still can’t know exactly what Denisovans were like. But scientists are no longer working with a faceless genome.

A population once revealed by a tiny finger bone is gradually emerging as a group of real people, with distinctive faces, varied lives and a place in our own ancestry. Real people who are now a part of our own genetic heritage.

A 250-Million-Year-Old Habit May Explain How Crocodiles Outlasted the Dinosaurs

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

It was their preference for warmer climates.

Some 66 million years ago, an asteroid slammed into Earth, bringing the age of giants to a catastrophic end. T. rex, Triceratops, and all other non-avian dinosaurs vanished. Only one branch of the dinosaur family tree survived—birds.

But what about another great prehistoric lineage? How on Earth did crocodiles survive?

After all, the broader crocodile lineage had already existed for roughly 180 million years when the asteroid struck. Its evolutionary family tree included colossal predators that could attack dinosaurs, armored plant eaters, and strange creatures that looked nothing like the crocodiles we know today.

A new study offers a surprising explanation. Despite their extraordinary diversity, crocodiles and their ancient relatives spent roughly 250 million years following a remarkably consistent rule: follow the warmth.

That seemingly simple preference may have put some of them in exactly the right places when disaster struck.

Today’s crocodilians are variations on an exceptionally effective ambush-predator design. Their armored bodies lurk unseen beneath the waterline, while eyes and nostrils perched high on their skulls remain exposed. Powerful tails propel them forward, exploding into action with muscular jaws seizing unsuspecting prey.

It’s a successful design, but for most of their evolutionary history, crocodile relatives were anything but predictable.

More than 200 million years ago, phytosaurs patrolled Triassic rivers looking uncannily like modern crocodiles, complete with long snouts and armored bodies. Their nostrils, however, sat much farther back, near their eyes. Whether phytosaurs belonged to the crocodile evolutionary branch or were more distant relatives remains debated. Either way, they were an extraordinary example of evolution producing similar solutions to similar problems.

But the crocodile family tree was never limited to this aquatic ambush-predator blueprint.

Some crocodile relatives were powerful land hunters, evolving body plans that looked more like dinosaurs than modern crocodiles. Poposaurus stalked prey on two legs, with feet remarkably similar to those of bipedal dinosaurs. Meanwhile, Fasolasuchus was a colossal four-legged predator that may have reached 9 meters (30 feet) in length, potentially ranking among the largest land predators outside the dinosaurs.

Other branches took an entirely different evolutionary path and became herbivores. Simosuchus, a small crocodile relative from Madagascar, had a short, pug-like snout and unusual leaf-shearing teeth adapted for eating vegetation.

And in the Triassic, aetosaurs like Desmatosuchus roamed the landscape covered in bony armor, with enormous spikes protruding from their shoulders. They looked more like miniature tanks than crocodiles.

Metriorhynchus evolved into an ocean-going predator, complete with flippers and a powerful tail adapted for swimming. Meanwhile, enormous semiaquatic creatures such as Deinosuchus stalked Cretaceous waterways, with some estimates putting the largest individuals at around 10 to 12 meters long. These giants were capable of preying on dinosaurs that ventured too close to the water.

“They have occupied almost every conceivable ecological role,” Alessandro Chiarenza, a paleontologist at University College London and the study’s lead author, told Reuters.

Today, only a fraction of that diversity remains. Almost everything about crocodile relatives changed over the centuries, from what they ate to how they moved.

But something else barely changed at all.

To investigate, Chiarenza and his colleagues assembled an evolutionary tree containing more than 400 living and extinct species, drawing on over 7,000 fossil records and extensive modern observations.

They compared these records with 52 reconstructions of Earth’s ancient climates, stretching back 245 million years. Their habitat models focused on nonmarine crocodile-line animals, rather than every ocean-going offshoot.

The results revealed that despite evolving radically different bodies, diets, and lifestyles, crocodile-line animals largely retained their ancestral preference for warmth.

This wasn’t simply a matter of crocodiles living in the tropics. For much of their history, the tropics weren’t their only option.

Around 50 million years ago, when Earth was approximately 10° Celsius warmer than today, crocodile relatives inhabited high-latitude regions of North America, Europe, and even Antarctica.

As the planet cooled, suitable habitats shifted toward the equator, and crocodile populations largely followed. In other words, rather than evolving to withstand increasingly cold conditions, they generally moved to places that still offered the warmth they needed.

Their climatic preferences weren’t completely fixed. The researchers identified changes in temperature preferences around 94 million years ago and a shift toward drier conditions among certain Australian crocodile relatives around 34 million years ago.

But the broader pattern remained remarkably consistent. Rather than continually evolving new tolerances, crocodiles generally followed the climates they already preferred.

Scientists call this climatic niche conservatism: the tendency of a lineage to retain its inherited environmental preferences over evolutionary time.

It’s a strategy that sounds almost stubborn. But 66 million years ago, it became a lifeline.

The Chicxulub impact didn’t just kill animals in the immediate vicinity of the collision. It sent enormous quantities of dust and other particles into the atmosphere, reducing the sunlight reaching Earth’s surface.

Temperatures plummeted, photosynthesis was disrupted, and food webs began to collapse. For animals dependent on stable temperatures and abundant food, the consequences could be devastating.

With sunlight reduced by 5% or 10%, tropical and subtropical regions retained climates suitable for crocodile relatives. Only the most extreme scenario, involving a 15% reduction, eliminated suitable habitats globally. The researchers noted that this particular scenario is considered unlikely.

The results contrast with an earlier 2020 study involving several of the same researchers, which found that an asteroid-induced impact winter drastically reduced suitable habitats for non-avian dinosaurs. The new findings suggest that the same global catastrophe could have affected two ancient lineages very differently, partly because of where suitable habitats remained.

Living crocodilians are cold-blooded, requiring far less energy than comparably sized warm-blooded animals. They can endure long periods without food, and some enter dormant states when conditions deteriorate. These characteristics would have been especially useful after the impact, when the collapse of plant productivity disrupted food supplies.

Smaller body sizes, semiaquatic lifestyles, and flexible diets probably helped some surviving crocodile relatives, too.

The simulations don’t prove that tropical refuges saved crocodiles, nor do they explain why particular species survived while others disappeared. But they reveal how geography and inherited climatic preferences could have tipped the odds.

There’s also a sobering lesson for modern crocodilians. Their ancestors often survived changing climates by moving to places that remained suitable. But today’s crocodilians face a world increasingly fragmented by roads, farmland, cities, and other human barriers. The habitats they need aren’t always connected, and moving to a better location may no longer be so simple.

Crocodile relatives spent 250 million years experimenting with extraordinary ways to live, becoming giants, herbivores, land hunters, and marine predators. Yet one of the traits that may have helped their lineage endure was something they changed surprisingly little: their preference for a warm place to live.

Bat mothers’ hearts beat faster when they hear their pups

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:00 AM EDT

A single bat colony contains hundreds or even thousands of the winged mammals. But with less-than-ideal eyesight and so many neighbors, how do mothers identify their pups amid the extremely crowded conditions? Writing in a new study published today in the journal Frontiers in Ethology, researchers say it often comes down to the subtle, unique vocalizations emitted by their individual offspring. To assess their responses, the team relied on an even more delicate biological signal—the mother bat’s own heartbeat.

The investigation began when biologists at Japan’s Doshisha University noticed a surprising—but consistent—behavior among the Japanese house bats (Alionoctula abramus) in their laboratory colony. Whenever a pup wandered too far from its roosting area, only its mother raced to the rescue.

“We knew that pups emit isolation calls—acoustic distress signals—when separated. But seeing this strict behavioral selectivity made us wonder: what is happening inside the mother when she hears these calls?” recalled study co-author and Doshisha University biologist Kazuki Yoshino-Hashizawa.

To learn more, Yoshino-Hashizawa and his colleagues monitored four mother bats who each recently birthed a litter of three pups. Some of the litters only contained females, so they decided to exclude the young males from the study to avoid sex-based variations. At two days old, the team recorded clips of the female pups’ tiny isolation calls. These function like the cries from a human baby, and each vocalization has its own unique tones.

However, Japanese house bats grow quickly. The new generation started making echolocation calls at about eight days old, and they ceased isolation calls after around 12 days. After roughly one month, they began flying like adults and set off on their own within the colony. The researchers then recorded their adult echolocation calls, as well.

Around 40 days after the pups’ birth, the team isolated each mother and played them the various audio clips while monitoring their heart rates using an ECG machine. As a control, they also played the recording of a random adult male bat. The study’s authors quickly saw that a mother bat’s heart rate sped up when they heard cries of their own pups, but not the pups that belong to other bat moms. Surprisingly, the mother bats’ heart rates boosted the most from recordings of the older, independent pups. Researchers theorize this might relate to the bats’ memory of more recent calls, but that idea requires more study to confirm.

Still, don’t get overly sentimental. The team also recorded strong heart rate reactions when the mothers heard calls from the random adult male. It’s possible the bats interpreted the sounds as a threat, but the team stopped short of categorizing the reactions as good or bad.

“‘[Heart rate measurement] cannot distinguish between positive emotions, such as maternal motivation, and negative ones, such as stress or alarm,” Yoshino-Hashizawa added.

Instead, the study authors suggest heart rates may be a solid starting point when trying to analyze bat behaviors and responses to their everyday environment. At the same time, a mother bat’s heart certainly seems to patter a bit more for her pups.

“[This] suggests that these vocalizations instantly physiologically prime the mother to engage in maternal behavior,” explained Yoshino-Hashizawa.

Astronomers Found Something They Thought Couldn’t Exist: A Green Star

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Astronomers had never before convincingly identified a green star.

The universe is full of red stars, blue stars, and white stars. But green? Despite billions upon billions of stars scattered across the cosmos, astronomers had never convincingly identified a green star.

Now, they’ve found one. And the explanation is stranger than you might expect.

Stars come in different colours because they have different surface temperatures. Relatively cool stars glow reddish-orange. Hotter stars appear white or bluish-white, with the hottest giving off a distinctly blue tint.

You might reasonably expect stars with intermediate temperatures to appear green.

In fact, some stars emit their greatest amount of visible light at green wavelengths. Our own Sun is a good example. Its spectrum peaks around the green part of the visible spectrum, depending on how the light is measured.

But nobody looking at the Sun from space would really describe it as green. It’s essentially white. The explanation lies in the difference between emitting green light and looking green.

Stars don’t emit just one colour. They produce a continuous spectrum of light containing many wavelengths, much like the colours of a rainbow. The proportions change with temperature, but the different colours are always mixed together.

When a star’s spectrum peaks in green, it still produces plenty of red and blue light. Our eyes combine those wavelengths and perceive the result as white. This is why you can find stars that look reddish, orange, white, or bluish, but not the vivid green of a traffic light or a laser pointer.

Astronomers describe this behaviour using something called blackbody radiation. It’s a mathematical model that predicts how an object radiates light based on its temperature

But it turns out that one unusual class of dead stars can break the pattern.

WD J1330+6435 is a white dwarf, the dense stellar remnant left behind after a star exhausts its nuclear fuel and sheds its outer layers. Our own Sun will also eventually become one.

White dwarfs are extraordinary objects. They typically pack a mass comparable to the Sun’s into a sphere roughly the size of Earth. Their gravity is immense, and although they no longer sustain the nuclear fusion that powers ordinary stars, they remain extremely hot for billions of years as they slowly cool.

WD J1330+6435 has an estimated surface temperature of around 4,240 Kelvin, or about 3,970°C. That would ordinarily make it look reddish-orange.

But it also has an atmosphere; and that atmosphere is really unusual.

Astronomers had already studied the star because its spectrum contained unusual amounts of lithium, sodium, and calcium. These elements are clues that the white dwarf has accumulated rocky material from bodies that once orbited it. That material could have come from shattered asteroids or other remnants of its former planetary system.

The new research suggests that this chemical pollution is also responsible for its green appearance.

Basically, the star looks green because its atmosphere acts a bit like a filter.

White dwarfs can have extremely dense atmospheres. In some of the coolest ones, the gas is packed so tightly that atoms constantly bump into each other.

This changes the way the star’s atmosphere absorbs light. In WD J1330+6435, sodium absorbs a broad swath of yellow and orange light, while calcium absorbs some light at the violet end of the spectrum.

With those colours partly removed, more of the remaining light falls in the green range. As a result, the star would appear a pale yellowish-green, often described as chartreuse. Chartreuse is also a French herbal liqueur that has been made by Carthusian monks since the 18th century.

“The traces of sodium in the atmosphere of this white dwarf absorb the orange part of the spectrum, leaving behind a ghostly green glow,” explained lead author Mark Hollands of the University of Warwick in a university statement.

No astronomer has directly photographed WD J1330+6435 as a resolved green disc. The object is extremely faint and far too distant for us to see its surface directly.

Researchers have only reconstructed what the star would look like if a human observer could approach it closely enough. But they’re still pretty confident it’s green. I mean, chartreuse.

They started with stellar spectra, measurements that reveal how much light an object emits at different wavelengths. A spectrum is essentially a detailed breakdown of starlight. By examining which wavelengths are missing or diminished, astronomers can identify chemical elements in a star’s atmosphere and investigate the conditions there.

The researchers used atmospheric models to reproduce these observations. They then passed the resulting spectra through mathematical models of human colour vision, calculating how the light would appear to our eyes.

They even generated realistic-looking images of the stars, accounting for the way brightness and colour change toward the edges of a stellar disc.

There are uncertainties. The star’s measured spectrum is somewhat noisy, so its exact shade could differ from the model. However, the team is confident that the star really would have a green appearance.

And WD J1330+6435 isn’t entirely alone. The researchers identified two other, much paler greenish white dwarfs. One of them, WD J0851+1543, has a higher-quality spectrum that supports its unusual colour.

The researchers also identified white dwarfs that would appear pink, purple, yellow, or cyan, their unusual colours shaped by the chemistry of their atmospheres. Such stars are rare: just five of the 152 nearby white dwarfs studied had markedly unusual colours.

These exceptions reveal something remarkable. A star’s colour can tell us not just how hot it is, but also what it’s made of and, sometimes, what happened to its planets. In the case of WD J1330+6435, that strange greenish glow may carry the chemical traces of worlds destroyed long ago.

For decades, astronomers had good reasons to believe green stars couldn’t exist. It turns out the universe found a loophole.

The study, “Any colour you like: The diverse chromaticities of white dwarfs,” was published on 15 October 2026 in RAS Techniques and Instruments.

Flying Can Use Less Energy Than Swimming, and these Birds Have Been Doing It All Along

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists thought swimming was the most energy-efficient way for animals to travel.

An Andean condor and a trumpeter swan weigh roughly the same: about 11 kilograms. Both can fly, and both have to overcome gravity to stay in the air. So you might be tempted to think that they use up comparable amounts of energy.

The swan spends around 99% of its flight time flapping its wings. The condor, just 0.1%. And according to new research, the condor uses 79% less energy to cover the same distance.

While the swan relies primarily on its muscles, the condor has learned to harness an enormous, invisible source of energy: the atmosphere itself. In fact, it’s so efficient, that under the right conditions, its flight can be more energy-efficient than swimming.

Swimming is generally the “cheapest” way to travel from an energy standpoint. It makes a lot of sense; water supports an animal’s weight, allowing fish and other swimmers to move without constantly fighting gravity.

Flying comes next. Sure, you can “ride the wind”, but you need to generate lift to stay airborne, which can require considerable energy. Running is typically the most expensive. Land animals have to support their own weight while repeatedly accelerating their limbs and bodies.

Scientists describe this relationship using a quantity called the cost of transport: the energy required to move a given distance, often calculated relative to body mass. Think of it as the animal equivalent of fuel economy.

Now, an international team of 68 scientists challenges the classic thought on the cost of transport. Much of our understanding, they say, comes from experiments conducted in laboratories, where fish swim in tanks, birds fly in wind tunnels, and mammals run on treadmills. Those controlled environments make it easier to measure energy expenditure, but nature doesn’t really work like that.

“To us, the sky might just look like background for flying animals, but it is an essential resource that can dramatically change the cost of a journey,” says first author Dr. Martina Scacco.

If you’ve ever watched an eagle or vulture circle around the sky, you’ve probably seen it: they can remain airborne for long periods of time while barely flapping their wings.

These birds are masters of exploiting physics.

When the Sun heats the ground, the ground warms the air above it. In some places, pockets or columns of warmer air rise through the atmosphere. These are called thermals.

A soaring bird can enter one of these rising columns and circle upward, gaining altitude without having to generate all that lift through muscular effort. Then, once they’re high up, it can glide toward its destination, gradually losing altitude until it finds another thermal.

It’s kind of like taking an elevator to the top of a building and then gliding down toward the next one.

This “free” energy comes from the Sun, which heats the landscape and drives atmospheric motion. But from the bird’s perspective, it’s an external energy subsidy.

So far, nothing new. But that’s where the new study comes in.

To find out how much energy birds actually spend travelling, the researchers assembled an unusually large dataset.

They combined 17 years of GPS and accelerometer records from approximately 850 individual birds and bats belonging to 38 species. They also analysed four years of radar observations of smaller flying birds, divided into 12 size categories.

Altogether, the analysis covered nearly 200,000 GPS-based flight segments and thousands of additional radar observations. The animals ranged from tiny birds weighing little more than 12 grams to enormous soaring birds weighing over 11 kilograms.

GPS tracking showed where and how fast animals travelled. Accelerometers, which measure movement, helped researchers distinguish between energetically expensive wingbeats and more economical gliding or soaring.

The team then matched the animals’ movements to information about wind and rising air currents, allowing them to estimate the energetic cost of travelling through real atmospheric conditions.

Across seven species of vultures, the researchers analysed flights representing more than 4.3 million kilometres of travel. That’s enough to circle the Earth more than 100 times. Collectively, the birds accumulated approximately 128,000 days of flight.

Yet depending on the species, they spent only 0.1% to 7% of that time flapping their wings. The Andean condor, the heaviest vulture in the comparison, flapped least of all.

Granted, these birds are exceptional flyers to begin with; but the secret lies in how they use the energy that was already present in their surroundings.

And when the researchers translated their movements into estimated energy costs, some birds proved more economical travellers than swimming animals.

The study uncovered another seemingly surprising pattern. In general, larger species tended to spend less time flapping and were more efficient flyers.

That sounds counterintuitive. Surely a heavier bird needs more energy to stay airborne?

Well, in absolute terms, yes, larger animals generally require more energy. But the question here is how much energy they spend moving a given distance relative to their mass.

Large soaring birds have evolved wings and flight behaviours that let them extract considerable benefit from rising air. Under favourable conditions, they can travel enormous distances with relatively little muscular effort.

But there’s a catch: larger birds are more dependent on finding suitable atmospheric conditions.

Smaller birds are more flexible, they don’t need to rely on thermals as much, but they spend more time flapping their wings and use up more energy as a result.

Perhaps the most surprising finding is that some soaring birds travel more efficiently than swimming animals, overturning the traditional ranking of animal locomotion.

But the findings could also reshape our understanding of migration. A longer route with favourable winds and thermals might require less energy than a shorter journey through difficult conditions.

That’s particularly relevant as climate change alters atmospheric conditions. Changes in wind and rising air currents could affect how much energy birds need to migrate, and potentially where they can travel.

For now, these energy costs are modelled estimates rather than direct metabolic measurements. But the message is clear: the cost of getting somewhere depends not just on the animal, but also on the environment carrying it; and we need more complex studies to truly map that out.

Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Birds living in cities appear to react differently to men and women, and scientists are struggling to explain why. An international research team discovered that urban birds, including great tits, house sparrows, and blackbirds, tend to fly away sooner when approached by women than by men. The surprising behavior raises new questions about how birds perceive the people around them.

The study took place in five European countries, where male and female participants (matched for height and clothing) walked directly toward birds in city parks and other green spaces.

On average, men were able to approach about one meter closer than women before the birds flew away. The researchers measured this response by recording how close a person could get before a bird took flight (termed flight initiation distance).

Published in the British Ecological Society journal People and Nature, the findings showed the same pattern in Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain. The difference also appeared across all 37 bird species examined, including easily startled magpies and pigeons that typically allow people to approach much more closely.

The researchers believe their findings indicate that city birds can distinguish between male and female humans. However, they have yet to determine which characteristics the birds recognize or why they appear more cautious around women.

Professor Daniel Blumstein of the University of California, Los Angeles, who helped conduct the research, said the results were convincing despite the lack of an explanation.

"I fully believe our results, that urban birds react differently based on the sex of the person approaching them, but I can't explain them right now. We used bleeding-edge comparative analysis techniques that showed our findings were consistent across cities and species, but we simply don't have a conclusive explanation yet."

Dr. Yanina Benedetti of the Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, another member of the research team, said the discovery challenged her expectations about how birds respond to human observers.

"As a woman in the field, I was surprised that birds reacted to us differently. This study highlights how animals in cities 'see' humans, which has implications for urban ecology and equality in science. Many behavioral studies assume that a human observer is neutral, but this wasn't the case for urban birds in our study."

Scientists have proposed several possible explanations for the difference. Birds might be responding to chemical signals such as pheromones, variations in body shape, or differences in how men and women walk. For now, however, these ideas remain speculative, and additional experiments will be needed to identify the actual cause.

Dr. Federico Morelli of the University of Turin, a co-author of the study, said the unanswered question is one of the discovery's most fascinating aspects.

"This is maybe the most interesting part of our study," said Dr. Federico Morelli from the University of Turin, a co-author of the study. "We have identified a phenomenon, but we really don't know why. However, what our results do highlight is the birds' sophisticated ability to evaluate their environment."

The findings suggest that urban birds may be sensitive to human characteristics that people themselves rarely notice. Identifying those signals could help researchers better understand how wildlife assesses potential threats in environments shared with humans.

Dr. Benedetti said: "Urban birds clearly react to subtle cues that humans do not easily notice. Follow-up studies could focus on individual factors such as movement patterns, scent cues, or physical traits, testing them separately rather than grouping them under observer sex. This approach would help identify the specific cues birds detect."

More Research Is Needed to Explain the Bird Behavior

Although the investigation included 2,701 observations, the researchers emphasize that their findings are still preliminary. Further studies will be necessary to establish whether the difference between birds' responses to men and women consistently appears in other settings and populations.

For now, the discovery points to an intriguing possibility: Birds sharing our cities may be paying attention to subtle differences between people that scientists have yet to fully understand.

What night is van Gogh’s ‘The Starry Night’? New analysis says it’s in July.

Published Oct 22, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

The swirling bluish-white mistrals, towering cypress trees, and glittery stars in Vincent van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” (1889) have captivated our imaginations for generations. Despite never selling a painting during his lifetime, van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” has since inspired a hit song in 1972, draws visitors to the Museum of Modern Art in New York City, and adorns everything from journals to scarves and tote bags.

But what particular night above Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France, did the troubled post-impressionist painter actually put on the canvas? A new analysis points to July 22, 1889. During the early morning hours at that time of year, a waning crescent moon, the planet Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in Taurus appeared close together—all of which are seen in the painting. A study published today in the journal Scientific Reports details the findings.

According to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, van Gogh was inspired by the view from his window at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in southern France. He spent twelve months there 1889–1890, seeking reprieve from his mental illnesses and was also diagnosed with a form of epilepsy.

Like many of his paintings that year, “The Starry Night” was created in several sessions during the day, under different weather conditions. The village nestled below the hills was also based on other views, and could not be seen from his window. Although van Gogh reconstructed some of the features in the night sky as he saw them, he also altered the celestial shapes and added a sense of glow.

“Van Gogh assigned an emotional language to night and nature that took them far from their actual appearances,” the museum writes. “Dominated by vivid blues and yellows applied with gestural verve and immediacy, ‘The Starry Night’ also demonstrates how inseparable van Gogh’s vision was from the new procedures of painting he had devised, in which color and paint describe a world outside the artwork even as they telegraph their own status as, merely, color and paint.”

Using astronomy to pinpoint which day inspired the painting began with UCLA art historian Albert Boime in 1984 and Harvard University astronomer and astrophysicist Charles Whitney in 1986. The two scholars reached conflicting conclusions, and neither of them displaced the prevailing religious and symbolic interpretations of the painting.

Historically, the painting has been dated based on a letter van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo around June 18, 1889. However, the waning gibbous moon from mid-June does not account for the slender crescent seen in the painting. By July 22, 1889, the Hyades star cluster rose in the eastern sky alongside Venus, and the moon’s shape looked closer to the crescent on the canvas. This new study uses observational geometry to find an answer.

“To an observational astronomer, the cluster of stars in ‘The Starry Night’ is the Hyades in Taurus,” said study co-author Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim.  “But the moment I read the earlier studies explaining it as Aries, I heard the painting calling to me.” Kim is the CEO at South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a PhD candidate at Kyung Hee University.

For this research, the team combined astronomical simulations and measurements of star positions, with historical weather records, and van Gogh’s own letters. They also used earlier sketches of the landscape to reconstruct the narrow field of view represented in “The Starry Night.”

According to the team, the crescent moon, Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in the constellation Taurus would have been visible around dawn on July 22, 1889. This configuration generally corresponds to what is portrayed in the painting. Weather records suggest that the night sky around July 22 would have been clear enough for this astronomical arrangement to be visible. Additionally, the frequent mentions of cicadas in van Gogh’s letters suggest that the region was seeing a period of hot weather, which matches the recorded temperatures in July 1889 better than in June.

The team also verified that the painter’s field of view in another painting titled “Starry Night over the Rhône” (1888) with photographs taken on site was painted when the sky was in Aries. Using the same method, the team also believes that the stars in “Café Terrace at Night” (1888) may be the Summer Triangle.

“I hope this work prompts a renewed, multidisciplinary look at the sequence and working methods of the Saint-Rémy paintings, and at Van Gogh’s movements that summer,” Kim said. “Our conversation with Van Gogh’s other skies is still ongoing. We plan to tell those stories one by one.”

Scientists discover a surprising secret behind the human brain’s wrinkles

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Most primates have relatively large brains covered in distinctive folds and grooves. However, common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) are an unusual exception. These small monkeys have brains that are almost entirely smooth, with very little folding. Scientists at the German Primate Center (DPZ), the Leibniz Institute for Primate Research in Göttingen, have now identified several cellular processes that help explain this difference.

Using miniature brain structures known as organoids, the researchers discovered that certain neural progenitor cells, which produce neurons, develop differently in marmosets than in humans. These differences involve the cells' structure, the speed at which they divide, and the timing of their development. Together, these changes reduce the number of neurons produced, contributing to the smaller size and smoother surface of the marmoset brain. Published in Science Advances, the findings provide new insights into primate brain evolution and could eventually improve our understanding of human brain development disorders.

Why Some Primate Brains Are Smooth Instead of Folded

Brain size and the extent of surface folding are important features that distinguish different primate species. The human brain, for example, has a highly folded surface resembling the ridges and grooves of a walnut. This arrangement greatly expands the brain's surface area, creating room for billions of neurons that support complex thinking and other advanced mental abilities.

Scientists believe that the common ancestor of modern primates already possessed a moderately sized brain with at least some folding. Yet not all primates living today retain these characteristics.

Common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), small monkeys native to South America that are becoming increasingly important in biomedical research, have brains with almost no visible folds. Researchers at the German Primate Center set out to determine why their brains develop so differently from those of humans and other primates.

"We wanted to understand which changes at the cellular level cause the marmoset brain to grow less and form fewer folds," explains Lidiia Tynianskaia, one of the study's two first authors and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution at the DPZ. "At the beginning of development, the common marmoset brain exhibits the typical structure and composition of a large, folded primate brain. As development progresses, processes must therefore occur that effectively slow down the production of nerve cells."

Scientists Discover What Slows Brain Growth

To investigate these developmental differences, the team compared brain organoids grown from human and common marmoset cells. Their attention centered on neural progenitor cells, the cells responsible for generating neurons during brain development.

These progenitor cells play a major role in determining the eventual size and shape of the brain. The more neurons they produce, the greater the potential for the cerebral cortex to expand and develop folds.

The researchers found that the marmoset's smaller, smoother cerebral cortex is not the result of a single developmental change. Instead, several differences involving distinct types of progenitor cells work together to limit neuron production and brain folding.

"Our investigations have shown that certain progenitor cells in the common marmoset divide significantly more slowly than in humans," explains César Mateo Bastidas Betancourt, also a first author of the study and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group. "Other progenitor cells have a simpler structure than their human counterparts, with fewer processes, and are therefore less proliferative. Both of these factors ultimately result in fewer nerve cells, which contributes to a smaller size and less folding of the cerebral cortex in marmosets."

The scientists also identified important differences in when certain progenitor cell characteristics and behaviors emerge during development. Compared with humans, marmosets have a shorter overall period during which these cells can multiply rapidly.

Together, the slower rate of cell division, simpler cell structures, and shorter window for rapid growth help explain why the marmoset brain produces fewer neurons and develops far fewer folds.

Miniature Brains Reveal Important Developmental Clues

Much of the research relied on brain organoids, small, three-dimensional structures grown from cells that reproduce important features of early brain development. These miniature models allowed scientists to investigate developmental changes across multiple samples and compare the timing of key processes.

"The study combines the advantages of in vivo and in vitro methods," says Michael Heide, head of the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution. "Organoids are well-suited for obtaining statistically robust results because such sample sizes are not feasible in primates. We subsequently repeated some key experiments in fetal brain tissue to confirm the results from the organoids."

One particularly important finding involved the relationship between development in organoids and development in actual marmoset brains. The researchers determined that a marmoset brain organoid grown for 50 days closely matches the developmental stage of a natural marmoset brain at day 90.

Establishing this relationship was essential because brain organoids and actual brains do not necessarily develop at the same rate. By identifying corresponding developmental stages, the scientists could make meaningful comparisons and interpret their experimental findings more reliably.

What Smooth Monkey Brains Reveal About Human Brain Evolution

The findings help explain how relatively small differences in the behavior and developmental timing of brain-building cells can contribute to major differences in brain size and folding among primates.

They also offer a potential explanation for how the wide variety of primate brain shapes and sizes emerged over evolutionary time, including why some species developed relatively smooth brains while others possess much larger, heavily folded ones.

Beyond their evolutionary significance, these discoveries provide a foundation for investigating how the human brain develops and what happens when those processes are disrupted. A better understanding of the cellular mechanisms that control neuron production and brain growth could ultimately help researchers study developmental disorders affecting the human brain.

The World Is Set to Blow Past 1.5 Degrees Celsius of Warming and the Consequences Could Be Devastating

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Under the most optimistic scenario, global warming would rise 1.7 to 1.8 degrees above pre-industrial levels, despite renewables expanding considerably over the last decade.

This article originally appeared on Inside Climate News, a nonprofit, non-partisan news organization that covers climate, energy and the environment. Sign up for their newsletter here.

A new report from the United Nations Environment Programme suggests that limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels is no longer a realistic outcome, even under the most ambitious emissions-reduction scenarios.

The report’s lowest-warming scenario, which would require steep cuts in greenhouse gas emissions, would only limit peak global warming to 1.7 to 1.8 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels.

The 2015 Paris Agreement, adopted by almost every country in the world, set a goal of keeping global warming below 2 degrees Celsius (3.6 degrees Fahrenheit) while pursuing efforts to limit the increase to 1.5 degrees. The United States has withdrawn from the agreement twice under President Donald Trump, most recently last year.

The report’s projections do not mean the world should abandon the 1.5 degree target, said Inger Andersen, UNEP’s executive director, at a press briefing.

“Every additional fraction of a degree matters, and every additional year of warming above 1.5 will increase climate impacts such as deadly floods, heatwaves, wildfires that have plagued nations this year,” Andersen said.

Robert Kopp, a climate scientist and professor at Rutgers University in New Jersey, said the report’s findings were not surprising. Even in 2015, he said, the 1.5 degree target was at the edge of what was considered feasible.

Reaching the report’s lowest warming scenario would require emissions to fall by 18 percent by 2030, 45 percent by 2035 and 69 percent by 2040, relative to 2025 levels. It would also require removing substantial amounts of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, an effort that would likely involve not only reforestation, but also the rapid development of carbon-capture technologies that remain in the early stages of development.

Meanwhile, climate impacts are mounting, putting pressure on efforts to adapt to global warming. Western Europe saw thousands of heat-related deaths last spring and summer, a period which encompassed four of the region’s 10 most severe heat waves since 1950.

Still, the report highlights the expansion of renewable energy as an area of progress. Despite what Jan Minx, head of the Evidence for Climate Solutions working group at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, described as “bleak” predictions for global warming, the growth of renewable energy capacity, particularly wind and solar, has outpaced expectations. Minx co-authored a chapter in the report.

Global renewable electricity capacity expanded three times faster than was anticipated in 2015, according to the report, while solar photovoltaic capacity grew seven times faster.

Setu Pelz, a research scholar at the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis who co-authored a chapter in the report, said countries must sustain that momentum to triple renewable capacity by 2030, a priority in keeping with Paris Agreement targets.

Global emissions in 2025 were comparable to those a year prior—a “welcome divergence from a growth trend of past decades,” Anne Olhoff, the director of the UNEP Copenhagen Climate Centre and the report’s lead author, said at a press briefing.

But the expansion of clean energy has yet to drive down fossil fuel emissions. According to the report, emissions from the combustion of oil, gas and coal continue to rise.

“We provide some evidence that can inform the discussions about 2030, 2035 and 2040 targets in these sectors, so that we can see the same level of ambition in the fossil fuel transition as we have seen in the real renewable capacity scale-up,” said Pelz.

Released annually, UNEP’s emissions gap report is part of a growing body of research warning that current climate policies fall short of what is needed to meet international temperature targets. Under existing policies, global warming could reach 2.8 degrees by 2100—far above the Paris Agreement’s 2-degree threshold.

When asked what a world that is more than 2 degrees Celsius warmer than pre-industrial levels might look like, Andersen referenced recent extreme weather events.“Think of the heat wave … in much of Europe last year, think of the wildfires that we are seeing in Scandinavia as an example, think of droughts and harvest failures,” she said.

“Think of that happening much more frequently.”

What Is Driving the Global Resurgence of Mangrove Forests?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Mangroves on the island of Mauritius in the Indian Ocean. Adam Burton

After decades of losses, mangrove trees are gaining ground on coastlines around the world, often in places they have never grown before. Paradoxically, scientists say, warming temperatures and the destruction of inland forests are helping to power the revival.

From the bayous of Louisiana to the backwaters of the Irrawaddy in Myanmar, from the lagoons of West Africa to the billabongs of northern Australia, mangroves are making a dramatic and unexpected comeback.

What a difference a couple of decades can make.

In 2007, ecologists from six countries asked in a letter published in the journal Science whether we would soon be living in “a world without mangroves.” One of the world’s most distinctive, biodiverse, and carbon-rich ecosystems was in rapid retreat in the face of encroaching agriculture, construction of sea walls, ports, and marinas, and rising sea levels. The mangroves seemed doomed.

But in recent months two separate studies have painted a very different picture. A high-resolution analysis of satellite imagery published in Science in June found that the stunted, salt-tolerant trees are now undergoing an “unexpected expansion,” healing after decades of destruction.

At the same time, a report on the State of the World’s Mangroves, published in September by a group of NGOs under the umbrella of the Global Mangrove Alliance, found that “mangroves are now increasing in extent, year on year,” said coauthor Mark Spalding of The Nature Conservancy.

It’s not that the destruction of mangroves has ended. But the Science study finds that new mangrove growth between 2010 and 2023 covered approximately 3,200 square miles, outpacing losses of around 2,400 square miles. Worldwide, mangrove cover is now back to the levels last seen in the 1980s.

The new findings are the surprising outcome of the most detailed analysis yet attempted of satellite imagery of mangroves over the past four decades, says Pete Bunting, a remote sensing analyst at Aberystwyth University in Wales, who worked on both studies. It shows that previous assessments had provided “a substantial underestimation of mangrove gains” over the past 15 years, says the Science study’s lead author Zhen Zhang of Tulane University in Louisiana.

“For the first time, these unique ecosystems are expanding at a faster rate than they are declining,” says Catherine Lovelock, a coastal ecologist at the University of Queensland, who was not involved in either study.

However, not all ecologists are celebrating.

For one thing, mangroves are recovering in some places thanks to environmental degradation elsewhere. For another, says Norman Duke, an ecologist at James Cook University in Queensland, Australia, and lead author of the 2007 letter, mangrove quantity should not be confused with quality. Lost old-growth mangrove forests, which harbor the most wildlife, are being replaced by less biodiverse and resilient new stands, he says. Contrary to the new studies, “the world’s mangroves are not bouncing back.”

A coral reef around the roots of mangrove trees in Raja Ampat, Indonesia. Wendy Mitchell / Mangrove Photography Awards 2026

Mangroves are unique trees, evolved with specialized roots that anchor in shallow, intertidal waters with slow currents, no frost, and plenty of sediment. Mangrove forests are critical biodiversity hotspots, supporting everything from barnacles to tigers, and are vital fish nurseries for the oceans. For coastal communities, they yield rich harvests of crabs and shellfish and form a protective barrier, absorbing winds and tidal surges during storms, and even moderating the energy of tsunamis. They also store large amounts of carbon — typically four times more than nearby tropical rainforests.

Zhang has recorded the biggest revivals in the river estuaries of northern Australia and river deltas in India. In each case, the new growth extends across more than 450 square miles. But the most surprising turnaround has been in Southeast Asian countries, which were once the scene of rapid mangrove destruction. Since 2010, they have accounted for more than 40 percent of global gains.

Myanmar is “still the most severely deforested country among major mangrove-holding nations,” says Zhang. But since 2010, its mangroves have grown in area by 10 percent. Indonesia tells a similar story. After major losses in past decades, due primarily to a boom in shrimp and fish aquaculture, mangroves have been stable since 2005, when the boom began to abate. The country remains home to around a quarter of the world’s mangroves.

So, what is going on? Why the dramatic upturn for a global ecosystem once considered all but doomed by human invasion and rising seas? The new remote-sensing analysis, despite its greater resolution, allows only tentative conclusions. “We don’t fully understand what is driving these gains,” says Bunting. His mapping cannot distinguish planted trees from natural recovery, for instance. But there is growing evidence that natural regeneration is powering the resurgence.

Sometimes, mangroves are recolonizing areas previously taken by humans but since abandoned. During the late 20th century, coastal communities across the tropics ripped out millions of acres of mangroves in East and Southeast Asia alone to make way for ponds where they raised fish and shrimp for lucrative international markets. But many of these ponds soon became unproductive through disease, pollution, or poor management, and were abandoned.

But such recolonization is only part of the story. “Some 65 percent of mangrove gains are in new places where mangroves have not grown before,” the State of the World’s Mangroves report finds. Sometimes climate change is helping extend the range of mangroves polewards. On the Mississippi Delta, they are overtaking salt marshes as warmer winters eliminate mangrove-killing frosts, restricting habitat for birds and foraging mammals that need open ground.

False-color satellite images show the growth of mangroves at the mouth of the Matamuhuri River in Bangladesh between 1990 and 2023. Source: Landsat imagery. Courtesy of Pete Bunting

The majority of the gains, however, “seem to occur in river mouths and deltas and are being driven by sediment coming off the land,” says Bunting. On reaching the ocean, the sediment settles, creating mudbanks that mangroves swiftly colonize. The unsettling paradox is that, in many cases, mangroves along coastlines are returning thanks to the destruction of their forest counterparts inland. The loss of trees and their roots destabilizes soils, causing erosion and the flow of sediment into rivers.

That is what has happened along the Yangon estuary in southern Myanmar. Part of the giant Irrawaddy Delta, the estuary stretches some 25 miles from the ocean to the country’s biggest city, Yangon. The estuary lost more than half its mangroves between 1988 and 2011 as rice farmers moved onto its shores and replaced mangroves with rice fields, erecting dikes to protect them from the salty water. But the mangroves have since made a dramatic comeback. By 2023, their coverage had increased almost tenfold to more than 13,000 acres, according to Phyu Phway Thant of the Myanmar Maritime University in Thanlyin, who has mapped the growth from satellite imagery.

The rice fields have not gone. Instead, almost all the growth was mangroves colonizing new mudbanks created from sediment dislodged by upstream sand mining and deforestation, which was driven by illegal logging and the expansion of agriculture along tributaries of the Yangon River.

This is not unusual. The growth of mudbanks in many estuaries has coincided with an increase in sediment brought down by rivers to coastal waters over the last 20 years, says the State of the World’s Mangroves report. Other examples include parts of the giant Amazon Delta in Brazil, the shores of peninsular Malaysia, and the North Island of New Zealand, which is at the southernmost limit of potential mangrove habitat.

“Rising sea levels should in theory restrict mangrove growth, as seas invade the intertidal areas where they grow,” says Lovelock, but at the mouths of many rivers, sediment deposition is creating land faster than the seas are engulfing it, creating new habitats that mangroves swiftly colonize. And once established, the dense networks of mangrove roots trap more sediment — enough, the State of the World’s Mangroves report concludes, to keep pace with current rising tides in many places.

Of course, this positive feedback loop can be confounded by upstream dams, which drastically reduce the amount of sediment being carried toward the ocean, stopping the growth of intertidal mud flats, and causing deltas to be drowned.

Shrimp farms in Prachuap Khiri Khan province in Thailand have displaced mangrove forests. Ingram Publishing

For this reason, there are serious concerns among ecologists about the fate of East Africa’s largest expanse of mangroves, on the delta of the River Rufiji in Tanzania. In recent years, new mangrove stands have grown on mudflats in the outer delta, says Bunting. But that growth could now abruptly halt. In August, the Tanzanian government completed the Julius Nyerere hydroelectric dam some 100 miles upstream. The environmental group WWF has estimated that the dam will trap most of the 16.6 million tons of sediment flowing down the river each year — initiating a cycle of delta retreat and mangrove loss.

The growth of new mangrove stands has been accompanied by a growing awareness among some governments and coastal communities of their value. This is especially true in Indonesia and other countries bordering the Indian Ocean that were hit by a tsunami in 2004 that killed more than 200,000 people. Villages that had kept their mangroves were much better protected than those that had converted them to fishponds.

A resulting fashion for planting new mangroves has had mixed results, however. Tens of millions of dollars have been spent on planting mangroves across the tropics in recent years, according to the NGO Wetlands International. But “the majority of these restoration projects have failed,” it said in a recent report. More than 80 percent of seedlings have died or been washed away by the tides.

In response, some conservation groups have begun helping communities manage coastal landscapes to encourage natural regeneration. On the north shore of Java, villagers have erected some 10 miles of near-shore brushwood barriers to create calm water where seedlings cast off by mangroves have become established.

And in the West African state of Guinea-Bissau, Wetlands International is recruiting locals to encourage the natural return of mangroves to abandoned rice fields. “We open up dikes and clean the old natural canals” to allow water carrying mangrove seedlings from nearby stands to return, says Abdoulaye Ndiaye, who runs the program. Nature does the rest. More than 6,000 acres have been restored since 2015.

Meanwhile, the greener landscapes seen in satellite images do not necessarily indicate that biodiversity has recovered, says Uday Pimple of King Mongkut’s University of Technology in Bangkok, who has been doing extensive field research in the mangrove swamps of Thailand.

In Phang Nga province in western Thailand — an area where tin mining, charcoal burning, and shrimp farming previously decimated coastal mangroves — he found that 90 percent of mangrove recovery since the 1980s has been through the natural regeneration of a range of mangrove species, creating biodiverse forest. But restored mangrove forests in Trat province in eastern Thailand, which appear identical on satellite images, were largely planted with a single species of mangrove, resulting in much less biodiversity.

A man plants mangrove seedlings in Banda Aceh, Indonesia. Hand-planted mangroves rarely reach maturity. Chaideer Mahyuddin / AFP via Getty Images

Clearly, the prognosis for mangroves remains contested. Bunting says, “It is quite possible that the future trend will be similar to the last 15 years,” with gains continuing to exceed losses. And in a study of the likely fate of mangroves in a world 2 degrees Celsius warmer, Daniel Friess, a colleague of Zhang at Tulane University, found reasons to believe the trees could thrive. Because, at least in some places, they seem well able to keep pace with rising sea levels. Because increased rainfall will often increase soil erosion inland and sediment deposition along shorelines, giving mangroves even more room to expand. And because warmer temperatures should push mangroves further outside the tropics, replacing many more salt marshes in Australia and the southern U.S., for instance, for better or for worse.

But Duke argues that the new mangrove stands colonizing the tropics may not be as resilient as their predecessors to the more severe weather triggered by climate change. Or as useful in capturing and holding onto carbon. “They are immature stands, not fully developed in either biomass or biodiversity,” he says. That will leave them ever more vulnerable “as damaging [weather] events become more severe and more frequent.” Recent research appears to support his pessimistic presumption.

Field studies in China’s Hainan province found that mature mangrove forests recovered much more quickly from tropical cyclones, with their high winds, strong rainfall, and storm surges, compared with younger forests. The researchers stressed the importance of protecting old-growth mangroves rather than relying on new stands to replace them. Without such a strategy, humanity may yet face what Duke feared: “a world without mangroves.”

Distant space ‘hamburger’ hides a baby gas giant

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 21, 2026 12:04 PM EDT

A cosmic Quarter-Pounder is providing us with a near-perfect view of a distant gas giant’s earliest moments. Known as Gomez’s Hamburger (or GoHam for short), the massive protoplanetary disk surrounds a young star located in the Scorpius-Centaurus stellar region of our home galaxy about 453 light-years away from Earth. As researchers explained in a study recently published in The Astrophysical Journal, GoHam’s vantage from Earth is virtually unparalleled for anyone hoping to glimpse the subtle layers of dust and particles that will one day coalesce into a humongous gas giant.

Early observers first spotted GoHam in 1985, but initially pegged it for a planetary nebula as far as 6,500 light-years from Earth. Astronomers have since revised their assessments with data gathered by the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) in northern Chile. As its full name implies, ALMA measures millimeter-and-smaller wavelengths, allowing scientists to map the miniscule dust grains and gaseous molecules that compose distant objects. GoHam is a particularly unique subject for ALMA, because its position makes it a new benchmark example for testing models of how planets evolve.

“GoHam gives us a rare and clear view of the vertical and radial structure of a very large, nearly edge‑on disk,” explained study co-author and University of Minnesota astronomer Charles Law.

According to Law, its mixture of cosmic winds, large formational asymmetries, massive disk size, as well as the potential for planetary formation makes GoHam a “perfect laboratory.” With ALMA, Law and his colleagues analyzed GoHam’s layers of carbon monoxide, and sulfur-bearing molecules. As predicted, the lightest gas is in the highest layer above the formation’s midplane, while the heavier sulfur components lay beneath it. Meanwhile, a thin but concentrated section of millimeter dust is visible in the midplane itself.

Comparisons to GoHam’s fast food namesake only go so far, however. Its dust clouds lean north–south, with one side containing brighter and wider emissions. This likely stems from a disturbance such as a cosmic vortex, which may capture additional material for planetary formation. Dim, thin carbon monoxide emissions along the disk’s northern section also suggest GoHam’s central star is blowing gases into deep space as what’s known as photoevaporative wind.

Astronomers also noted that GoHam is one of the largest protoplanetary disks ever observed. At nearly 93 billion miles wide and with an exponentially larger dust mass than the typical protoplanet disk, GoHam may ultimately host not just one gas giant, but an entire planetary system.

The Terrifying Future of States’ Criminalizing Pregnant People Is Already Here

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The idea of charging people who have abortions with murder is gaining traction on the right. But a report finds that people are already being prosecuted for their pregnancy outcomes.

Anti-abortion rights demonstrators march to the Supreme Court for the 52nd annual March For Life in Washington, DC, on January 24, 2025.

Since the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade in 2022, the idea of charging people who have abortions with murder has gained terrifying new traction. During her brief stint as White House press secretary, Katie Zacharia became just the latest prominent conservative to draw attention for her ties to a radical wing of the anti-abortion movement that wants to charge people who have abortions with murder. This year alone, state lawmakers floated 14 bills to charge people who have abortions with homicide. (The idea is still so unpopular across the political spectrum that none of those bills passed.) Yet this whole debate is, in a sense, a red herring, because hundreds of people a year are already being prosecuted for their pregnancy outcomes. The vast majority of those cases involved a live birth where the mother was suspected of using drugs, but in a rising minority of cases since Dobbs, people are also being criminalized for pregnancy loss.

I spoke with Dana Sussman, senior vice president at Pregnancy Justice, about a report the organization published today finding that in the first three years after the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, prosecutors have initiated at least 902 cases across the country charging individuals with crimes related to pregnancy, pregnancy loss, or birth.

I’ve edited our conversation for brevity and clarity.

Amy Littlefield: What’s new in this report, which looks at pregnancy criminalization in the first three years after Dobbs?

Dana Sussman: When Roe was overturned, we suspected that pregnancy loss would become an increasingly criminally suspect event, and we unfortunately are starting to see that play out in the data. This report finds a significant acceleration in pregnancy loss prosecutions. We now have documented 70 pregnancy loss prosecutions in the first three years post-Dobbs.

AL: I was struck by the breakdown of allegations prosecutors made in the cases you found. There were 871 allegations of substance use during pregnancy, 77 allegations of a lack of prenatal care, and just 13 related to obtaining, attempting or researching an abortion. What does this tell us about who is being criminalized for pregnancy post-Dobbs?

DS: This is the continuation of a trend that we’ve been seeing for decades. The way that pregnancy is policed and prosecuted in the United States is through the lens of substance use. The foundation for prosecuting pregnancy is based in the war on drugs from the 1980s, and the way in which Black women, in particular, were targeted for charges of child abuse when they were deemed substance users, primarily with crack cocaine and this obsession over a “crack baby epidemic” that has been thoroughly debunked. Three state supreme court decisions used pregnancy and substance use as the way to redefine who a person is [thereby extending child abuse protections to fetuses] under their state laws: Alabama, South Carolina, and Oklahoma, which consistently rank among the top three states that criminalize pregnancy.

AL: What did you find about the race and socioeconomic background of the folks who were criminalized for their pregnancy outcomes in this report?

DS: In the ’80s, ’90s, and early 2000s, we were seeing disproportionately Black women being charged with crimes in connection with pregnancy, and that has shifted since the mid-2000s until the present. The majority are white women at this point, but poverty is a very big indicator, as it is across the criminal justice system. So we’re looking at poor white women as the majority in the data set at this point. There are a few different theories from scholars about why this is. [Legal scholar] Michele Goodwin has said that Black women were the canary in the coal mine; cruelty by the state is tried out on Black women, and then it spreads more broadly. AL: So it’s mostly low-income white women in a small number of states. I’m looking at the map: 381 of the cases in South Carolina, 222 in Oklahoma, 204 in Alabama, and then there’s states like my home state of Massachusetts where there’s zero.

In South Carolina, it was in the late ’90s; in Alabama, it was 2013; and in Oklahoma, it was 2020. So that kind of unleashed permission for prosecutors across the state to apply laws in this way. At the same time, we suspect that we are seeing more pregnancy loss cases in our data year upon year post-Dobbs, and I don’t think that should be a surprise, either. If we ban abortion and we make pregnancy loss suspicious because in some places provision of abortion has been made illegal, then we are creating an environment in which we are inviting law enforcement in to investigate how some of these pregnancies ended. AL: After the Supreme Court decision in Dobbs in 2022, there were predictions that we would see a mass number of criminal cases related to abortion, because all of a sudden providing abortion was a crime in about a dozen states. How does the reality compare to those expectations?

DS: We are seeing what we expected: that this was going to fall most harshly on people who use substances during pregnancy and were going to be charged with child abuse or neglect, and that pregnancy loss would be an increasingly suspect event. I remember getting lots of questions about period-tracking apps and wiping that from your phone, and I was like, if they want to criminalize pregnancy, they have other ways to do it, and they’ve been doing it. This is not going to be some new dragnet, it’s going to be the same old ways that they’ve been doing it for a very long time, and that has played out. That was the case before Dobbs, and it remains the case now.

People should keep their community of people who know as small and as trusted as possible, because the most common scenario [triggering criminal prosecution] is getting reported by other people.

The Anchor Positioning API allows us to anchor one element to another. For example, we might anchor a floating tooltip element to its trigger, so that no matter where that trigger is on the page, the tooltip will always be connected to it.

If you’ve ever tried to solve this sort of problem yourself, you know it’s surprisingly tricky. For example, we might want our tooltip to float above the anchor, but what if the anchor is near the top of the viewport?

The original Anchor Positioning API gave us most of the tools needed to solve this problem easily in CSS. It allows us to specify fallback positions; if the target element doesn’t fit above the anchor, it can flip automatically to the bottom.

Astronomers uncover new secrets of the Universe’s first five minutes

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have measured the amount of helium produced during the Universe's first five minutes with unprecedented precision, offering a clearer picture of the conditions that existed shortly after the Big Bang. The achievement, made by an international research team that includes scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, represents a major advance in precision cosmology.

The findings, presented in five papers published in The Astrophysical Journal, are based on 130 hours of observations using the Large Binocular Telescope. By analyzing distant galaxies, the researchers reduced the uncertainty in this fundamental measurement to just 0.5%, making it three times more precise than previous estimates.

The improved accuracy provides scientists with a powerful new tool for investigating the origins of the Universe and testing the Standard Model of physics. Such fundamental tests have a long history of advancing scientific understanding and contributing to technological breakthroughs.

"This is a physics experiment on a grand scale and one of the biggest findings in my entire 40-year career," said Evan Skillman, University of Minnesota College of Science and Engineering Distinguished Professor in the School of Physics and Astronomy. "It's a fundamental number that tells us specifically about the conditions of our Universe in its first five minutes. It has diagnostic power that speaks directly to the Standard Model of Physics."

According to the Big Bang theory, the Universe began expanding from an extraordinarily hot, dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago. Three major lines of evidence support this explanation: the continuing expansion of the Universe, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and the amounts of light elements such as helium and deuterium that formed during its earliest moments.

Although cosmic expansion and the microwave background have been studied extensively, measurements of primordial helium have historically received less attention. Determining its original abundance more accurately allows researchers to investigate the physical conditions that prevailed when the Universe was only minutes old.

To achieve their new level of precision, the team departed from the conventional method of estimating primordial helium. That approach involves observing large numbers of galaxies and extrapolating the results to determine how much helium existed before later generations of stars altered their chemical composition.

Instead, the researchers selected 15 exceptionally pristine, small, distant galaxies. These galaxies have undergone relatively little chemical evolution, making them valuable cosmic "time capsules" that preserve clues about the Universe's early composition.

Powerful Telescopes Deliver Record Precision

The team used sophisticated spectrographs developed at Ohio State University to examine more than 10 helium spectral lines and 15 hydrogen lines simultaneously. Studying these signals together made it possible to identify and correct subtle sources of measurement error that earlier studies could largely overlook.

Such small effects become especially important when researchers are trying to reduce uncertainty to less than 1%. Accounting for them was essential to achieving the new 0.5% precision.

"The MODS spectrographs took 12 years to build from conception to first light on sky," said Richard Pogge, College of Arts and Sciences Distinguished Professor of Astronomy at Ohio State University. "This is the kind of project we designed them to do, and to see them deliver is enormously satisfying. It's not every day you can help build instruments that measure something fundamental about our Universe."

Beyond refining estimates of the Universe's earliest chemical composition, the new measurement provides an important test of the Standard Model of physics. The amount of helium created shortly after the Big Bang depends on the physical conditions at that time, including the types of elementary particles present.

Using their precise helium measurement, the researchers were able to calculate the number of neutrino families in the early Universe. Neutrinos are extremely light subatomic particles, and their behavior plays an important role in scientists' understanding of fundamental physics.

The results therefore do more than strengthen existing evidence for the Big Bang. They also offer a more precise way to check whether established physical theories accurately describe the Universe during its first few minutes.

"We promised a half-percent uncertainty in our proposal, and we got there," added Skillman. "In the unpredictable world of science, that doesn't happen very often."

The international collaboration brought together scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, Ohio State University, Gonzaga University, Northwestern University, The University of Texas at Austin, Indiana University, University of California Santa Cruz, University of Illinois, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, and TRIUMF.

No, actually. There will be 53 mayoral candidates’ names on the ballot on Oct. 26. But Torontonians tend to ignore the so-called fringe, perhaps burned by eras when kooky or frightening candidates got too much airtime. We rarely meet, never mind evaluate, let alone choose, a promising unknown.

Hoping to find a bubble worth filling, I went in search of options — and actually found a little hope. Before I name names, though, here’s how I did it.

First, I used the Toronto Star’s Vote Compass tool, which showed how my takes on the city’s challenges, costs and priorities compared to candidates’ positions. Meanwhile, voters are left adrift in a sea of unknown candidates. All alone, they need to seek out and analyze information on not just policy, but integrity, experience and the ability to actually get the job done. Lucas believes a party system helps voters discern who’s worth their time. Without them, he says, it’s not surprising people gravitate towards known quantities.

I’m not quite sold — to me, independence and coalition-building are unique, important aspects of municipal governance we should try to strengthen, not abandon. Also, parties can be risk-averse and boring, stoking stereotypes and prejudice. But Lucas did sway me when he said the muscle of party machinery can help new candidates find their feet. “It’s a lonely enterprise,” he said, “and if you don’t already have a network of support behind you, it can be really challenging to break through.” That, plus parties have money.

Which brings me to mayoral candidate Laura Dean, who has raised $25,000 for her campaign. It’s more than she expected, she told me. Dean has spent more than a decade advising Ontario municipalities on land use (including housing starts!) as a partner at a Bay Street law firm. It’s a career she chose after time spent at the Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing and the now-defunct provincial climate secretariat. That last bit is what made this environmental journalist give her a call.

“When this election cycle came up, I was really shocked by the tone and just the quality of the discourse — it’s so poor. And, more, extremely polarizing,” Dean said about deciding to run. “The Canadian public so desperately needs more options and good quality conversation.”

Dean isn’t starry-eyed about her prospects and that’s fine. Plenty of worthwhile candidates need to try and try again. The point is, not every unknown is kooky or scary. Dean said the enthusiasm she’s received has her already considering her options for 2030. While many people say they’re glad she’s running, she said, they aren’t ready to fill her bubble just yet. Torontonians are practiced in voting for the person they consider okay (I guess) as they try to block the candidate they can’t stand. Maybe next time, we’ll believe we deserve better.

Snakes will bite, rattle their tails, and even spit venom to escape danger. But of all the world’s species, southeast Asia’s dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) relies on an especially fascinating escape method. When threatened, the diminutive serpent rolls away like a speeding wheel.

An adult dwarf reed snake typically measures less than 12 inches long, but its unique locomotive maneuvers allow it to retreat as fast one foot per second—about four times faster than it travels by simply slithering on the ground. It’s not a flailing getaway, either. The snake also easily rolls along a straight path while somehow keeping its head both stable and facing forward.

Now, researchers finally have a better understanding of how the dwarf reed snake achieves such dizzying acrobatics. In a study published today in the journal Current Biology, a team at Japan’s Toho University detailed the specific physiology that enables its locomotion after filming the snakes with high-speed cameras. Essentially, the snake doesn’t rotate its entire body in a singular, forward motion—it whips one side of its body forward towards the ground while an arched portion swings down by its head in what the study’s authors call a “stroke.” The snake then alternates between its left and right side coming into contact with the ground for each successive stroke. Essentially, it’s like twirling a rope in a lateral circle while holding one end in your hand—except that end is the snake’s head.

The cumulative efforts bypass the traditional idea of a wheel requiring something like a central axle. Instead, this ingenious, highly controlled movement allows the animal to keep its head and sensory organs upright and facing ahead while traveling at such high speed. This is compounded by the dwarf reed snake directing regions of its body to engage in large, alternating rotations while stabilizing its head.

By gaining better insight into these biological mechanisms, researchers also highlight the difficulty in trying to predict an animal’s capabilities based solely on its physiology. A snake may have primarily evolved to slither—but that doesn’t mean it can’t get creative when necessary.

When zoology professor Daniel Baldassarre traveled to the remote Galápagos Islands of Wolf and Darwin with two other researchers, getting there proved quite a challenge. With no beaches to land a boat on, they had to approach the islands’ cliffs in a small dinghy and wait for a lull in the waves before jumping onto sharp, black lava rocks.

But their efforts paid off. On that trip, they saw a bird that very few people have ever set eyes on in the wild: the vampire ground finch. These finches are only found on Wolf and Darwin, and feed on the blood of much larger birds called Nazca boobies that also live on the islands.

Up close, Baldassarre and his colleagues noted the vampire finches’ blood-stained bills and their throats visibly engorged with blood. They also saw them in action.

“We observed scores of vampire finches clamouring all over the backs, tails, and wings of boobies, opening up substantial wounds with their sharp beaks, and drinking their fill of blood,” they wrote in an article for The Conversation.

“Blood is a notoriously difficult food source to process and extract nutrients from, which probably partly explains why so few animals have evolved to feed on it,” Baldassarre tells Popular Science. He suspects that on these islands where freshwater is extremely rare, blood might serve as a water source for the finches.

It’s not the only component of the vampire finches’ diet, however. Based on unpublished data collected by Baldassarre and his colleagues, blood makes up about one-tenth of the vampire finches’ diet.

“These finches are quite opportunistic, so they will eat just about anything they can find,” Baldassarre says. “We observed them feeding on everything from seeds to insects to broken seabird eggs.”

One theory is that vampire finches rely more on blood feeding in periods of extreme drought, when other resources are scarce. But more research is needed to see if this idea holds up in practice.

Vampire finches weren’t always vampires, scientists think. The relationship between vampire finches and boobies probably started off with the finches eating parasites found on the skin and feathers of the boobies. This would have been mutually beneficial because it provided a cleaning service for the boobies and food for the finches.

But at some point in evolutionary history, in the process of picking off parasites, the finches started to create lesions on the boobies’ skin, and eventually began feeding on their blood.

Over time, their bills became more scalpel-like, perfect for making skin incisions. Their digestive system also changed. When Baldassarre and his colleagues studied the microbes found in vampire finches’ guts, they found that their gut microbiome was very different from that of other, non-blood-consuming finches on the Galápagos Islands, something they attribute to their unique diet.

Until recently, vampire finches were considered a subspecies of the sharp-beaked ground finch (Geospiza difficilis).

“But they have now been designated as their own species [Geospiza septentrionalis] because of the differences they have evolved because of blood feeding,” Baldassarre notes.

Baldassarre and his colleagues have seen vampire finches gang up on and kill nestling boobies,“but that was quite rare,” he points out.

“In most cases, the finches seem to inflict just enough damage to break the skin and extract some blood, but not too much to harm the boobies significantly.” This is similar to many parasite-host relationships, such as fleas, ticks, and mosquitoes on mammals. “You can kind of think of the vampire finches as giant mosquitoes,” Baldassarre says.

As seen in a segment on vampire finches in David Attenborough’s documentary A Perfect Planet, the boobies seem to tolerate the vampire finches rather than chasing them away. There are likely a few reasons for this, Baldassarre says.

“For one, as in large animals being bitten by mosquitoes, often the damage is minimal and not noticeable, so it doesn’t elicit a strong reaction. Also, the boobies sometimes have a vested interest in staying put and guarding their spot in the colony, or perhaps nest or nestlings, so may not want to fly away. Finally, the finches seem to target hard-to-reach areas on the boobies like the base of tail feathers, so it’s likely difficult for the boobies to deter them.”

I, for one, was feeling very sorry for the boobies being constantly hounded by these small vampire birds. Until I learnt that Nazca boobies practice something called “obligate siblicide,” which essentially means that the first-hatched chick routinely attacks and kills its younger sibling. It’s a harsh, bird-eat-bird world in the Galápagos Islands.

Black voters test the socialist left's ceiling

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Black voters may determine whether Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) can build on breakthroughs in the 2026 elections.

Why it matters: This year's primaries showed that DSA's path runs through older Black voters who wield enormous influence in Democratic primaries — particularly in the South — but have been slower to embrace insurgent candidates.

By the numbers: Black Democrats are more likely to like than dislike leaders who identify as democratic socialists, but nearly two-thirds remain neutral toward them.

  • 21% of Black Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents told Pew Research Center they like such leaders, compared with 11% who dislike them. Another 65% were neutral.
  • Among Black Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents, 23% of those ages 18–49 like leaders who identify as democratic socialists, compared with 20% of those 50 and older.

State of play: Angie Nixon's upset win in Florida's Democratic U.S. Senate primary made the Black state representative the first DSA member to win a major-party statewide nomination.

  • Her politics blend labor organizing and civil-rights protest: The former union organizer fought voting-rights battles with sit-ins and a bullhorn and opened a bookstore amid Florida's school book bans, hosting Black-history workshops and organizing groups.
  • Her vote share rose in precincts with younger and larger Black populations, an Axios analysis of election results found.

Donavan McKinney's Detroit victory also showed a DSA-backed candidate can break through in a center of Black political power.

  • The DSA-endorsed state representative defeated Rep. Shri Thanedar 51.9%-48.1% in Michigan's 13th District, pairing deep Detroit roots and a legislative record with labor and local support.

Yes, but: A July Redbox Research poll for A Fight Worth Having found former Rep. Cori Bush (D-Mo.) leading Rep. Wesley Bell (D-Mo.) 52%–20% among voters under 35 and 46%–36% among those 35–49.

  • But only 19% of the Black likely-primary electorate in the poll was under 50.
  • Bell ultimately defeated Bush 59%-37%.

Zoom out: Nationally, 39% of Democratic-aligned adults under 30 like democratic socialist leaders, compared with 26% of those 50 and older.

  • In 2016, an NBC analysis of 25 state exit polls found Bernie Sanders beat Hillary Clinton 52%-47% among Black voters under 30.
  • In South Carolina's 2020 primary, Black voters made up 56% of the electorate, and 69% of Black voters over 45 backed Joe Biden, according to Edison exit polling.

Between the lines: New America's Theodore Johnson tells Axios older Black voters often impose a "viability hurdle" before taking a chance on candidates.

  • Johnson said younger Black voters can be "central" to an insurgent coalition but "insufficient" without broader support.

What they're saying: Darius Jones of the National Black Empowerment Action Fund warns against reading frustration with establishment Democrats as socialist conversion.

  • Younger Black voters are "increasingly impatient with the pace of results," he said, but that is "not a wholesale endorsement of the DSA in any way."
  • Marcus Board Jr., a political science associate professor at Howard University, said DSA's difficulty connecting class politics to race is "definitely a hurdle."

The other side: DSA says it is "committed to growing our membership into a more diverse, multiracial, multigenerational movement" by organizing around Black workers, child care, housing and food security.
