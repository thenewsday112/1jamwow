<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/ai-and-machine-learning/whos-responsible-clinical-ai-ama-has-some-thoughts" hreflang="en">Who’s responsible when clinical AI goes wrong? AMA weighs in</a>

Who’s responsible when clinical AI goes wrong? AMA weighs in" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Building on its recent guidance for organizations’ use and governance of clinical AI, the American Medical Association on Thursday gave a deeper breakdown of how it believes different parties should be responsible for decisions, actions and failures involving the technology.

The latest framework from AMA’s Center for Digital Health and AI addresses liability issues the group said are currently unanswered by existing liability frameworks. Those structures, it said, could leave physicians facing "liability for risks they cannot reasonably prevent, while patients may struggle to seek remedies when AI causes harm."

Other key issues with the status quo liability frameworks, AMA said, are ambiguity around when product liability applies to AI; how contract terms and disclaimers from developers can distance responsibility from risk control; and whether deploying healthcare organizations hold various responsibilities specific to AI, such as local testing or outcomes monitoring.

AMA said the clinical AI liability framework it’s advancing "recognizes that multiple parties may bear liability for the use of AI when something goes wrong," and then aligns that accountability based on a party’s ability to prevent harm. Responsibility is spread out in areas where control is shared, but it does not become interchangeable, and no party’s obligations disappear because another party also bears some,” AMA wrote in a brief outlining its thinking.

As for the physicians AMA represents, the liability framework calls for doctors to "be responsible for the clinical judgment they can meaningfully exercise, based on the system’s intended use, known limitations and the information available at the point of care."

The group also said that oversight from a physician "cannot substitute for adequate evidence, appropriate system design or the conditions necessary to make clinical review meaningful." Physicians should also retain the authority to decline an unproven AI system, or those "whose clinical validation, usefulness or place in the standard of care remains uncertain," without penalty from their organization, AMA said.

"Patients must have meaningful avenues for recourse when clinical AI causes harm, and physicians must be able to use these technologies with confidence that responsibility reflects what they can reasonably know and control," AMA President Willie Underwood III, M.D., president of the AMA, said in a release on the liability framework. "As AI assumes more clinical functions, developers, healthcare organizations and physicians each have important responsibilities. We need clear expectations that protect patients, support sound clinical judgment, and ensure accountability remains with those best positioned to prevent harm."

The liability framework breaks down in detail one aspect of a broader clinical AI governance framework unveiled by AMA and its Center earlier this month. Those principles and focus areas will guide how AMA works with providers and stakeholders as it tackles issues like licensure and regulation for the fast-moving technology.

"We cannot afford to wait for these questions to be answered through trial and error in patient care," AMA CEO John Whyte, M.D., told Fierce Healthcare of the efforts earlier this month. "The AMA's new framework sets out our ambitious agenda to address these questions. … It will guide our work with physicians, patients, technology developers and policymakers to ensure clinical authority is earned through evidence, how oversight must evolve with risk, and how accountability is maintained."

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/why-oscar-health-putting-focus-personalization-aca-exchange-plans" hreflang="en">Why Oscar Health is putting a focus on personalization for ACA exchange plans</a>

Why Oscar Health is putting a focus on personalization for ACA exchange plans" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Another open enrollment period is set to begin on the Affordable Care Act's exchanges, and the team at Oscar Health is focusing on the unique ways it can innovate in the turbulent market.

Oscar is growing the number of counties in its geographic footprint by 24% in 2027, offering plans across 713 counties in 21 states. It is also investing in new specialized plans, such as its RetireEase offering, and in expanding coverage options designed for individuals with certain conditions.

Janet Liang, president of Oscar Insurance, told Fierce Healthcare that it's critical for plans to learn as much as they can about the individual consumer, so that they can shape benefits that effectively meet the needs of the people who are coming to the marketplaces for coverage.

"We are completely dialed in to the individual consumer," she said. "We know the people who are in the ACA, and it's a broad, broad range of people. It's not one size fits all, and what we do is we spend the time to understand who comes into the ACA and what are the services and needs that individuals have."

That includes putting a focus on collecting plenty of feedback from existing members, and what they're searching for as existing enrollees, as well as collecting data on broader consumer behaviors on the exchanges.

The RetireEase plan, for example, was born from this thinking, Liang said. The offering, which is launching in five states next year, aims to support members in the transition to Medicare coverage, targeted to people aged 55 to 64.

Benefits include set prices for key services in this age range, from preventive care to physical therapy to cardiology. Members that enroll in RetireEase can also access specific supports designed for the handoff to Medicare, for caregivers and transportation if needed.

Liang said this population tends to have unique concerns about affordability as employers shift their approaches in a way that is leading more people to retire early, leaving them on a fixed income. It's also an age range where people begin to develop challenging chronic conditions that will need to be managed in the future.

The team took that feedback and those particular needs it heart when building out RetireEase, Liang said.

Oscar's suite of specialized offerings includes some of its most popular plans, she said. In addition to the new launch, Oscar is expanding the reach of its existing specialized plans. HelloMeno, its plan designed for women navigating menopause, will grow to 15 states by 2027, for example.

Liang said that these plans have higher Net Promoter Scores than its traditional plans, and they also tend to have higher retention rates than the broader offerings.

"Based on the retention rate and the high NPS scores, we know that we're meeting an unmet need," she said.

And the Oscar team is already planning for the future around these specialized plans, and has determined where to go next for 2028. Expanding this slate is a central part of Oscar's broader strategy around the ACA, which also includes enhancements to bronze, silver and gold plans as well as a focus on individual coverage health reimbursement arrangements, or ICHRA.

ICHRA plans have rebranded as Custom Health Option and Individual Care Expense, or CHOICE. But in these models, an employer offers workers a stipend to purchase an exchange plan, rather than offering coverage directly.

Liang said Oscar believes this is an area that will continue to grow. She said that they're seeing higher numbers of employers requesting quotes for potential CHOICE arrangements, and Oscar has doubled its membership between 2025 and 2026.

She added that Oscar expects to triple its CHOICE market share by 2029.

"So there's an obvious interest by employers to reduce their costs," Liang said. "And we also look at markets where there's just a lot of interest by both small employers, regional employers, to just start off. What you see is new employers and new companies that are forming will go straight to CHOICE instead of starting on the traditional insurance path."

Doximity launched DoseCheck, a new safety feature within Doximity Ask designed to catch unsafe or incorrect medication dosing recommendations generated by artificial intelligence.

Doximity Ask is the company's clinical decision support tool.

Rather than relying on an LLM to double-check another LLM, DoseCheck adds a deterministic, rule-based verification layer, checking AI-generated dosing claims against structured information including FDA labeling and professional guidelines, the company said. By checking against FDA labeling and professional guidelines, DoseCheck increases accuracy to more than 99%, the company claims.

Doximity tested nine widely used AI models with over 8,000 dosing questions based on real physician queries. At least 2.3% of dosing claims made by these models were wrong, and 43.8% of detected errors were serious or potentially lethal.

According to the analysis, common sources of errors include dosing newer drugs, failing to account for patient-specific factors such as kidney function, age, weight and medical history, and mistakes involving units, decimal points or drugs with similar names.

"AI is probabilistic. Drug dosing can’t be," said Louis-Antoine Mullie, M.D., chief AI researcher at Doximity. "DoseCheck doesn’t ask a language model to grade its own homework. It checks dosing claims against a structured, authoritative database, so physicians get the same verified dosing answer every time."

DoseCheck is free and has been rolled out to all Doximity Ask users across mobile and desktop.

Large language models recommend more up-front testing, referrals and treatment than the physicians who actually saw the patients and at more than three times the cost, according to a study published in NEJM AI.

Researchers from the Technion, Stanford University and collaborating institutions tested 20 general-purpose and four specialized medical AI systems on records from 200 real outpatient and emergency visits. Each received the clinical presentation without the physician’s assessment and plan. The team priced the recommended tests and referrals in both the AI and physicians’ plans using the same 2026 Medicare rates.

Across the 20 general-purpose systems, AI-suggested care cost a mean of $314 per visit, compared with $97 for care recommended by attending physicians. Suggested care was more costly in 180 of 200 cases. The average cost of care per AI system ranged from $209 to $599.

Diagnostic testing rose from $71 per visit in the physicians’ plans to $176 across the systems, about $105 more per visit. The referral rates for the physician’s plans were 0.15 per visit compared to 0.87 for the LLM’s plans, which, at the corresponding Medicare rates, is $111 more per visit, according to the study.

"The four specialized medical systems showed comparable excess (2.7× to 3.5×), so medical fine-tuning did not curb—and may reinforce—cost escalation," the study authors wrote.

The researchers used MTSamples, a publicly available collection of 4,999 real de-identified medical transcription sample reports contributed by practicing clinicians as reference material for medical transcriptionists. The notes are documentation from real patient visits, but they are a convenience sample assembled for transcription training rather than a representative draw from U.S. ambulatory practice, the study authors wrote.

"Under a single standard prompt, every AI system we evaluated, general purpose and medically specialized alike, suggested more diagnostic tests, medications and specialist referrals than the treating physician on the same cases," the study authors wrote.

LLMs appear optimized to be comprehensive and avoid missing diagnoses, and may show an analogous tendency toward exhaustive workup, the study authors noted.

The researchers argued that much of the excess can be reduced through better prompting, suggesting cost inflation is partly a deployment and design problem rather than an unavoidable feature of AI.

Researchers reevaluated 19 models using different instructions. A simple cost-aware prompt telling models to recommend only clinically necessary, cost-effective items reduced average recommended costs from 3.2 times physician costs to 2.5 times.

A stronger "parsimonious-but-safe" prompt told models to focus on the three most likely diagnoses, explicitly rule out dangerous "cannot-miss" conditions, and recommend only tests whose results would change management. That cut costs to just 1.4 times physician costs. But diagnostic agreement with physicians fell in 17 of the 19 systems, illustrating why cost reductions need to be evaluated alongside other changes in the recommendations, the study authors noted.

"Getting the diagnosis right matters, but so does the path you take to get there," said Prof. Dvir Aran of the Technion, the study’s corresponding author, in a statement. "Accuracy and safety have dominated how clinical AI is trained and evaluated. Efficiency needs the same attention: whether each test or referral is likely to help the patient."

Letter From the Editor-in-Chief: What a Decade of Bedtime Stories Taught Me About Health

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

I’ve read to my children each night at bedtime for as long as I (and they) can remember. We started with the likes of “Goodnight Batcave” and the lesser-known-in-the-US but oh-so-wonderful collection of verse-driven classics from former UK Children’s Laureate Julia Donaldson.

We moved on to B.J. Novak’s anarchic love letter to the power of words (and to great typography), “The Book With No Pictures.”

We eventually worked our way up to Roald Dahl’s wickedly dark back catalog, discovering more complex characters, language and plotting as we went.

When my eldest, Amelia, hit 6, it was time to roll out the big guns.

Over the last 5 years (she’s now 11), I’ve read the seven Harry Potter books aloud twice, complete with all the voices: once to Amelia on her own, once to both her and her brother, Henry, two years her junior.

I had feared she wouldn’t be keen on another helping, but Amelia immersed herself once more in the world of Hogwarts with no hesitation and took immense joy in watching Henry discover for himself every twist and turn on Harry’s journey.

We have done the same thing with Tolkien’s “The Hobbit” and his fantasy masterpiece “The Lord of the Rings,” and are currently midway through “The Two Towers” on our second go around.

There’s a huge amount of reciprocal joy we take from this nightly ritual, beyond the practical benefits of providing a calming demarcation between the end of the day and the wind-down to sleep.

For me, it comes as either a break in my working day or a signal that it’s over, depending on how hectic things are.

For the kids, it’s an all-too-rare chance to get me all to themselves, no deadlines, screens, or other distractions to pull me away from being anything but 100% present.

For all three of us, it’s a literal (and literary!) warm blanket — a chance to escape under the covers to familiar worlds and familiar faces. It is, in short, incredibly good for our well-being.

And yet, in the world of health information — including on this site — thousands, if not millions, of reading hours are dedicated to the benefits of nutrition, sleep, and exercise as the building blocks of good health. Reading itself rarely makes that shortlist.

For me, it unequivocally deserves a place on it. Research seems to support that case. Even if we need more of it to really understand how and why.

A recent editorial in Psychological Medicine co-authored by Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology at Cambridge University, draws on a broad range of research linking reading for pleasure to better mental health and greater empathy.

The positive impact of reading on young people — just like Amelia and Henry — lso carries evidence.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/ai-and-machine-learning/industry-voices-other-ai-divide-already-opening-healthcare" hreflang="en">Industry Voices—The other AI divide is already opening in healthcare</a>

Industry Voices—The other AI divide is already opening in healthcare" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

The debate over AI and inequality has largely focused on people—who will have access to the technology, who will benefit from it and who risks being left behind. But inside healthcare, another AI divide is already taking shape. This one is between the hospitals that have the money, talent and infrastructure to put AI to work and those that don't.

The numbers are striking. According to federal data on hospital AI adoption, 71% of U.S. hospitals used predictive AI integrated with their electronic health records in 2024. But adoption reached 96% among large hospitals compared with just 59% among small hospitals. Among hospitals belonging to multi-hospital systems, 86% used predictive AI. Among independent hospitals, it was only 37%.

That gap matters because AI is quickly becoming part of how hospitals survive, not simply an innovation project. The same federal data show some of the fastest-growing hospital AI applications are decidedly practical, including billing and scheduling. These are exactly the kinds of labor-intensive functions where financially stressed hospitals need to find efficiencies.

Yet those hospitals may be the least equipped to make the investment.

Hospitals remain extraordinarily dependent on people to operate. According to the American Hospital Association, workforce spending accounted for about 60% of hospital expenses in 2025, exceeding $1 trillion. Total hospital expenses grew 7.5% that year, more than twice the growth in hospital prices.

This creates a dangerous catch-22. The hospitals with the greatest need to use technology to redesign labor-intensive operations frequently have the least capacity to invest in doing so.

A large health system can afford an AI pilot that fails. It can hire an innovation team, absorb a lengthy implementation, build governance and compliance infrastructure, and try again if the economics don't work. A safety-net, community or independent hospital often has far less room for error. A multi-year software contract that might eventually produce savings still requires money today.

And buying the technology is only the beginning.

Successful AI deployment requires workflow redesign, change management, integration, measurement and governance. Even among hospitals already using predictive AI, the federal data show meaningful variation in how comprehensively models are evaluated for accuracy, bias and performance after implementation. Smaller, rural and independent hospitals also continue to lag their larger counterparts in adoption.

If we leave the market to work this out one hospital at a time, we should expect that divide to compound. Systems that successfully automate expensive workflows can reinvest those savings into their next technology investment. They build institutional knowledge. Their employees get better at implementation. They become more attractive partners to technology companies looking for sophisticated customers and proving grounds.

The hospitals that wait don't simply remain where they are. They lose ground. The answer cannot be telling every hospital to develop a better AI strategy. We need to change the ownership and financing model for healthcare AI.

Hospitals already know how to collaborate when acting collectively creates an advantage. Group purchasing organizations allow them to aggregate purchasing power rather than negotiate for every product independently. Clinical research networks allow institutions to pool patients, expertise and infrastructure to answer questions no single organization could address as effectively alone.

We should apply the same instinct to AI. Instead of requiring every health system to independently buy, implement and govern variations of the same technology, health systems should increasingly pool their purchasing power, operational expertise and capital to build common AI capabilities. And smaller, independent and safety-net organizations need a path into those collaborations rather than simply another vendor contract.

The venture community has a responsibility here as well. We need financing structures that acknowledge the economic reality of the organizations that really need to adopt technologies. That could mean performance-based models where payment is tied to verified savings rather than large upfront commitments. It could mean consortium models in which multiple health systems share implementation resources and governance.

And increasingly, it should mean giving health systems ownership in the companies and infrastructure they help create. Ownership matters because the economics of AI are changing quickly. As the technology itself becomes increasingly commoditized, enduring value will come from distribution, proprietary workflows, real-world implementation knowledge and access to the environments where healthcare actually happens. Health systems contribute enormously to each of those things. Under the traditional vendor model, however, they can provide the workflows, clinical expertise and proving ground and then pay someone else an annual fee for the resulting product.

That bargain deserves to be reconsidered. Collective ownership can help address the trust problem, too. AI adoption isn't only a technology decision. It requires decisions about validation, monitoring, clinical safety and who is accountable when something goes wrong. Federal researchers found that hospitals increasingly rely on multiple parties to evaluate AI, with 74% reporting more than one entity accountable for evaluation. Smaller hospitals should not each have to recreate that infrastructure from scratch.

Shared infrastructure could include common validation standards, clinical oversight, monitoring and implementation expertise. That would give more hospitals access not only to AI, but to the capabilities required to deploy it responsibly.

None of this means every hospital needs to own every AI company, or that traditional vendors disappear. It means we should stop assuming that the only scalable model for healthcare innovation is one technology company selling essentially the same capability to thousands of hospitals one contract at a time.

The AI divide between hospitals is already here. If adoption continues to depend primarily on the size of an organization's balance sheet, the institutions best positioned today will keep accumulating advantages while those with the least room for error fall further behind.

AI gives hospitals a real opportunity to break their dependence on increasingly expensive, labor-intensive ways of operating. But the organizations that need that opportunity most cannot be the last ones able to afford it. Preventing that outcome will require more than making AI cheaper. It will require giving more health systems the ability to build, govern and share in the technology's value, transforming them.

AI should become something hospitals build together, not another advantage reserved for those that could already afford to move first.
