Honey bees are bullying other bees, scientists discover

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Honey bees are proving to be formidable competitors for food in the UK, and scientists have discovered that they frequently use aggression to drive other pollinators away from flowers. New research has documented how honey bees chase, displace, and intimidate rival insects, providing the first detailed evidence of how extensively they interfere with other species' access to food.

The study, conducted by researchers at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) and published in Ecological Entomology, followed pollinator activity over two years at five locations in and around Cambridge, England. Each site was situated within 300 meters of an established honey bee colony.

Researchers observed insects visiting bramble (Rubus fruticosus) and ivy (Hedera helix), two flowering plants that provide important supplies of nectar and pollen. Both attract large numbers of insects during their flowering periods, making them useful places to investigate competition for food.

Honey Bees Dominate the Competition for Flowers

To examine how different pollinators interact, the team divided flower visitors into five groups: honey bees, bumble bees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and an 'other' category containing butterflies, beetles, and wasps.

Over 93 hours of observation, the scientists documented encounters in which one insect interrupted another's feeding or prevented it from reaching a flower. Each interaction was classified according to its outcome. A 'win' occurred when one insect gained access to a flower at the expense of another, which experienced a 'loss' of feeding opportunities. A 'share' meant both insects were able to access the flower without conflict.

The results revealed a clear pattern. Locations with greater concentrations of honey bees generally had fewer other pollinators and experienced more competition for flowers. Honey bees also emerged victorious in a larger proportion of their encounters with other insects.

On bramble flowers, doubling the density of honey bees was associated with a 14% decrease in bumblebee abundance. On ivy, higher honey bee numbers were linked to reduced numbers of solitary bees and hoverflies.

The honey bees did not simply reach flowers before their competitors. They frequently initiated aggressive encounters, driving other insects away to secure feeding opportunities.

Other pollinators actively avoided honey bees in 28% of observed interactions on bramble and 36% of those on ivy. In at least 20% of the encounters honey bees won on either plant, they physically pushed aside or chased away competing insects.

Aggressive behavior initiated by species other than honey bees was uncommon. However, one insect stood out as an exception.

The ivy bee, a solitary species not native to Britain that was first documented in the country in 2001, was the only pollinator observed actively challenging honey bees. Unlike most other insects, ivy bees sometimes defended their feeding spots by chasing or displacing honey bees, successfully competing for access to flowers.

Climate Change Could Make Competition Worse

The findings are part of a broader research effort to understand competition among pollinators and identify ways to reduce its ecological effects.

The results also come as beekeeping continues to expand across the UK. The number of registered beekeepers increased from 19,000 in 2010 to 47,000 last year, reflecting growing numbers of managed honey bee colonies.

Researchers warn that rising temperatures could add to the pressure on wild pollinators. Their observations showed that competitive encounters became more frequent during warmer conditions.

One possible explanation involves changes in flower nectar production. When plants experience 'heat stress', they can produce less nectar, potentially forcing more insects to compete over a smaller supply of food.

Temperature also affects different pollinators in different ways. Bumblebees and hoverflies are sensitive to high temperatures and may forage more effectively in cooler weather. Honey bees, by contrast, generally become more active as temperatures rise. Previous research found that increasing the temperature by 12°C caused a tenfold increase in the number of honey bees leaving their nests.

Together, these differences suggest that continued warming could give honey bees an even greater competitive advantage over some wild pollinators.

Protecting Wild Pollinators as Honey Bee Numbers Grow

Lead author Victoria Buckle, a PhD student at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, emphasized that the research is intended to improve conservation efforts rather than discourage people from keeping honey bees.

"The aim of our study was not to discourage beekeeping, but to better understand how competition happens between wild and managed pollinator species with the aim of helping to conserve our wild pollinators, which play such an important ecological role.

"We found that most interactions when competing for food were instigated by honey bees and they had a greater number of 'wins', resulting in a loss of forage for other pollinators. Bumblebees, solitary bees and hoverflies would avoid, be displaced by and on some occasions be chased off by honey bees.

"An interesting exception was the ivy bee, which would win contests more often against honey bees. It is the first time, to our knowledge, such behaviors have been observed and shows ivy bees are able to 'hold their own' in such encounters.

"However, honey bees outcompeted all other wild pollinators and based on our findings, future predicted warming in the UK is only likely to further exacerbate interference competition from dominant honey bees. Therefore, it's important to find ways to ensure honey bees and wild pollinators can continue to coexist, particularly in locations with a high density of hives, such as urban areas."

The latest PowerToys gives your mouse 2 new tricks for free – how to use them

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Our gifting experts are obsessively searching for holiday gifts that are unique, thoughtful and suitable for any budget.

PowerToys is one of my favorite free Windows utilities. With more than 30 tools, it offers something for everyone from beginners to power users. Here's what's new for mousing.

Ever have trouble using your mouse? Sometimes selecting a large block of text proves to be a challenge. Other times, your mouse cursor may get in the way of what you’re trying to type or see. Now Microsoft can help you resolve both issues via the latest version of its free PowerToys utility.

Also: 5 apps I always install on new Windows PCs – and most are free

Popping up in the official release of PowerToys version 0.102 are two new tools for you to mouse around with. One is called Mouse Button Lock, while the other is known as CursorHide. I’ll explain how to use each one. But to try them yourself, you’ll first need to install or update PowerToys.

To install the program from scratch, go to the PowerToys Microsoft Store page and click the Download button. Or, you can head to the PowerToys GitHub page and click the correct exe file for your flavor of Windows. To update an existing installation, open PowerToys from the Start menu or the System Tray and click the button to check for updates or select the notice to grab the latest version.

With PowerToys running, select the category for Input & Output on the left and click Mouse Utilities. You can then check out the details on both Mouse Button Lock and CursorHide and turn on the switches for either or both. Now here’s how they work.

Selecting a block of text normally requires that you hold down your left mouse button for the entire process. But if you’re trying to select a long block that extends to multiple sections or pages, that maneuver can be tricky.

With Mouse Button Lock, you’re able to hold down a mouse button for a second or two and then release it. You then move your mouse toward the end of the block you wish to select, and the text is automatically selected along the way. Then just click your mouse button again to stop the selection.

This capability has already been available through the ClickLock setting built into Windows. To access this with or without PowerToys, go to Settings and select Accessibility. Scroll down to the screen to the Interaction section and select the option for Mouse. Click the setting for ClickLock. You can then turn it on and set the duration in seconds to determine how long you must hold down your left mouse button to activate it.

If this functionality is already built into Windows, why enable it in PowerToys? The PowerToys flavor is much more versatile. You can choose the left, right, or middle mouse button as the trigger. You can also set the duration more precisely in miliseconds. Plus, you’re able to choose the distance in pixels during which your selection will remain. On my end, I chose the right mouse button, set the duration to 1,500 ms (1.5 seconds), and increased the distance to 100 pixels.

To try this in action as I set it, move your cursor to the start of the area you want to select and then hold down the right mouse button for around 1.5 seconds. Release the button and then move your mouse to the end of the area you want to select.

Also: How to make Windows more like MacOS

You should see the text being selected along the way. When you’ve reached the end, click the right mouse button again, and the selection will stop. It sounds more intricate than it is. After you try it a couple of times, I think you’ll find it a more convenient way to select a large area of text.

Does the mouse cursor ever get in the way when you’re typing text or just trying to see something on the screen? CursorHide will hide the mouse cursor while you’re typing or after a certain period of inactivity so it doesn’t obscure whatever is on the screen.

For this one, click its entry in the Mouse Utilities section in PowerToys and turn on the switch. You can then choose to hide the cursor during most kinds of keyboard input, after a certain period of cursor inactivity, or both. If you choose cursor inactivity, you’re able to set the delay to determine how long until the cursor disappears, anywhere from 1 to 60 seconds. I enabled both options and set the delay to 5 seconds.

Now open an app, document, or file in which you can type text. Move the mouse so you can see the cursor. As soon as you start typing, it should disappear. Move the mouse to show the cursor again. Next, wait for the number of seconds that you chose for the delay, and the cursor should again vanish. Move the mouse to view it again.

While you’re here, you may want to try some of the other mouse utilities. I like Find My Mouse, which helps you locate a mouse pointer or cursor that’s playing hard to get. I also sometimes use Mouse Highlighter, which visually highlights the area I click on with the mouse.

Also: PowerToys now lets you control your monitor from the taskbar

PowerToys is one of my favorite free Windows utilities. With more than 30 tools, it offers something for everyone from beginners to power users. Take time to explore some of the other tools to see which ones you find helpful and then just try them. You can easily turn each one on and off and use your favorites whenever you want.

NASA’s Curiosity rover reveals a spectacular new view of Mars with a mystery on the horizon

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA's Curiosity rover has captured a spectacular new panorama of Mars, providing its clearest look yet at a series of mysterious cliffs shaped by wind. Scientists have been eager to examine these distant formations up close, and the latest images offer an exciting preview of what lies ahead. Taken at 8:30 a.m. local Mars time, the scene reveals striking blue tones across the foreground while morning sunlight illuminates rugged cliffs known as yardangs along the horizon.

Curiosity recorded the panorama using its Mastcam on August 11, during the mission's 4,982nd Martian day, or sol. Six separate photographs were transmitted to Earth and combined to create the sweeping view. Unlike most Mastcam photographs, the images were processed without white balancing, allowing the scene to retain the distinctive colors and lighting of the Martian morning.

Curiosity Approaches Mysterious Martian Cliffs

The yardangs stretch approximately 10 miles (16 kilometers) across the northwestern portion of Mount Sharp, a 3-mile-tall (5-kilometer-tall) mountain that Curiosity has been climbing since 2014. The rover recently achieved a major milestone, gaining 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) in elevation, a greater climb than any other rover has completed on Mars.

As Curiosity continues toward these unusual formations, researchers anticipate increasingly detailed views of the cliffs. More importantly, they hope that examining the rocks firsthand will help explain how the remarkable landscape developed.

What Mars' Ancient Rocks Reveal About Its Past

Mount Sharp contains numerous layers of sedimentary rock, each preserving evidence from a different chapter of Martian history. Investigating these layers has helped scientists reconstruct a time billions of years ago when lakes and streams existed in this region of Mars.

As the planet's environment changed, those bodies of water eventually disappeared, leaving deposits of salty minerals behind. Once additional material stopped accumulating on the mountain, powerful winds may have gradually eroded portions of the existing rock, carving out the distinctive yardangs visible today.

Yet the unusual appearance of this particular layer has raised questions about its origins. Its color differs from the surrounding terrain, and its rock layers are tilted in an unexpected direction. Scientists suspect that processes beyond ordinary wind erosion may have contributed to its formation.

"The yardang layer looks out of place. It's the wrong color, the layers tilt at an odd angle, and it almost appears plastered on," said Ashwin Vasavada, Curiosity project scientist of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "But that's what makes it exciting to reach. No one is sure exactly what created this layer, but one idea is it may be ash deposited by ancient volcanic eruptions."

NASA Plans to Investigate the Cliffs in 2027

Curiosity is now in the second year of its fifth extended mission, which began with an investigation of unusual spiderweb-like boxwork ridges. Over roughly the next year, the rover will continue traveling through rock layers containing abundant sulfates and carbonates, minerals that provide evidence of ancient drying conditions on Mars.

Mission scientists aim to reach the bottom of the yardang formations sometime in 2027. Once there, Curiosity should be able to examine the mysterious cliffs using its robotic arm, gathering unique measurements that could help determine how they formed and reveal more about the environmental changes that shaped Mars billions of years ago.

What night is van Gogh’s ‘The Starry Night’? New analysis says it’s in July.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

The swirling bluish-white mistrals, towering cypress trees, and glittery stars in Vincent van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” (1889) have captivated our imaginations for generations. Despite never selling a painting during his lifetime, van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” has since inspired a hit song in 1972, draws visitors to the Museum of Modern Art in New York City, and adorns everything from journals to scarves and tote bags.

But what particular night above Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France, did the troubled post-impressionist painter actually put on the canvas? A new analysis points to July 22, 1889. During the early morning hours at that time of year, a waning crescent moon, the planet Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in Taurus appeared close together—all of which are seen in the painting. A study published today in the journal Scientific Reports details the findings.

According to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, van Gogh was inspired by the view from his window at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in southern France. He spent twelve months there 1889–1890, seeking reprieve from his mental illnesses and was also diagnosed with a form of epilepsy.

Like many of his paintings that year, “The Starry Night” was created in several sessions during the day, under different weather conditions. The village nestled below the hills was also based on other views, and could not be seen from his window. Although van Gogh reconstructed some of the features in the night sky as he saw them, he also altered the celestial shapes and added a sense of glow.

“Van Gogh assigned an emotional language to night and nature that took them far from their actual appearances,” the museum writes. “Dominated by vivid blues and yellows applied with gestural verve and immediacy, ‘The Starry Night’ also demonstrates how inseparable van Gogh’s vision was from the new procedures of painting he had devised, in which color and paint describe a world outside the artwork even as they telegraph their own status as, merely, color and paint.”

Using astronomy to pinpoint which day inspired the painting began with UCLA art historian Albert Boime in 1984 and Harvard University astronomer and astrophysicist Charles Whitney in 1986. The two scholars reached conflicting conclusions, and neither of them displaced the prevailing religious and symbolic interpretations of the painting.

Historically, the painting has been dated based on a letter van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo around June 18, 1889. However, the waning gibbous moon from mid-June does not account for the slender crescent seen in the painting. By July 22, 1889, the Hyades star cluster rose in the eastern sky alongside Venus, and the moon’s shape looked closer to the crescent on the canvas. This new study uses observational geometry to find an answer.

“To an observational astronomer, the cluster of stars in ‘The Starry Night’ is the Hyades in Taurus,” said study co-author Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim.  “But the moment I read the earlier studies explaining it as Aries, I heard the painting calling to me.” Kim is the CEO at South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a PhD candidate at Kyung Hee University.

For this research, the team combined astronomical simulations and measurements of star positions, with historical weather records, and van Gogh’s own letters. They also used earlier sketches of the landscape to reconstruct the narrow field of view represented in “The Starry Night.”

According to the team, the crescent moon, Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in the constellation Taurus would have been visible around dawn on July 22, 1889. This configuration generally corresponds to what is portrayed in the painting. Weather records suggest that the night sky around July 22 would have been clear enough for this astronomical arrangement to be visible. Additionally, the frequent mentions of cicadas in van Gogh’s letters suggest that the region was seeing a period of hot weather, which matches the recorded temperatures in July 1889 better than in June.

The team also verified that the painter’s field of view in another painting titled “Starry Night over the Rhône” (1888) with photographs taken on site was painted when the sky was in Aries. Using the same method, the team also believes that the stars in “Café Terrace at Night” (1888) may be the Summer Triangle.

“I hope this work prompts a renewed, multidisciplinary look at the sequence and working methods of the Saint-Rémy paintings, and at Van Gogh’s movements that summer,” Kim said. “Our conversation with Van Gogh’s other skies is still ongoing. We plan to tell those stories one by one.”

Coffee calories have jumped nearly 40%. Sugar isn't the biggest culprit

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Americans are getting more calories from their daily coffee, even though the amount they drink has barely changed. Researchers at the Gerald J. and Dorothy R. Friedman School of Nutrition Science and Policy at Tufts University have identified a surprising reason for the increase: non-dairy creamer, which has overtaken sugar and milk as the largest contributor to added calories in coffee.

The study, published online in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, examined dietary information from 30,000 American adults aged 20 and older. Between 2003 and 2018, average coffee consumption stayed relatively constant at approximately 13 ounces per day. However, the average number of calories consumed from coffee increased from 43 to 60 per day, a rise of nearly 40%. By 2018, the leading sources of added coffee calories were non-dairy creamer, sugar or syrup, and dairy milk.

"Coffee consumption has been associated with several health benefits. However, coffee is often accompanied by the addition of sugar and cream, which can contribute to an increased consumption of calories from added sugar and saturated fat," said Fang Fang Zhang, senior author of the study and Neely Family Professor at the Friedman School. "The more we know about what people are consuming, the better we can understand the link between nutrition and overall health."

How Researchers Tracked Changes in Coffee Habits

To investigate how Americans' coffee habits evolved, the research team examined eight consecutive cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), covering 2003 through 2018. The nationally representative dataset included 30,000 adults aged 20 and older, each of whom completed two 24-hour dietary recalls.

Researchers separated coffee beverages into two broad groups: non-combination beverages and those containing additional ingredients. They then classified the additions into several categories, including dairy milk, half-and-half, dairy cream, non-dairy milk, non-dairy creamer, sugar/syrup, sugar substitutes, and other ingredients such as alcohol and cocoa powder.

Some participants reported consuming pre-mixed coffee beverages/powder, making it necessary to estimate the amounts of individual ingredients they contained. To do this, the researchers used the USDA Food and Nutrition Database for Dietary Studies corresponding to each survey cycle, along with linked Standard Reference codes.

Non-Dairy Creamer Consumption More Than Doubled

The analysis showed that Americans' overall coffee intake, including coffee consumed without additions, remained largely unchanged between 2003 and 2018. What changed was the amount and type of ingredients being mixed into those beverages.

In 2018, dairy milk was the most commonly added ingredient by weight, averaging 21.4 grams per day. Non-dairy creamer followed at 8.1 grams per day, while sugar accounted for 4.4 grams per day. Consumption of dairy milk, sugar, and sugar substitutes showed no statistically significant changes over the study period.

Non-dairy additions, however, followed a very different pattern. Their average consumption rose from 3.65 grams per day in 2003 to 10.5 grams per day in 2018. Much of this growth came from non-dairy creamer, which increased from 3.37 to 8 grams per day. Non-dairy milk consumption also climbed sharply, rising from 0.27 to 2.45 grams per day.

Other coffee additions increased slightly as well, with an average gain of 0.5 grams per day.

Why Non-Dairy Creamer Now Adds More Calories Than Sugar

Although dairy milk remained the most common coffee addition by weight, non-dairy creamer had become the leading source of added calories by 2018.

On average, non-dairy creamer contributed 18.5 calories per day, compared with 16 calories from sugar and 11 calories from dairy milk. In 2003, non-dairy creamer had contributed just 7.71 calories per day, illustrating how dramatically its role in Americans' coffee consumption had changed.

"We found that non-dairy creamer being added to coffee has now passed both sugar and dairy for its contribution to the total energy consumed from coffee. Non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, which is calorie dense. This could explain the increased calories that non-dairy creamer is contributing," said Yongyi Pan, the study's lead author and a Ph.D. candidate at the Friedman School. "Even though it has been on the market since the 1950s, non-dairy creamer has increased in popularity in recent years as consumers seek plant-based or dairy alternatives."

Sleep, Work Hours, and Coffee Consumption

The researchers also investigated whether coffee consumption differed across groups of adults based on their daily routines and overall diet quality.

They found that people who slept fewer hours generally drank more coffee than those who slept longer. A similar pattern emerged among adults who worked longer hours, who tended to consume more coffee than those with shorter work schedules.

However, coffee consumption was comparable among people with higher and lower overall diet quality, suggesting that drinking more or less coffee was not strongly associated with how healthy their broader diets were.

The researchers acknowledged that the study relied on participants recalling what they had eaten and drunk, which can introduce measurement errors. Because food and beverage consumption can vary considerably from day to day, self-reported dietary information does not always perfectly reflect people's usual habits. The research team took steps to improve the reliability of its estimates.

Co-author Bingjie Zhou contributed to the research while pursuing a Ph.D. at the Friedman School.

Experts: How are climate scientists using artificial intelligence?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence (AI) is developing at a rapid pace and is increasingly being used by scientists to better understand climate change.

AI is perhaps best-known for its use in “large language models”, such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini.

From weather forecasting to analysing satellite images, climate scientists are using various types of AI to develop faster and more efficient ways to study the warming planet.

However, the technology comes with no shortage of risks and challenges, including overreliance on unverified results, intensive energy use and exacerbating existing inequalities in climate science.

Carbon Brief spoke to 10 experts working across a range of climate-related fields – from developing climate forecasts to analysing changes in the energy grid – about how AI is being used.

Their answers are set out below, grouped by theme.

Dr Doug McNeall, science lead in data-driven climate modelling at the UK Met Office:

One of the most significant recent developments has been the success of AI weather-prediction models, which learn from large volumes of weather data to predict the next few hours ahead and then repeatedly feed those predictions back into themselves to generate forecasts up to two weeks into the future.

These models have demonstrated that AI can provide accurate predictions while requiring far less computing power than existing numerical models [which rely on mathematical equations to simulate the climate].

One of the main challenges is that AI weather-prediction models have been developed for relatively short timescales, where large amounts of observational data [for example, temperature, rainfall or sea ice extent data] is available and model performance can be evaluated quickly and rigorously.

Climate prediction is fundamentally different. To provide useful climate information, we need to make projections for future climates that lie outside the observational record.

One promising route forward is, therefore, to use AI to emulate or accelerate these existing models, rather than relying solely on purely data-driven approaches. This blended approach could make climate risk information far more accessible and affordable to produce, while retaining the physical understanding [based on mathematical and scientific principles] that underpins confidence in climate projections.

Prof William Collins, professor of AI for climate science at the University of East Anglia:

We’re seeing a really exciting renaissance in both weather and climate science…Machine learning [a subset of AI that uses algorithms to let computers learn from data and make predictions without being explicitly programmed] has had an enormous impact on numerical weather prediction.

So, for example, the European Center for Mid-Range Weather Forecasts is now deploying an AI forecast system as part of their official communication of weather predictions to the European community, alongside the traditional forecast system. AI systems have become good enough that they’re absolutely competitive with the world’s leading numerical weather prediction…So that’s been quite remarkable.

Much of machine learning and AI involves learning from data and climate represents a future for which we do not have data. We’re literally trying to predict the future. We don’t have observations of it. So, where does the data come from to inform the models? That is, at the moment, the central challenge that the climate community is attempting to overcome.

Dr Amirpasha Mozaffari, researcher in Earth artificial intelligence at the Barcelona Supercomputing Centre:

AI forecast models do well on typical weather, but underperform physics-based models on record-breaking and unprecedented extremes – precisely the events that matter most for adaptation [to climate change].

Solving that – whether through hybrid models [which combine AI with traditional models], physical constraints [in which rules are implemented based on the laws of physics to help the models make realistic forecasts] or new ways of evaluating models on what they have not seen – is the central open problem for the field.

Dr Stefano Materia, researcher in climate variability and change at the Barcelona Supercomputing Centre:

Besides the climate modelling and prediction framework, the proliferation of Earth-observation satellites has created a massive influx of geospatial data (for example, images showing deforestation or sea ice melt). AI is used to translate raw pixel data into actionable climate intelligence.

Methane tracking and ecosystem monitoring are two examples of this kind of application. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas, but leaks are largely invisible and hard to track. AI algorithms now automatically scan hyperspectral imagery [which uses data from across the electromagnetic spectrum] from satellites to pinpoint specific industrial methane leaks globally, alerting operators in near real-time.

At the same time, machine-learning models process thousands of satellite images daily to detect illegal logging, track land-use changes and monitor glacier retreat long before humans could manually review the footage.

Dr Clare Eayrs, polar research scientist at the Environmental Fluid Dynamics Lab at New York University:

In sea ice research, machine learning is now used across the sea ice modelling workflow…The most mature application is extracting more information from observations, for example, mapping fine-scale sea ice deformation from ship radar and classifying snow layers in micropenetrometer profiles [which measure the hardness or softness of different layers of snow].

Forecasting is the most visible application in this field. Ice Net [a sea ice forecasting system] was an early demonstration that a deep-learning system [which uses models inspired by the layers of a human brain to process information] could outperform a leading physical model in seasonal Arctic forecasts. Machine-learning systems are now used to predict [climate] variables such as sea ice concentration, thickness and drift.

Prof Cristiana Stan, vice-chair of the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP):

AI now appears across all major parts of the WCRP. [For example,]…the ”weather prediction model intercomparison project” [an initiative to create a centralised database of physics- based, machine-learning and hybrid model forecasts] includes physics-based numerical weather prediction models, hybrid [models] and AI weather prediction models. Because it is focused on operational models, the project offers a direct comparison between state-of-the art systems. [It] is intended to accelerate the deployment of the next generation of operational models and contributes to development of metrics needed to assess relative strengths and weaknesses of models.

Downscaling is another area that is harnessing AI power to refine the coarse-resolution model output into high-resolution.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is performing its seventh assessment report (AR7)…This time, for the very first time, there’s a model intercomparison project just for models using AI [for weather and climate forecasting]. So that is a real milestone…It’s a very exciting proof of principle or proof of concept and, I think, the harbinger of many more developments of this kind to come.

[For more on how model intercomparison will inform IPCC AR7, see Carbon Brief’s recent guest post.]

A few years ago, it took two or three years for a new architecture to make its way from the machine-learning community into weather and climate work. Today it is a matter of months: diffusion models, foundation models and generative ensembles have all arrived in our field almost as soon as they appeared elsewhere. That speed opens up things that were previously out of reach.

And the cost of running a global forecast has dropped by orders of magnitude…which, in principle, changes who can produce weather and climate information at all.

Prof Priya Donti, assistant professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and co-founder of Climate Change AI:

As we introduce more solar, wind and other renewable energy into the power grid, we need to rethink how we actually manage the various sources on the power grid…

AI is used in a few different ways here. One of them is just trying to get better predictions of solar and wind power production in order to reduce that uncertainty and allow us to plan with a little more foresight.

Another big one is helping to actually optimise and control the various components we have on power grids – the power generators, the batteries, flexible loads like industrial loads or the batteries in your electric vehicles or heating and cooling systems.

Dr Carlos Rodriguez-Pardo, postdoctoral researcher at Politecnico di Milano, affiliated scientist at the European Institute on Economics and the Environment and at the Euro-Mediterranean Center on Climate Change:

AI is already useful at every stage of the climate policy cycle.

The first stage is measurement: understanding what is happening, what has been tried and who is exposed. Machine learning helps us process satellite imagery to estimate emissions, improve air quality monitoring, harmonise fragmented policy databases and map a scientific literature that has grown far beyond what any human team could read.

The second stage is modelling futures. The models we use to explore climate and economic scenarios are computationally very expensive, so AI-based emulators [simple models that can be used to represent more complex mathematical models] that reproduce their behaviour at a small fraction of the cost are transforming what we can do. Instead of a handful of scenarios, we can explore thousands and quantify uncertainties that were previously out of reach.

Reinforcement learning [a type of AI in which a computer programme learns to make decisions through a process of trial and error] is also allowing us to study how multiple actors, such as countries or electricity producers, compete and cooperate under different policy designs. This can reveal strategies that classical analysis misses.

The third stage is evaluation, where causal machine learning is extending the toolkit we use to answer whether a policy actually worked. That is a different – and much harder – question than predicting what will happen next.

The WCRP has established a task team to address this very question. The focus areas considered by the task team include modelling and projections, data and observations [and AI] impacts and services, engagement with the private sector supporting development and integration of AI and machine learning into climate research and applications and the scientific rigour and trustworthiness of AI and machine learning methods.

I think the IPCC’s eighth assessment [likely due in the late 2020s] may itself largely be written using large language models. Basically, the job of the people writing the IPCC report is to synthesise…One could certainly argue whether or not that needs to be done by people. AI should be telling us the known unknowns, not the known knowns… The thing that’s value-added [by AI] as part of that assessment process is to tell us what isn’t certain, what isn’t known well. Because then that tells you where the science should be heading.

[In September 2026, the IPCC established an “informal group” on AI to “provide timely guidance on the responsible use of AI and emerging digital technologies in IPCC processes”.]

AI will also be an integral part of the eighth Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, with machine-learning codes running alongside traditional climate models.

The inclusion of AI in climate and land-vegetation modelling represents a paradigm shift. Rather than replacing physical models entirely, hybrid modelling merges the mathematical rigour and physical consistency of traditional process-based Earth system models [advanced computer models that combine physical, chemical and biological processes to simulate the climate] with the pattern-recognition power and speed of machine learning.

This new approach will be able to overcome computational bottlenecks, replace unresolved subgrid processes [which are too small to be captured by the grid cells of a computer model], improve the terrestrial biosphere – finally narrowing down the uncertainties on the terrestrial carbon sink and accurately pinpointing emergent constraints and non-linear tipping points.

Much of what we know about Antarctic sea ice comes from the satellite era, which begins in 1979. That is a short record for a system with strong decadal variability. A great deal of information about earlier conditions is contained in ship logs, whaling records, expedition photographs and early aerial and pre-1979 satellite imagery. Machine learning is well suited to extracting it. Lengthening the observational record would improve almost everything else we do, including training and testing AI systems.

AI is frequently thought of as large generative models, like [those] developed by large tech companies. But that is not mostly what is useful in AI for tackling climate change.

People talk a lot about energy usage of AI and negative impacts on society, including on the climate, as a result of that energy usage and associated negative impacts like water usage. These are very real worries. However, it is worth noting that the massive energy and other requirements of AI systems are referring to those large-scale generative and agentic systems. They’re not mostly about the kinds of AI systems that are actually used to fight climate change.

I think the general public thinks of AI as this one specific thing…often large language models, chatbots, etc…People who are making allocation decisions for who funds scientists might have a mismatched understanding of what needs to happen, which means the right science doesn’t necessarily get funded.

Frontier models [which are at the cutting edge of AI research] are built almost exclusively in the global north, trained on data that is densest where observations are richest and validated against benchmarks that reflect the same regions.

Cheap inference [in which a trained AI model applies its learned patterns to new, unseen data] does not fix that on its own: being able to run someone else’s model is not the same as having a say in what it is trained on, how it is evaluated, or whether it serves your decisions.

Without deliberate effort to share data infrastructure, evaluation metrics that centre on people’s wellbeing and genuine co-production of knowledge, AI risks automating and amplifying the existing divide in the global climate information system rather than closing it.

If done well, [developing AI-based Earth system models] could dramatically lower barriers to entry. A small research group, or an agency in a data-poor region, could adapt a pre-trained model on modest hardware rather than building bespoke systems from scratch. That matters because [the] data is thinnest and the resolution gap is largest exactly where vulnerability to climate [change] is highest.

Dr David Rolnick, associate professor in the school of computer science at McGill University and cofounder of Climate Change AI:

Climate action should fundamentally be about democratisation of action and equitable response to climate change, both in terms of mitigation and in terms of adaptation. The consolidation of large-scale AI can often work counter to those goals by consolidating power in certain geographies and stakeholders, and by defining action based upon the goals of those stakeholders.

When you build technology in specific institutions, they do not necessarily understand the needs of the majority of the world’s population or local stakeholders from other geographies and other entities.

The biggest challenge is trust…Good performance is necessary, but it is not enough. We also need independent evaluation, physical credibility, process-based validation, robustness and some degree of interpretability.

The risk I worry about most is that the apparent success of these systems could be read as a reason to invest less on observations. AI is not a substitute for observation, physical understanding or careful evaluation. Especially for Antarctic sea ice, where observations of sea ice and snow thickness remain sparse and understanding of snow-ice processes remains limited, we need more, targeted observations, not fewer. It also makes the case for documenting them – and their uncertainties – far better than we do now.

The challenge is always making sure that you’re getting the right answer for the right reason and the risk is getting a plausible answer that is “truthy”, but not the truth…Ask a computer for an answer and it will give you one. And it’s very good. It will always obey. It will always give you an answer. The question is whether or not that answer is valid or not.

The first and foremost risk is AI diverging attention from other forms of climate action. AI is not a silver bullet. It is not an answer in itself. And where it is useful, it is only one part of the solution.

Prof Detlef van Vuuren, a senior researcher at the PBL Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency [in a 2026 Carbon Brief interview]:

There are now many more proposals being submitted than in the past, as people can use AI tools. So our science discipline has to start learning how to deal with this, both in terms of what we feel is allowed in creating [scientific] articles – what is allowed in reviewing [and] what is allowed in proposal writing. This is something that we have to learn.

The partnership between academia, the data centres and private industry is going to be integral to our success going forward. And I think the challenge is going to be navigating that relationship in a way that respects open science, uses open source code, etc…

You do not want to be in a situation where we have something like a large language model where it’s not open source…[We need] fairness, accountability, transparency and explainability.

High-resolution outputs can look exact when they are not; uncertainties compound in invisible ways and predictions that feed into policy need honest, well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, communicated in decision-relevant terms.

Like any other technology, AI should augment human judgement in climate decisions, not replace it. As researchers, it is our responsibility to ensure the field serves as a force for social progress.

Meta’s advertising growth in Europe is slowing now that residents have the option for less of their personal data to influence what advertisements they see.

Advertising revenue in Europe continued to decelerate in the three months through September, the company said on Wednesday, underperforming its global figures. In the region, advertising revenue grew 18 percent year-on-year, slowing from the previous two quarters and lagging the 33 percent growth seen in the US and Canada.

Though Meta did not explicitly blame the introduction of the Less-Personalized Ads model on Wednesday, chief financial officer Susan Li had previously warned “we’re seeing the impact of less personalized ads offering in Europe now that it’s fully rolled out, and that may be an additional headwind,” in July’s second quarter results follow-up call. Li told investors on Wednesday the company has “a lot of runway” to increase ad conversions and efficiency, as it is “continuing to find ways to either enhance the actual performance of the recommendation engine.” Rothschild & Co Redburn’s Ball believes the company will be able to use AI to improve its ad targeting in Europe despite working with fewer personal data signals.

Investors were keen to understand how this fits into Meta’s personal AI agent, Muse, launched last month, questions about which dominated the call. I Worked for a Whole Day Using Only the iPhone Duo. Here’s How It Went

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An iPhone 18 Pro is $1,199. An iPad is $449. If you want both—the iPhone as your primary device and the iPad to handle some work when you’re away from your desk—you'll be spending a total dollar amount that’s pretty close to the $1,999 price of Apple’s first folding smartphone, the iPhone Duo.

So can the Duo’s 7.6-inch screen pull double duty as a phone and a workstation? Does it make sense to shell out a few hundred more dollars for a single gadget that can theoretically handle the workload of two devices?

I wrote my entire iPhone Duo review on Apple’s new handset. I used a note-taking app called Bear, which was mercifully optimized for the Duo’s larger screen. (Read my review and you’ll hear about how most apps aren’t optimized; it could take weeks or months for everything to look right.) But surprisingly, I didn’t hate the experience.

I have run this experiment before on Android foldables and even smart glasses with virtual workspaces, but iOS has its own unique hurdles. Notably, iOS doesn’t recognize a Bluetooth mouse or trackpad by default. Instead, you have to go to the phone’s accessibility settings and enable AssistiveTouch—then you’ll get an iPad-like cursor that lets you navigate the Duo’s interface and select text without constantly reaching for the touchscreen.

Even with the cursor, small tasks like pasting hyperlinks into a written piece or quickly Googling topics for additional research are slower and more cumbersome, so I left those for the end when I moved everything into WIRED’s content management system. Even then, I had to switch over to my desktop for the final touches.

Apple also imposes arbitrary multitasking restrictions in iOS because, well, Apple likes to control how people use its devices. For example, if you open two apps in split screen mode, you can only view them in an equal 50-50 split. Unlike most operating systems that let you adjust how much of each app you can see by dragging the border in between the windows, iOS leaves you stuck with a view that gives both apps equal screen space. If you want to use the iPhone in portrait orientation and stack two apps vertically, one on the top part of the screen and the other on the bottom, you’re out of luck. In portrait mode, you can only pin video apps to the top with another app below.

One thing Apple did get right is app pairs. You can “pin” two apps together, and they will live in your Recents, ready when you need them. Say you always want to look at your email and calendar together; you can pair them and find them in Recents whenever you need them. (Tap the bar in the middle to lock and save the pair.)

My keyboard of choice is an old Seenda folding Bluetooth keyboard that is admittedly not very good. I imagine we'll see an array of compact keyboards hitting the market soon—like Nillkin or ProtoArc keyboards already available now—to cater to workaholics who need to work everywhere. I also used one of Apple's cases, the iPhone Duo Folio with Kickstand, to keep the phone propped up. You can use various MagSafe kickstands, tripods, or cases with the Duo.

I typed most of the day, but I also had to juggle between apps to respond to coworkers or hop on a Zoom call. Thankfully, the Slack iOS app updated the day before my testing to fully support the iPhone Duo's larger screen and vertical navigation, making it very easy to bounce between multiple conversations in the two-pane view. I still think Apple should add a feature like the iPad's Slide Over, where you can “hide” a floating app off to the side and bring it back in when you need it. It’d be the perfect spot for messaging apps like Slack, WhatsApp, or Messages, and it wouldn’t interrupt your split screen experience.

Zoom wasn’t optimized for the Duo, though my editor said the video quality from the inner and outer selfie cameras was “really good.” I used the folio kickstand to Zoom on the big 7.6-inch screen until we realized my video was stuck in an awkward portrait orientation, so I laid the phone down on the table and pointed the 5.4-inch outer screen toward my face, which made it feel like a traditional laptop experience.

The app that annoyed me the most was Gmail. Even though Google updated the Google Search app to be compatible with the iPhone Duo early this week, the rest of Google's workspace apps currently look awful on the Duo. I imagine full support will come very soon, if not on Friday, when the Duo officially launches. The two-pane view in Gmail (or any email app) is a game changer for clearing a bunch of emails when you’re on the go.

By 6 pm, battery life was sitting around 45 percent, which is very impressive. That's with around six hours of screen-on time.

You probably won’t want to replace your laptop with the iPhone Duo. And anyone accustomed to the iPad’s larger screen may feel cramped on the Duo's 7.6-inch canvas. But with a kickstand and the right portable keyboard? Knocking out focused, light work on the Duo is entirely doable. Things will get even more interesting once Apple Pencil support arrives later this year.

But as I packed up my keyboard, I couldn’t shake a familiar feeling. Maybe we’ll realize a single do-it-all device isn’t as great as it’s chalked up to be. Maybe it is better to kick back with a dedicated MP3 player. Or snap photos with a camera. Or write in a notebook. Or work off a laptop.

Speaking of which, did you hear Apple’s making a touchscreen MacBook?

In Libyen eingetroffen – EDA bestätigt: Schweizer Geisel aus Niger befreit

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Einige Elemente auf SRF.ch funktionieren nur mit aktiviertem JavaScript.

Die Schweizer Staatsagehörige war am 13. April 2025 in Niger entführt worden. Sie sei nun in Bengasi in Libyen von Schweizer Mitarbeitenden der Bundesverwaltung in Empfang genommen und medizinisch betreut worden, so das EDA auf Anfrage von SRF. Ihr gehe es den Umständen entsprechend gut, heisst es weiter. Aus Gründen des Persönlichkeitsschutzes macht das EDA keine weiteren Angaben.

«Ich bin erleichtert, dass die entführte Schweizerin nach mehr als eineinhalb Jahren wieder in Freiheit ist. Ich danke allen involvierten Stellen im In- und Ausland, die sich intensiv für die Freilassung eingesetzt haben», so Bundesrat Ignazio Cassis auf X.

Das EDA hatte nach eigenen Angaben nach Bekanntwerden des Entführungsfalls eine Task Force gebildet, um die Freilassung der Geisel zu erreichen: «Der Task Force gehörten unter anderem das Bundesamt für Polizei Fedpol, der Nachrichtendienst des Bundes sowie weitere Bundesstellen und kantonale Behörden an. Das EDA spricht allen nationalen und internationalen Partnern seinen Dank für die gute Zusammenarbeit in diesem Fall aus. Insbesondere mit Österreich tauschte sich die Schweiz von Anfang an regelmässig aus.»

«Zwei Geiseln, eine Österreicherin und eine Schweizerin, sind nach eineinhalb Jahren Gefangenschaft in Niger freigelassen worden und dank der Intervention des stellvertretenden Oberbefehlshabers Saddam Haftar in Bengasi eingetroffen», hatte zuvor der libysche Sender al-Masar berichtet.

Generalleutnant Saddam Khalifa Haftar habe sich auf Bitten der Familien für ihre Freilassung eingesetzt, teilte der Sender auf der Plattform X weiter mit.

In einem von al-Masar ausgestrahlten Video und auf Fotos der libyschen Armee wirkten die beiden Frauen erschöpft. Unter einem Porträt von Marschall Khalifa Haftar sitzend bedankten sie sich auf Französisch bei ihren Befreiern.

«Ich wurde vor einem Jahr und sechs Monaten als Geisel genommen. Jetzt bin ich froh, frei zu sein, und danke Marschall Khalifa Haftar und seinem Sohn, General Saddam Haftar, für alles, was sie für meine Freilassung getan haben», sagte die 69-jährige Schweizerin in dem Video. Ähnlich äusserte sich darin die Österreicherin. Sie sei ein Jahr und neun Monate eine Geisel gewesen.

Libyen ist seit dem Sturz von Machthaber Muammar al-Gaddafi im Jahr 2011 von Spaltungen geprägt. Zwei rivalisierende Regierungen ringen um die Macht: die von der UNO anerkannte Regierung von Abdelhamid Dbeibah mit Sitz in Tripolis und eine weitere im Osten des Landes, die vom mächtigen Marschall Khalifa Haftar und dessen Umfeld kontrolliert wird.
