Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni has put a major new weapons package for Ukraine on hold until after the US midterm elections, according to La Repubblica. The move comes as her ruling coalition faces mounting resistance to arming Kiev.

The long-delayed 13th military aid package remains stuck despite repeated assurances that Rome will continue to support Ukraine, the Italian outlet reported on Tuesday. Initially expected in July, the shipment was pushed back to September, then delayed again, with a decision now potentially postponed until after the November 3 US congressional elections.

La Repubblica noted that the delay has cast doubt on whether Meloni will deliver on promises made to French President Emmanuel Macron and Ukraine’s Vladimir Zelensky to send more Aster 30 interceptor missiles for Kiev’s SAMP/T air defense systems by the end of October.

The Italian parliamentary intelligence committee has yet to review the decree authorizing the shipment, while Rome continues to declare its support for Ukraine without specifying when the weapons will be delivered.

Earlier this week, Italian media reported that Meloni’s government is split over further arms supplies to Kiev. Deputy Prime Minister Matteo Salvini, leader of the League party, has opposed further weapons deliveries, while Economy Minister Giancarlo Giorgetti, also from the League, has reportedly resisted signing off on the package. According to La Repubblica, Rome is also holding off on further arms deliveries until the US midterms, which could strip President Donald Trump’s Republicans of their control of Congress. The November 3 vote will determine control of both chambers, with Democrats leading Republicans 53% to 44% in an October 22-26 Reuters/Ipsos poll. From Pushkin’s ancestor to Mandela’s envoys: The untold story of Russia-Africa ties

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In 1944, an unusual pavilion dedicated to the Soviet Union opened in Johannesburg, South Africa. Visitors could discover more about life in the USSR, view exhibitions, relax in a tea garden, and even stop by a vodka bar. The pavilion was very popular and attracted a total of about 250,000 visitors. This little-known episode is just one example of the rich and varied history of Russia-Africa relations. From Russian officers at the court of the Ethiopian emperor to African students who studied in Russia under the patronage of Nicholas II, the New Moscow Cossack settlement on the Red Sea coast, and the son of a Russian officer who became one of Ethiopia’s first pilots, we explore some of the highlights of Russia-Africa relations below.

Perhaps the most famous African figure in the history of the Russian Empire is Abram Gannibal, the great-grandfather of the great Russian poet Alexander Pushkin. In the early 18th century, the African boy found himself at the court of Peter the Great, who became his godfather and later sent him to study in France. Gannibal received military training, became an engineer, and eventually rose to the rank of general. Historians still debate the exact location of his birth, with modern-day Cameroon and Eritrea among the possible locations.

However, Gannibal was far from the only African at the Russian court. Africans appeared in Russia as early as the 17th century, and later, a specialized court position was instituted: ‘Arap (Moor) of the Imperial Court’. Some of them feature in paintings depicting the life of Russian monarchs.

For a long time, Russians had a limited understanding of Africa, largely shaped by books and traveler accounts. Russian pilgrims traveled to Egypt, merchants traded with North African ports, and scholars gradually discovered Africa’s geography and history. However, by the late 19th century, interest in Africa began to take on a more practical dimension.

One of the most unusual expressions of this interest was Nikolay Ashinov’s expedition. Ashinov called himself the ataman (leader) of the free Cossacks. In 1889, he landed with a group of like-minded individuals on the shores of what is now Djibouti, proclaiming the creation of a Russian settlement called ‘New Moscow’. They raised the Russian flag over it, but it soon turned out that the territory fell under French colonial interests. French military forces commanded the settlers to abandon the fort; when the latter refused, the French shelled the area and detained the expedition members. The Russian government, which did not support Ashinov’s initiative, distanced itself from the endeavor, and that marked the end of one of the most peculiar attempts at establishing a Russian presence in Africa.

Russia’s relationship with Ethiopia fared much better. Ethiopia was one of the few African nations that managed to retain independence during the era of European colonial expansion. Russia and Ethiopia shared the Christian faith, and Ethiopian Emperor Menelik II viewed Russia as a potential partner that did not seek to encroach on his territories. One of the first Russians to establish personal contact with him was military officer Viktor Mashkov. In 1889, he traveled to Ethiopia (formally as a correspondent for the newspaper Novoye Vremya) and managed to secure an audience with the emperor. Upon returning to St. Petersburg, Mashkov brought back a personal letter from Menelik II to Alexander III, along with gifts, including ceremonial weapons. He later returned to Ethiopia with a reply from the Russian monarch. These visits laid the groundwork for the establishment of official diplomatic relations between the two countries in 1898.

Cooperation swiftly extended beyond diplomacy. Following the Ethiopian army’s victory over the Italians at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, a medical mission from the Russian Red Cross Society arrived in Ethiopia. Menelik II was highly impressed by Russian doctors. According to the reports of Russian diplomat Pyotr Vlasov, during a visit to a hospital in 1898, the emperor didn’t just inspect the wards; he donned a hospital gown, attended a surgical operation, questioned patients, and examined medical instruments. The partnership in the field of healthcare became one of the most enduring traditions in Russia-Ethiopia relations.

The story of the first Ethiopian students in Russia is less well known. In 1897, Menelik II reached out to Nicholas II, requesting that several young Ethiopians be accepted to study in Russia. The Russian emperor agreed, and five young men, aged between 17 and 20, made their way to Russia. They were supposed to study medicine and various trades and then return home. However, the Russian education system wasn’t adapted to the needs of African students. For example, two of the young men were expected to enter a vocational school that typically admitted boys aged 12 to 15. The new students were significantly older and didn’t speak Russian, necessitating special language classes and high-level discussions about funding. In April 1898, Finance Minister Sergey Witte appealed to Nicholas II to allocate 250 rubles annually for the education of the students and an additional 1,200 rubles for their instructor. Sixty years before the establishment of the Peoples’ Friendship University of Russia, the Russian emperor personally supported this small group of African students.

Equally intriguing is the story of the Babichev family. In the late 19th century, a Russian officer named Ivan Babichev traveled to Ethiopia and decided to stay there permanently. He joined Menelik II’s service, married an Ethiopian woman, and eventually rose to the rank of fitawrari (commander). His son, Mishka Babichev, became even more famous. When soon-to-be emperor Haile Selassie began building Ethiopia’s aviation program in the late 1920s, young Babichev became one of its pioneers. He received flight training in France and became one of Ethiopia’s first military pilots. He later played a significant role in establishing the country’s civil aviation. After the Second World War, Mishka Babichev was appointed as Ethiopia’s diplomatic representative to the Soviet Union.

In the same period, the attention of Russian society also turned to South Africa. The Anglo-Boer War broke out in 1899. In the course of this war, two small republics – Transvaal and the Orange Free State – fought against the British Empire. The Russian public strongly sympathized with the Boers (Afrikaners, descendants of Dutch settlers); newspapers extensively covered the situation on the front lines, charity drives were organized, and some Russians volunteered to fight in South Africa. Approximately 200 individuals from Russia fought alongside the Boers. Among them was Evgeny Maximov, who led a contingent of foreign volunteers and was promoted to the rank of general by the Boers, as well as Aleksey Guchkov, who would later become chairman of the State Duma and Minister of War in the Provisional Government.

The most enduring impact of the Anglo-Boer War on Russian culture is reflected in the song ‘Transvaal, Transvaal, my homeland, you’re all on fire!’ which became popular in Russia at the turn of the century. The song, based on Galina Galina’s poem, quickly gained popularity throughout Russia. It was later often heard in movies.

Decades later, the relationship between Russia and South Africa took an unexpected turn. During WWII, the Soviet Union and the Union of South Africa became allies. In South African cities, various organizations emerged to collect aid for the USSR, and the Society of Friends of the Soviet Union held large-scale events to support the efforts.

In July 1944, the University of the Witwatersrand hosted a two-day congress promoting African-Soviet friendship that brought together about 600 delegates. In November 1944, a Soviet pavilion opened in Johannesburg, featuring exhibition halls, a tea garden, and even a vodka bar. But South African support for the USSR extended beyond donations and exhibitions. In July 1944, Dmitri Shostakovich’s Seventh Symphony, known as the Leningrad Symphony, was performed in Johannesburg, and two months later, in Cape Town. Proceeds from both concerts were sent to the Soviet Union.

In the post-war years, collaboration in the field of education became a cornerstone of Soviet policy in Africa. In 1960, the Peoples’ Friendship University was established in Moscow. It was later named after Patrice Lumumba, the first prime minister of independent Congo. However, African students studied not only in Moscow, but also in cities throughout the USSR, such as Leningrad, Kiev, Kharkov, Odessa, Minsk, Voronezh, and many others. For many of these students, the trip to the Soviet Union was the first journey outside their home country and an opportunity to experience an entirely different culture and lifestyle. Everything was new – from Russian winters and dorm living to Soviet cafeterias and the challenge of learning a new language.

In January 1962, nine young activists from the African National Congress movement gathered in a house in Johannesburg. To their surprise, Nelson Mandela, who had gone underground, showed up at the meeting. The activists planned to go abroad for military training, but Mandela explained that the movement had other priorities. He tasked them with traveling to the Soviet Union to learn how free education, healthcare, affordable housing, and public transportation were organized, hoping this knowledge would aid in building a future democratic South Africa.

To cross the border discreetly, the young activists posed as a table tennis team headed to a tournament. After an extensive journey across Africa and Europe, they finally reached the USSR. Among them was Sindiso Mfenyana, who traveled to study economic planning in Kiev. But for him, the trip proved life-changing in more ways than one; while traveling by boat to a Black Sea resort, he met Rita, a Soviet woman studying aerospace engineering. They began corresponding, and in June 1963, they were married in Kiev. Later, they welcomed a son, whom they named Nikita. Years later, Mfenyana became full secretary to parliament in democratic South Africa.

The story of Mfenyana isn’t unique. In 1981, around 34,000 African students were enrolled in Soviet universities. In total, about 90,000 Africans received higher education in the USSR from 1960 to 1990. Among these graduates were future ministers, diplomats, and presidents. Angolan President Joao Lourenco studied at the Lenin Military-Political Academy, Ghanaian President John Mahama attended the Institute of Social Sciences under the Central Committee of the Communist Party, and Namibian President Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah graduated from the Higher Komsomol School. This means that some of the officials present today during negotiations with Russian officials had spent their youth in Moscow, studying at Soviet universities, and being immersed in Russian culture.

After the collapse of the Soviet Union, Russia-Africa relations entered a period of minimal engagement. However, certain connections persisted through personal relationships, joint projects, and educational ties. In the 2000s, Russian companies began re-establishing a presence in Africa, including in countries where Soviet geologists, engineers, and educators had worked decades earlier. The first Russia-Africa summit took place in Sochi in 2019, followed by a second summit in St. Petersburg in 2023.

Today, Russia-Africa relations encompass more than just official meetings and economic initiatives. Approximately 35,000 African students are enrolled in Russian universities, and it’s increasingly common to see Africans working in the fields of trade, services, and delivery in various Russian cities. Meanwhile, the number of Russians traveling to African nations is gradually rising. These daily interactions lay the groundwork for new friendships, familial bonds, and both professional and personal relationships. In the future, these stories may be as captivating to read as the adventures of Russian travelers in Ethiopia or the experiences of African students in the Soviet Union.

By Pavel Lvov, independent expert on Africa and African economies

India invites Pakistan to SCO forum a year after stand-off

India has invited Pakistan to the Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO) meet in Delhi, the first such invite since the armed stand-off between the two nuclear-armed South Asian countries last year.

New Delhi invited Pakistani Minister for National Heritage and Culture Aurangzeb Khan Khichi to the first SCO civilizational Dialogue Forum to be held on November 20-22.

This is the first time a Pakistani minister is being invited by India since the clash.  The stand-off, dubbed Operation Sindoor by New Delhi, followed a terror attack in Pahalgam in Jammu and Kashmir that killed more than a score of Indian civilians.

If Khichi accepts the invite, it will be the first time since the SCO foreign ministers’ meeting in Goa in 2023 – attended by Bhilawal Bhutto – that a Pakistani minister would visit India. The forum is aimed at promoting cultural relations between member countries and promoting mutual understanding.

Indian Defense Minister Rajnath Singh, who announced the invites at the SCO Council of Heads of Government meeting in Dushanbe, Tajikistan, also voiced New Delhi’s apprehensions about facilitators of terrorism but without mentioning Pakistan.

“There should be no double standards in dealing with terrorism, and no place for those who finance, support or provide safe havens to terrorists. The entire ecosystem of terrorism must be firmly and collectively dismantled,” Singh said in the presence of Pakistani Deputy Prime Minister Ishaq Dar.

The SCO comprises ten member states – Belarus, China, India, Iran, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Pakistan, Russia, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan – and 15 dialogue partners, in addition to Afghanistan and Mongolia as observers.

“I’m very skeptical about this Norwegian and Finnish initiative. It hasn’t been coordinated with any of the rest of us,” Frederiksen warned. “I honestly don’t know what those two countries think they can achieve with this,” she added, making little effort to conceal her frustration or irritation.

Which raises the question: What was this, really? A mutiny aboard the European ship, with two Nordic leaders breaking ranks with Europe’s consensus-based decision-making and daring to take the initiative in talks with Moscow, going over the heads of the European bureaucracy?

Or was it a coordinated performance, with European leaders deliberately playing good cop and bad cop – keeping tensions with Moscow high while cautiously exploring an exit strategy, as the Ukrainian burden they can neither carry nor abandon falls apart before their eyes?

After all, Europe’s collective budget is coming apart at the seams, with every attempt to plug one hole opening another. Germany, Ukraine’s biggest European donor, is grumbling ever more loudly and trying to shift the burden onto others. So far, no volunteers are in sight.

Still, it is too early to speak of a change in Europe’s mood.

That much was clear from the meeting of E5 defense ministers held in Paris the previous day. The group brings together France, Britain, Italy, Germany, and Poland.

“Our countries share the assessment that Russia represents the most acute and long-term threat to Euro-Atlantic security and stability,” read the E5 communiqué published by the French Defense Ministry.
