COP17: Developed countries ‘fall far short’ of UN targets for nature finance

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The amount of finance for nature delivered to date “falls far short” of what is needed to meet the UN’s global goals, according to a report released ahead of the COP17 biodiversity summit.

The global stocktaking report, prepared by the UN Convention on Biological Diversity (UN CBD) ahead of this month’s summit in Armenia, finds that developed nations are failing to close the $200bn “biodiversity finance gap”.

Initiatives designed to support this goal are also stumbling, with a special “framework” fund for biodiversity receiving less than $140m from developed countries in the past two years and the “landmark” Cali Fund receiving only $6,000 from private companies.

The availability of finance – or lack thereof – is likely to be a key point of contention in negotiations at COP17 in Yerevan, Armenia, which begin on 19 October.

At COP15 in 2022, 196 countries agreed to an ambitious global deal to reverse biodiversity loss by 2030, dubbed the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF).

Goal D of the agreement, shown below, states that countries must “close” a $700bn “biodiversity finance gap” by 2030.

This overall $700bn is made up of two parts: changes to nature-harming subsidies; and raising financial resources to support biodiversity.

Target 18 within the text calls for countries to “identify by 2025, and eliminate, phase out or reform incentives, including subsidies, harmful for biodiversity…progressively reducing them by at least $500bn per year by 2030”.

The remaining $200bn should be met by “mobilising” resources, according to target 19 of the GBF. It states that countries should “substantially and progressively increase the level of financial resources from all sources” to “at least” $200bn per year by 2030.

The overarching $200bn can be from national budgets, multilateral development banks or money “mobilised” from the private sector.

However, the target also specifically states that “at least $20bn per year by 2025 and…at least $30bn per year by 2030” should be public biodiversity funding provided to developing countries by developed countries – or others that “voluntarily assume” their obligations.

According to the global stocktaking report prepared by the CBD, between 2020 and 2023, countries mobilised a cumulative total of just $186.4bn. This is equivalent to an average of less than $50bn per year.

While the report – published on 10 September 2026 –  cautions that it is “still too early to draw any conclusions”, it adds that this total “falls far short” of what is needed to close the $200bn-per-year biodiversity finance gap.

It continues to specify that the milestone of raising “at least $20bn per year by 2025” in public finance was also “likely not achieved”.

The report states that reporting countries raised a cumulative total of just $17.7bn in international public funding for biodiversity, across four years from 2020-23, as shown in the figure below. This amounts to an average of just $4.4bn per year.

These figures are supported by a report from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which suggests that global biodiversity finance reached $74.9-92.4bn per year between 2023 and 2025.

It adds that, of this, international public finance contributed around $16.1bn per year on average – nearly $4bn below the 2025 goal.

Moreover, the OECD report says that only $7.9bn in 2023 to $8.5bn in 2024 was “biodiversity-specific” funding, with another $5.7bn and $6.5bn, respectively, coming in the form of funds for other priorities, in which support for biodiversity was a secondary aim.

While the GBF sets targets for nature finance in 2025 and 2030, developed countries should be making similar contributions every year, according to thinktank Third World Network.

There are two main channels of international public finance that developed countries can use to meet their biodiversity finance commitments under the GBF. These are official development finance (ODF) and the Global Biodiversity Framework Fund (GBFF).

Separate figures for these channels also show financing falling behind the subtargets set out in the GBF.

This includes the Global Biodiversity Framework Fund (GBFF), which was established at COP15 in 2022.

It is currently housed under the Global Environment Facility (GEF) and reports directly to the biodiversity COP.

Despite an initial flurry of pledges for the initiative, developed nations had contributed less than $400m to the fund, as of July this year, according to data shared with Carbon Brief by the GEF.

Between August 2023 and 30 June 2026, the GBFF has received only $387.2m in signed commitments from 11 countries and one subnational government (Quebec in Canada).

The figure below shows that, after the initial pledges ahead of COP16 in 2024, there have only been a handful of new contributions to the fund.

Elsewhere, the Cali fund was created at the COP16 nature negotiations in Colombia in 2024.

It is the first-ever global fund for companies profiting from genetic data to contribute to conservation goals.

According to a working paper from the Grantham Research Institute at the London School of Economics, if all 21,690 eligible firms shared their profits according to the fund’s rules, they could raise $3.6bn for nature annually. The paper adds that, under a realistic uptake from companies, this could generate $929m annually for nature.

To date, however, the fund has received only two contributions, totalling $6,000.

Ahead of COP17 in Armenia, experts tell Carbon Brief that finance will be the most closely watched element of the stocktake of progress.

For developing countries that “hold most of the world’s biodiversity and bear the greater burden of implementation” of nature targets, the need for this financing is “very real”, says Lim Li Ching of the Third World Network speaking to Carbon Brief. She adds:

“These amounts fall far short of the GBF’s target 19. Developed countries have regrettably not met their legal obligations…[T]his failure should not be used as an excuse to pivot to the private sector or market-based mechanisms, which struggle to raise finance at scale and often deepen social and ecological harm.”

Options for private and “innovative” finance include debt-for-nature swaps, biodiversity offsets and carbon credits, but these are harder to monitor and have often faced criticism.

The Cali Fund is one avenue for companies to contribute directly to the CBD’s goals, with dedicated, transparent allocations for conservation projects and Indigenous peoples.

Dr Siva Thambisetty, associate professor of law at the London School of Economics, tells Carbon Brief that the fund is still a voluntary measure and it is up to countries “to create a constructive, ethical obligation” for companies to contribute. She adds:

“The private sector is doing what it always does: minimising outgoings and liability and maximising freedom to operate. I don’t believe the shift we need can be generated internally by businesses alone without outside pressure. States with jurisdiction over business need to take action.”

A non-profit initiative called the Cali Watch finds that “not a single government” has passed a law requiring companies to contribute to the fund. It estimates that eight companies alone should contribute more than $2bn, as part of what it calls their “Cali debt”.

Dr June Rubis, the Indigenous peoples and local communities lead on the Cali Fund’s steering committee, tells Carbon Brief that she is “worried about the pace” at which biodiversity finance is moving. Rubis continues:

“Each part of the finance architecture needs to start sending credible signals at the same time: developed countries on the $20bn commitment; donors on predictable finance for the GBFF; companies on Cali Fund contributions; and the Cali Fund itself on allocation and access. If one part moves while the others are repeatedly deferred, confidence in the wider biodiversity bargain becomes harder to sustain.”

She points out that for Indigenous peoples and local communities, “success also depends on whether finance can actually be accessed” to support communities’ priorities. She concludes:

“[H]eadline commitments matter, but so do the pathways through which money reaches people.”

Climate issues are taking a back seat in B.C.’s election

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Shannon Waters has been reporting on B.C. politics for nearly a decade. Since joining The Narwhal in 2024, her work has focused on energy, resource extraction and Indigenous Rights.

The leaders of B.C.’s three main political parties spent nearly two hours on Oct. 14 vying to be the province’s next premier. BC NDP Leader David Eby, Conservative Leader Lorne Doerkson and Green Leader Emily Lowan faced more than a dozen questions each from moderator Shachi Kurl during the only debate of the election campaign.

Not one of those questions focused on environmental issues or climate change. Nor did any of the leaders question each other about their proposed environmental policies during the open debate portions of the broadcast.In fact, the only leader to mention the climate crisis was Lowan as she introduced herself to the audience. “I am the only leader on this stage that feels the decisions being made in the legislature, especially as it relates to the cost of living crisis and the climate crisis,” Lowan said, claiming she is the only one of the three party leaders who “has worked a minimum-wage job in the past decade.”

The lack of discussion about climate issues in the debate marked a continuation of an election campaign that has been focused on affordability issues. The NDP is promising to hike taxes on top income earners, cap prices on groceries and gas and freeze car insurance rates for three years.

The BC Conservatives are promising to cut income taxes for everyone, slash regulations in a bid to spur economic development and boost funding for police.

Both parties are promising billions in spending on health care and infrastructure without a lot of detail on where that money will come from. The province currently faces a deficit of nearly $14 billion.

Meanwhile, the BC Green Party’s 2026 campaign is also focusing heavily on kitchen-table issues. Lowan has proposed a wealth tax the party estimates would bring in billions of dollars that could be used to improve housing affordability and health care access.

The top concerns for voters are either cost of living and affordability or jobs and the economy, with health care and housing typically rounding out the top three. Fourteen per cent of respondents to a recent Angus Reid poll put environment and climate change as their most important issue when considering who to vote for in this election. Just three per cent of respondents told pollster Research Co. the environment is the most important issue facing British Columbia today.

Compare that to October 2019, when Angus Reid found climate change was the top issue among Canadian voters.

What politicians are saying — or not saying — is likely tied to what voters have been telling pollsters lately.

Eby announced his party’s climate commitments over the Thanksgiving weekend, 19 days into the campaign. Most of them are already government policy or continuations of the NDP’s previous commitments, including protecting 30 per cent of the province’s land base by 2030. The Green Party put out its climate platform on Oct. 10, which includes a promise to halt new fossil fuel projects and end “subsidies, tax preferences and public financing that encourage the expansion of fossil-fuel production.”

The BC Conservatives have yet to mention climate policy during the campaign, except to state “unrealistic climate objectives” have stymied B.C.’s ability to launch a liquefied natural gas (LNG) industry.

So why aren’t climate issues as prominent as they once were for many voters?

Voter priorities have shifted even from this summer, as wildfires cast a pall of smoke across much of B.C. and climate change was front of mind for a substantial portion of B.C. voters. In July, Angus Reid found 25 per cent of B.C. respondents listed climate change as their top concern, putting the issue in third place behind cost of living and health care.

And there are plenty of issues for British Columbians to fret over these days, Jamie Lawson, an associate professor of political science at the University of Victoria told The Narwhal. Many voters are experiencing what academics call a polycrisis. That’s when multiple crises are happening at the same time, Lawson added, citing the examples of democratic decline, climate change, a cost of living crunch and the threat of artificial intelligence eating into employment opportunities.

“The thing about a polycrisis is that it implies that pressing problems go well beyond your top three,” he said.

“I think at the moment, a lot of people who do indeed care about the climate are faced with making choices. … They may be no less concerned about climate, but we might be in a case of trying to save the furniture.”

Economic anxieties tend to crowd out worries about climate change, according to Kathryn Harrison, a political science professor at the University of British Columbia who studies climate and energy policy. That’s partly because issues like the cost of groceries and gas are immediate and personal, while the impacts of climate change can seem less personally affecting.

“People know how much they’re paying for eggs, but nobody knows if their house is going to be the one that will burn down,” Harrison said. “There’s this uncertainty about who will bear those risks.”

Meanwhile, some politicians have leaned into the idea that addressing one crisis at a time is the best approach, Iglika Ivanova, senior economist and co-executive director at BC Policy Solutions, said.

“People are worried about their jobs and about prices, so we need to deal with these immediate concerns,” Ivanova said of the way top politicians have been framing the current moment. “Once we sort that out, only then can we look at climate change.”

The political tendency to focus on the short term is short-sighted, she argued.

“It almost seems like they’re looking for an economic initiative that would generate money as soon as possible, rather than taking a longer-term view on what kind of an economy are we building?”

Lawson sees the federal government’s push to fast-track major projects across the country looming large over the B.C. election.

“I think the way the NDP and the Conservatives [in B.C.] have really pressed for this accelerated extraction reflects the way in which the Liberal Party federally has gone flat out for maximizing and diversifying that kind of extractivist economy,” he said.

The NDP, for instance, has pledged to double B.C.’s critical mineral exports, while the Conservatives are promising broad deregulation to make it faster and easier to build projects like LNG facilities.

While the Conservatives might not want to talk about climate policy, Harrison thinks the NDP stands to benefit when voters are thinking about climate issues.

“Historically, climate and environment have been an issue that the NDP has had an advantage over the Conservatives on,” she said. “I think it’s reasonable for climate-concerned voters to say, ‘Tell us what you’re going to do on this issue that we care a lot about and that reflects a crisis that is only getting worse.’ ”

On the other hand, after years of not living up to some of its promises — climate-focused and otherwise — the NDP may have trouble convincing some voters the party will do as it promised if it forms government again. During the 2024 election campaign, the NDP promised to bring in a cap on oil and gas emissions, finish implementing recommendations from the 2020 old-growth forest strategic review and deliver a $1,000 grocery rebate — none of which happened in the past two years.

“Will some of the most climate-concerned voters vote again for the NDP because they fear that the Conservatives will be worse?” Harrison wondered. “Will they vote Green or will they stay home?”

Plenty of British Columbians may be fed up with the NDP but Lawson noted the Conservatives have gone through a lot of upheaval in the past couple of years, including the recent resignation of former leader Kerry-Lynne Findlay just 113 days after taking the reins of the party and only two days before Eby made the snap election call.

He also cautioned governments do not always do what they said they would while on the campaign trail.

“The B.C. party system is in flux and we’ve never, in recent years, had platforms that gave reliable insight into what governments do afterwards.”

Regardless of who forms B.C.’s next government, climate and environment should be central to its policies, Ivanova argued.

“Climate change is impacting us already and it will only impact us more,” she said. “It has immediate costs today and then there’s also health issues around wildfire smoke and all sorts of things that I think we ignore at our peril.”

Arbeitszeitreform: Wackelt der Acht-Stunden-Tag?

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Zwölf Stunden am Stück in Deutschland arbeiten? Union und SPD streiten erbittert über die Reform des Arbeitszeitgesetzes. Es geht um Flexibilität, Tarifverträge und den Schutz von Beschäftigten.

Leuna, im ostdeutschen Bundesland Sachsen-Anhalt, ist einer der größten Standorte der Chemieindustrie in Deutschland. Mehr als 100 Unternehmen haben sich hier inzwischen angesiedelt. Noch recht neu ist eine Bio-Raffinerie für holzbasierte Chemikalien, die der finnische Biotechnologiekonzern UPM in Leuna gebaut hat. Dafür sucht das Werk Mitarbeiter.

Auf einem großen Jobportal wird unter anderem ein Chemikant für die Inbetriebnahme neuer Anlagen gesucht. Vorausgesetzt wird die Bereitschaft, in Schichten mit einer Dauer von zwölf Stunden zu arbeiten. Wie kann das sein? Das deutsche Arbeitszeitgesetz erlaubt als Standard maximal acht Stunden Arbeit pro Werktag. Möglich ist eine Verlängerung auf maximal zehn Stunden, wenn die Mehrarbeit wieder ausgeglichen wird. Und: Zwischen zwei Schichten müssen mindestens elf Stunden Ruhezeit liegen.

To view this video please enable JavaScript, and consider upgrading to a web browser that supports HTML5 video

Wirtschaftsverbände und die konservativen Parteien CDU/CSU fordern schon lange eine Flexibilisierung der Arbeitszeit. Arbeitgeberpräsident Rainer Dulger verweist auf die europäische Arbeitszeitrichtlinie, die eine wöchentliche Höchstarbeitszeit von 48 Stunden erlaubt. "Die Spielräume werden fast überall genutzt, nur bei uns nicht", sagte Dulger im August der Deutschen Presse-Agentur.

Besonders in Branchen mit saisonalen Schwankungen, wie im Tourismus, bei Projektarbeit oder unregelmäßigen Arbeitsspitzen wie in der Gastronomie finden Unternehmen die bestehenden Regeln oft zu unflexibel. Sie würden Personal gerne effizienter einsetzen. "Das Arbeitszeitgesetz stammt aus der Ära von Telefax und Wählscheibe. Es kennt weder Notebooks noch Kita-Öffnungszeiten", so Dulger.

Union und Sozialdemokraten haben 2025 in ihrem Koalitionsvertrag vereinbart, des Arbeitszeitgesetz zu reformieren. Wörtlich heißt es: "Deshalb wollen wir im Einklang mit der europäischen Arbeitszeitrichtlinie die Möglichkeit einer wöchentlichen anstatt einer täglichen Höchstarbeitszeit schaffen - auch und gerade im Sinne einer besseren Vereinbarkeit von Familie und Beruf."

Allerdings hätte SPD das Gesetz am liebsten unangetastet gelassen, musste den Wunsch der Union aber akzeptieren. Die Sozialdemokraten verweisen darauf, dass längere Arbeitszeiten in Ausnahmen bereits möglich sind. Für Rettungsdienste beispielsweise, das Gesundheitswesen und Sicherheitsdienste. Auch in Kraftwerken, Raffinerien und eben auch in der Chemieindustrie darf bis zu zwölf Stunden am Stück gearbeitet werden.

Selbst dem Bundeskanzler scheint diese Ausnahme-Regelung bis vor Kurzem nicht geläufig gewesen zu sein. Anfang September 2026 besuchte Friedrich Merz die UPM-Raffinerie in Leuna. Er sei "völlig überrascht" gewesen, dass die Anlagen im kontinuierlichen Zwölf-Stunden-Schichtbetrieb laufen, erzählte er auf dem Deutschen Arbeitgebertag im Oktober in Berlin. Mit Zustimmung des Betriebsrats und ordnungsgemäß vereinbart.

Der Schichtleiter habe von einem "super guten" Arbeitszeitmodell gesprochen, das drei Arbeitstage pro Woche umfasse. "Er hat gesagt, wir haben viel Zeit für die Familien, das ist eine super Einteilung so." Das zeige, dass es gut wäre, das Arbeitszeitgesetz generell zu lockern, schlussfolgerte Merz. "Über acht Stunden zu arbeiten, ist keine Ausbeutung, sondern das kann für die Betriebe, auch für die Menschen in den Betrieben, eine gute Chance sein, ihre Arbeitszeiten flexibler einzusetzen", sagte der Kanzler und CDU-Vorsitzende auf dem Arbeitgebertag und fügte hinzu: "Wir sollten es machen."

Widerstand kommt allerdings aus der SPD und von den Gewerkschaften. Letztere lehnen eine Änderung des Arbeitszeitgesetzes ab. Die Gewerkschaft ver.di argumentiert, deutlich längere Arbeitstage hätten negative Folgen für die Gesundheit der Beschäftigten sowie Nachteile für Menschen mit Familien- und Pflegeaufgaben. Arbeitnehmer könnten in einzelnen Branchen zudem unter Druck geraten, längere Schichten gegen ihren Willen zu akzeptieren.

Entsprechend dem Koalitionsvertrag arbeitete im Juni 2026 das damals noch von Bärbel Bas geführte Bundesministerium für Arbeit und Soziales einen ersten Entwurf für die Reform aus. Allerdings versehen mit zusätzlichen Bedingungen: Ein Wechsel auf eine wöchentliche Arbeitszeit sollte nur dort möglich sein, wo Arbeitgeber und Gewerkschaften die Arbeitsbedingungen in einem sogenannten Tarifvertrag festlegen. Und das auch nur, wenn gleichzeitig Regelungen zum Gesundheitsschutz aufgestellt werden und wenn es eine elektronische Erfassung der Arbeitszeiten gibt.

Welche Idee dahintersteckt, erläuterte die arbeits- und sozialpolitische Sprecherin der SPD-Bundestagsfraktion Annika Klose. Flexibilisierung sei "keine Einbahnstraße". Im Koalitionsvertrag sei ausdrücklich auf die Vereinbarkeit von Familie und Beruf abgezielt worden. "Dass es also auch mal möglich ist, das Kind am Nachmittag zum Fußballtraining zu bringen und danach weiterzuarbeiten - aber eben nicht, dass mein Chef mir gegen meinen Willen sagt: Du hast morgen die Schicht von 9 bis 22 Uhr."

Die Union wies den Entwurf umgehend als indiskutabel zurück. Auch die Arbeitgeber schäumten. "Dieser Entwurf muss zurückgezogen und völlig überarbeitet werden", forderte Arbeitgeber-Präsident Rainer Dulger in einer Stellungnahme. Er strotze "vor Misstrauen". Der Zentralverband des Handwerks sprach von einem "Bruch mit den im Koalitionsvertrag vereinbarten Zusagen". Seitdem liegt die Reform auf Eis.

Bundeskanzler Friedrich Merz stellte auf dem Arbeitgebertag nun Bewegung in Aussicht. "Das ist in der Tat überfällig. Ich glaube, dass wir das auch relativ schnell machen können, dass wir die Flexibilität des Arbeitsmarktes herstellen können."

Ob der neue SPD-Bundesarbeitsminister Alexander Schweitzer das genauso sieht, bleibt allerdings abzuwarten. Aus seiner Partei kam Anfang Oktober ein Papier zur Arbeitszeitreform, das einer Kampfansage gleichkommt. "Selbstbestimmt statt dereguliert" steht über den sieben Seiten, die von der SPD-Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Arbeit, der Jugendorganisation Jusos, der Arbeitsgemeinschaft "60plus" und den Frauen in der SPD unterzeichnet wurde.

In dem Papier beharren die Sozialdemokraten nicht nur auf den Punkten, die schon im Juni in dem Entwurf von Bärbel Bas auftauchten. Sie fordern darüber hinaus, Arbeitgeber und Beschäftigte sollten zukünftig "gemeinsam und einvernehmlich" über Arbeitszeiten entscheiden. Es sei nicht "im Sinne einer zeitgemäßen Reform des Arbeitszeitrechts", wenn nur die Chefs bestimmen könnten, wann die Beschäftigten Feierabend machen.

Politisch gesehen ist die Auseinandersetzung über die Arbeitszeitreform längst mehr als eine Debatte über Paragrafen. Sie ist ein Grundsatzstreit darüber, wie viel Flexibilität die Arbeitswelt künftig verträgt und wie stark der Staat die Beschäftigten dabei schützen soll. Sie ist aber auch ein weiterer Stolperstein für die ohnehin angeschlagene Regierung in Berlin.

Pluralistic: Expedia amazoned hotel rooms (15 Oct 2026)

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

No matter where you shop, you're getting screwed by Amazon. Amazon has found a way to make everything you buy – at Target, at a mom-and-pop shop, even direct from the manufacturer – more expensive. And they're not the only one: Expedia's done the same thing, but for hotel rooms.

America's affluent top decile – the 10% of richest Americans – do the majority of America's shopping, a phenomenon that has only been accelerated by America's precipitous slide into oligarchy, which has exhausted the savings and the credit-card limits and the home equity of the majority of Americans, who increasingly can't afford to buy anything:

If you want to sell things in America, you must sell them in the place where the top decile shops, and that's Amazon. For merchants, that is an incredibly expensive proposition: 51-60% of every dollar you spend with an independent merchant on Amazon is claimed by Amazon through junk fees, listing fees, commissions, processing fees and other scams:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/04/cheating-at-fraud/#absentee-rentier

Merchants don't have 51-60% margins they can afford to turn over to Amazon. To remain viable while selling on Amazon, they have to raise prices. Amazon doesn't mind if prices go up, so long as Amazon's price remains competitive. So Amazon imposes a requirement on its suppliers called "most favored nation," which requires sellers to offer their best price on Amazon:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/02/25/most-favored-nation/#price-fixing

Practically speaking, that means that when you raise your price on Amazon, you must raise your prices everywhere by the same amount, which means that no matter where you shop (Target, Walmart, the corner store or direct from the factory), you pay the Amazon Tax.

The Amazon Tax is a prime (you should forgive the expression) example of enshittification, in which an intermediary (a business that sits between buyers and sellers, AKA, "a platform") screws both customers and sellers to benefit itself.

But Amazon isn't the only example of a powerful digital intermediary that has used its market power to impose an economy-wide tax. Wherever you look online, you find digital giants who grew the way Amazon grew (buying all of their competitors) and then did what Amazon did (squeezing buyers and sellers, making prices higher everywhere).

That's what happened with Expedia. Almost every travel site you can find online has been purchased by Expedia (Hotels.com, Travelocity, and Orbitz), and the ones that aren't Expedia subsidiaries were acquired by its main rival, Priceline (Booking.com, Kayak, Agoda and even Opentable). Nearly all the hotel rooms are booked by a tiny minority of travelers – largely that same top decile that Amazon has so thoroughly captured and locked in – and hoteliers can't live without their business, and they're almost all Expedia and/or Priceline customers. Expedia accounts for 80% of all hotel bookings by dollar value.

Expedia's position, in other words, is basically Amazon's position, so Expedia is doing exactly what Amazon is doing: they charge huge fees to hotels, and then force the hotels to raise their prices everywhere through a "most favored nation" deal:

https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.wawd.369601/gov.uscourts.wawd.369601.1.0.pdf

But – as Lee Hepner writes for the BIG newsletter – Expedia has figured out ways to out-Amazon Amazon. They've copied one of Amazon's sleaziest businesses, and made it worse:

https://www.thebignewsletter.com/p/how-expedia-rigs-travel-markets-and

Here's Amazon's sleaziest commercial practice: when you search Amazon, the top results for your search aren't the best matches for your queries; they're products sold by the companies willing to pay the most for search placement, in a "real-time auction" that runs every time you search. The cost of winning that auction comes out of your pocket through higher prices: the first result for an Amazon query is, on average, 29% more expensive than the best match. The top row is 25% more expensive. On average, the best match for your query is 17 positions down the screen, on the second page, where almost no one ever bothers to scroll:

https://pluralistic.net/2023/11/03/subprime-attention-rent-crisis/#euthanize-rentiers

This rip-off is Amazon's most profitable line of business, more profitable than AWS or Prime. It makes the company more than $80b/year. And – incredibly – Expedia's found a way to make it even worse.

Like Amazon, Expedia auctions off its search results. They call this their "VIP listing" program. Practically speaking, hotels have to pay for this, because it boosts booking rates by 42% (the first ten results on an Expedia search-results page get 62% of the clicks). VIP listing is expensive for the merchant – but it's even more expensive for you and me.

That's because Expedia requires hotels in its VIP listings programs to raise their prices on non-Expedia sites by 10-25%…INDEFINITELY.

Let me repeat that: to stay in business, hotels need to be on Expedia. To stay in business on Expedia, hotels have to raise their prices to pay Expedia's junk fees, but they have to raise their prices everywhere else. But to really stay in business on Expedia, hotels have to use the VIP program, which is even more expensive. And – one more time – hotels in the VIP program have to jack up their prices everywhere else, INDEFINITELY. This means that, throughout the economy, hotel prices are rising at a superinflationary rate, because Expedia – who command 80% of the market – are pushing those prices up.

This goes a long way to explaining why US hotel rates rose 58% from a 2020 average of $103/night to a 2025 average of $162/night. It explains why hotel prices rose by 5% last year, which is double the rate of inflation. And this is why Expedia stock is up 65% since the start of the Iran war, a fact that Expedia CEO Ariane Gorin attributes to "higher margins" from "higher prices":

https://s202.q4cdn.com/757635260/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/Q2-2026-Earnings-Presentation-Final.pdf

In his coverage of the Expedia lawsuit – which brought many of these details to light – Hepner notes that hotels themselves have undergone near-total cartelization, with Hilton, Marriott, and Choice International owning the majority of all hotels after a string of acquisitions that went largely unchallenged by slumbering antitrust regulators around the world. These consolidated chains have also jacked up prices and enshittified their service, slashing housekeeping and check-in staff and allowing their facilities to slip into disrepair.

This, too, is characteristic of monopolization: whenever a monopoly takes over part of its supply chain, the rest of the supply chain consolidates to prevent itself from being ripped off by the new giant. This is the story of publishers consolidating to defend themselves from Borders/B&N, Walmart and Costco, then Amazon; and it's the story of literary agencies consolidating to resist the power of those overgrown publishers.

And it's the story of health-care, which saw consolidation in pharmaceuticals leading to mergers among hospitals, who screwed the insurers, who also consolidated.

In every case, it's the workers and the people who rely on them that get the shitty end of the stick. Hotel guests, readers and patients pay more for inferior products. Writers, editors, doctors, nurses, hotel staff and housekeepers get lower pay and worse working-conditions:

https://pluralistic.net/2024/12/18/loose-flapping-ends/#luigi-has-a-point

There is nothing wrong with the idea of an intermediary. Most writers can't publish and sell their own books, and few travelers can navigate finding hotels in strange cities without help. The problem of intermediaries is that when intermediaries gain monopoly power – by buying their rivals and capturing buyers and sellers – they are able to squeeze both. As Tim Wu says, this is the moment when a company that's supposed to be helping becomes a predator, acquires "main character syndrome" and makes every transaction all about the middleman, at the expense of the people buying and selling:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/08/05/absentee-landlord/

There are so many reasons for inflation and the cost of living crisis: wage stagnation, wars of choice in Iran, environmental cataclysm after environmental cataclysm. But under cover of these real factors is a cohort of what Matt Stoller calls "economic termites," parasites that have infested the walls of our economy and are devouring it from within.

You can't solve this by shopping. Calling the hotel direct won't fix it: they have to be on Expedia to survive, and to be on Expedia, they have to sign up for VIP service, and to keep VIP service, they have to make their prices higher everywhere. To fix this, we need systemic action: the corporate death penalty for the companies that are consuming the structural members of our society.

Trump Says the Border Is Secure. Texas Gov. Greg Abbott Keeps Declaring an Emergency.

Last month, Texas Gov. Greg Abbott repeated a claim he’s made for the past five years. He stated that the number of people crossing the Texas-Mexico border illegally “posed an ongoing and imminent threat” across the state. In doing so, he renewed a border security disaster proclamation that has greatly expanded his powers without going through the state Legislature.

Abbott’s declaration stands in sharp contrast to the numbers reported by the Trump administration, which says the drop in the flow of migrants crossing the border has been unprecedented and has reached historic lows since it took over in January 2025.

Yet, since Abbott first declared an emergency at the border during the Biden administration in 2021, he’s renewed or amended the proclamation at least 69 times — 21 of those during Trump’s tenure.

The Republican governor, who is running for his fourth term next month, has consolidated power like no Texas governor before him, building on his predecessors’ efforts to strengthen what by design is supposed to be a “weak governor” state as the constitution limits what can be done without legislative approval, ProPublica and The Texas Tribune have reported. One of the methods he’s used to do that is issuing executive orders and disaster declarations that allow him to shift funds, suspend regulations and award no-bid contracts at times of emergency.

Since January 2025, two of the top agencies involved with border enforcement — the Department of Public Safety and the Texas Military Department — have awarded more than $5 million in noncompetitive contracts citing the emergency for items like a Ford F-150, rifles and apartments, state records show.

“I think it’s unprecedented,” said Cal Jillson, a political science professor at Southern Methodist University in Dallas. “I don’t think that there is a similar example, certainly not as to the length of the emergency, and certainly not after the emergency has seemed to pass.”

Abbott’s office did not respond to specific questions for this story, including about the noncompetitive contracts, but Deputy Press Secretary Madeline Waschak said in a written statement that the governor has been clear that border security is an ongoing priority.

“The damage caused by four years of failed border policies under Democrat rule did not disappear overnight, and the progress we have made will not sustain itself without continued vigilance,” she wrote.

“Securing the border requires constant, deliberate action to combat illegal crossings, cartel smuggling, fentanyl trafficking, and other criminal activity that threatens public safety in Texas. Governor Abbott will ensure the tools remain in place to safeguard our state as we work alongside the Trump Administration to deter, arrest, detain, and deport illegal immigrants,” she added.

At the time of Abbott’s first border declaration, four months into President Joe Biden’s term, Border Patrol was making more than 180,000 apprehensions along the southwest border in a single month. According to Abbott, the federal government’s inability or unwillingness to enforce immigration laws had created a humanitarian crisis in many communities in Texas.

“The Biden Administration’s open-border policies have enabled escalating violence from the cartels, dramatically increased the risk of human trafficking, and created a severe crisis of illegal drugs crossing into the United States,” the order stated.

Since then, apprehensions along the southwest border have dropped on average to about 8,000 a month this year.

The border security declaration remains Abbott’s longest continuously running emergency proclamation. Abbott similarly renewed his 2020 COVID-19 disaster declaration for three years, even as he downplayed the severity of the pandemic.

“This is the quiet power of the governor,” said Brandon Rottinghaus, a professor of political science at the University of Houston. “It’s not something often that they brag about because the more that it becomes a political issue, the more that there might be some contestation of it.”

Other than border security, the governor’s second longest-running emergency declaration is related to droughts. He declared a state disaster in July 2022 and within days amended it bringing the total of counties to 189. He’s continued to renew it, but now it applies to about a third of the counties originally identified.

State lawmakers have repeatedly tried — and failed — to curb the governor’s powers under the state’s disaster act. Last year, legislators from both parties filed at least a handful of measures that would mandate Abbott get approval from the Legislature if he wanted to extend a disaster declaration, but none passed.

Abbott’s emergency declarations have been accompanied by record spending on border security efforts. Texas budgeted over $11 billion during the Biden administration and allotted an additional $3 billion last year, after Trump took office.

It’s all part of Abbott’s Operation Lone Star, an initiative he launched in March 2021 contending it was needed to stem the smuggling of drugs and people into the country through Texas. Because of the emergency declaration, police could charge immigrants with criminal trespassing for crossing the border through private property, and the governor was able to shift money from other agencies to fund the operation and build border barriers, deploy the National Guard and get help from other states.

Much of the funding for Operation Lone Star is funneled through the Texas Military Department and DPS, two of the agencies responsible for major enforcement efforts. But the governor’s share of border spending has also ballooned since Abbott took office over a decade ago. State lawmakers allotted his office nearly $230 million last year to distribute through grants, largely to local prosecutors and government agencies helping the state with Operation Lone Star.

Though the situation on the ground has changed, one aspect of the emergency declaration has remained constant: the ability of state agencies to award noncompetitive contracts.

DPS and the Texas Military Department have awarded at least six noncompetitive contracts so far during the second Trump administration for work related to the operation where they cite an emergency. This includes a more than $2 million contract to Texas-based CTC Gunworks LLC for rifles and pistols for active-duty personnel. CTC Gunworks officials could not be reached for comment.

Officials with the Texas Military Department referred questions about its procurement process to the governor’s office. A DPS spokesperson said in a written statement that even though the disaster declaration allows for expedited purchases, the department still follows best practices and “aims to select vendors offering the best value, determined by cost, quality, and ability to meet the specific disaster response requirements.”

Regarding the specific contracts, the DPS spokesperson said they were purchases tied to existing state contracts.

The continued funding and expansion of Operation Lone Star has come even as it’s been plagued with problems. In 2022, an investigation by ProPublica, the Tribune and The Marshall Project found that the state’s reported success included arrests unrelated to the border or immigration and counted drug seizures made by troopers from across the state who were not directly assigned to the operation. The Tribune and Army Times also exposed poor working conditions, pay delays and suicides among National Guard members deployed as part of Operation Lone Star.

During a Tribune-hosted event this week with state lawmakers from El Paso, Democratic state Rep. Mary González, vice chair of the Appropriations Committee, said state legislators have already started to scale back when it comes to border funding and there’s interest from both sides of the aisle to continue to do so in the upcoming legislative session.

“I think that the problem is that some of that border security money still lies in the governor’s office which has no checks and balances and because we are still under an emergency — which is not an emergency anymore — an emergency border security statement which he can just use that money, you have seen some questionable appropriations under the veil of border security,” she said.

Abbott, however, has repeatedly said that Texas’ work in securing the border is not over.

“We must secure the border not just for the next two years of the Trump administration, but we gotta do it forever,” Abbott said at a press conference in early October where he discussed the federal government’s $7.5 billion reimbursement for border security expenses the state had incurred.

Rottinghaus said that Texas will always have to confront border crossings and drug trafficking, but the existence of those problems don’t always justify an emergency declaration.

“Even someone who strongly supports the governor’s board of policies might ask whether they want a future governor of the opposite party using the same interpretation of emergency authority for years,” Rottinghaus said, adding that the issue is that “governors, once they get a power, are hesitant to give it back.”

L'île Maurice est secouée par les pires émeutes depuis des décennies après qu'une femme a été blessée par balle par la police

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

La police mauricienne a imposé un couvre-feu nocturne pour tenter de contenir les pires émeutes qu'ait connues l'île de l'océan Indien depuis des décennies.

Les manifestations contre les pénuries d'eau et le coût élevé de la vie, qui ont débuté il y a une semaine, ont dégénéré en violence vendredi lorsqu'une femme a été blessée par balle par la police à un barrage routier.

Les circonstances de la fusillade ne sont pas claires, mais le policier a depuis été arrêté et placé en détention provisoire.

Le Premier ministre Navin Ramgoolam a ordonné le couvre-feu mardi, mettant en garde contre ceux qui « agissent contre les intérêts du pays », qui est un centre névralgique du tourisme et des services financiers.

« Ceux qui ont perpétré ces actes devront en assumer les très lourdes conséquences, conséquences que méritent leurs actes antipatriotiques », a-t-il déclaré dans une allocution à la nation.

Le gouvernement a annoncé la prolongation du couvre-feu jusqu'à vendredi matin, conformément aux mesures convenues lors d'une réunion spéciale du cabinet mercredi.

Le rapport détaillait les pillages, la possession de substances explosives, les incendies criminels et les dommages causés aux commissariats, aux feux de circulation et aux caméras de surveillance lors des manifestations, et faisait état de sept policiers blessés.

Dans un communiqué distinct, le ministère de l'Éducation a annoncé l'annulation des cours jeudi et vendredi dans les établissements scolaires, prolongeant ainsi la suspension déjà en vigueur. Il a précisé suivre la situation de près et qu'une déclaration ultérieure concernant la réouverture des écoles serait publiée.

Des informations vérifiées à portée de main

Au moins 94 personnes ont été arrêtées depuis le début des émeutes, et le gouvernement indique qu'elles pourraient être poursuivies en vertu des lois antiterroristes.

Parmi les personnes arrêtées, 16 sont mineures.

« Compte tenu du nombre de mineurs impliqués dans les émeutes », le gouvernement a déclaré qu'il envisagerait d'abaisser de 14 à 12 ans l'âge auquel les mineurs peuvent être détenus et poursuivis en justice.

Elle a indiqué que les agents de la police spécialisés dans la cybercriminalité surveillaient constamment les réseaux sociaux et enquêteraient sur les personnes publiant des discours haineux et incitant à la violence.

Des manifestants ont brûlé des pneus et bloqué des routes alors que les troubles s'intensifiaient au cours du week-end, des commissariats et d'autres infrastructures publiques étant vandalisés.

Ces troubles ont entraîné la fermeture des écoles et des transports publics, et les ambassades étrangères ont émis des alertes appelant leurs ressortissants à la prudence.

La police a signalé des incidents et des arrestations malgré le couvre-feu initial. Selon le dernier communiqué du gouvernement, la plupart des arrestations ont eu lieu mardi.

Lorsque les manifestations contre la pénurie d'eau ont commencé, le gouvernement a réagi de manière proactive en fournissant davantage d'eau aux ménages, selon Zoë McCathie, analyste chez Signal Risk, une société basée en Afrique du Sud.

Cette escalade a créé une situation plus « inhabituelle pour la sécurité du pays », le gouvernement ayant eu recours à la fermeture des écoles et à l'instauration d'un couvre-feu, a-t-elle déclaré à l'émission Newsday de la BBC.

« On n'a pas vu de manifestations de cette ampleur et de cette intensité depuis environ 1999. La réponse du gouvernement est donc logique et nécessaire », a-t-elle déclaré.

Bien que le gouvernement soit parvenu à « contenir le pire des troubles », il reste possible que des manifestations reprennent dans certaines régions, les pénuries d'eau devant persister jusqu'à la saison des pluies en décembre, a-t-elle déclaré.
