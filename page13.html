In Bulgaria, record low voter turnout triggers a November 1 presidential run-off in a poll colored by divisions over Ukraine war

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Low voter turnout has triggered a run-off for the Bulgarian presidency. The vote was colored by strong divisions over Russia's war in Ukraine, and Sofia's relations with the Kremlin.

Record low turnout in Sunday's Bulgarian elections mean voters are expected to head to the polls on November 1 to cast ballots in a presidential run-off.

Polling stations opened at 7 a.m. and closed at 8 p.m. (0500-1800 GMT), with official results expected within 48 hours, but voter turnout was less than 40%, far short of the 50% required.

Voters were choosing between candidates with vastly different views of the war in Ukraine and relations with Moscow and the vote came as drone attacks on ships in the Black Sea off the Bulgarian coast brought home the significance of the conflict in Ukraine for the former Soviet satellite state.

Although the presidency is largely ceremonial, the head of state in Bulgaria can appoint interim governments during political crises, is commander-in-chief of the armed forces and is able to veto legislation.

Frontrunner in the elections is incumbent President Iliana Yotova, who took over the position this year after current Prime Minister Rumen Radev stepped down to take part in the parliamentary elections that led him to power.

Yotova, 62, who surveys show as having 41-47% of the vote, favors dialogue with Moscow as the way to bring peace to Ukraine and opposes military aid to Kyiv.

A win for Yotova would give a boost for Radev, who also advocates dialogue with Moscow. Victory would make Yotova Bulgaria’s first elected female president.

Her main challenger is Andrey Gyurov, a former caretaker prime minister, who is polling at 28-31%.

Gyurov, 50, who is backed by the pro-Western opposition, is an outspoken critic of Radev's foreign policy and strongly supports the Euro-Atlantic ties of Bulgaria, a European Union and NATO member state. As caretaker prime minister, Gyurov signed a security cooperation agreement with Kyiv.

The leader of the pro-Russian far-right Vazrazhdane party, Kostadin Kostadinov, is in third place on 10-13%.

Altogether 27 pairs of candidates for president and vice president are competing in the first round, with nearly half of them openly pro-Kremlin.

After casting her ballot in Sofia, Yotova called for peace, stability and unity, urging an end to divisions among Bulgarians. Her main challenger, Gyurov, voted in Blagoevgrad, calling for a Bulgaria that looks ahead and offers families greater economic security. Turnout was estimated at 15.1% by 3 p.m. local time (1300 GMT), according to polling agency Myara, Bulgarian media reported.

To win outright, a candidate must secure more than half the valid votes, with more than half of eligible voters taking part. Otherwise, the top two candidates advance to a runoff on November 1.

The war in Ukraine triggered by Russia's unprovoked invasion is one of the central themes in the election, particularly after four merchant ships were allegedly hit by drones off Bulgaria's coast this month in suspected spillover from the conflict. The attacks have prompted Bulgaria to seek assistance from its allies to strengthen maritime security.

Satellite internet antennas from the Starlink network — an important part of Ukraine's defense capabilities — were also set on fire near the capital, Sofia.

While Radev has said that the attacks seemed to be to the advantage of Russia and summoned the Russian ambassador, his government has declined to blame the incidents directly on Moscow.

The strikes have nonetheless raised concerns over Russia’s intentions to escalate the conflict.

Rising energy prices and inflation that have caused economic hardship to many in Bulgaria, the EU's poorest member state, are also a main issue addressed by candidates.

Many voters are also looking to stop government corruption, a persistent concern in the country, and hope for a stable government after multiple elections and coalition breakdowns in recent years.

Saudi Arabia looks to US for an AI edge as Yemen's Houthis up pressure

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The US is helping Saudi Arabia fight the Houthi group in Yemen with AI-assisted targeting. But AI is not a magic bullet, experts say, and there are technological, political and human rights issues to consider.

Under attack from the militant Houthi group in neighboring Yemen, Saudi Arabia has asked numerous allies for help.

That includes the United States, even though media report that US President Donald Trump refused when Saudi ruler Mohammed bin Salman asked him to take direct military action against the Houthis.

However the US is providing Saudi Arabia with intelligence, which includes a targeting tool that uses artificial intelligence, or AI. A September report by US broadcaster CNN, quoting several unnamed American officials, said that between 100 and 200 US soldiers were in Saudi Arabia as part of new joint force command to facilitate this.

The US personnel were helping the Saudis "use a targeting tool that allows military officers to see in real-time what is happening on the battlefield," CNN reported.

Officials said the system in Saudi Arabia was similar to that known as the Maven Smart System, or MSS for short, which the US military has also used as it sought targets in Iran, Iraq and Syria.

MSS came out of what's known as Project Maven, the US's flagship artificial intelligence program for the military, first launched in 2017. MSS itself is an AI-enabled digital platform that "aggregates, organizes and visualizes vast streams of intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance data," analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies in Washington explained in a June text.

The software system identifies patterns in data coming from, for example, live satellite feeds or drone cameras and then labels potential targets such as possible rocket launchers, tanks or suspicious vehicle convoys.

Previously human military analysts would have had to look at a wide array of different sources to try to find those, the CSIS researchers wrote. It was difficult and, in the case of hours of satellite or drone footage, almost impossible. But MSS now brings hundreds of sources together, analyses them all, then flags possible targets. It also calculates the best method of hitting the target.

Tech commentator Bilawal Sidhu, a former Google product manager, has described the MSS set up as like a "Google Earth for war."

Using the MSS dramatically speeds up what is known as "the kill chain" — that is, the time it takes to detect a target, decide if it's dangerous and then order a strike.

When the US military used MSS while attacking Iran in late February, the AI-powered system facilitated hits on more than 1,000 targets within the first 24 hours of the war. Previously American soldiers were hitting about 100 targets a day.

Up until recently, the long-simmering civil war in Yemen had been comparatively calm following an informal ceasefire negotiated in 2022.

The Houthi rebel group has held large parts of northern and western Yemen since 2015. Yemen's internationally recognized government and other groups held territory across the south and east. That latter is supported by Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates.

The Houthi's latest military campaign, which began in September, has seen the group take further control of Yemen's coast. They also ramped up attacks against Saudi Arabia again, hitting the country's main airport and oil facilities. So far, though, military campaigns against the Houthi group by Saudi Arabia, the United States and others have all failed to significantly weaken the group.

This is partially because the Houthis take a "zero-sum approach," Farea al-Muslimi, a research fellow focused on Yemen at UK think tank Chatham House, told DW recently. The group's founding father argued that contemporary Muslim societies were too "soft," and that poverty and hardship are a divine test, al-Muslimi said.

"They don't care if the Israelis bomb the airport or the US bombs a hospital," the expert said. "It actually serves them very well because their ideology is all about martyrdom and sacrifice."

There are certain technical issues users have noted with the MSS in the past that could also prove problematic in Yemen.

The Houthis tend to be highly mobile, using truck-mounted ballistic missiles and small drone swarms as they operate from hills and canyons in rugged countryside. It's possible that fighters might be long gone before they can be attacked  even with the help of AI-assisted targeting.

Though MSS is known to work best in relatively unpopulated areas, factors such as changes in desert terrain, dust storms, high wind, snowfall and dense foliage — and even deliberately planted decoys — can reduce its success rate.

There is also a worrying ethical aspect to shortening the kill chain. A human is always supposed to verify the target that the AI-assisted tool has identified and decide how to react.

But it doesn't always work that way. For example, among the sites MSS targeted in Iran on the first day of the war was a school. An American missile killed almost 200 people at a primary school in southern Iran, most of them children aged between seven and 12.

In its military campaign in Gaza, Israel — arguably the Middle Eastern country with the most advanced military-use AI — has also used AI-assisted targeting. An Israeli system known as the Gospel, or Habsora, is the closest to MSS in the way it works.

But, as has been documented elsewhere, including by Israeli and British journalists and in the recent documentary Naza, the use of such AI targeting tools have also been highly problematic because of the numbers of civilian casualties that can result.

"On the one hand [in Gaza], AI has enhanced commanders' situational awareness, lessened the human costs of tunnel mapping, improved the speed of military strikes, and boosted the survivability of troops," analysts Frederic Wehrey and Andrew Bonney wrote for War on the Rocks, a specialist media outlet. "Yet the benefits afforded by such functions have been overshadowed by mounting civilian deaths reportedly resulting from this technology's safeguards being loosened or eliminated."

All of which is why Antony Loewenstein, an independent journalist based in Australia who has researched the impact of Israel's AI-assisted technology, doesn't believe similar technology will lead to the defeat of the Houthi group.

"I don't think there's much evidence at all that using an AI tool is going to make much difference on the ground, except for causing huge amounts of civilian casualties," Loewenstein, who authored the book, "The Palestine Laboratory: How Israel Exports The Technology of Occupation Around the World," told DW. "And I think what the US and the Saudis will find is that the Houthis have an incredibly high tolerance level for destruction."

Prague honors Roma resistance fighters against Hitler

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

More than 1,400 Roma fought against the German Nazi occupation in Czechoslovakia. A new exhibition in Prague showcases the stories of partisans and soldiers whose fates barely received any attention for decades.

For more than half a million Roma in Czechia and Slovakia, October 15, 2026, marks an important milestone.

That day, an exhibition on a largely forgotten chapter of Czech history opened at the Museum of the Romani National Revival in Prague. The exhibit explores the role of Czech and Slovak Romani resistance fighters during the Nazi German occupation and the dismantling of Czechoslovakia from 1938 to 1945.

Until now, Czech and Slovak Roma appeared in the history of World War II almost exclusively as victims of the Holocaust against Roma and Sinti. In the so-called Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, Nazi Germany murdered approximately 90% of the Czech Roma during World War II.

However, what is less well known is that a large number of Czech and Slovak Roma fought against the German occupiers as partisans in resistance groups and as soldiers in the Czechoslovak Underground Army, or Defense of the Nation, ON.

The exhibition at the Museum of the Roma National Movement therefore marks a turning point in how the Roma are viewed during World War II. At least that is the hope of museum director Emil Scuka, a lawyer, former prosecutor and one of the best-known Czech Roma activists.

"We were not only victims; we were also heroes," Scuka said in his speech to several hundred attendees at the exhibition opening, most of whom were Roma from Czechia and Slovakia. "Many people, not even among the Roma themselves, are aware that we Roma fought against Nazism," he noted. In his view, "organizing and opening the exhibition was something my generation owed to these Roma heroes and today we are repaying that debt."

Scuka called on those present: "Bring your children and grandchildren here so they can see for themselves and realize that the Roma have heroes too!"

He also pointed out that this topic had been completely marginalized in Czech and Slovak historical writing for decades and that the Roma had therefore decided on their own to document the fates of their war heroes.

The exhibition presents the detailed life stories of 100 of the most significant Roma partisans and Roma soldiers from the Czech underground army and resistance groups who fought against the Nazis.

"We found over 1,400 Romani fighters in the archives. From among them, we selected the 100 most interesting stories for this exhibition," said exhibition curator Radoslav Scuka at the opening.

Among these figures is Anton Facuna, a sergeant in the American intelligence agency Office of Strategic Services (OSS), who was flown to Slovakia in October 1944 on a secret mission. Slovakia had been a puppet state of Hitler from 1939 to 1945.

Another is Josef Serinek, known by the nickname "Black Partisan from Vysocina," who managed to escape from the Nazi concentration camp for Roma in Lety and subsequently founded and commanded the large Capajev-Cerny partisan unit in the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands.

In 2022, Czech President Milos Zeman posthumously awarded him the Medal for Heroism. Toward the end of his life, Serinek shared his memories with historian Jan Tesar, who later wrote the three-volume book "Ceska cikanska rapsodie", which translates to Czech Gypsy Rhapsody.

Except for Tesar's book, the history of Roma resistance against Nazism has not been researched by Czech and Slovak historians to this day. "I am not aware of any scholarly historical work that deals with Roma resistance against Nazism," Prokop Tomek, a historian at the Military History Institute in Prague, told DW.

"The number of 1,400 Roma who took part in the fight against Nazism as members of the resistance or in the foreign army does not strike me as an exaggeration and corresponds to the proportion of the Roma population in Czechoslovakia," Tomek said, adding that the "exhibition on the Roma resistance against Nazism during the war is undoubtedly groundbreaking."

To this day, many Roma in Czechia and Slovakia do not publicly acknowledge their ethnic identity. This is partly due to experiences with the Holocaust during the Nazi era that have been passed down through generations. Czech historians and politicians continue to debate the extent to which the Czech authorities of the Protectorate were complicit in the extermination of the Czech Roma.

However, one thing is certain: the police registration of all Roma, which had been enshrined in law in Czechoslovakia since 1927, facilitated the extermination of the Roma by the German Nazis.

According to estimates by the Czech government, approximately 250,000 Roma live in the country today. In the 2021 census, however, only 21,000 people identified as Roma.

Of all ethnic groups in Czechia, which has a population of 10 million, the Roma are among the three largest ethnic minorities in terms of numbers. However, they are the most socially disadvantaged, have very little political representation, and are therefore scarcely present in Czech politics.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

As Israel prepares to vote, Gadi Eisenkot is leveraging his Moroccan heritage in Likud strongholds such as Sderot. From historical divides to October 7, identity and trauma are redefining the city's political loyalties.

Ahead of Tuesday's elections, party advertisements greet people arriving by train to the outskirts of the western Israeli city of Sderot.

Campaign posters with various slogans promote the right-wing Likud and the far-right Otzma Yehudit parties. However, one poster is seen more than any others: that of the liberal Yashar party.

Its slogan, "Rosh Memshala Dialna," combines the terms "prime minister" in Hebrew and the words "of our own" in Moroccan Arabic.

And there is a good reason why Eisenkot's campaign in Sderot and other cities on Israel's sociopolitical periphery focuses on his Moroccan roots.

Sderot — located in the Negev desert, about 75 kilometers (45 miles) to Tel Aviv's south and just a kilometer away from the Gaza border — is a city with a large Moroccan Jewish community. In recent decades, the city has become symbolic of Moroccan-Jewish culture in Israel, finding its expression in music, cinema and state politics.

Historically, Sderot is considered a bastion of the Israeli right, and especially a stronghold of Prime MinisterBenjamin Netanyahu's Likud. In 2022, Likud received more than 40% of the votes in Sderot, compared with 23% nationwide. Other members of Israel's far-right coalition received a lot of support there in the last election.

This pattern reflects deeper historical divisions in Israel between Ashkenazim, or Jews of European origin, and Mizrahim, or Jews mostly from Middle Eastern and North African backgrounds. According to estimates, half of Israel's Jewish population is of Mizrahi origin, amounting to about 4 million people.

Mizrahi Jews have faced discrimination and neglect since the early days of the Israeli state. This inequality still shapes the economic and social reality in Israel ― and its political discourse.

The issue has received special attention in the 2026 campaign. Eisenkot, Netanyahu's major challenger, is of Moroccan origin and grew up in Eilat, a city with a large North African Jewish population. Eisenkot's Yashar party accused Likud of sidelining its Mizrahi politicians. The campaign slogan, "Netanyahu: Let the Mizrahim give interviews,” mocked that omission.

On live television, the right-wing journalist Yinon Magal — one of Netanyahu's most vocal supporters — claimed that Eisenkot was being controlled by elitist Ashkenazim and called him a "monkey."

Netanyahu, who is of European origin, condemned the remark.

Morocco-born Daniel Ben-Simon, an award-winning Israeli journalist and a former member of parliament, told DW that Eisenkot's embrace of his roots might have been forced upon him by Magal's comments — but it has historical value nevertheless.

"People don't use terms like 'Dialna' with pride," Ben-Simon said, referring to Eisenkot's embrace of the Arabic term "of our own" in campaign posters. He said Israeli society had long tried to erase the Arab identities of North African Jews.

Eisenkot's embrace of his identity serves as a way of "leaving the frame" set by society. "By using such a term, you're creating a new identity: an Israeli-Moroccan identity," Ben-Simon said. "Eisenkot is the first to allow this double identity to be expressed and still feel at home."

After Israel's founding, the ruling socialist party, Mapai, whose leadership was largely Ashkenazi, pursued discriminatory policies against Mizrahim. Mizrahim were often expected to "Europeanize” rather than preserve their own culture, heritage, or Arabic language.

These inequalities also took a material form. In the state's early decades, while European immigrants often received better housing and jobs, many Jews from Arab-speaking countries were sent to Ma'abarot, or temporary transit camps, often in remote areas with little infrastructure. Many later became "development towns,” which remained underfunded compared with Israel's central regions. That in turn resulted in poverty. Sderot, in the south, is one of the best-known development towns.

For many Mizrahim, limited access to jobs, education and infrastructure symbolized long-standing exclusion by the Mapai establishment. Likud, founded as a right-wing alliance in 1973, used this frustration effectively under Menachem Begin. Mizrahi support for Begin helped produce the Mahapach, or upheaval, when Likud first defeated the socialist camp and took power. Since then, Mizrahim have often been associated with Likud, even though the party has never had a Mizrahi leader.

Over the past two decades, Sderot's proximity to Gaza has made the city a frequent target of rocket attacks launched by the militant group Hamas. During the Hamas-led terrorist attacks on October 7, 2023, the city of Sderot was hit especially hard. More than 50 people were killed.  In the three years since the attack, the city's population has grown by about 5,000 people, bringing it to 40,000, with many young families making the town their home, both as a result of idealism and government benefits.

Walking around the town, it's hard not to notice that the so-called national-religious community is becoming increasingly dominant. Protein shakes for teenagers: Good or bad idea?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Protein products are marketed as a way of building muscle and losing weight — and they're very popular with young people. They also claim to promote healthy growth in children. Is this true? How much protein is healthy?

You can take it as a powdered drink, or in a snack bar. Puddings and certain breads are also promoted as "sources of protein," and protein-enriched products like Kindershake and Teen Balance are specifically designed to appeal to a young people — and it appears to be working.

In a representative survey of almost 1,000 parents by the University of Michigan in 2024, just under half said their children, who were aged 13 to 17, had consumed protein products over the previous year. The survey found that boys were more likely to do so than girls, especially in connection with sport, or to build muscle. Girls took them to balance their diet, and to lose weight.

According to the statistics portal Statista, between March 2024 and March 2025 the fitness protein product market in Germany recorded a huge jump of around 25% in retail revenue.

Proteins are key building blocks for our metabolism. They play an important role in building up and repairing tissue in our muscles, skin and organs. Proteins are the basis for the formation of enzymes that regulate chemical reactions in the body, and of antibodies that are vital for our immune system.

According to the German Nutrition Society (DGE), healthy adults should consume 0.8 grams of protein daily per kilogram of body weight. A woman weighing 65 kilos should eat 52 grams of protein per day, for example.

But they can do this without special protein products. A portion of oat flakes with an almond drink for breakfast, a baked potato with curd cheese for lunch, and two slices of wholemeal bread with peanut butter in the evening — objective achieved.

Young people should consume a little more, as they are still growing. For them, the formula is 0.9 grams of protein per kilo of body weight. A teenager weighing 45 kilos should therefore eat 40.5 grams of protein per day. People over 65 and children aged one to four should have a little more: one gram of protein per kilo of body weight.

Older people tend to eat too little protein, often because their appetite decreases. But a study done in Germany between 2015 and 2017 showed that children and young people there often consume too much. Around half of six- to 17-year-olds ate more than double the recommended daily amount of protein.

Too much protein can be harmful in the long term, according to the DGE. Various studies show that children who eat too much protein, particularly animal protein, often become overweight. Some enter puberty early.

Above all, too much protein can put excessive strain on the kidneys, causing damage. The kidneys have to convert the amino acids that constitute the building blocks of proteins into urea before they are excreted in urine. Damage is particularly likely if a person doesn't drink enough fluids, or has pre-existing kidney damage — but adolescents are also at greater risk.

A person's protein requirement is greater if they are very physically active. Among other things, according to a DGE position paper, proteins can help maintain or build up muscular mass and strength, and support recovery after strain.

The paper states that, depending on physical condition and what they are training for, sportspeople should consume between 1.2 and 2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight, divided into several portions throughout the day. These recommendations only apply to people who train for more than five hours a day.

Proteins are of interest to people who want to lose weight because they are good at satisfying hunger. People usually lose more weight by following a protein-rich diet rather than another. Long-term, though, the most important thing to look at is: How many calories am I consuming, and how many am I burning?

Overall, plant proteins usually contain somewhat fewer essential amino acids than animal protein. "Essential" because the body cannot make them by itself – it has to take them in as food.

However, wholegrain products, pulses, or nuts are also higher in fiber, vitamins, and unsaturated fats – all of which are healthy. The DGE therefore advises that protein intake should be increased primarily with sources of plant protein.

Protein powders and bars are often enriched not only with vitamins and minerals but also with unhelpful ingredients like sweeteners, sugar, additives and flavorings. The German Sport University in Cologne even found doping substances in protein products that were praised for building muscle.

Adolescents in particular might experience an unpleasant side-effect of consuming protein powders. These usually consist of milk proteins. There are various ways in which milk proteins can increase the skin's production of sebum, and this can trigger or exacerbate acne, although a direct link between milk protein products and acne has not been definitively proven.

Specialists are also skeptical about protein shakes, fearing that they could lead to young people eating fewer normal meals, and thus consuming less fiber, micronutrients, and healthy fats.

Protein-enriched foods are usually expensive. In a 2022 study, consumer organizations also found that foods that are naturally high in protein, such as cheese, curds, or soya drinks, often cost more if the protein content has been highlighted by the manufacturer.

Not even sportspeople with a high protein requirement need to supplement their diet with special protein products. The DGE advises that they should aim to satisfy their requirements by optimizing their diet, and says there is no scientific evidence that our bodies require protein-enriched food supplements when put under strain.

However, the experts do say that it may make sense for people to use protein preparations if they have allergies, problems with digestion, chewing, or swallowing, or if their appetite is very low.

Relations between the two countries deteriorated in 2025, when the US imposed tariffs on Brazil in retaliation for legal proceedings against former president Jair Bolsonaro, who was later convicted of coup-plotting.

Speaking in a hoarse voice, Lula thanked his campaign team and Brazilians, particularly young people who took to the streets to defend democracy. He described the result as a "loss for negativity, lies."

The re-elected president also promised better regulation of big tech platforms, after Meta was the subject of legal complaints over alleged "interference" throughout the polarizing campaign.

Several Latin American leaders congratulated leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva on his victory.

Chilean President Jose Antonio Kast said he had spoken to Lula and congratulated him, adding that "Chile and Brazil have a lot of shared future ahead."

Mexico’s left-wing President Claudia Sheinbaum congratulated Lula on his victory, writing on X: "Long live democracy, the people of Brazil won. Congratulations, President Lula!"

The presidents of Uruguay, Paraguay, Panama and Bolivia also congratulated Lula in posts on X.

Cuba’s president congratulated Lula on X, calling him "dear brother" and praising his victory as a win for peace, sovereignty and social justice.

Brazilian leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has won a fourth term in office, defeating right-wing challenger Flávio Bolsonaro in a presidential runoff, electoral authorities said.

Lula overcame his first-round defeat to Bolsonaro to secure victory in the runoff. The nearly 81-year-old leader won 51.7% of the vote, compared with 48.2% for Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro.

The campaign was marred by scandals and mudslinging. Sunday’s vote was thrown into confusion after a last-minute Supreme Court ruling extended voting by two hours, following Lula’s claim that buses had been taken off the roads in several cities, preventing some voters from reaching polling stations.

Pollsters said higher-than-expected turnout appeared to have benefited Lula. Conservative candidates in Brazil typically lead early in the count, while left-wing candidates gain ground as votes from poorer, more populous northeastern states come in.

In regards to foreign policy in his next term, Lula will have to manage relations with Washington while maintaining his anti-imperialist stance, as the Trump administration increasingly asserts its influence across the region. Flavio Bolsonaro had campaigned on a promise of closer cooperation with Washington.

Around 159 million Brazilians were eligible to vote. Under Lula’s left-wing Workers’ Party, unemployment fell to a record low, poverty declined, the minimum wage rose and deforestation in the Amazon decreased. Congress also approved the gradual introduction of a five-day working week with two days off.

However, rising living costs, high public debt, Lula’s spending policies and a scandal involving alleged pension fraud weighed on his government. The economic challenges left many voters dissatisfied with his record and open to a return of the Bolsonaro family to power.

Lula finished the first round with 45% of the vote, two percentage points behind Flavio Bolsonaro. He became the first candidate since Brazil restored direct presidential elections in 1989 to overcome a first-round deficit and win the runoff.

The former trade unionist, who served as president from 2003 to 2010 before returning to office in 2023, now faces a challenging fourth term with a strong right-wing opposition.

Brazil's Supreme Court has moved to extend voting time in the presidential election until 7 p.m. local time (2200 GMT), giving voters an additional two hours.

The decision comes amid complaints from Lula's team that public bus services had been hampered, blocking people from getting to polling stations.

At the same time, Bolsonaro's team complained of weather conditions in certain regions making it more difficult for people to get out and vote.

President Lula has requested an extension to poll opening hours after buses were taken out of circulation in Sao Paolo, the country's largest city, which he said prevented many people from voting.

"We are asking the Supreme Court to extend the electoral process so that those who were effectively barred from voting, due to the dirty tactics of mayors, can still cast their votes," Lula said, according to AFP.

Lula said 40% of buses in the city had been prevented from going into service, a claim that matched local media reports saying 75 buses had been blocked from leaving garages.

"I've never complained about anything in any election before. Whether I won or lost, I'd just accept it and get ready for the next one. But this time, something abominable is happening," he said.

"No one should give up on voting just because some scoundrel of a business owner or some crooked, criminal mayor tried to stop the people from casting their ballots."

Brazilians in Germany have voted overwhelmingly for Lula, according to figures posted by Brazilian Embassies across the country and seen by DW's Portuguese service.

The left-wing incumbent, Lula, won 79.09% of the ballots cast by the 20,923 voters across Germany. This marks an increase on the 71.9% he won in the first round.

Brazilian voters in Germany could vote at embassies in Berlin, Hamburg, Cologne, Frankfurt and Munich.

The German capital saw the greatest support for Lula, with 88.77% of the votes going his way. Frankfurt was the city with the greatest support for Bolsonaro, but on 29.03% he still trailed his opponent by some margin.

The turnout also increased in the second round in comparison to the first round when it stood at 19,322. However, for both rounds, only around half of the 41,552 eligible voters turned out to vote. Across the world, there are some 918,900 eligible voters for Sunday's election.

Although he hasn't endorsed either candidate, US President Donald Trump is close to the Bolsonaro family.

Not only did the senior Bolsonaro imitate Trump's rhetoric and policies, he also refused to concede defeat after losing to Lula in 2022, leading to his imprisonment for an attempted coup, provoking comparisons with Trump's own defeat to Joe Biden in 2020.

On the other side, Lula has presented himself as a defense against US interference.

The right "wants to govern with the American flag," Lula said after casting his ballot on Sunday.

A Bolsonaro win would mark a further move in Latin America toward pro-US far-right leaders, following in the footsteps of countries such as Colombia, Chile and Peru.

The president of Brazil's Superior Electoral Court, Kassio Nunes Marques, has said he expects the winner of Sunday's vote to be announced officially between 8.30 p.m. and 9 p.m. (2330 and 0000 GMT), according to A Folha de Sao Paolo.

Brazil uses an electronic voting system meaning that ballots can be tallied quicker than in elections where paper votes are counted by hand.

However, the gap between the winner and loser is expected to be small, with the two running head-to-head in pre-election polls.

The head of Brazil's Supreme Federal Court, Edson Fachin, has called for the candidates to respect the result of Sunday's vote.

"It's important to remember that all authority and power comes from the people and, thus, it is fundamental that the choices that derive from the manifestation of popular sovereignty are respected," he said as he left his local polling station, according to A Folha de Sao Paulo.

"Brazil needs peace and calm to, from tomorrow, be able to confront the major challenges in many areas that demand the presence of the state."

Some observers have posited that Flavio Bolsonaro could refuse to accept a Lula victory, like his father before him, with the potential for violence to erupt.

Brazil is heading back to the polls, but the impact could be felt worldwide.
