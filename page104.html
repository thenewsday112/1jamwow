"Je me retrouve sans travail parce que j’avais envie de faire un gros caca" : à Toulouse, un livreur dénonce son éviction de Boulanger

l'essentiel L'humoriste toulousain Nuno Di Porto affirme avoir été écarté des livraisons pour Boulanger après un différend sur l'accès aux toilettes. Son témoignage, publié sur Instagram, a fait réagir l'enseigne, qui dit examiner la situation.

"Je me retrouve sans travail parce que j’avais envie de faire un gros caca." La formule prête à sourire, mais Nuno Di Porto, humoriste et comédien installé à Toulouse, assure que l’histoire est bien réelle. Dans une vidéo publiée sur Instagram vendredi 23 octobre, il affirme avoir été écarté de son activité de livreur pour Boulanger après un différend avec un responsable concernant l’accès aux toilettes.

Une publication partagée par Nuno Sarmento (@nunodiporto)

De son vrai nom Nuno Sarmento, le Toulousain travaille depuis le début de l’année pour une entreprise de transport chargée d’effectuer des livraisons pour l’enseigne d’électroménager. Selon son récit, l’arrivée d’un nouveau directeur aurait changé les règles sur son lieu de travail.

Jusqu’alors, les quelque vingt livreurs disposaient de quatre sanitaires. Mais l’accès aux deux toilettes du premier étage leur aurait été interdit. Ne resteraient donc que deux toilettes au rez-de-chaussée, dont une sans éclairage fonctionnel.

Mardi, alors que les sanitaires éclairés étaient occupés, Nuno aurait tenté de se rendre à l’étage. Le directeur lui aurait alors reproché de ne pas respecter les nouvelles consignes.

"Je comprends parfaitement qu’une entreprise impose des règles. Mais encore faut-il que les conditions permettent de les respecter", écrit le livreur sur Instagram.

D’après lui, les conséquences ne se seraient pas fait attendre. Son employeur lui aurait annoncé qu’il lui était désormais interdit de venir travailler pour Boulanger. Une décision qu’il estime injuste, assurant faire partie des livreurs les mieux notés par les clients.

L’humoriste a donc décidé de raconter sa mésaventure sur les réseaux sociaux, mêlant indignation et humour scatologique. Dans une seconde publication, il insiste : "Être directeur ne donne pas tous les droits. Le respect doit aller dans les deux sens, quel que soit le poste occupé !"

Une publication partagée par Nuno Sarmento (@nunodiporto)

Son témoignage a suscité une réaction de Boulanger. Sous sa publication, le compte officiel de l’enseigne indique être entré en contact avec lui pour comprendre les faits.

Sollicité par La Dépêche du Midi, le service de presse confirme avoir pris connaissance de l’affaire et assure la traiter "avec le plus grand sérieux". "Le bien-être et la dignité de toutes les personnes qui travaillent au sein de notre écosystème sont une priorité", précise l’entreprise, qui dit avoir ouvert un dialogue avec l’intéressé et mené des échanges internes.

Boulanger ne confirme toutefois ni les restrictions d’accès aux sanitaires ni les circonstances de la mise à l’écart dénoncée par le livreur. Reste donc à savoir si Nuno pourra reprendre ses livraisons pour l’enseigne.

I've exorcised 50,000 people... and I believe the devil is now using technology in the fight for our souls

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published: 15:00 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 20:54 GMT, 25 October 2026

An American exorcist, who claims to have evicted demons from 50,000 people over 50 years, has described what he believes is a terrifying new way the devil is claiming victims.

Bob Larson became famous in the 1980s with a radio call-in show called Talk Back, where he performed ‘live’ exorcisms over the phone.

He now conducts the ceremonial practice via Skype and Zoom, charging a set fee ranging from $295 to $395 for a one-hour remote video session.

But his latest warning concerns what people encounter online, from social media and spiritual websites to AI chatbots, which he believes can draw vulnerable users toward the occult.

He told the Daily Mail that he has exorcised people who he believes became possessed after using ChatGPT and AI chatbots.

These are Larson’s religious interpretations of the experiences he describes. His accounts alone do not establish that chatbot use caused demonic possession.

‘I worked with a young woman the other day who was talking to a chatbot, and this thing was pushing her deeper and deeper into very strange types of thoughts, and finally got to the point where it was telling her to commit suicide,’ said Larson.

He said the woman was looking for love and connection and developed a romantic attachment to the chatbot, describing it as a ‘love relationship with this thing'.

Bob Larson became famous in the 1980s with a radio call-in show called Talk Back, where he performed ‘live’ exorcisms over the phone

His account alone does not establish the full contents of the conversation or what caused the woman’s distress. Even if a chatbot produced harmful responses, that would not demonstrate a supernatural explanation for her experiences.

Larson also believes chatbots and spiritual websites can draw people searching for love or answers into witchcraft and the occult.

‘Sometimes it is a connection they make with a voice that speaks to them through ChatGPT or whatever,’ he said. ‘But they are looking to communicate with another realm. They're looking for an answer.’

Larson believes possession often follows trauma or dysfunction, when people become angry at God and dabble in witchcraft or the occult. That explanation reflects his beliefs rather than a medical assessment of the people he describes.

The US Conference of Catholic Bishops says thorough medical, psychological and psychiatric assessments should precede referral for a major exorcism, which requires a bishop or a priest authorized by the local bishop.

Its guidance also calls for input from medical and mental health professionals when investigating possession claims.

Larson’s claims have also faced scrutiny on television.

A 2019 episode of Dr Phil featured a couple who said they had undergone exorcisms, with host Phil McGraw challenging their belief that demons were responsible for their experiences and suggesting psychological explanations.

His latest warning concerns what people encounter online, from social media and spiritual websites to AI chatbots, which he believes can draw vulnerable users toward the occult

Larson maintains that his work is a form of Christian ministry, using prayer and commands in Jesus’ name to expel what he believes are evil spirits.

His latest warning focuses on how technology is changing the way people encounter what he considers the occult.

In previous decades, people would experiment with Ouija boards, he said, but he believes temptation is now often found online or on social media.

One woman, he claimed, encountered a demon after visiting a ‘twin flame’ website, based on the New Age idea of a spiritual match who is the other half of someone’s soul.

‘She followed all they were telling her about how to meet your twin flame, your true love, this spiritual being who will come to you,’ Larson explained.

‘Well, she ended up having sexual demonic experiences, and that completely derailed her life.’

That description is Larson’s characterization of the woman’s experiences and does not establish that a supernatural encounter occurred.

His interest in exorcisms began while travelling in Southeast Asia, where he first witnessed an exorcism he described as ‘very raw, very violent, very physical’.

The encounter left him wondering whether demonic possession might happen in America.

When he began half a century ago, he said: ‘Outside of the Catholic Church, most churches said little about it, didn't know anything about it. There were no books on the subject.’

While he offers services via Skype and Zoom, Larson still attends churches to perform exorcisms

His first encounter in America with what he interpreted as possession occurred while giving a speech at a Christian youth group, when a young girl approached him.

Larson said: ‘She said, “I don't believe in your God.” I said, “Well, why don't you believe in God?” She said, “I believe in the devil.” I said, “Well, then, why do you believe in the devil?” She said, “I have sex with the devil".’

He described it as ‘going from zero to 100’ and admitted he had little idea what to do.

‘The demon started speaking. Now, what was particularly interesting is this demon actually claimed to be from England, and this is a 15-year-old girl from rural Missouri who is speaking in the Queen's English, in a beautiful British accent,’ Larson said.

The account is his recollection of the encounter. A reported change in accent or behavior does not, by itself, establish demonic possession.

A former missionary helped him perform what Larson described as an exorcism.

‘I did the best that I could. I didn't even know enough to get her address to follow up and see how she was doing. It was just sort of a state of shock,’ he said.

His admission that he did not follow up also leaves the girl’s subsequent condition unclear.

He then studied Catholic treatises and books from around the world, teaching himself how to perform exorcisms.

He said: ‘One thing that really troubled me was that I couldn't talk to priests and pastors about it. Nobody wanted to talk about it, and they ostracised me. So I had to go my own way because the organised church wanted nothing to do with it.’

People began contacting him through his radio show.

He said: ‘So I'm really curious, and then it just started happening. People started coming to me. People would call my radio show, and in the middle of talking with me, they would manifest demonic behaviour.’

For Larson, an exorcism begins with questions about a person’s background, including their family, religious history and current beliefs, according to his Spiritual Freedom Church website.

The ritual aims to expel what the practitioner believes is an evil spirit through prayer and commands invoking Jesus Christ.

In a 2017 account, Cronkite News described Larson using a cross, a Bible and holy water, pressing the cross and Bible against a person’s head and back while those around them prayed and ordered the supposed spirit to leave.

His approach differs from the Catholic Church’s formal rite, which includes prayers, Bible readings, blessed water and the sign of the cross.

The Church’s guidance also cautions against too readily attributing a person’s behavior to the devil and emphasizes careful assessment before proceeding.

Larson has also taken his rituals online, arguing that distance does not prevent him from performing them.

‘Then I started doing it on Zoom, and CNN sent out a crew, and they said every priest, every pastor we talked to says you can't dispel spirits on Skype,' he said.

‘I said, “Sure, why not? Jesus would have done it.” I think Jesus would have used Skype and Zoom or whatever technology was available, and by God's direction, been a pioneer to try all these new ways to connect with people.’

He says some exorcists working for him previously underwent his rituals themselves, and that he still performs exorcisms five to six days a week.

Above all, he believes there’s hope, saying: ‘Christ is more powerful than the devil. Stop playing Satan's game and start putting your life in relationship with God.

‘And exorcisms might be the thing that will change you into a healthy, loving, productive person.

‘No matter what a person has been through, no matter what trauma they've experienced, no matter what dysfunction has happened in their life, no matter how much they've been beaten down, with Christ there is hope, and with deliverance and exorcism there is hope.’

Explosions rang out in the Saudi capital on Sunday (October 25, 2026), as the kingdom was due to reopen Riyadh international airport despite repeated attacks by Yemen's Houthis.

Saudi Arabia, which leads a military coalition fighting the Iran-backed Houthis, has faced a barrage of strikes from the group in recent weeks.

The fighting erupted after a years-long truce between the Houthis and Yemen's Riyadh-backed government fell apart, and has shifted the focus of the wider U.S.-Iran war.

Riyadh's international airport said flights would resume on Sunday (October 25, 2026) after a two-week closure following the Houthi strikes.

However, it remained unclear to what extent the airport was operational.

The Houthi fighters have carried out waves of strikes on the airport, including an attack this month that killed 13 people and wounded more than 300, the deadliest in the kingdom in more than a decade.

Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree warned that the facility was still "unsafe" and a "military target".

"The Yemeni Armed Forces warn all international, regional, and local airlines — as well as staff and travellers — against using King Khalid Airport in Riyadh, as it is unsafe and serves as a military target for our forces," he said.

"The Saudi regime shall bear the consequences of any operations at the airport."

An AFP journalist in the city heard three explosions in the early hours of Sunday (October 25, 2026), with residents receiving alert messages for a "hostile aerial threat" urging them to stay in a safe place.

The warning came as the Saudi-led coalition fighting the Houthis said it had downed a ballistic missile launched by the group towards Riyadh, as well as two drones in the capital area.

"Air defences intercepted and destroyed a ballistic missile launched by the terrorist Houthi militia toward Riyadh," the coalition posted on X.

Later Sunday (October 25, 2026) morning, the coalition said its forces intercepted a cruise missile fired by the Houthis at the kingdom's southern city of Khamis Mushait.

Attacks appeared to target areas across the south, with warnings also issued for Abha city and Namas Governorate.

"The bombardment has been very heavy since this morning — may God keep us safe. Everything is shaking because of the explosions," one resident in Khamis Mushait told AFP on Sunday on condition of anonymity.

The attacks came after the Houthi fighters said that — in the space of 24 hours — they targeted three Saudi airports, including in Riyadh, energy installations, a pipeline and an air base.

The Saudi civil defence agency said it had also responded to falling debris in the Mecca and Medina regions on Saturday (October 24, 2026) after intercepting two Houthi missiles.

The Houthis denied targeting the cities, the two holiest in Islam, calling the Saudi claim a "huge lie".

The Houthis also fired at the southern Yemeni city of Aden, the interim seat of the Saudi-backed government, saying they targeted a naval base and military camp overseen by Saudi forces.

The flare-up in Yemen, with the Houthis taking control of the entire Red Sea coast in a lightning offensive last month, has further destabilised the region and disrupted oil markets.

The Houthi attacks have dealt a blow to Saudi Arabia, the world's biggest exporter of crude, disrupting businesses, airlines and residents, and rattling its drive to shift its international image.

Storms batter Europe hitting power supplies, flights and phones

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

River Eden in Carlisle, north-west England, swollen on Oct 25. Hundreds of flights were cancelled or diverted because of storms across northern Europe.

LONDON – Storms battered northern Europe on Oct 25, cutting power to more than 150,000 homes, forcing the cancellation of hundreds of flights and silencing phone networks.

A runner in a marathon in northern France was seriously injured runner when a tree was felled by strong winds during the race.

More than 82,000 homes in Britain, nearly 60,000 in France and at least 10,000 in Germany had power cuts inflicted by the passage of storms Boelo and Austen within 24 hours of each other, authorities in the countries said.

In the Argyll and Bute region of western Scotland, winds of up to 135 kmh were recorded. Trees and power cables brought down by the storm blocked roads and rail lines across Scotland and northern England.

Britain’s Energy Networks Association indicated that more than 400,000 homes had lost power at some stage during the storms.

Part of the pier at Brighton the southern England coast, unused since a fire in 2003, blew away in Storm Austen on Oct 23.

The powerful winds also battered northern France across the Channel as Europe battled its first big autumn storms of the year. Experts have warned of more extreme weather events because of global warming.

A runner was injured in the northern French city of Lille near the start of the city’s marathon, which was being staged for the first time in 31 years.

According to local newspaper La Voix du Nord, the victim was a 25-year-old man. Race organisers told AFP the runner’s life was not in danger.

French energy company Enedis said that at the peak of storm Austen on Oct 24, some 288,000 homes had no electricity.

Some 59,000 remained without power at midday Oct 25, mainly in Normandy and Brittany. It said 2,600 Enedis technicians and contractors were working to re-establish power.

In Germany, Austen cut power to 10,000 homes in and around the south-western town of Friburg-Brisgau on Oct 25. The town’s main hospital said it had to limit service to emergency cases because of the blackout that also cut the mobile phone network.

Two people were seriously hurt on Oct 24 by scaffolding blown away by storm Austen’s winds during an autumn market at the town of Nastatten, German police said.

Germany’s DWD weather agency predicted more powerful winds of up to 100 kmh on the northern coast on Oct 25.

The storms also hit the Netherlands and Belgium, where falling trees injured four people in Brussels on Oct 24.

Hundreds of flights were cancelled or diverted because of the storms across northern Europe.

Amsterdam’s Schiphol airport, one of Europe’s busiest hubs, cancelled dozens of flights on Oct 25.

AFP counted some 90 flights cancelled on the airport website, mainly to European cities including Paris, Rome and Dublin. Dozens of flights due to land at the airport were also cancelled.

Flights in northern England and Scotland were also hit by the storms. AFP

Michigan Gov. Gretchen Whitmer says America is ready to elect a female president

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Michigan Gov. Gretchen Whitmer pushed back Sunday on comments made by former first lady Michelle Obama last year that U.S. voters aren’t ready to elect a female president.

“Listen, I have got a lot of colleagues who I think are probably interested in running for president, and I’ve heard everyone say, ‘You can’t have this person or that person,’” the governor told NBC News’ “Meet the Press.”

“I would put my money on any Democratic governor in the country against [Vice President] JD Vance or [Secretary of State] Marco Rubio in 2028,” she added, pointing to two Republicans widely expected to run for president in two years.

Asked directly whether a woman could be the best choice to beat a potential GOP presidential nominee in 2028, Whitmer simply said, “I do.”

In 2025, Obama said in public remarks: “As we saw in this past election, sadly, we ain’t ready. That’s why I’m like, don’t even look at me about running, because you all are lying. You’re not ready for a woman. You are not.”

Several women, including Whitmer and former Vice President Kamala Harris, are widely speculated to be potential candidates in the 2028 Democratic presidential primary.

On Sunday, Whitmer did not deny that she’s exploring a potential run for president in 2028, saying that she’ll decide on her next steps after she departs from her post as governor in January.

“When I exit office … I’m going to take a little time off and figure out what I want to do next, so I’ve got nothing new to share with you, unfortunately,” Whitmer told “Meet the Press” moderator Kristen Welker.

Earlier this year, Whitmer denied that she planned to run for president but quickly walked back the comments.

“There will be a robust group of people running for president,” she said at the time, adding, “I will not be one of them in 2028, I can tell you that.”

Later the same day, the governor said: “I never thought I would run for governor. So I guess I should know better to never say never.”

Several other Democratic governors, including California Gov. Gavin Newsom, Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker, Maryland Gov. Wes Moore and Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro, are also widely regarded as potential 2028 presidential candidates. Each has been crisscrossing the country ahead of the midterms campaigning on behalf of Democratic gubernatorial candidates in competitive races.

Whitmer on Sunday highlighted her support for former public health official Amy Acton, the Democratic nominee for governor in Ohio.

Earlier this week, Acton’s opponent, GOP businessman Vivek Ramaswamy, released a TV ad featuring Acton’s mother calling her a “liar.” Acton has publicly shared that she’s been estranged from her mother for nearly 50 years and has accused her late stepfather of sexually abusing her. Acton and her brother were removed from her mother’s custody in 1978 after her stepfather, Cledith Arthur, was arrested on a child rape charge. Acton’s mother has not responded to requests for comment.

“I’m going to stay focused on getting people like Amy Acton over the finish line. What’s happened to her in Ohio is absolutely abhorrent, and any survivor knows there’s always a backlash. But this is what makes it harder for women to seek help after they’ve been sexually assaulted in this country,” Whitmer said Sunday. “It is unreal what’s going on, and that’s why we’ve got to — we’ve got to win races like this and elect good people who are going to keep the people at the heart of their work, like Dr. Amy Acton.”

Ramaswamy’s decision to air the ad has faced widespread bipartisan condemnation, some of it from GOP Sen. Jon Husted of Ohio, who is locked in a tight re-election race.

Alexandra Marquez is a politics reporter for NBC News.
