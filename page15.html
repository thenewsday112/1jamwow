Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar ("Straight") party speaks at a campaign rally ahead of Israel's general election, October 25, 2026, in Tel Aviv, Israel. | Photo Credit: AP

Israeli election contender Gadi Eisenkot on Sunday (October 25, 2026) vowed to unite the country as he held his final major rally before the vote, while Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu received fresh backing from U.S. President Donald Trump.

Mr. Netanyahu has campaigned heavily on his security credentials, but Mr. Eisenkot has cast him as unfit to lead and pledged a state commission of inquiry into the failings that led to Hamas's October 7, 2023 attack under the incumbent's watch.

Polls had shown Mr. Netanyahu and Mr. Eisenkot running neck and neck before a ban on new polling took effect in the three days before Tuesday's (October 27, 2026) vote.

On Sunday (October 25, 2026), Mr. Trump urged Israelis to vote for Mr. Netanyahu, citing the U.S.-Israel war against Iran as an example of "winning together".

"When you go to the Polls please remember that Bibi has been an outstanding Warrior Prime Minister," Mr. Trump wrote on his Truth Social platform, using Mr. Netanyahu's nickname.

At his event, Mr. Eisenkot pledged to heal divisions inside Israeli society after Mr. Netanyahu's divisive tenure, and voiced "a commitment to security and the economy".

"Our goal is not for one side to defeat the other. That is not our way," he said on Sunday (October 25, 2026) to an audience of hundreds.

"We will work to bring all Israelis together again — those who voted for us and those who did not."

A relative newcomer to politics — in contrast with Mr. Netanyahu and his 30 years on the scene — Mr. Eisenkot's pledge to unite Israelis has resonated with some voters, and for many the ex-army chief's plain-spokenness is part of his appeal.

"I feel that he's a rock for us... he's someone I can trust," Yael Shkedi, a 66-year-old jewellery maker, said at Mr. Eisenkot's rally.

"Amid all this chaos, what really matters to me is this feeling of someone who can somehow bring everyone together, almost like a father figure."

In his speech, Mr. Eisenkot said he would "always look you in the eye and tell the truth", and pledged to "take responsibility for both successes and failures".

He also appealed directly to wavering supporters of Mr. Netanyahu's Likud party, urging them to change course.

"Deep down, you too know that Israel cannot go on like this," he said. "Israel needs change."

Mr. Netanyahu's critics accuse him of putting his political survival above the greater good, and he is currently facing trial on corruption charges, which he denies.

Rally attendee Chen Sharon, an army reservist, said she wanted "people that I can count on" in office.

"We need someone that we can actually believe his values and what he represents to us," she said.

Over the course of the campaign Mr. Netanyahu has played to Israeli voters' opposition to the creation of a Palestinian state, and posters have been splashed across the country with the slogan "Either the Right or Palestine".

Mr. Netanyahu also touted the still-unresolved war that he launched against Iran alongside Mr. Trump, seeking to tar his main rival as being soft on the Islamic republic.

Appearing after Mr. Eisenkot's rally on Channel 14, Mr. Netanyahu again suggested the ex-general and his allies "want to create a Palestinian state, a catastrophe for Israel".

"They will not destroy Iran's nuclear programme," he told a boisterous studio audience. "We've done 90% of the work, 10% remains. Who is going to do it? Mr. Eisenkot and the Muslim Brotherhood? No. Only I can do it."

Mr. Eisenkot has said he is opposed to a Palestinian state, but has vowed to crack down on extremist West Bank settlers who have attacked Palestinians with impunity.

Mr. Netanyahu on Sunday (October 25, 2026) posted an AI-generated image of a mushroom cloud engulfing Tel Aviv, alongside text attributed to a right-wing political commentator calling Iran an existential threat and arguing that all the other issues discussed this election season were trivial in comparison.

In a post on X addressing Mr. Netanyahu on Saturday (October 24, 2026), Mr. Eisenkot accused the premier of playing up the Iran threat when it suited him politically.

"You have once again failed spectacularly and blatantly to achieve the war objectives you yourself set," he added.

Longtime opposition leader Yair Lapid, who has promised to join a coalition with Mr. Eisenkot, said Mr. Netanyahu had "lost his grip".

"Yesterday, Mr. Netanyahu announced that U.S. security aid was over and that sanctions would crush our economy. This morning, he posted a video in which an atomic bomb lands on Tel Aviv," he said on X.

"This man has lost his grip. We absolutely must send him home."

Published - October 26, 2026 08:43 am IST - Rio de Janeiro

Opposition presidential candidate Sen. Flavio Bolsonaro arrives at his election night event after the presidential runoff in Brasilia, Brazil, Sunday, October 25, 2026. | Photo Credit: AP

Brazil's President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a vocal critic of President Donald Trump, was reelected on Sunday (October 25, 2026) in a result that provides relief to critics of widening U.S. influence in Latin America.

With 100% of the vote counted, Brazil's electoral court announced that the soon-to-be 81-year-old Mr. Lula had a lead of nearly 4.4 million votes — 51.8% of the total vote — over opponent Flávio Bolsonaro. The announcement capped an evening of chaos at polling places across Brazil, following a last-minute decision to extend voting by two hours.

Late Sunday (October 25, 2026), Mr. Bolsonaro refused to concede defeat, calling the move to extend voting hours unprecedented — though both he and Mr. Lula made the request — and asked for the election results to be annulled, saying they were impossible to accept.

Mr. Lula's triumph bucks a trend in Latin America where increasing numbers of right-of-centre presidential candidates allied with Mr. Trump have been winning. Mr. Lula's victory leaves Mexico and Brazil, Latin America's two most populous nations, with left-of-centre leaders. It also positions Latin America's largest economy, with a GDP of $2.6 trillion per year, to remain independent of the U.S., despite the pressure of increased tariffs imposed by Mr. Trump.

A video published by Mr. Lula's campaign shows the veteran leader celebrating with First Lady Rosângela da Silva and allies after his runoff victory was confirmed by Brazil's electoral court.

Wearing star-shaped red glasses, he kissed his wife. Hundreds of his supporters began gathering on São Paulo's main artery to celebrate the victory.

In Rio de Janeiro, cars beeped their horns and a woman dressed in red — the colour of Mr. Lula’s supporters — shouted “You lost! Cry harder!” to four men in yellow and green sipping beers, Mr. Bolsonaro's signature colours. A huge roar echoed in the street as the result was confirmed.

Rodrigo Valentim, a 51-year-old businessman and supporter of Mr. Bolsonaro, said that another Lula government would harm the economy. He pointed to financial markets' positive reaction to the result of the first round, when Mr. Bolsonaro bested Mr. Lula.

“I don't understand how people can vote for Mr. Lula,” he said. “The country is collapsing.”

Mr. Bolsonaro had yet to respond to Mr. Lula's apparent victory, which, if confirmed by electoral authorities, will give him his non-consecutive fourth term as President. The next term begins on January 5, 2027.

The electoral court's announcement ended an evening of confusion at polling places after Brazilian Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin ruled three minutes before the scheduled end of voting to keep polls open for two more hours, following requests by both candidates. Mr. Lula's request came after issues with buses that provide transportation to polls in Sao Paulo and Salvador, two cities that tend to favour the incumbent. Mr. Bolsonaro's request was due to heavy rains in Brazil's south, where he is favoured.

In Brasilia, volunteers who oversaw the vote count at a school debated whether they needed to stay after the announcement to extend voting, but most left 15 to 20 minutes after polls closed and they had given count information to electoral authorities. At a polling station in Rio de Janeiro, the voting machines had already been switched off minutes after polls were set to close and couldn't be turned back on.

The decision delayed tallying the votes by two hours. It was unclear how many polling stations stayed open after the scheduled closing time.

Mr. Bolsonaro’s ally Nikolas Ferreira noted that Mr. Zanin, who made the decision, is Mr. Lula's former lawyer.

Mr. Bolsonaro bested Mr. Lula by more than 2.2 million votes in the first round of the election earlier this month. Claude Cohen-Tannoudji in 2009. | Photo Credit: Pruneau (CC BY-SA)

The French physicist Claude Cohen-Tannoudji, who helped develop techniques to cool atoms to barely above absolute zero using laser light, passed away on October 22, aged 93. His work revolutionised physics by giving scientists a way to manipulate atoms and probe their quantum properties, work for which he won a piece of the physics Nobel Prize in 1997.

As it happens, Cohen-Tannoudji also had many connections with physics in India, including acknowledging the work of a little-known — even in India — physicist who had died three decades before the prize was awarded.

Cohen-Tannoudji was born in French Algeria in 1933. He moved to Paris when he was 20 to study mathematics, before lectures by the physicist Alfred Kastler convinced him he should be studying physics instead. He soon joined a group led by Kastler and Jean Brossel in 1955 and then spent most of his career at the institution since called the Laboratoire Kastler Brossel.

Conventionally, lasers are good at delivering energy to objects, so the idea of using them to cool atoms down can seem counterintuitive. The key detail is that laser light also has momentum.

In one technique called Doppler cooling, scientists shine laser beams at atoms from different directions. The lasers are configured such that an atom moving towards one laser beam is more likely to absorb photons from that source than from the beams on other sides. When the atom has absorbed a photon, its own energy briefly rises before falling again as the atom spits the photon back out.

Here is the catch: the atom always absorbs photons in one direction but spits it out in random directions. So the kick provided by the exiting photon is cancelled by the kicks the atom is getting in multiple directions — whereas the reverse-kick happens in only one direction: in the direction of that one laser beam. So as the atom repeatedly absorbs and emits photons, its average kinetic energy, and therefore its temperature, drops.

Clever though it was, Doppler cooling had a lower limit: as the atom cooled, the lasers became less effective at slowing it further because they could no longer distinguish the direction of motion as easily. In 1988, the U.S. physicist William Phillips led a team that produced atoms colder than the Doppler cooling limit — and Cohen-Tannoudji and his peers, but especially Jean Dalibard, explained how this was possible. It had to do with the polarisation of light and the energy levels of the electrons in the target atoms.

Their explanation proved important because it led to a new technique called Sisyphus cooling, named for the doomed figure in Greek mythology who had to keep rolling a boulder uphill only for it to roll down again. Here, an atom’s kinetic energy is repeatedly converted to potential energy, which is ‘pumped out’ using a technique similar to Doppler cooling.

The name of the technique comes from a different way to visualise the atom’s experience, so to speak. Lasers are used to create a space where the atom, in motion, is forced to move from a part where it has lower potential energy to where it has more. The same thing happens when you climb a staircase: as you go from the ground floor to the first floor, you expend kinetic energy as you move but your body also accumulates more potential energy.

Once the atom is at the ‘top’, a process called an optical pump cools it down to have less energy than when it was on the ground floor. Then it is forced to climb another set of stairs. This way, repeatedly, the atom can be cooled to extremely low temperatures.

Cohen-Tannoudji’s group developed methods to cool atoms to a few nanokelvins — billionths of a degree above absolute zero — paving the way for advanced atomic clocks and atom interferometers.

In 1994, Cohen-Tannoudji contributed a three-page essay for a special issue of the Indian journal Current Science commemorating physicist S. Pancharatnam, who had worked with C.V. Raman in Bangalore (now Bengaluru) and later with George William Series in Britain. Unfortunately, Pancharatnam passed away in 1969, when he was only 35.

Pancharatnam did most of his work before tunable lasers became widely available, and in the same areas as Cohen-Tannoudji. In his article, Cohen-Tannoudji recalled Pancharatnam’s “series of beautiful papers” on optical pumping and remarked that recent developments in the field would have pleased him. He also acknowledged that some of the theoretical foundations of (then) contemporary laser cooling techniques had been considered well before the experimental proof could be devised.

The same issue of Current Science also featured an essay by the British physicist Michael Berry, who recalled visiting Bangalore in 1987 and discussing the work of Pancharatnam with Sivaraj Ramaseshan, which he said helped him appreciate the connection with Pancharatnam’s previous work and Berry’s own in the 1980s.

Cohen-Tannoudji visited Bangalore again in early 1999, where he participated in an Indo-French workshop at the Indian Institute of Astrophysics. He also visited the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in Mumbai on the same trip, where he delivered the first Homi J. Bhabha Lecture, followed by a lecture in New Delhi.

He also co-authored a 2001 paper in Physical Review Letters with C.S. Unnikrishnan that reported an unusual phase of matter called a Bose-Einstein condensate in a thin gas of helium — a significant achievement in atomic physics.

An anecdote: We invited him to the wonderful 60th birthday meeting of Sir Peter Knight at the Royal Society in London in 2007. He gave a wonderfully inspiring evening speech, blowing everybody away. But instead of boasting about his successes, he shyly came up to me and asked, "Jens, do you think that was appropriate?"

Cohen-Tannoudji was widely regarded as a generous figure in a profession that more than rarely featured fierce competition, and competitive personalities. Alain Aspect, who won a share of the physics Nobel Prize in 2022, has said that while some senior physicists considered Aspect’s early work (on experimentally testing the Bell’s inequalities) to be a waste of time, Cohen-Tannoudji was encouraging and helped Aspect be taken seriously.

In one 2015 interview to Weizmann Direct, a publication of the Weizmann Institute in Israel, Cohen-Tannoudji recalled organising meetings bringing Israeli and Arab, including Palestinian, scientists together for week-long discussions. He signed a letter in 2021 calling for the release of doctor and human rights activist Binayak Sen. A tributary statement from the Laboratoire Kastler Brossel after his passing also recalled his willingness to discuss ideas with anyone who approached him.

Según la UNESCO, que lo nombró Patrimonio de la Humanidad en 1996, tiene unos 25 millones de años, lo que lo convierte en el lago más antiguo del mundo, y alcanza aproximadamente los 1.700 metros de profundidad, lo que también lo hace el más profundo de toda la Tierra.

Pero más allá de esto, también destaca por su capacidad. Este enclave natural alberga alrededor de 23.000 kilómetros cúbicos de agua, y los estudios sugieren que concentra cerca del 20% de las reservas mundiales de agua dulce superficial no congelada. Conocido como las “Galápagos de Rusia”, este lago es uno de los lugares más especiales de nuestro planeta.

Situado en el sur de Siberia, en Rusia, y próximo a la frontera con Mongolia, el Baikal se extiende entre la provincia de Irkutsk y la República de Buriatia. Está rodeado de montañas y bosques, en una región de inviernos extremadamente fríos. De hecho, parte de su superficie suele quedar cubierta de hielo entre enero y mayo, aunque el calendario de congelación varía según la zona y las condiciones meteorológicas. En algunos lugares, el hielo incluso puede superar los dos metros de grosor.

Pese a encontrarse en una región remota, el Baikal no ha escapado a la contaminación. Uno de los episodios más preocupantes en la región comenzó en 1966, con la puesta en marcha de una fábrica de celulosa en sus inmediaciones. La actividad industrial generó vertidos que despertaron la preocupación de los científicos por la calidad del agua y sus efectos sobre un ecosistema único.

El lago alberga más de mil especies de plantas y alrededor de 1.500 variedades de animales, muchas de ellas endémicas, como el ómul del Baikal (Coregonus migratorius), uno de sus peces más emblemáticos. Este animal es pescado, ahumado y vendido en todos los mercados alrededor del lago, siendo uno de los productos más representativos de la gastronomía de la región.

Otro de los animales más característicos de este enclave natural es la foca del Baikal (Pusa sibirica), una especie que vive exclusivamente en sus aguas dulces. Se trata de uno de los pocos pinnípedos del mundo adaptados a este entorno y del único mamífero que habita de forma permanente en el lago siberiano.
