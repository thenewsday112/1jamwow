Exclusive: Secret pay-for-play text rocks Trump White House

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Top Trump adviser Ric Grenell allegedly outlined a stunning pay-for-influence offer to a Turkish billionaire last year: access to "POTUS," "VIP events" and administration officials, according to a text message, reviewed by Axios, that the White House counsel is investigating.

  • "Once the money comes to the company here, I will arrange the donation to Kennedy Centre [sic], and you'll be joining the VIP events and get close with the administration, even with POTUS," the May 3, 2025, text says.
  • Grenell has called the accusations "lies" and denied sending the text message.

Why it matters: Administration officials say the text is a key piece of evidence in the White House's investigation — ordered by President Trump — into whether Grenell abused the president's trust and misappropriated a potential multimillion-dollar donation to the Kennedy Center, which Grenell led at the time.

  • Grenell also served in another Trump-appointed capacity: as special envoy to Venezuela, where Turkish businessman Turhan Mildon also had contracts.
  • "We need to make you the 'American face' down in V," Grenell allegedly wrote in the same 2025 text message to Mildon, whose attorneys sent the text, at least one financial document and other correspondence to the White House counsel's office, according to sources familiar with the investigation.
  • Mildon is married to another Trump adviser: former U.S. attorney for New Jersey Alina Habba, who stormed into the Oval Office on Oct. 9 and accused Grenell of hoodwinking her husband into making a Kennedy Center donation that never arrived, Axios first reported.

Zoom in: At the heart of the alleged scheme is a financial transaction involving a private Houston-based defense company's software and the Democratic Republic of Congo, Habba and Mildon's attorneys have told the White House.

  • Mildon alleges that at Grenell's direction, Mildon wired $17.6 million to Texas Armament & Technology. He says the money was to be split in roughly two parts: one for the Congo deal and nearly $6 million for the Kennedy Center.
  • To back up his claims, Mildon provided the 2025 text that discussed the Kennedy Center donation and Congo "software." In the text to Mildon, the sender also says, "There is an e-mail that was sent to you, can you please take a look at it for me ?"
  • That email also was furnished to the White House, as was a May 29, 2025, financial record showing $17.6 million was wired to the company, known by its initials TXAT, sources said.

It would be 17 months before Mildon learned that the Kennedy Center never received the money that he thought was included in the wire he sent at Grenell's direction, Habba told the president.

The intrigue: Here's where the story gets mysterious and murky. It's unclear what the Congo contract was for, how it related to the Kennedy Center or what the financial specifics were.

  • The text doesn't mention any financial numbers, either for TXAT or the Kennedy Center. But a source familiar with the situation said that other correspondence furnished to the White House does have financial specifics.
  • The financial transaction sent to TXAT didn't appear to come from Mildon's bank account, as Habba initially indicated to Trump.

Instead, the $17.6 million was wired to TXAT from Congo's "Ministère des Finances" in Kinshasa, the capital city, where Mildon has business and access to finances, a White House official said.

  • A spokesman for Mildon would not comment. Spokespeople for TXAT and Congo's government couldn't be reached.
  • "We're trying to find out whose money this is," the official said.

Complicating the matter further: Habba's numbers kept changing after she first alerted Trump to the alleged theft of the Kennedy Center money.

  • At first she said the Kennedy Center contribution was for $17 million.
  • Then she said it was about $6 million after talking to Mildon. The exact figure seems to be $5.6 million.

The other side: Grenell, who declined to comment for this article, previously told Axios that the claims against him are "absurd."

  • "I've never asked for one cent to be sent somewhere else pretending it was for the Kennedy Center, and I've never received any payment from him at all," he said. "Make him give proof to what he is saying, not make these absurd allegations."
  • When told of the text message's exact wording, Grenell hotly denied he sent it because "center" was spelled in British English as "centre," a source briefed on his thinking said.
  • Grenell's allies also have questioned everything from Mildon's background as a businessman to his associates, his age (he's 29 but some records indicate he's 25) and his $10 million wedding last month to Habba, 42, whom he began dating last year.

Friction point: As the New York Times examined Mildon's activities in Venezuela, he had a falling out with Grenell, with whom he had been friendly since 2024.

  • Mildon met Habba in 2025 at the Kennedy Center.
  • The Times previously reported aspects of the text message concerning Venezuela and the money from Congo.

Inside the room: On Oct. 9, when Habba arrived at the White House, she was "pretty animated," said one administration official, and exclaimed at one point, "Ric stole the money!"

  • The problem, however, was that Habba "didn't have all her stuff in order. She went in there half-cocked without all the facts yelling about Grenell stealing. And the facts keep changing," the source said. "That doesn't mean Ric is out of the woods. This looks really shady."
  • After Habba leveled her accusations, Trump called White House Counsel Will Scharf into Oval Office and asked him to look into her claims, sources said.

Trump and Scharf then phoned Grenell, who denied everything and said he would have instantly alerted everyone to the idea of a $17 million contribution.

  • "There's no way this is true. This is crazy," Grenell told Trump at one point, according to a source briefed on the discussion.

Behind the scenes: Scharf grew skeptical of Habba's claims early last week because she and Mildon's attorneys were initially slow to furnish any records to help his inquiry, said a source. They submitted the documents on Friday.

  • Scharf is still gathering evidence and plans to verify the digital footprint of the 2025 text, the source said, noting that a printout of the message has the same image of Grenell that appears on his smartphone contact card display.
  • Kennedy Center insiders also have contacted the White House and said they frequently saw Mildon at exclusive events at the facility, indicating he was a big donor. That includes appearances in the vice president's box, next to the president's, a source said.
  • "Here's where Alina might be telling the truth: Mildon did get access. So why did he get access? Was he doing business in Congo with Ric? Why so much access if the contribution was never paid?" one of the administration officials asked.
  • "There's a lot of questions. And POTUS wants answers."

Supreme Court won't revive accused 9/11 mastermind's plea deal

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court denied on Monday a bid by Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, the accused mastermind behind the 9/11 terror attacks, to reinstate plea agreements that would have spared him and two co-defendants the death penalty.

Why it matters: The denial keeps the death penalty on the table for the accused 9/11 plotters as they move closer to their June 2028 military trial date, when the government hopes to finally secure convictions more than 25 years after one of America's deadliest terrorist attacks.

Driving the news: The Supreme Court released an order list Monday identifying the cases it intends to hear and denying those it won't, like the petition in Khalid Shaikh Mohammad, et al. v. United States.

  • The order doesn't explain why the appeal was rejected.
  • Justice Brett Kavanaugh recused himself from the decision. He served as associate counsel to President George W. Bush, where he reportedly assisted with legal and policy discussions following 9/11.

Catch up quick: The government and the three men entered into plea agreements in 2024 that would have spared them the death penalty.

  • The decision was met with outrage and pushback from some 9/11 victims' families.
  • Then-Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin withdrew the agreements days later, declaring that "responsibility for such a decision should rest with me."
  • Mohammed and his co-defendants, Walid bin 'Attash and Mustafa al-Hawsawi, appealed that decision. While a military court initially sided with the defendants, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit sided with Austin, prompting their final appeal to the Supreme Court .

Zoom out: Mohammed and his co-defendants were first arraigned in 2008 for their alleged roles in the attacks involving four hijacked planes that struck the Twin Towers in New York City and the Pentagon, with a fourth crashing in Pennsylvania.

  • Mohammed and his fellow defendants have been held at the U.S. military prison at Guantánamo Bay, Cuba, since 2006.
  • Prosecutors had sought a January 2027 trial date, but a judge determined that timeline would leave too little time to resolve outstanding pretrial issues.

What we're watching: The new tentative trial date is for June 5, 2028. Go deeper: Khalid Sheikh Mohammed to stand trial in 2028 over 9/11 attacks

Nur 16 Milliarden: Ist das wirklich eine Katastrophe für die Unis?

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Die Regierung hat sich auf ein Uni-Budget für 2028-2030 geeinigt. Elisabeth Hofer über ein reales Minus, Personalabbau, Ineffizienzen und warum die Debatte über Studiengebühren zu schwarz-weiß geführt wird.

Jeder Abschluss unter 17 Milliarden Euro wäre eine „absolute Katastrophe“, hatte Sebastian Schütze, Rektor der Universität Wien, vergangene Woche gewarnt. Am Samstag hat sich die Regierung auf das Uni-Budget für 2028 bis 2030 geeinigt: 16 Milliarden Euro. Das ist mehr als die rund 15,6 Milliarden, die im Finanzrahmen ursprünglich vorgesehen waren und exakt so viel, wie die Unis in der laufenden Periode bekommen.

„Für die Unis ist das eine schlechte Nachricht, weil es nicht einmal die Inflation abdeckt“, sagt Elisabeth Hofer, Innenpolitikredakteurin der „Presse“, im Podcast „Was wichtig ist“. „Sie können mit diesen 16 Milliarden weniger anfangen als bisher, weil das Geld weniger wert ist.“ Die Politik argumentiere mit dem allgemeinen Spardruck: Alle müssten einen Beitrag leisten, zumal die Uni-Budgets in den vergangenen Jahren stark gestiegen seien.

Was die Unis mit dem Geld alles finanzieren müssen, etwa die Ärztegehälter an den Uni-Kliniken, ist noch offen. Das wird nun in den Leistungsvereinbarungen zwischen Wissenschaftsministerium und den einzelnen Universitäten ausverhandelt. Dass gespart werden muss, steht für Hofer aber fest: „Es wird nicht ohne Personalkürzungen gehen, und das wird vor allem junge Nachwuchswissenschaftlerinnen und -wissenschaftler treffen.“ Bei den Studierenden werde man die Einschnitte erst später merken.

Wie stark welche Fächer betroffen sind, hänge von den Prioritäten der Rektorate ab, zum Beispiel davon, ob man kleine Fächer mit wenigen Studierenden wegen ihrer internationalen Reputation weiterführt. Dass Wissenschaftsministerin Eva-Maria Holzleitner (SPÖ) die Medizin in den Vordergrund stellt, erklärt Hofer mit zwei Gründen: Das Medizinstudium ist das teuerste, und die Botschaft, die Gesundheitsversorgung sei gesichert, ist politisch wichtig. Holzleitner habe sich ernsthaft um mehr Geld bemüht, Finanzminister Markus Marterbauer (SPÖ) aber keine Extras zugestanden. Hofers Fazit: „Na, besser wird‘s nicht.“

Gleichzeitig hält Hofer die Debatte über die Effizienz des Hochschulsystems für berechtigt. Der Rechnungshof habe auf Doppelgleisigkeiten hingewiesen, die zum Teil politisch bedingt seien: „Es ergibt nicht viel Sinn, dass man in einem relativ kleinen Land wie Österreich teilweise an vier Standorten das Gleiche studieren kann.“ Auch lange Studiendauern und eine vergleichsweise hohe Abbruchquote kosteten viel Geld. Man könne etwa bei den Mindeststudienleistungen nachschärfen.

Das ändere nichts daran, dass jede Investition in Universitäten und Forschung eine gute sei, betont Hofer: Akademikerinnen und Akademiker verdienen im Schnitt mehr, zahlen mehr Steuern und sind seltener arbeitslos.

Bei den Studiengebühren wehrt sich Hofer gegen eine Debatte, die vor allem darauf abzielt, ausländische Studierende fernzuhalten. Österreich profitiere davon, internationale „Brainpower“ ins Land zu holen. Über Gebühren zu diskutieren, sei aber fair. Ein valides Gegenargument sei, dass Bildung in Österreich stark vom Elternhaus abhängt, Gebühren könnten eine weitere Hürde schaffen. „Die Diskussion über Studiengebühren ist immer schwarz oder weiß“, sagt Hofer. „Vielleicht muss man einmal über clevere, innovative Ideen nachdenken.“ Etwa über Modelle, bei denen der Staat das Studium vorfinanziert und motivierte Studierende über Stipendien entlastet.

Für die Proteste der Studierenden, die Hochschülerschaft ruft für Donnerstag zur nächsten Großdemonstration auf, hat Hofer Verständnis. Schon Rechnungshofpräsidentin Margit Kraker habe gesagt: Wer bei den Unis kürzen kann, kann auch bei der Parteienförderung kürzen. „Es ist eine politische Entscheidung, wo man kürzt und wo nicht – und man kürzt eben bei den Unis.“ Viel herausholen werde man angesichts der knappen Spielräume allerdings nicht.

Feds Suspect Ransom Negotiator Ran ShinyHunters Extortion Site

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

U.S. federal investigators pursuing the ShinyHunters hacking group that recently stole highly sensitive data from the FBI have identified executives at two different ransomware negotiation firms who maintained unusually close relations with the extortion group, KrebsOnSecurity has learned. Sources close to the investigation say one of the executives in the United States even helped ShinyHunters operate a website that pressured victims into paying a ransom.

Hackers aligned with ShinyHunters have claimed credit for dozens of major data breaches since 2019, when the group was primarily operated by cybercriminals living in France. As those original members were arrested or jailed one by one, control over the ShinyHunters brand eventually passed to “Rey,” identified last year as a teenage hacker from Amman, Jordan. According to the FBI, ShinyHunters has extorted more than $70 million from victims so far this year.

In late September, ShinyHunters hacked the FBI recruitment website fbijobs.gov and taunted the agency by threatening to publish the stolen files, which reportedly included medical and psychological records, Social Security numbers and home addresses, and information about each employee’s unit and specialization.

The FBI jobs site hack has prompted the agency to move with uncommon speed to arrest, detain and question people who had any communications with Rey or other ShinyHunters suspects. Reuters reported on October 3 that Rey had been detained by Jordanian authorities and was cooperating with the FBI.

Rey was able to assume control over the hacking group because one of its alleged senior members had just been nabbed. On the evening of September 15, 2026, authorities in the Netherlands arrested convicted hacker and ShinyHunters suspect Pepijn van der Stap. Dutch police reportedly used flash-bang grenades in an effort to stun and apprehend Van der Stap as he was logged in to various online accounts.

According to a source close to the investigation, the authorities arrested Van der Stap while he was using his Signal instant messaging account, which allowed them to read recent communications with frequent contacts — including ransomware negotiators based in Canada and the United States.

Multiple sources with knowledge of the matter told KrebsOnSecurity that Van der Stap’s messages were the key to unraveling a murder-for-hire plot that emerged immediately following his arrest. The Dutch news outlet RTL reported last month the two murders were allegedly to be committed abroad, and that there are indications Van der Stap gave the order for the attacks (there is no indication those orders were ever carried out).

On October 9, KrebsOnSecurity broke the news that the FBI arrested a 54-year-old Canadian cybersecurity executive named Edward Dubrovsky in connection with the ShinyHunters investigation. Dubrovsky, who recently published a book on handling cyber extortion that recommended always engaging with the hackers, was charged with threatening to disclose stolen or hacked data and interfering with interstate commerce through extortion, according to court records. Politico reports prosecutors have asked that Dubrovsky remain in custody pending trial, and that records related to his case remain under seal.

A source close to the investigation told KrebsOnSecurity that Van der Stap’s Signal messages showed he had frequent conversations with Mr. Dubrovsky and with at least one executive at a large U.S.-based consulting firm, and that these executives often knew the details about new ShinyHunters victims before the victims themselves had even learned of the breach.

That source shared another potentially explosive finding by federal investigators: the Dutch hacker’s chats showed Mr. Dubrovsky knew ShinyHunters had stolen highly sensitive data from FBI servers, and that he never informed the bureau of the data breach prior to ShinyHunters threatening on Twitter/X to publish the agency’s deeply personal personnel files.

KrebsOnSecurity has learned from two sources close to the ShinyHunters probe that FBI investigators believe at least one executive at a U.S.-based consulting firm played an active role in operating the now-defunct ShinyHunters victim extortion website “shinywire[.]com.” KrebsOnSecurity is not naming the U.S. company or the suspected executive(s) for now because they have not yet been charged with a crime, but it is a prestigious firm with more than 1,000 associates.

A section of the homepage for the now-defunct Shinywire website.

The Shinywire website was active from approximately May 3, 2026 until October 16 and had entries for nearly 100 organizations hacked by ShinyHunters. Each entry urged victims to engage with the cybercrime group and discuss terms for payment. The pages also included samples of stolen files and promised those files would be published online if the victim ignored their demands.

The following screenshot shows a cached portion of the Shinywire extortion page for Intuitive Surgical, an American medical technology company that was breached in March 2026 after an employee fell for a targeted phishing email.

The Shinywire website, as indexed by urlscan.io. This listing is an extortion demand targeting Intuitive Surgical, an American medical technology company breached in March 2026 by a targeted phishing email attack that compromised an employee’s credentials.

Victim organizations that refused to engage or negotiate after seven days would find all of their stolen data published on the ShinyHunters victim shaming blog (a website that is only reachable via the anonymity network Tor).

In a story published October 15, The Globe and Mail wrote that a few months before Edward Dubrovsky was arrested by the FBI, the Canadian cybersecurity executive alleged in court documents he had become collateral damage in a campaign by a former business partner to “destroy” another industry executive.

The story noted that Dubrovsky filed the lawsuit in Ontario this year against Daniel Tobok, the former chief executive of Cypfer, a cybersecurity firm founded in Toronto with offices in the U.S., Britain, the Netherlands, the United Arab Emirates, and Mexico. The two first became business partners in 2017 and later developed Cypfer together, where Mr. Dubrovsky was a managing partner and chief operating officer. But by 2024, the two had a falling out, with Dubrovsky accusing Tobok of gradually excluding him from business matters and making “increasingly capricious business decisions.”

Mr. Tobok reportedly denied the allegations and filed a counterclaim, accusing Mr. Dubrovsky of accessing confidential information and unfairly competing with Cypfer through work at another firm called CyberSteward Inc. He also said Mr. Dubrovsky has misrepresented himself as a Cypfer founder, and is not a shareholder.

According to interviews with several former colleagues of Mr. Tobok, as far back as eight years ago he was known to ransomware purveyors by a particular nickname: Ghostbyte. In 2018, a Canadian company was hacked by Dharma, a prolific Russian-language ransomware affiliate program that first surfaced in 2016. The victim company told the Dharma ransomware representative they were leaning toward paying because some of the files were unrecoverable after being locked, but that they needed a better guarantee that Dharma would keep their word if paid.

A redacted copy of the Dharma rep’s reply is pictured below, and it shows the ransomware group urged the victim to contact a person at a Canadian IT firm that helps companies seeking such guarantees. The Dharma rep recommended the victim contact the email address ghostbyte@tutanota.com.

“If you want guarantees, you can write to the IT company,” the Dharma reply advised. “Take the phone and negotiate with them. This IT company often solves such problems in Canada. You can contact them and sign a contract for services. You’ll still have to pay.”

An email reply from the Dharma ransomware group to a victim, recommending they contact an email address that is connected to Mr. Tobok.

It’s unclear if Mr. Tobok ever used that Ghostbyte address offered by the Dharma representative, and he has not responded to multiple requests for comment. However, the breach tracking service Constella Intelligence finds an email address Mr. Tobok has frequently used — dtobok@me.com — is associated with two online accounts where the chosen username was “ghostbyte,” including a Snapchat account.

Incidentally, Constella also finds the Dharma rep’s email address luckygoodlucky@tuta.io is associated with a cryptocurrency address that processed 38 transactions totaling 43 bitcoin, ~$3.5 million at today’s value (the most recent transaction for this wallet is dated February 2019).

In a statement shared on October 14, Cypfer said the company’s business does not include making or facilitating ransomware payments, and that Cypfer learned of Dubrovsky’s arrest when the news first broke in the media.

“We had no prior knowledge of Mr. Dubrovsky’s arrest,” a spokesperson told KrebsOnSecurity via email. “CYPFER has not received any outreach from law enforcement and, to our knowledge, is not the target of any law enforcement investigation.”

The statement said CyberSteward and Cypfer are separate companies with no officers, directors or employees in common, and that Cypfer was recently acquired by the Apros Group, a holding company led by Jason Hogg, a former FBI special agent and former CEO of Aon Cyber Solutions Group. In 2020, Aon aquired Cytelligence, a security consulting firm where Mr. Tobok was founder and CEO and Mr. Dubrovsky was a managing partner.

It is not illegal in the United States to pay hackers for an encryption key to unlock servers infected with ransomware, or in exchange for a promise to delete any stolen files and refrain from publishing them online. While victim organizations can land in legal hot water if they pay a ransom to a group that is sanctioned by the U.S. Treasury Department, ShinyHunters is not among the ransomware groups currently sanctioned by the United States or by any other government.

Experts say ransomware victims are typically directed by their insurance provider to work with a specific incident response firm. But not all cyber forensics and breach response companies will agree to facilitate payments to ransomware groups on behalf of their clients. As a result, once victim firms make a decision to pay, the firms that eschew rewarding extortionists are invariably replaced by companies that have no such qualms.

In December 2024, ShinyHunters began extorting the education technology company PowerSchool, stealing private information on 62 million students and 9.5 million teachers in the U.S. and Canada. PowerSchool reportedly paid a $2.85 million ransom demand, and a “frequently asked questions” statement (PDF) about the breach says PowerSchool was assisted in that effort by CyberSteward (ShinyHunters affiliate Matthew Lane, 20, of Sterling, Mass., was sentenced in November 2025 to four years in prison for his admitted role in the PowerSchool hack).

But that ransom payment did not stop future extortion attempts using the same stolen data. By May 2025, individual school districts were reportedly receiving separate ransom demands using samples of the same stolen files. While nobody has alleged ShinyHunters was behind the second wave of extortion against PowerSchool users, the incident drives home the reality that there is no technical way to verify the threat actors have deleted the stolen data as promised, or prevent others from abusing the same records.

A number of leading cyber incident response firms, including Coveware and Unit 221B, have consistently warned victims against paying ShinyHunters, noting that “destroyed” victim data is often kept, resold or used to extort the company’s clients directly later on.

The FBI echoed those sentiments in an alert on May 15, 2026 advising victims against paying the ShinyHunters group. Meanwhile, ShinyHunters members have been harassing and threatening employees at Coveware and Unit 221B for offering this advice.

The following screenshots show just how annoyed the ShinyHunters leaders were with Coveware’s relentless advice against paying the group. The first image is from a conversation on May 6, 2026 that took place between the group’s main account on Telegram and a co-conspirator. In that chat, snippets of which were shared with KrebsOnSecurity by a trusted source, ShinyHunters complained that Coveware was harming the group’s payment rates.

The operator of the ShinyHunters account on Telegram speaking privately with a co-conspirator and complaining that Coveware was harming their business.

“Go on my website, majority of those companies would’ve paid if it weren’t for Coveware giving them misinformation,” the ShinyHunters account wrote. “Someone (100% Coveware) is going out of their way to make sure i am not paid.”

In another conversation on April 11, 2026, the ShinyHunters Telegram account privately said they were trying to “eliminate the problem,” which they said was Coveware. Coveware declined to comment for this story.

That latter dynamic, he said, is an inherent conflict of interest, as the negotiator has every incentive to make things more favorable to the attackers because they are financially incentivized to negotiate and pay out, and to not negotiate down on the price.

Even Republicans Are Waking Up to Trump’s Corruption

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

And, somewhat shockingly, so are Democratic elites.

Donald Trump walks on the south lawn of the White House on October 18, 2026.

Donald Trump’s near-total ability to get away with unprecedented corruption has always been one of the most frustrating aspects of his time atop US politics. Trump has been far and away the most crooked president in American history, a genuine gangster who makes earlier criminal presidents such as Warren Harding and Richard Nixon look like small-time hoodlums. His corruption extended from his brazen disdain for the Emoluments Clause in his first term (which allowed him to pocket millions of dollars from political supplicants trying to influence him by paying to stay at his hotels) to his family’s grand-scale self-enrichment in his second term (mostly through business deals in crypto and real estate, which have ballooned the Trump clan’s net worth by billions).

Yet Trump has paid virtually no price for this record-breaking larceny. Neither of his impeachments stemmed from his love of graft; the first involved political misconduct and the second from his incitement of the January 6 insurrection. And congressional Democrats, with a few notable exceptions such as Senator Elizabeth Warren, have been reluctant to challenge Trump on his personal corruption.

The political logic behind this reluctance to make corruption a political issue went something like this: Trump has a cult-like hold over the Republican electorate, so there is no convincing his voters that he is enriching himself. Further, voters care more about their own economic well-being than about what the Trump family is up to, while Republican elites are attuned to foreign-policy issues and threats to the constitutional order. Based on this reasoning, Democrats pursued a dual strategy of talking about economic issues to the electorate while trying to win over congressional Republicans on impeachments focused on Russiagate or Trump’s attempted coup.

My Nation colleague Chris Lehmann has repeatedly hammered the Democratic Party’s political elite for its aversion to making corruption a political issue. On my podcast, The Time of Monsters, last June, Lehmann rightly lamented the mental blinkers of thinking that kitchen-table economics and Trump’s corruption are separate issues. During the Watergate scandal, popular anger at Nixon’s slush fund intensified because of the concurrent rise in inflation. Under Trump, one could easily harness rage at the affordability crisis by contrasting the struggles of everyday voters with the orgy of self-enrichment in the White House, indulged in by both the president and his plutocratic cronies. David Sirota of The Lever has often made similar arguments about the necessity of foregrounding an anti-corruption message.

The good news is that Trump’s invulnerability on corruption seems to be ending. Two crucial groups are starting to wake up to the danger of a mobster president: a rising minority of Republican voters and many Democratic Party political consultants.

Nearly 2 in 3 Americans say President Donald Trump is using the presidency to enrich himself—and most of the increase over the past year has come from people who lean Republican, according to a Washington Post-ABC News-Ipsos poll.

Among Republicans and independents who lean Republican, 34 percent believe Trump is enriching himself, up from 22 percent in September 2025. Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents were already nearly unanimous, at 88 percent a year ago and 92 percent now, while independents who don’t lean toward either party were unchanged.

Another striking fact is that in this poll, the percentage of independents who believe Trump is corrupt remained steady at a very high level: 72 percent.

Further, a Fox News poll released last Wednesday made clear that corruption was a top concern for a supermajority of voters. Asked what issues they were concerned with, corruption came out on top, with 88 percent concerned, including 60 percent saying they were extremely concerned. Intriguingly, the other issues that voters are worried about are economic. As Fox reports, “The top five voter concerns are government corruption (60% extremely concerned), inflation (58%), gas prices (50%), government leadership (49%), and healthcare (48%). And double digits more trust the Democrats than the Republicans to handle each of those.”

These polls (and other earlier ones) vindicate the argument long made by writers such as Lehmann and Sirota: Democrats need to hammer the Trump administration on corruption and link that with broader frustration about the economy.

On October 10, Politico reported that Democrats are “leaning hard into anti-corruption messaging in the final weeks of the campaign as new research suggests that linking corruption to Americans’ economic struggles can boost Democrats into the home stretch.” Josh Turek, the Democratic Senate candidate in Iowa, summed up the new argument succinctly: “We have a corruption crisis in this country, and it is leading to an affordability crisis.”

Similar arguments are being sounded by other Senate candidates, notably Jon Ossoff in Georgia, James Talarico in Texas, and Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan. According to Talarico, “We have an affordability crisis because we have a corruption crisis.” Speaking at a rally on Saturday, Ossoff thundered, “The Mar-a-Lago mafia really has taken American corruption to spectacular new heights. This corruption is why nothing works for ordinary people.”

The new focus on corruption as an economic issue is welcome, but it is also just a start. While Trump’s grandiose personal corruption offers a massive target, it is merely a symptom of the larger problem of the plutocracy that has swallowed up so much of American politics. Beyond the midterms, anti-corruption politics can win only if Democrats deliver on the promise of reining in the oligarchs and giving ordinary people a bigger share of the economic pie. A failure to deliver on economic populism will only prepare the groundwork for another Trump-style demagogue.

How This Snack Brand Gained 929k Followers by Mocking Influencers

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In Person of Interest we talk to the people catching our eye right now about their projects past and present. Next up, we chat with Jay, who cofounded popcorn brand Daadi Snacks with his family but has become internet-famous for his anti-influencer videos.

There’s a standard playbook that most consumer product good (CPG) companies use when launching their company into the marketplace. They might make a few cute reels or TikToks showing the behind-the-scenes process, maybe host an in-person pop-up or two, but most importantly, they send thousands of dollars of product to influencers to help get the word out.

Jay (who prefers to keep his last name off the internet), the 30-something cofounder of Daadi Snacks, decided to burn that playbook to the ground when launching his family’s popcorn company.

Instead of sucking up to influencers, he started calling them out for their over-the-top and ridiculous antics. Parodying their annoying parlance, by opening each video with an “OHMYGODYOUGUYS” or a nasally “Come with me…” before skewering his latest victim, such as the American tourist upset that baristas in Italy refuse to mimic her Dunkin’ coffee order.

With this strategy, Daadi Snacks has amassed 929,000 followers and counting, which has turned Jay into the very thing he spends his time making fun of: an influencer. Or is he a content creator? Surprising for someone who never even had an Instagram account until 2022.

But unlike the folks he skewers, Jay likes to use his powers for good, aiming to be “the Keith Lee of New York City” and shining a light on spots that don’t tend to get attention. We called him up to speak about how his family started Daadi Snacks, the impact of his anti-influencer reels, and if it really has helped him sell any popcorn at the end of the day.

How did your family get into the snack business?

My grandma made this popcorn for our family growing up for as long as I can remember. We had floated the idea of commercializing it and turning it into an actual product, but it was more just like an idea than anything else. When COVID hit, I was working remotely, and we were just looking for ways to stay in touch with my grandparents, my mom, my uncles. I brought it up to them. “Why don't we use this time inside to actually start working on this?” My family worked on the recipe. I thought about marketing, branding, and promotion. When things started to normalize in 2021 and 2022, we spent two years iterating on what we wanted our first two flavors to be.

There's no denying that social media is an important part of growing brands, especially CPG brands. Did you think you'd need to work with influencers to get the brand out there?

I didn't get on Instagram until 2022! I never had one before then. There was one specific well-known food influencer—I've made a video about them—and I think I had a pretty terrible interaction with them. So I just thought, why don't I just start posting and stop relying on other people to shout us out? That's how I kind of landed on that.

Having lived in New York City for so long and observing the way it's changed over the last ten years, I had a longer-term vision. If I can build my own platform to market our snacks and our business, why can't I use that to try to be like the Keith Lee of New York City? Not to the extent of what he's done, but just promoting places that typically don't get promoted. Places that have been around for 30 or 40 years, whose storefront isn't super aesthetic, with Gen Z fonts. I thought that would be really cool.

You have never reached out to or responded to influencers for collabs?

Correct. I have not reached out or responded. And honestly, it's been the other way—influencers have reached out to us for samples or collabs.

When did you post your first reel skewering influencers? Was there one particular video that really launched everything?

I want to say that was around springtime last March, if my memory serves me. Before that, sales were slow but steadily growing. We were doing pop-up events to get the word out. Then in June we had hundreds and hundreds of orders come in within like two days (I don't even know the exact number), which was just crazy.

I think the inflection point was when I made a video naming who it was that influenced me to start posting videos about not liking influencer culture. When I did that and people heard the story, that's when we went from around 70,000 to over 130,000 or 140,000 followers in a week or two.

Why do you think people are so engaged and responsive to what you're doing?

In New York City it just feels like there's this need for some degree of counterculture. It's been so overwhelming in terms of the growth of these NYC-based influencers with millions of followers, just drowning in brand deals. I think people have felt the need to push back on it for a while.

You nail that restaurant influencer voice so well. Why do you think these accounts are as successful as they are, even if you punch holes in what they're doing?

I think for a lot of the NYC influencers, their content doesn't connect super well with people who actually live here—but it can connect really well with people who aspire to live here. There was this article about how this has been a very mechanical way of repurposing the whole Carrie Bradshaw thing, selling people on a manicured, aspirational idea of being a West Village girl. New York doesn't need influencers to find great food.

You've accidentally become a content creator/influencer. Do you think there's a difference between the two, or are they one and the same?

To me, an influencer’s content basically just revolves around a perpetual cycle of brand deals, which they then market to their viewers, which then propels them to get more brand deals, and so on. Honestly, I feel like because we've been on this ridiculous post-COVID run. Beauty brands have so much money, and a lot of these CPG brands have scaled so fast—it's just been unchecked. People haven't had to put in the effort.

I make it a point to never go anywhere that DMs me and asks me to review their stuff. It's crazy some of the stuff you get offered once you build any kind of following. But I've never taken PR, never taken free stuff. I'm very clear about where I stand on all that.

When do you decide to make a response video? Is it just a gut thing, like something's annoyed you enough?

I'm very opinionated, and I legitimately feel like I'm right. I pick up my phone, stop everything I'm doing, and record my 30-second video in my office chair in the same crappy apartment lighting.

El Niño Brings Exotic Sea Life to the California Coast

A olive ridley sea turtle near Seal Beach, California. NOAA

A powerful El Niño is delivering unusually warm waters to the California coast, and with them, an array of tropical sea creatures rarely seen there.

These include ​flying fish, manta rays, and a venomous yellow-bellied sea snake, a species not seen in the state since the 2015 El Niño, Reuters reports. Meanwhile, anglers have landed yellowfin tuna, wahoo, and dorado, unusual catches in the typically cool waters off of California.

The summer saw a spate of great white shark sightings, and more recently, fishers have spotted hammerhead sharks and whale sharks. Whale sharks “do come through when the water is warm,” fisherman Carl Sbarounis told the Los Angeles Times, “mostly during El Niño.”

For the first time ever, scientists have recorded nesting sea turtles in California, near Seal Beach and in Huntington Beach. If their eggs hatch, the baby turtles “will likely one day return to nest to carry on future generations on these very same beaches,” said Jeff Flocken, president of the Aquarium of the Pacific in Long Beach. The aquarium began housing roughly 200 sea turtle eggs in September to protect them from a rare tropical storm.

During El Niño, warm waters pool in the eastern Pacific, fueling more heat waves and extreme weather around the world. The effect is especially pronounced in California, which is seeing exceptionally warm waters and unusually high sea levels. Scientists expect this year’s El Niño to be the strongest in living memory.

But while warming is driving exotic creatures to California shores, it is also playing havoc with native sea life. The added heat is spurring one of the largest algal blooms in history, which now stretches from Southern California to the coast of British Columbia. Dead and dying pelicans, loons, and grebes are turning up on Southern California beaches. And kelp forests, which are still recovering from recent marine heat waves, could be decimated by El Niño, experts warn.

How to Cook Brown Rice in a Donabe 玄米の炊き方 (土鍋)

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Learn how to cook tender, pleasantly chewy Japanese short-grain brown rice in a donabe (Japanese clay pot). I share the rice-to-water ratios, soaking times, and cooking steps I use for both the HARIO Gohangama and regular donabe pots.

This post may contain affiliate links. Please read my disclosure policy.

Donabe Brown Rice is Japanese short-grain brown rice cooked in a traditional Japanese clay pot. The donabe (土鍋) retains heat well, helping the grains cook gently and evenly. The rice comes out tender and slightly chewy, with a mild, nutty flavor.

Looking for a version with white rice? See my How to Cook White Rice in a Donabe guide.

I cook Japanese short-grain white rice in my HARIO Gohangama every day, but I occasionally switch to brown rice for its nutty flavor and pleasantly chewy texture. I love that I can use the same donabe for both!

Brown rice needs more time to soak and cook than white rice, but once you know how much water to use and when to lower the heat, it’s easy to make in a donabe.

If you’d like to cook brown rice using a different method, try my guides for making it in a rice cooker or an Instant Pot!

You’ll need just two ingredients: Japanese short-grain brown rice and water. You can also add a little salt to reduce bitterness, if desired.

In Japan, we measure uncooked rice using a special rice cooker cup. One level cup, called ichi-go (一合), holds 180 ml (¾ US cup).

Brown rice has its bran layer intact, so it needs more water and a longer soaking time than white rice. The right rice-to-water ratio helps the grains cook evenly without becoming too firm or mushy.

The manufacturer of HARIO Gohangama recommends a 1:1.5 rice-to-water ratio (1 part rice to 1.5 parts water). I’ve found this ratio works well in my own cooking, too.

Example: 180 ml rice x 1.5 = 270 ml water

For a regular donabe clay pot, I recommend starting with a 1:1.6 rice-to-water ratio.

Example: 180 ml rice x 1.6 = 288 ml water

Note: The rice-to-water ratio depends on your donabe’s material, thickness, lid design, and cooking method. Some pots absorb more moisture or release more steam than others. Use the recommended ratio for your donabe as a starting point, then adjust the water slightly based on your preferred rice texture.

Not sure how much rice to cook? Use this chart to find the right number of servings for uncooked rice. Note: Serving sizes and cooked rice volumes are approximate.

My step-by-step photos show the HARIO Gohangama, but instructions for a regular donabe are also included.

Here are my tried-and-true tips for cooking tender brown rice in a donabe every time.

To Store: While the rice is still hot, divide it into individual microwave-safe glass containers to help keep it moist. Cool promptly, then freeze for up to 1 month. For the best texture, I prefer freezing rice rather than refrigerating it, as refrigeration can make the grains dry and firm. If refrigerating, use a microwave-safe ohitsu rice storage container.

To Reheat: Remove the lid and cover the frozen rice with a damp paper towel. Microwave until steaming hot throughout. Reheating time varies depending on the portion size and microwave wattage. Alternatively, reheat the uncovered container in a steamer basket over simmering water for about 10 minutes, or until heated through. Use a heat-safe container suitable for steaming.

Food Safety Tip: Cool cooked rice quickly and refrigerate or freeze promptly. Avoid leaving it at room temperature for extended periods, as harmful bacteria can grow in improperly stored rice.

The HARIO Gohangama is my favorite donabe because its built-in whistle and clear glass lid make cooking rice easier. The whistle signals when to turn off the heat for white rice or lower it for brown rice, while the glass lid lets me monitor the cooking without opening the pot. Together, they take much of the guesswork out of cooking rice.
