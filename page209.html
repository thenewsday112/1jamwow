Trump's Russia waiver draws instant bipartisan backlash

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Top Republicans and Democrats alike lined up Saturday to condemn President Trump's decision to waive recent sanctions on Russia, calling it "irresponsible" and "a deal with the devil." Why it matters: Trump already faces a bipartisan effort to overturn his Russian diesel deal when Congress returns after the election.

  • Now he may have to deal with even broader outrage over sanctions Congress approved just a month ago.

Driving the news: Late Friday night, Trump issued an executive order waiving implementation of the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, which he signed in September.

  • The act would have imposed broad sanctions not only on Russia, but on other countries buying its oil.

What they're saying: Democrats forcefully criticized Trump's decision.

  • "By giving the Russian economy life support, Trump's deal with the devil keeps getting dirtier," Senate minority leader Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) wrote.
  • "Trump's latest surrender to Putin again betrays our Ukrainian partners and defies clear congressional intent of legislation designed to punish Russia for its illegal war of aggression," Rep. Don Beyer (D-Va.) posted on X.
  • "These waivers have nothing to do with our national security, & everything to do with sucking up to Vladimir Putin. I hope my Republican colleagues will join me in condemning this craven, cowardly action," Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) said on X.

Republicans were equally unsparing.

  • "We should be increasing economic pressure on Russia, not easing up. We need to use every tool at our disposal, including fully implementing the Graham sanctions and my REPO Act, to squeeze Putin until he ends his illegal and barbaric war in Ukraine," Rep. French Hill (R-Ark.) wrote. "I have requested a classified briefing on the rationale for this decision."
  • "Beyond irresponsible. What part of enforcing a law passed 86-11 by the Senate does the President and his advisors not understand?" Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) posted on X. "It's unconscionable that this administration is capitulating to a Russian dictator who illegally invaded Ukraine and butchers innocent civilians every single day."
  • Sen. John Cornyn (R-Tex.) posted simply: "Lindsey would be appalled."

Graham's sister, Sen. Darline Graham (R-S.C.) told the AP that she disagrees with Trump's decision.

  • "The president and I share the same goal to end the war, get gas and diesel prices down, and the [Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act] bill is one of the tools he can use to do that," she said, adding that it was up to Trump if he uses it or not.
  • "Personally, I would choose to keep the pressure on Russia ... because I don't trust Putin."

One soon-to-depart congressman, Rep. Don Bacon (R-Neb.), hinted at the trouble Trump may face when Congress returns.

  • "Congress has a lame-duck session. The President has an agenda: supplemental, reconciliation, Ballroom, etc… I think the agenda is dead. Many of us won't fund these things knowing he is propping up a dictator's invasion and throwing an ally under the bus," Bacon wrote.

For the record: The White House defended Trump's decision.

  • "President Trump has been clear that he wants to see this brutal war come to an end. As talks continue, this Act provides the President with additional authorities to pursue a peaceful resolution," spokeswoman Taylor Rogers said in a statement.
  • "Enforcing the sanctions provisions now would impede ongoing U.S. efforts to negotiate a durable and comprehensive peace agreement between Ukraine and Russia. The President retains the ability to exercise these authorities at any time if he determines that doing so would advance national interests."

What to watch: The question now is just how hard Congress will push back when it returns in a few weeks.

In dieser Jane Austen-Welt schimmelt es [premium]

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Aus ihrer Neuverfilmung von »Sinn und Sinnlichkeit« hat die britische »Blue Jean«-Regisseurin Georgia Oakley den Glamour verbannt: Auf Norland Park schimmelt es, im Zentrum steht nicht die große Liebe, sondern die Beziehung zweier Schwestern.

Ihr Langfilmdebüt „Blue Jean“ wurde vor vier Jahren mit viel Aufmerksamkeit und zahlreichen Auszeichnungen bedacht. Fiel es Ihnen danach leicht zu entscheiden, in welche Richtung Sie der nächste Karriereschritt führen wird?

Georgia Oakley: Mir hat mal jemand gesagt, dass eine Karriere das ist, was passiert, während man Pläne für andere Filme macht. Da ist etwas dran. Schon vor den Dreharbeiten zu „Blue Jean“ hatte ich damit begonnen, auch an anderen Ideen zu arbeiten und die habe ich dann im Anschluss wieder aufgegriffen. Zusätzlich habe ich angefangen, neue Projekte zu entwickeln. Ich hatte eine relativ klare Vorstellung davon, welche Geschichte ich als Nächstes erzählen wollte. Doch dann landete das Angebot für „Sinn und Sinnlichkeit“ dank der Produktionsfirma in meinem Schoß, als eine Art Querschläger, der meine Pläne auf den Kopf gestellt hat. Ich hatte nicht unbedingt vorgehabt, mir einen Film dieser Größenordnung vorzunehmen. Aber das Drehbuch von Diana Reid gefiel mir einfach zu gut.

Für das Werk von Jane Austen hege ich schon lange eine große Faszination. Nicht nur für ihre Sprache und Themen, sondern auch dafür, wie ihre Arbeiten im Laufe der Zeit immer wieder neu betrachtet und umgesetzt wurden, ohne je ihren Reiz einzubüßen. Als jemand, der selbst englische Literatur studiert hat, war es durchaus ein Traum von mir, mir einmal eine ihrer Geschichten vorzunehmen. Und ich liebe die Geschichte der beiden Schwestern in „Sinn und Sinnlichkeit“. Deswegen konnte ich mir die Gelegenheit, für eine Neuverfilmung meinen Hut in den Ring zu werfen, einfach nicht entgehen lassen.

Wie erklären Sie sich die ungebrochene Begeisterung, mit der Austens Romane bis heute rezipiert werden?

Ich habe Austens Arbeit immer als unglaublich subversiv empfunden. Ihre Sprache und ihre Geschichten gelten ja als leichtfüßig, witzig und romantisch. Aber in den Themen, die sie verhandelt, ist sie immer auch enorm politisch und kritisiert die gesellschaftspolitischen Umstände ihrer Zeit. „Sinn und Sinnlichkeit“ ist ein hervorragendes Beispiel, es geht um den Platz der Frau in der Gesellschaft, um Erbschaftsgesetze und die Institution der Ehe.

Als ich das Drehbuch und dann auch den Roman noch einmal gelesen habe, habe ich eine Version dieser Geschichte erkannt, die ich so noch nicht gesehen hatte. Da gab es durchaus noch Dinge zu entdecken und Elemente, mit denen man die klassischen Erwartungen an einen Austen-Film unterwandern konnte. Bei vielen Adaptionen fühle ich mich – anders als in den Büchern selbst – irgendwie auf Armeslänge gehalten. So als würde mir die Menschlichkeit der Figuren fremd bleiben, weil sie von der Geschichte überdeckt werden. Oder auch von einer gewissen visuellen Umsetzung. Das wollte ich unbedingt anders machen.

Sie haben einmal davon gesprochen, dass sich eine gewisse Glamourisierung in britischen Historienfilmen breitgemacht hat, die Sie stört.

Die gibt es nicht nur in Filmen. Denken Sie an all die prächtigen, mit viel Geld und Sorgfalt gepflegten Landhäuser, die es in ganz England gibt und die jedes Jahr zahllose Besucherinnen und Besucher aus der ganzen Welt anlocken. Die sind eigentlich das mit Millionen von Pfund unterstützte Produkt einer Denkmal-Fetischisierung, die mit der Realität wenig zu tun hat. Weder der historischen noch der heutigen.

Auf unterschiedlichste Weise, aber ein gutes Beispiel ist der Landsitz zu Beginn der Geschichte, also Norland Park. Mir war es wichtig, ein Haus zu finden, das eben nicht einer großen Stiftung oder so gehört, sondern das wirklich noch von einer Familie bewohnt und betrieben wird. Dem man die damit einhergehenden finanziellen Nöte ansieht. In dem es hier und da schimmelt und die Teppiche seit Jahrzehnten nicht erneuert wurden. Um 1800 war es in solchen Anwesen kalt und dunkel, und mir war es wichtig, dass man das spürt. Für glattpolierten Glamour hatte ich in den Kulissen oder Kostümen meiner Version von „Sinn und Sinnlichkeit“ keinen Platz, denn ich habe nach etwas Wahrhaftigerem gesucht.

Sie haben bereits das Subversive an Austens Arbeit erwähnt. Sehen Sie als lesbische Filmemacherin auch eine Queerness darin?

Ich hatte kaum mit der Arbeit begonnen, als jemand zu mir sagte: „Du weißt schon, dass das Austens queerster Roman ist, oder?“ Das war durchaus nicht an mir vorbeigegangen, auch weil ich einmal einen tollen, literaturwissenschaftlichen Essay mit dem Titel „Jane Austen and the Masturbating Girl“ gelesen hatte. Da ging es unter anderem darum, dass Marianne Dashwood im Grunde so etwas wie die frühe Version einer queeren Figur ist. Alles, wofür sie steht und was sie in Sachen Emotionen und Leidenschaft ausdrückt, unterwandert das damals herrschende Frauenbild. Sie passt in keine der vorgesehenen Schubladen, ist viel weniger definierbar oder binär, als es Frauen damals eigentlich gestattet war. Da kann man schon – genau wie übrigens mit Blick auf Austens eigene Biografie, die nie geheiratet und stets mit ihrer Schwester zusammengelebt hat – von einer inhärenten Queerness im weiteren Sinne sprechen, finde ich. Den Status quo abzulehnen ist für mich jedenfalls queer, und all das schwingt in meiner Begeisterung mit. Selbst wenn es im Film eine untergeordnete Rolle spielt.

Ihr Fokus im Film liegt auf jeden Fall auch weniger auf den romantischen Liebesbeziehungen, ohne dass Sie natürlich darauf hätten verzichten können…

Stimmt, und ich glaube, das war das Erste, worauf ich bei der Drehbuchlektüre angesprungen bin. Im Zentrum stehen klar die beiden Schwestern, was meiner Lesart des Textes komplett entspricht. Die Beziehung von Elinor und Marianne ist es, die diesen Roman von anderen Austen-Geschichten abhebt. So wie Diana das in ihrem Skript umgesetzt hatte, folgt sie durchaus einem klassischen RomCom-Bogen. Nur dass die beiden Figuren, die sich ineinander verlieben, dann getrennt werden und schließlich wieder zusammenfinden, eben die Schwestern sind. Das fand ich aufregend. Die Männer sind durchaus mehr als schmückendes Beiwerk, aber das Herz der Geschichte und die komplexeste Paardynamik ist die von Elinor und Marianne. Weshalb den richtigen Schlusspunkt für den Film zu finden auch die größte Schwierigkeit war.

Trump waives new Russia sanctions in the "national interests"

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

President Trump waived new sanctions on Russia late Friday night, saying it was in the "national interests" to hold off on the sweeping levies under the Graham Act that he signed just last month.

Why it matters: Trump is easing American pressure on Russia despite a bipartisan chorus calling for him to do the exact opposite.

  • It comes a week after his surprise deal to let Russia sell diesel internationally, which Republicans have condemned as loudly as Democrats.
  • It also puts Republican candidates making their final midterm sprint in the position of having to defend the president's warmer relationship with a country voters overwhelmingly see as an American enemy.

The latest: Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky lamented Trump's decision in a post to X Saturday morning.

  • "This Act could have helped enormously – and still can! But it needs to actually work, and every voice in support of it matters," he wrote, amid fresh Russian attacks on Ukraine's energy infrastructure.

Driving the news: The White House released the text of an executive order late Friday night detailing the implementation of the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, which Trump signed in September.

  • The bill, named for the late Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.), imposed broad new sanctions on Russia and countries that do business with it, including buyers of its oil.
  • The order notes that the law allows the president to "waive the application of any sanctions provision with respect to a foreign person, any restriction with respect to a person, and any duty."
  • "I have submitted to the Congress a certification that the issuance of such a waiver at this time is in the national interests of the United States," Trump said in the order.

What they're saying: Republicans and Democrats alike blasted the waiver.

  • "By giving the Russian economy life support, Trump's deal with the devil keeps getting dirtier," Senate minority leader Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) wrote.
  • "Trump's latest surrender to Putin again betrays our Ukrainian partners and defies clear congressional intent of legislation designed to punish Russia for its illegal war of aggression," Rep. Don Beyer (D-Va.) posted on X.
  • "These waivers have nothing to do with our national security, & everything to do with sucking up to Vladimir Putin. I hope my Republican colleagues will join me in condemning this craven, cowardly action," Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) said on X.
  • "Beyond irresponsible. What part of enforcing a law passed 86-11 by the Senate does the President and his advisors not understand?" Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) posted on X. "It's unconscionable that this administration is capitulating to a Russian dictator who illegally invaded Ukraine and butchers innocent civilians every single day."
  • Sen. John Cornyn (R-Tex.) posted simply: "Lindsey would be appalled."

For the record: The White House defended Trump's decision.

  • "Enforcing the sanctions provisions now would impede ongoing U.S. efforts to negotiate a durable and comprehensive peace agreement between Ukraine and Russia," spokeswoman Taylor Rogers said in a statement.

The big picture: The waiver caps a historic week in recent U.S.-Russia relations, where Trump abruptly eased pressure on multiple fronts.

  • Whether it was the diesel deal, or a fight over Ukraine's ability to use Starlink to defend itself against Russian munitions, or a public denial of Russian involvement in the Iran war, Trump has spent the last few days shifting America's position on an old foe.
  • By waiving the Graham Act's so-called secondary sanctions, Trump will also avoid putting fresh pressure on China and India, major buyers of Russian oil.

The bottom line: The louder the bipartisan anti-Russia chorus gets, the more Trump doubles down on pulling back the pressure.

EU plant KI-Revolution: Viele offene Fragen

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Im KI-Wettlauf sind die USA und China weit vorne. Die Europäische Union will ihre Wirtschaft mithilfe von KI ankurbeln. Doch wichtige Aspekte wie Arbeitnehmerschutz sowie Sicherheit und Klimaschutz sind ungeklärt.

Stepptänzer wirbeln über eine kreisförmige Bühne in der Mitte einer dunklen Halle. Das Publikum aus politischen Entscheidungsträgern und Tech-Managern schaut gebannt zu. Der Internationale KI-Gipfel in Dublin in dieser Woche wollte mehr sein als nur eine weitere Bürokratenkonferenz - er sollte ein spektakuläres Signal senden: Europa ist bereit für seine KI-Revolution.

Unter den mehr als 1500 erwarteten Gästen waren unter anderem der Mitgründer und Vorstandsvorsitzende von Google DeepMind, Demis Hassabis, sowie Microsoft-Präsident Brad Smith. Hinzu kamen hochrangige Vertreter der Europäischen Union und der irischen Regierung. Die Veranstaltung war ein zentraler Bestandteil der irischen EU-Ratspräsidentschaft.

Allerdings provozierte das Fehlen kritischer Stimmen, die den vorherrschenden KI-Enthusiasmus hätten ausgleichen können, Vorwürfe. Die Konferenz betreibe einseitige Werbung für die Technologiebranche. Der Gipfel sei "fast wie eine politische Werbesendung für KI" gewesen, sagte Barry O'Sullivan, Professor für Künstliche Intelligenz am University College Cork, der DW.

Das übergreifende Thema war die Frage, wie KI dazu genutzt werden kann, Europas schwächelnde Wirtschaft wieder in Schwung zu bringen, Innovationen zu fördern und zu den USA sowie China aufzuschließen.

In Diskussionsrunden mit zahlreichen Führungskräften wurden die potenziellen Vorteile deutlich herausgestellt: die frühzeitige Erkennung von Krebs, bessere Warnungen vor Extremwetterereignissen und die Optimierung der Stromverteilung zur Energieeinsparung. Damit diese Versprechen jedoch zu verlässlichen Dienstleistungen werden, sind weiterhin umfangreiche Tests und Validierungen erforderlich.

Irland hat ein großes Eigeninteresse an diesen Themen. Das Land beherbergt die europäischen Niederlassungen mehrerer der weltweit größten Technologieunternehmen und ist stark von den Steuerzahlungen dieser Konzerne abhängig. Im Jahr 2025 zahlten ausländische multinationale Unternehmen 28,8 Milliarden Euro Körperschaftsteuer und trugen damit 87 Prozent der gesamten irischen Einnahmen bei.

Daraus ergibt sich für Dublin ein klares Interesse daran, europäische Regeln mitzugestalten und gleichzeitig Investoren nicht abzuschrecken. Europas finanzielle Schlagkraft bleibt jedoch deutlich hinter der der USA zurück: Laut dem AI Index der Stanford University beliefen sich die privaten KI-Investitionen in den USA im Jahr 2025 auf 286 Milliarden US-Dollar (rund 253 Milliarden Euro), verglichen mit lediglich 21 Milliarden US-Dollar (18,6 Milliarden Euro) in Europa.

Doch muss Europa überhaupt zu denselben Bedingungen konkurrieren? O'Sullivan ist der Ansicht, Europa sollte auf seine eigenen Stärken setzen, darunter Industrierobotik und spezialisierte Anwendungen: "Wir haben in Europa eine ganz besondere KI-Chance, und genau darauf sollten wir setzen."

Die Nutzung ausländischer KI-Modelle könnte europäischen Unternehmen helfen, schneller voranzukommen. Sie macht sie jedoch auch abhängig von Anbietern außerhalb Europas, die Preise ändern, den Zugang einschränken oder selbst entscheiden können, welche Funktionen verfügbar gemacht werden.

Die EU-Vertreter auf dem Gipfel betonten rasch, dass inzwischen Regelwerke geschaffen worden seien, um die Vorteile von KI zu nutzen und gleichzeitig ihre Risiken zu begrenzen. Michael McGrath, der irische EU-Kommissar für Demokratie, Justiz, Rechtsstaatlichkeit und Verbraucherschutz, sprach von einem Gleichgewicht zwischen dem Potenzial der KI und den "angemessenen Leitplanken", die erforderlich seien, um Vertrauen zu schaffen.

Das KI-Gesetz der EU (AI Act) verbietet bestimmte Praktiken und stellt strengere Anforderungen an besonders sensible Anwendungsbereiche. Die seit Juli 2026 geltenden Omnibus-Änderungen sollen die Einhaltung der Vorschriften vereinfachen, haben jedoch auch einige Schutzmaßnahmen verzögert. Regeln für Bereiche wie Beschäftigung und Bildung gelten nun ab Dezember 2027, während Vorschriften für KI in regulierten Produkten ab August 2028 Anwendung finden.

Die Europäische Kommission argumentiert, Unternehmen bräuchten Zeit, um sich an neue Standards und Leitlinien anzupassen. Kritiker hingegen sind der Ansicht, dass Schutzmaßnahmen unter dem Druck des internationalen Wettbewerbs aufgeschoben würden.

Für O'Sullivan stehen Regulierung und Innovation nicht im Widerspruch zueinander. "Die Vorstellung, man könne nicht gleichzeitig Regulierung und Innovation haben, ist schlicht Unsinn", sagte er und verwies auf die Pharmaindustrie und Medizintechnik.

Für die Demonstrierenden vor dem Tagungsort klingen die Zusicherungen von Politikern und Technologieunternehmen wenig überzeugend. Paul Murphy, irischer Abgeordneter der sozialistischen Partei People Before Profit, argumentiert, dass die europäischen Vorschriften zwar strenger seien als jene in den USA, die Messlatte insgesamt aber weiterhin niedrig liege. In den jüngsten Änderungen sieht er einen Schritt in Richtung Deregulierung. "Die großen Tech-Konzerne geben das Tempo vor, und von allen anderen wird erwartet, einfach mitzumachen", sagte er der DW.

Stattdessen fordert er eine gesellschaftliche Debatte unter Beteiligung von Zivilgesellschaft, Gewerkschaften und Politik darüber, wie sich die Technologie entwickeln soll und wie begrenzte Ressourcen wie Wasser und Strom eingesetzt werden sollten.

Fionnuala Ní Bhrógáin, Gewerkschaftsvertreterin und eine der wenigen kritischen Stimmen auf der Konferenzbühne, stellte die Frage, wem höhere Produktivität letztlich zugutekomme. Optimierung könne, so sagte sie, den Druck auf Beschäftigte erhöhen.

Auch Murphy befürchtet einen steigenden Druck, schneller zu arbeiten, bei geringerer Autonomie und stärkerer Überwachung. Ob die durch KI eingesparte Zeit bei E-Mails tatsächlich zum Nachdenken genutzt werden könne oder lediglich zu noch mehr Aufgaben führe, hänge von den Entscheidungen der Arbeitgeber und der Verhandlungsmacht der Beschäftigten ab.

O'Sullivan dagegen vertritt die Ansicht, dass KI, indem sie monotone Aufgaben übernehme, den Beschäftigten "mehr Zeit zum Nachdenken und für Kreativität in ihrer Arbeit" verschaffe.

In Irland ist die Frage nach Ressourcen besonders brisant. Im Jahr 2025 verbrauchten Rechenzentren 23 Prozent des gemessenen Stroms des Landes. Dazu gehören Cloud-Dienste und andere digitale Angebote, nicht nur KI-Anwendungen. Murphy argumentiert, dass ihr weiterer Ausbau die Stromkosten für Haushalte erhöhe und die Klimaziele gefährde.

Erneuerbare Energien könnten helfen, die steigende Nachfrage zu decken. Allerdings müsse zusätzliche saubere Energie gleichzeitig fossile Brennstoffe ersetzen, während auch Wohnungsbau und andere Industrien Netzkapazitäten benötigten. "Die Nachhaltigkeit von KI ist ein enormes Problem", stimmte O'Sullivan zu und forderte die Branche auf, ihren gesamten Energieverbrauch zu senken.

Vertreter der Branche räumten ein, dass klare Grenzen notwendig seien. Der leitende Wissenschaftler von Alphabet, Hassabis, bezeichnete die jüngsten KI-Hacking-Vorfälle als "erste Warnschüsse" und sprach sich für internationale Aufsicht aus. Microsoft-Präsident Smith erklärte: "Wir brauchen Gesetze. Wir brauchen Regulierung."

Sowohl auf als auch neben der Bühne herrschte Einigkeit: Es habe sich gezeigt, wie schwierig es sein könne, zunehmend autonome Systeme unter Kontrolle zu halten. Die Zusicherungen der Entwickler allein können Sicherheit nicht garantieren. Aber auch Regulierung wird nicht jedes Risiko ausschließen können. Für O'Sullivan und Murphy bleibt sie dennoch unverzichtbar.

Europa möchte die Entwicklung der KI aktiv gestalten, ohne auf die versprochenen Vorteile zu verzichten. Dieser Balanceakt ist schwierig und erfordert Entscheidungen über Investitionen, Ressourcen und die Verteilung der Gewinne. Selbst eine spektakuläre Eröffnungsaufführung kann diese Fragen nicht wegtanzen.

‘We’re not playing games’: Parents launch playground protests to fight school field sell-off

School looking to sell piece of open land beloved by people in Devon town to fund refurbishments

On Saturdays throughout autumn, residents of Totnes have met at a field on the banks of the River Dart to play ballgames, fly kites or simply sit on the grass and chat.

But it hasn’t just been about having fun – this has been a “play protest” aimed at trying to save a beloved piece of open land in the historic Devon market town.

The local comprehensive, King Edward VI Community College (KEVICC), has been given the go-ahead by the UK government to sell the 1.5-hectare plot – Lower Field – and it is likely houses will be built on it.

School leaders say they desperately need funds to revamp neglected buildings but campaigners claim the sell-off is an example of land that has been used by communities for decades being whisked away – a modern “enclosure” of public space.

“It’s devastating,” said Becca Robinson, who uses the field for women’s and girls’ fitness classes. “I’m trying to get women and girls to be more active and to get outdoors. Kurdinnen und Kurden gehören zu den größten Zuwanderungsgruppen in Deutschland. Dass dennoch vergleichsweise wenig über sie bekannt ist, liegt auch daran, dass es keinen kurdischen Staat gibt.

Die Zahl der in Deutschland lebenden Kurdinnen und Kurden lässt sich nur schwer genau bestimmen. Der Grund ist einfach und zugleich traurig: Es gibt keinen international anerkannten kurdischen Staat. Die Menschen stammen aus verschiedenen Ländern, vor allem aus der Türkei und Syrien. Eine eigene kurdische Staatsangehörigkeit gibt es nicht. In ihren Herkunftsländern haben viele Repressalien erlebt. Viele sind mittlerweile auch in Deutschland geboren.

Dennoch versuchte der Mediendienst Integration, mehr Klarheit über die Zahl und die Lebenssituation der Kurdinnen und Kurden in Deutschland zu schaffen. In einer Videokonferenz des Mediendienstes berichtete die Psychotherapeutin und Sozialwissenschaftlerin Çinur Ghaderi über neuste Studien und Statistiken, aber auch über Probleme und Anfeindungen Kurdinnen und Kurden gegenüber in Deutschland.

Ghaderi ist selbst 1970 im Irak  geboren und lehrt an der Evangelischen Hochschule in Bochum. Im Januar dieses Jahres war sie Mitautorin einer Studie, für die mehrere hundert Kurdinnen und Kurden in München befragt wurden.

Das Ergebnis ihrer Untersuchungen: Mit rund 1,5 Millionen Menschen gehören Kurdinnen und Kurden zu den größten Einwanderungsgruppen in Deutschland. Allein seit 2010 sind mehr als 250.000 Menschen vor allem aus Syrien nach Deutschland gekommen. Hauptgrund der Flucht war die zunehmende Verfolgung während des syrischen Bürgerkrieges.

"2011 ging die Bundesregierung von etwa 800.000 Kurdinnen und Kurden in Deutschland aus", sagt die Bochumer Wissenschaftlerin. "Zwischen 2010 und Juli 2026 registrierte das Bundesamt für Migration und Flüchtlinge knapp 608.000 Asylanträge."

Vor allem für Menschen aus Syrien hat sich die Situation in den vergangenen Monaten teilweise drastisch geändert. "Nach dem Sturz des Assad-Regimes 2024 haben Rückkehrbewegungen eingesetzt. Zugleich bleiben die Verhältnisse besonders im Norden und Nordosten unsicher", so Ghaderi. Dennoch wolle die Bundesregierung nun vermehrt Menschen nach Syrien abschieben und tue dies bereits, was zu erheblicher Verunsicherung bei den Betroffenen führe.

Menschenrechtsgruppen, so Ghaderi weiter, berichten von willkürlichen Übergriffen: Der syrischen Übergangsregierung werde vorgeworfen, besonders in Aleppo gewaltsam gegen Kurdinnen und Kurden vorgegangen zu sein. Auch Schwierigkeiten, nach der Rückkehr wieder an das eigene Eigentum zu gelangen, werden geschildert. Zwar hätten sich die zuvor heftigen Kämpfe in Syrien beruhigt, von einem sicheren Land könne trotzdem nicht die Rede sein, so Ghaderi.

2025 war die Türkei Deutschlands wichtigstes Abschiebeland. Dabei würden die Antiterrorgesetze trotz der PKK-Entwaffnung weiterhin weit ausgelegt.

Die Türkei spielt auch in der Geschichte der kurdischen Zuwanderung nach Deutschland eine zentrale Rolle. Ab etwa 1920 kamen zunächst Angehörige der Elite, darunter Studierende, Diplomaten und Intellektuelle, nach Deutschland. Ab 1961 folgten Hunderttausende Kurdinnen und Kurden im Zuge der deutsch-türkischen Anwerbeabkommen als Arbeitskräfte. Eine dritte große Zuwanderungswelle setzte vor allem ab 2010 ein, als Krieg und Verfolgung viele Menschen zur Flucht zwangen. Außerdem mussten Kurdinnen und Kurden in Deutschland immer wieder unter dem pauschalen Verdacht leiden, Anhänger der kurdischen Arbeiterpartei PKK zu sein, für die schon seit 1993 in Deutschland ein Betätigungsverbot besteht.

"Viele fühlen sich in Deutschland zuhause. Sie engagieren sich in Parteien, Kommunen und Vereinen", sagt die Wissenschaftlerin weiter. "Zugleich bleiben Krieg und Verfolgung in ihren Herkunftsländern sehr präsent".

Im August verabschiedete das türkische Parlament ein Gesetz, das eine eingeschränkte und an Bedingungen geknüpfte Amnestie für PKK-Kämpfer vorsieht. My Family’s Dark, Hidden History With Institutionalization

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

My great-great-grandfather was the namesake of an infamous institution for the disabled. From long-neglected records of the property, the lives of forgotten people were uncovered.

On the Fernald campus. From left to right: Walter Jr, Walter Sr., Tom (the eventual father of Barbara and Winton), Helen (my great-grandmother), and Katherine.

There is a single name across the front of my family gravestone: FERNALD. A simple slab tucked beneath a low-hanging spruce, it would be easy to miss among the grand monuments and lush gardens surrounding it at Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, Massachusetts. But for many people in the state, the name it bears can still incite a shiver of terror. It was once invoked by teachers and parents as a threat to misbehaving children: “Keep it up and I’ll send you to the Fernald.”

The Walter E. Fernald State School and Hospital, known to locals as the Fernald, sits on a hill in Waltham, Massachusetts, a few miles down the road from my family’s plot. Now an abandoned heap of earth and brick, from its founding in 1848 until its closure in 2014, the Fernald was the oldest institution for intellectually and developmentally disabled people in the Western Hemisphere. For decades, thousands of disabled, neglected, or “behaviorally troubled” children were brought there; many never left. In the mid-20th century, it became one of the nation’s most infamous institutions after journalists, activists, and lawyers brought to light the squalid conditions inside the Fernald, its neglect of patients, and even the medical experimentation, sponsored by corporations like Quaker Oats, that took place there.

In the spring of 2024, I stood before the family stone as my grandma was being buried. She had died a year earlier, and her name was being added to a long list on the back of the stone which began with that of our patriarch: “Walter E. Fernald.” For 37 years, he was the superintendent of the institution that was then called the Massachusetts School for Idiotic and Feeble-Minded Youth. When he died, 100 years before his granddaughter did, flags flew at half-mast across the state, The Boston Globe wrote that his life inspired “something approaching to awe,” and the institution he had run took his name—an honor that ultimately cemented his dishonor.

My grandma had been the family archivist: Cabinets in her home office overflowed with yellowed newspaper clippings and family trees printed from her 1992 Macintosh. I never paid much attention when she extolled our Mayflower origins or showed me photographs of scowling ladies who were apparently related to me. But in September 2022, early in my senior year of college, while I was mapping out a thesis about my older brother’s hospitalizations with a brain illness, I remembered the occasional conversations I had had with her about her famous grandfather. A quick Google search sent a shock of shame up my spine—there were a slew of stories about abuse at the institution he had once run, but also damning information about the man himself. He was labeled a “eugenicist,” a “sterilizer,” a “monster.”

This revelation led me to drive up to my grandma’s nursing home in Northampton, Massachusetts, to ask her about him. Her health was declining at this point, but she was adamant that I had it all wrong: Walter Fernald was not a monster; he was a remarkable man who would visit every single resident each night before bedtime and taught service to others  above all. She insisted that there were no “horror stories” to tell, no matter how much I “seem to want them.” I kept replaying that last point over in my head as I drove back to campus, past the fast fading leaves. Did I want them?

All I knew was that my obsession with Fernald’s legacy only grew. I was lucky that my grandma was such a fastidious record-keeper. I went through her archives, which had been tossed into bankers boxes and were sitting outside in the barn at her old house, and spoke to local historians, but I was only able to view a few of the tens of thousands of records belonging to Fernald that were held in the state’s archives. I could only access the small number that had the patient names redacted due to a draconian Massachusetts law that restricted access to institutional medical records indefinitely—rather than the standard 50 years as specified in the federal Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA). That meant that the Fernald records from 1848 were treated the same as those from 2014.

What I was able to uncover complicated both the condemnations of Walter Fernald that I found online and my grandma’s unequivocal praise. He was undoubtedly a fierce supporter of what was then a radical idea: that disabled children should be offered treatment and education rather than being abandoned or neglected by their families. Through his work at the institution and in local school districts, he created the foundations of what we now call special education. But while he was opposed to the practice of forcibly sterilizing residents, he promoted eugenicist ideas about their potential criminality and, for a period of his career, called for their lifelong segregation from the surrounding community. These ideas turned his Waltham institution into the archetype for a national state hospital system—hundreds of sister institutions were established from Maine to California that were designed to permanently warehouse the disabled under the guise of “public safety.” This system—now often forgotten, its abandoned institutions the backdrop to haunted houses or misremembered as asylums (which served the mentally ill and typically had short-term, rather than indefinite, stays)—was among the largest public investments in the country from the late 19th to the mid-20th century. At its peak in 1967, it held nearly 200,000 people, very often against their will; Walter Fernald was one of its original masterminds.

I never got to share what I had learned with my grandma. She died a couple months after my visit. Would she have been disappointed by who her grandfather really was? Vindicated? In denial? I only knew she would have been proud of me for trying to understand our family history. Now, as she was being interred beside Walter, I recognized all the names on the back of the stone. I knew the story. I remembered.

But as I stepped back from the gravestone, I felt my foot drop onto stone instead of grass. I looked down and saw I was standing on a barely visible mud-caked slab with a name I didn’t recognize: “Barbara M. Fernald 1921–1935.” I called to my mother, “Ma, who is this?” She came over and together we scraped the mud off with our soles. She didn’t know, perhaps a cousin of my grandmother’s who had died young. What had kept her name off the central stone? Beside Barbara’s stone I noticed an even smaller square with no words on it—a nameless grave.

Who was Barbara? Who lay beneath that bare slab? Soon after my grandmother’s funeral, Alex Green, a fellow researcher I had met through my work, contacted me. Alex, a lecturer in public policy at Harvard University who was then finishing a book about my great-great-grandfather, had commiserated with my struggles with the state’s exceptionally restrictive records law. He had sued the state to get access to records from Fernald that were over 100 years old and, in order to view them, agreed to have his notes checked each day to make sure he wasn’t recording confidential patient information. He was so frustrated by the obfuscation that he persuaded the Legislature to create a Special Commission on State Institutions to study, among other things, the state’s actions with regard to records and offer recommendations for ways to improve access to them. Signed into law in July 2022, the 15-person commission—the nation’s first disability rights-led human rights inquiry into the legacy of institutionalization—met for the first time in June 2023.

“This may be a bit of a shock,” he now told me, “but I am fairly certain Fernald had two grandchildren who died in institutions for children with intellectual disabilities.” He had come across offhand references to them in his research. Tom Fernald, my grandmother’s uncle, had two children who both had PKU, a now-treatable genetic condition that causes toxic buildup of the amino acid phenylalanine and leads to progressive brain damage. Barbara Fernald had died at age 13 at a private facility in Pennsylvania in March 1935. Her younger brother Winton had been admitted to Fernald’s sister school, the Wrentham State School, where he died at age 21 in June of 1946. That was all Alex had.

The private facility in Pennsylvania was long closed, and according to the local historical society, its records had almost certainly been destroyed or lost. But I knew Winton’s records must be out there, in some basement archive, but that the state was not going to give me access to them, even though I was related to him and he was long dead.

This restriction was not only absurd but hypocritical. I knew, despite the state’s supposed emphasis on protecting patients’ identities, that thousands of records from as recent as the 1990s had been left on the abandoned grounds of the Fernald for hordes of trespassers to steal and vandalize. I knew it because I’d seen the documents myself.

A couple months after my grandmother’s death, on a cold January morning in 2023, I sat in a car outside the metal gate leading to abandoned acres of the Fernald. I watched Bryan Parcival, a local photographer and Fernald historian I’d befriended through my research, jostle the gate’s lock. “If this doesn’t work we’ll have to hike on,” he said as he shook and twisted the key. With a sudden crack, the lock opened and fell to the ground. He pushed open the groaning gates and hopped back into the driver’s seat. “I got this key five years ago when the state had me take photos of this place. The guy who gave it to me, some maintenance man, died a few weeks later, so no one knows I have this.” He lifted the little silver key up to my face and smiled. “You ready?” he asked, and without waiting for my reply, he drove us past the “No Trespassing” signs and onto the property no one in my family had set foot on in decades.

I followed Bryan’s footsteps as he darted across the buckling wood floors into the Asylum building, which once served as the institution’s main hospital. Mountains of rusted wheelchairs lay crumpled on their sides beside wooden restraint chairs with thick black ropes. Spilling from cabinets was a stream of papers that lay beside books with broken spines, children’s drawings of butterflies, sheet music for “Ode to Joy,” forks, knives, and spoons. I picked up one of the loose papers, a patient record from 1995 titled “Psychotropic Medical Treatment Plan” with a person’s name, birthday, medications, symptoms, and a diagnosis of “chronic schizophrenia” clearly visible. It was one of thousands like it that dotted the floor.

Of course, the campus was technically off-limits. But in 2020 and 2021, the cameras Bryan had set up around the property recorded more than 5,300 incidents of trespassing, during which buildings were frequently damaged and records were stolen, urinated on, and graffitied. One video showed people shooting pornography in the abandoned library that had once housed one of the largest collections of disability literature in the nation. He shared all of this with the City of Waltham, which has owned the property since the institution’s closure. Nothing happened. A police spokesperson told me that they were unable to secure the property due to its size. Urban explorers shared their adventures openly on social media. A local teenager told me it was a rite of passage for kids in the city to visit the campus and that he personally had multiple boxes of patient records in his room that he had swiped. “Do you want to see them?” he asked.

I wrote an article in early 2024 for The Boston Globe about these blatant violations of both HIPAA and the state’s own more restrictive records law that had taken place with the knowledge of the state and the city. The Massachusetts Department of Developmental Services (DDS) blamed the state’s Division of Capital Asset Management and Maintenance (DCAMM) for failing to remove the records in 2014. The DCAMM responded by shifting the blame back to the DDS. Meanwhile, Waltham’s mayor stayed mum. The article changed little for the campus itself. Over the past two years, some of its oldest buildings have been destroyed by arson, and the town has opened a park on a corner of the property that includes miniature golf and a train. The state did remove thousands of records from the campus. According to Caitlin Ramos, the curator at the Massachusetts State Archives, they were extremely damaged and were ultimately all destroyed by the DDS.

But the article did have one major effect: People who were desperate to get some information about loved ones who had died at the Fernald began to reach out to me. One had heard that his sister had choked to death on peanut butter while she wasn’t being properly supervised, another wanted to track down a beloved aunt who had vanished during his childhood, and another had recently discovered that he had a cousin he had never been told about, and he wanted to find his unmarked grave and buy a proper headstone. The state had previously rebuffed them all, telling them it was to protect the privacy of the patients. What privacy was there on the moldy floors of the Fernald?

One of those who had been denied access was Susan Ruderman, a 64-year-old fifth-generation Bostonian who had long known that her mother, Betty, had a missing sibling. In January 1933, when Betty was 4, her half-brother didn’t come home after school. “Where’s Larry?” she recalled her older brother asking. Their mother turned to the two children and barked, “Never speak of him again!”

Despite her mother’s admonition, Betty occasionally mentioned her long-lost sibling to her daughter. In 2019, years after Betty’s death, Susan became concerned that she might be a carrier of PKU and decided to track down what happened to the lost boy, whose name was all she had to go by. Combing through the 1940 US Census, she found an entry for Lawrence Barry. He was listed as a resident of the Fernald. A request to the state archives for his records led to a typical bureaucratic response: She was told that she needed to hire a lawyer, go to court to prove lineage, and be appointed guardian of his estate. It was too much; she begrudgingly let Larry slip back into the well of silence where he’d sat for decades.

But in May 2025, the Special Commission on State Institutions that Alex had helped start and which he served as vice chair of, released its final report on the decades of mistreatment and disregard for records and graves at the Fernald and other state institutions. In response, in November of that year, Governor Maura Healey signed a law that opened the state’s institutional records to families and researchers 75 years after their creation or, if still held by the DDS or the Department of Mental Health, 50 years after the death of the patient. Alex estimates that 75 to 100 million formerly hidden records were found, received, and opened to the public and that more than 1,000 people have been able to access the story of their loved ones. (The state archive did not immediately keep track of access to institutional records under the new law, but between July 1 and October 13 of this year, 143 people received records; the vast majority are family members of former patients.) The new law also temporarily paused the destruction of documents, a de facto admission that the state had been destroying them. Massachusetts, once one of the most restrictive states for access to institutional records, overnight became one of the most transparent.

Susan made a new request and received Larry’s records within days. There was no evidence of PKU in Larry’s records, but what they did reveal was that he was admitted to the Fernald in 1933 at the age of 8 as a “neglected child,” and that he was determined to escape. A medical note dated November 17, 1933, a few months after he was brought to the Fernald, indicated that he was constantly getting into fights and sprinting from the campus into the surrounding neighborhoods. According to the records, after he was brought back on one occasion, he said, “[I’m] posted anyway, so what’s the use of being good?” The file documents years of constant escapes from wards, infirmaries, haylofts, and, once, from a bean patch on the school’s farm. He stole bikes, made it to train stations, and was caught by local residents and police in towns near and far. He was continually being brought back. His yearly files often ended with the same two sentences: “He had no visitors and no correspondence. Clothing is free.”

After an escape when he was 18, the institution gave up on tracking him down. The file continues with notes that the Fernald staff occasionally received phone calls reporting that he was in and out of prisons and other institutions. The final accessible record was from January 1951, when a Waltham police officer questioned a suspicious-looking man at the employee residence at the Fernald; the man claimed that he was a sailor from California and thought the place was a hotel. In fact, he was Larry Barry, returning to the place he hated so much that he’d escaped a hundred times but that was also the only home he’d ever known. The unnamed staff member who wrote the note told the police officer that Larry was not a patient anymore and “they were free to deal with him as they saw fit.” Larry was taken away by the police.

While many questions are left unanswered, Susan says this access was an unbelievable gift—an opportunity to finally get a glimpse of a family member who had been relegated to oblivion since that cold January day in 1933. She is determined to find out what happened to him after he was taken by the Waltham police in 1951, to find his grave, and, if necessary, give it a proper stone.

Just as it did for Susan and the dozens of other people who reached out to me for help, the change in the law meant I could access not only Walter Fernald’s records but also those of his grandson. Within a matter of days, I received Winton’s records from the DDS in an e-mail. It included his autopsy report, which gave him a mental age of 2 years and 10 months and a diagnosis of “imbecile,” a series of medical and psychological notes from his admission at the age of 1 to his death 20 years later, and two photos: one at age 7, standing in a field with a mischievous smile on his face, and another at age 20, in an oversize suit with a large white handkerchief protruding awkwardly from the chest pocket, staring grimly down against a black background. The records show that Winton was constantly shredding his clothes and, as he grew older, smashing his fists into windows. He often had to get stitches on his knuckles. He occasionally had visitors, but his parents, perhaps because of the trauma of institutionalizing two of their children, moved to Florida shortly after his birth and apparently rarely visited Massachusetts.

Wrentham was founded and run by one of Walter Fernald’s closest protégés, Dr. George Wallace. It is very likely that this is why Winton was brought there, and perhaps, though the records don’t reflect this, he received special attention because of his last name. But after Wallace’s sudden death in 1930, Wrentham, like most institutions in the period coinciding with the Great Depression and then the Second World War, had scant funding and dangerously few employees. One staff member per 50 residents was common. Whatever special attention he might have had was likely gone—it is no wonder he kept smashing his fist into windows.

In the final pages of his records, I found proof that Winton was indeed buried under that small stone square at the family plot at Mount Auburn. The files have no mention of his grandfather, the man who had bought that plot, whose name had been bestowed on the institution a few miles down the road, and who had helped create the very system of institutionalization in which his grandson had died. No, it just says Winton died with his mother at his bedside, that he was cremated and buried beside his sister, Barbara, laid to rest 10 years earlier. Unlike her, he did not get a name.

These records cannot do much. Written in the institution’s removed hand, they cannot truly tell his experience, what he was like, what he thought or felt or desired. His life was just one drop in a great ocean of forgotten lives. But is that so special? Despite our best attempts to archive the past, to keep the records from rotting, from potter’s fields to Mount Auburn, from institutions to families, isn’t forgetting the one inevitable thing?

But even so, why do I keep looking at the sad photographs of this faraway boy? What do I want from these “horror stories”? Why do I keep reading and rereading the records, especially returning to the rare moments when he speaks? Such as the January 3, 1941, examination in which he kept repeating, “So glad I’m going home” and “I hate this damn dump.” Or on November 12, 1935, when he kept begging the nurse, “Turn light on, Miss. It’s dark, Miss.” The game, the report says, was an “obsession with him and it was practically impossible to get him to stop.” He jumped up and down and up and down, flicking the light on and off and on and off, answering questions at random, until he finally declared, “No more.”

Oliver Egger is a writer and editor based in New Haven, Connecticut.
