The midterms are packed with ballot measures that could dramatically affect natural spaces.

When millions of voters fill out their ballots in November, they won’t just be choosing elected representatives. Many states are offering the chance to impact conservation and wildlife as well, through ballot measures focused on things like dedicating more money to habitat protection or mitigating the impacts of climate change. One could even reshape wildlife management in one of the country’s most picturesque mountain states — and also lead to the indiscriminate deaths of all kinds of wild animals.

And while ballot measures might seem like small potatoes in the grand scheme of electoral politics, they could have an outsized impact this year. The Trump administration has been systematically rolling back protections for endangered species, taking a hammer to longstanding pillars of climate regulation, and cutting funding to agencies like the National Park Service and National Forest Service, both of which oversee vast stretches of wildlife habitats. In many cases, states have had to step up, putting more resources into conserving the habitats and species within their borders, and ballot measures can help shape those decisions.

Here are the seven ballot measures that could transform ecosystems across the country this election:

This ballot measure would shorten environmental review periods for “essential projects” like housing, water systems, clean energy, and medical facilities, among others. In a state that’s been struggling to build what it needs fast enough, this could help speed essential clean energy projects that could help slow climate change. On the other hand, reducing California Environmental Quality Act review time, as this proposition would do, could also lead to increased habitat destruction, leading advocacy organizations like the Sierra Club to oppose it.

This amendment might seem a little in the weeds — and mostly in the interest of farmers and ranchers looking for tax breaks. But it would double (from 2,000 acres to 4,000 acres) the amount of land an owner can get tax breaks from if they protect it under the state’s Conservation Use Valuation Assessment program. As Yanshu Li, an Associate Professor of Forest Economics at the University of Georgia, told me, the amendment could incentivize landowners to set aside larger tracts of their property for wildlife habitat conservation, which would benefit species like the nine-banded armadillo and the northern bobwhite quail that call these habitats home.

Question 6 would create a “Nature for All Fund,” routing existing taxes on sporting goods, recreational vehicles, and money spent at golf courses to conservation. That fund could then be used on natural resource conservation, making it the first time the state has a dedicated funding source for conservation projects.

This proposition, similar to Massachusetts’, would direct existing sales taxes on sporting goods to a state fund dedicated to conservation and wildfire prevention. In a state where wildfires have torn through more than 2 million acres in the past decade, dozens of conservation groups are supporting this measure to protect habitats and species.

Here, voters are being asked to approve up to $500 million in bonds that can be used to fund water-related infrastructure projects like water treatment, waste disposal, and wetlands infrastructure. A boost to wetlands under the Arkansas proposal could help animals that live in those habitats, which are often overlooked in this region.

This measure would allow for $55 million in bonds to fund environmental and recreational projects. The largest chunk of this change — $25 million — would go to helping to protect the state from the impacts of climate change. Another $7 would be devoted to protecting Narragansett Bay and its watersheds, which would include funding to “restore natural habitats and control invasive aquatic plants,” according to the Rhode Island Current.

These ballot measures are all relatively straightforward so far, but there’s one question up to voters this fall that puts wild animals front-and-center — and is splitting hunters and anglers down an ethical centerline. Which takes us back to Colorado:

On its surface, this amendment seems fairly innocuous. It asks voters to enshrine “the right to hunt, fish, and harvest fish and wildlife by traditional methods” in the state’s constitution. Hunting and fishing are already big in Colorado, and they’re protected by statute, so this may not seem like a major change. But, as Rebecca Niemiec, co-director of Colorado State University’s Animal-Human Policy Center and a Colorado Parks and Wildlife commissioner, speaking for herself, told me, neither the text of the bill nor its proponents define what “traditional methods” of hunting and fishing would be allowed under this amendment.

“Read plainly, traditional methods could be interpreted to include practices like poisoning, leg hold traps, gill nets, or baiting and hounding,” Niemiec said. Many of these practices injure or maim animals, including ones that aren’t targeted: Steel-jawed traps and poisons don’t discriminate between, say, a deer, a wolf, and a lynx. All of these practices have been outlawed in Colorado over the years, either through other ballot measures or regulatory changes; in 1992, for example, Coloradans voted to ban hunting bears using bait and hounds, and four years later they banned leg-hold traps and snares.

The new amendment also continues on to propose: “establishing hunting and fishing as the preferred means of managing fish and wildlife populations.” This would leave far less room for wildlife agencies like CPW to make nuanced decisions or roll out alternative programs, like birth control or animal relocation. It also reduces their ability to take into account the preferences of people who value wildlife for reasons other than hunting.

“Biological science can inform decisions about how many animals can be killed without threatening a population,” Niemiec said. “But it can’t tell us what hunting practices are ethical, or how to balance hunting opportunities with the public’s desire to see wildlife alive — and protected from unnecessary suffering.”

The push for these constitutional protections, Niemiec told me, is part of a larger backlash against conservation groups. But in this case, it’s not as simple as hunter vs. non-hunter: Plenty of hunters and anglers support taking ethical considerations into account when making decisions about how to manage wildlife, and some hunting groups in Colorado have publicly spoken out against the amendment.

“If we’re going to enshrine anything in the Constitution, it should say we should be balancing the diversity of public interest in wildlife in all of our management decisions,” Niemiec said. “We need innovative methods, not traditional methods, if we’re going to deal with the complex challenges that our wildlife is facing moving forward in the state and throughout the country.”

Samsung Galaxy Z Flip8 Review: The Littlest Smartphone That Could

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you crave a compact phone that doesn’t feel like a brick in your pocket and slips easily into a small bag, the flip style is for you. Samsung’s eighth-generation clamshell is seriously refined, and as svelte and slick as it comes. It may not offer a tablet-sized screen or the fashionable new passport shape, but it is arguably the most practical type of folding phone.

Like almost every other phone this year, the Z Flip8 offers tiny improvements over its predecessor and costs $100 more. If you’re wondering whether it’s worth updating from last year’s Z Flip7, it’s not. The Flip8 is a teeny bit lighter and slimmer with a newer processor, and that’s about it. But if you’re looking to dip a toe in folding-phone waters or just want something small, the Z Flip8 should be in the conversation.

Everyone is excited about the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8’s sexy new form factor, but Samsung continues to iterate on the flip phone format for folks who prize compact devices. The Z Flip8 shaves off 0.02 inches (0.55 millimeters) and a third of an ounce (8 grams) compared to its predecessor, making it the slimmest and lightest option in this category. The improved hinge makes the crease slightly less noticeable and a little easier to open. I still found it a bit tricky to open one-handed, but you get the knack after a while. The new hinge is also supposed to be more durable.

The Flip8 has an IP48 rating, so you don’t need to worry about dropping it in the bath, but tiny dust and dirt particles (less than 1 millimeter) can still cause damage. It comes in pink, like my review unit, as well as graphite, cream, and mint. It still has a physical SIM slot, and the power button has a fingerprint sensor in it that works pretty well.

The screens are the same as last year's. It has a sharp 4.1-inch AMOLED display on the outside with a 60- or 120-Hz refresh rate. Open it up to reveal the 6.9-inch main display, also AMOLED but with a variable refresh rate (1 to 120 Hz) that’s better for battery life. Both screens are reasonably bright (2,600 nits peak), and I never struggled to read the screen outdoors.

Flip phones used to get palmed off with lesser processors, but the Z Flip8 boasts the Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 for Galaxy (the same cutting-edge chip that's in its two bigger siblings). It has 12 GB of RAM and 256 GB of storage. You can jump to 512 GB for an extra $200, but there’s no option to increase the RAM.

I encountered no stutters or lag and found performance to be generally excellent, whether jumping in and out of apps, firing up the camera, or gaming. The Z Flip8 can get warm, and this may throttle the processor somewhat if you’re playing a demanding game for a while (more than an hour). This is mostly down to the compact design.

The stereo speakers are better than I expected. They get quite loud without distortion, and they’ll serve you just fine for watching videos. Call quality is also solid, and slamming it shut to end a call never gets tired. I also like opening it to answer calls, but you must enable this in the Phone app settings under Flip options. Connectivity includes 5G (no mmWave), Wi-Fi 7, and Bluetooth 5.4, but there’s no ultra-wideband (UWB) for more precise positioning. The Fold8 models have Bluetooth 6 and UWB.

While it’s very capable, the Z Flip8 is only average when it comes to battery life, another drawback of its size. Samsung put in a 4,300-mAh silicon-carbon battery that’s good enough for casual use. If you’re on your phone a lot, you’ll want a power bank handy. Charging speeds are disappointing at 25 watts wired and 15 watts wireless, and I’m annoyed by Samsung’s refusal to implement the magnetic feature of Qi2.

If you’re a keen photographer, you can get many better camera systems in a range of Android phones for the same money or less. That said, the main 50-megapixel shooter is perfectly good in many situations, and most folks will be happy with the photos it captures. Some noise creeps in when it’s dark. The 12-megapixel ultrawide is useful when you want to cram a lot in or take a group selfie, but it’s a definite step down in quality. Sadly, there’s no telephoto lens, but you can emulate 2X zoom without losing detail by cropping, and it offers passable results in good lighting up to 10X.

I don’t see much reason to use the 10-megapixel punch-hole selfie camera outside of video calls, since you can flip it to get a cover-screen preview and use the main camera for selfies (a big benefit of this design). I also love Samsung’s Horizon Lock, which lets you shoot video with a steady horizontal plane no matter how you turn or move the phone.

Another new AI feature from Samsung is My FanCam, which lets you pick a subject to focus on in group videos and reframes them as though your chosen subject is the star. Handy for school plays or soccer games, if you remember to use it. (You can also apply it to old videos.) Speaking of AI, Samsung offers Now Brief to float up calendar events, reminders, and useful info like local weather, and Now Nudge to suggest adding dates to your calendar from messages, sharing locations or photos, and auto-filling forms.

The cover screen, or FlexWindow, has been a weakness for Samsung’s Flip line, and it’s still limited in the Flip8. On the plus side, you can use more apps with the cover screen, including most of Samsung’s apps (Health, Calendar, Browser), YouTube, Netflix, Google Maps, and Messages. But if you want other third-party apps, you still have to install Good Lock, then the MultiStar plug-in, and select the apps you want to use. It’s stupidly convoluted, and I don’t understand why it isn't preinstalled, especially since Samsung has virtually all its other apps on there, along with a few third-party apps I never asked for (Facebook, Spotify, and Instagram). This is admittedly a bugbear of mine, but it's disgraceful that Samsung is including bloatware on a phone this expensive.

I like that you can have YouTube on the cover screen, as you can prop it up and there’s a little toggle at the bottom to switch between full screen, above the camera lenses, or standard vertical format for scrolling. You’ll mostly use the inner screen, but I have sometimes used the Flip8 this way to prop up a video I’m half-watching on my desk, and it’s a nice option to have.

There’s only really one competitor for Samsung if you’re set on a flip phone, and that’s Motorola's Razr line. You can step down to a less powerful but much cheaper Razr at $800, or spend a little more to snag the superior Razr Ultra. The latter has a better camera, more RAM and storage, and charges much faster, but it costs $1,500. The awkward middle child, the Razr+ at $1,100, is a tougher sell (it has an older processor and shorter software support). You might consider last year’s Z Flip7 if you can find it discounted.

Sadly, this might be your last chance to get a Samsung flip phone. Persistent rumors suggest Samsung will sunset this form factor as the Fold8 takes off and goes head-to-head with the iPhone Duo, though Samsung hasn't officially confirmed it. If you do pull the trigger on the Flip8, you get an excellent seven years of platform and security software updates, potentially taking you all the way to 2033. Who knows, maybe small phones will be back in fashion by then.

One of Earth’s most powerful supervolcanoes is refilling with fresh magma

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

One of Earth's most powerful supervolcanoes is slowly refilling its enormous underground magma reservoir, thousands of years after producing one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recent geological history. Scientists at Kobe University have discovered evidence that fresh magma is accumulating beneath Japan's Kikai caldera, which unleashed a catastrophic eruption about 7,300 years ago.

The discovery offers a rare look at how giant volcanic systems rebuild their magma supplies after massive eruptions. It could also provide important clues about the behavior of other supervolcanoes, including Yellowstone in the United States and Toba in Indonesia, potentially helping scientists improve their ability to recognize warning signs of future eruptions.

How Supervolcanoes Store Enormous Amounts of Magma

Some volcanic eruptions are so immense that they dramatically reshape the landscape. The amount of magma released can be staggering, enough to bury all of New York City's Central Park beneath a layer roughly 12 kilometers (7.5 miles) thick.

When such enormous volumes of magma escape from underground, the rock above the emptied reservoir can collapse, leaving behind a broad, relatively shallow depression known as a caldera. These features are associated with some of the most powerful eruptions in Earth's history.

Famous examples include Yellowstone, Toba, and Japan's Kikai caldera, most of which lies beneath the ocean. Kikai last experienced a giant eruption approximately 7,300 years ago, producing the largest known volcanic eruption of the Holocene, the geological epoch that began roughly 11,700 years ago and continues today.

Although scientists know that giant caldera volcanoes can erupt more than once, they still have an incomplete understanding of how the enormous quantities of magma required for these events accumulate underground. This makes it extremely difficult to anticipate when another major eruption might occur.

"We must understand how such large quantities of magma can accumulate to understand how giant caldera eruptions occur," says Kobe University geophysicist Nobukazu Seama.

Scientists Discover a Massive Magma Reservoir Beneath Japan

Kikai's underwater location might seem like an obstacle to studying the volcano, but it actually gives researchers an important advantage. The surrounding seafloor allows scientists to carry out extensive geological surveys across the volcanic structure.

"The underwater location allows us to implement systematic, large-scale surveys," Seama explains.

To investigate what lies beneath Kikai, researchers from Kobe University collaborated with the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC). They used powerful airgun arrays to generate seismic pulses that traveled through the crust beneath the ocean floor.

Instruments called ocean bottom seismometers recorded how these waves moved through the underground rock. Because seismic waves travel differently through solid rock and regions containing molten material, scientists can analyze their behavior to investigate structures that cannot be observed directly.

The findings, published in Communications Earth & Environment, revealed a substantial region containing a high proportion of magma directly beneath the site of Kikai's massive eruption 7,300 years ago.

By examining the seismic measurements, the team was able to determine the approximate size and shape of the underground reservoir. Its position and extent suggest that it occupies the same magma storage system involved in the ancient eruption.

"Due to its extent and location it is clear that this is in fact the same magma reservoir as in the previous eruption," Seama says.

Fresh Magma Is Recharging the Ancient Supervolcano

Perhaps the most intriguing finding is that the magma currently beneath Kikai does not appear to be simply leftover material from its catastrophic eruption thousands of years ago.

Previous investigations revealed that a new lava dome has been developing near the center of the caldera over approximately the past 3,900 years. A lava dome forms when relatively thick, viscous lava accumulates around a volcanic vent rather than flowing freely across the surrounding landscape.

Chemical analyses also showed that volcanic material from this dome and other more recent activity differs in composition from the material released during Kikai's giant eruption.

That difference provides an important clue. It suggests that the magma now stored beneath the volcano was supplied by new injections of molten rock from deeper within Earth's crust or mantle, rather than remaining unchanged since the previous eruption.

"This means that the magma that is now present in the magma reservoir under the lava dome is likely newly injected magma," summarizes Seama.

Together, the seismic observations and chemical evidence help researchers develop a clearer picture of how a giant volcanic system can gradually rebuild its underground magma supply following a massive eruption.

This replenishment process does not mean that another catastrophic eruption is imminent. Instead, it reveals how a magma reservoir can be recharged over thousands of years, an important process that scientists need to understand before they can reliably assess the long-term behavior of supervolcanoes.

What the Discovery Could Reveal About Yellowstone

The findings could have implications far beyond Japan.

Researchers believe the process identified beneath Kikai may help explain why other enormous caldera systems, including Yellowstone and Toba, also contain large magma reservoirs relatively close to Earth's surface.

"This magma re-injection model is consistent with the existence of large shallow magma reservoirs beneath other giant calderas like Yellowstone and Toba," says Seama.

The proposed model suggests that, following a giant eruption, new magma can gradually enter and replenish an existing underground reservoir. Understanding how this happens could reveal important patterns in the long-term cycles of magma accumulation, volcanic activity, and major eruptions.

Researchers now hope to improve the seismic techniques used at Kikai so they can investigate the movement and storage of magma in greater detail.

"We want to refine the methods that have proved to be so useful in this study to more deeply understand the re-injection processes. Our ultimate goal is to become better able to monitor the crucial indicators of future giant eruptions."

By revealing how fresh magma accumulates beneath a volcano responsible for one of Earth's most extraordinary eruptions, the research brings scientists closer to understanding the processes that eventually make such colossal events possible.

This research was funded by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) (The Third Earthquake and Volcano Hazards Observation and Research Program (Earthquake and Volcano Hazard Reduction Research)) and the Japan Society for the Promotion of Science (grant 20H00199). It was conducted in collaboration with researchers from the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC).

Prediction Markets Are Accurate

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

No surprise to me but a new paper looks at the forecast accuracy of over one million prediction questions from Polymarket, Kalshi, Manifold and the forecasting platform Metaculus. A virtue of the new analysis is that it covers many different areas from business to elections to geo-politics. An event predicted to happen p% of the time happens about p% of the time. Can’t beat that.

Using approximately one million resolved contracts and questions across four collective forecasting platforms, twelve domains, and time horizons ranging from hours to years, we evaluate the calibration and accuracy of collective forecasts in real-world settings. We found that forecast probabilities were overall highly calibrated: events assigned a probability of p% occurred approximately p% of the time. This broad pattern was evident across platforms and many domains, although fine-grained analyses revealed systematic tendencies toward over- or underestimation in highly consequential areas. While this does not mean that forecasting platforms provide certainty about individual events, our findings suggest that large-scale aggregation of judgments contains meaningful information about real-world uncertainty; when examined in aggregate, the predictions generated by the platforms provided informative estimates of event likelihood.

Protecting Americans from Medicine

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Max Doppelt: In January 2025 my mom’s lung cancer spread to her brain. The drug with the best chance of helping her was approved in China but not here.

A doctor told me, almost as an aside, that if she were Chinese she could buy it at a pharmacy.

So I flew to Shanghai and came home with 26 boxes in my luggage. That was 19 months ago. My mom is still here, still working, and in her words has had an amazing three years.

The drug was approved in China in 2023. The FDA approved it in July 2025. It still is not for sale here. I know of at least 30 Americans who have made the same trip.

I have been scared to tell this story because I worried it could cost my mom access to the medicine keeping her alive. But the problem matters more than my silence. These drugs need to be developed and available here, and that means FDA reform.

A piece at KFF Health cover’s Max’s story and adds this insanity:

Rep. John Moolenaar (R-Mich.), who chairs the House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party, in June co-sponsored legislation with Rep. Debbie Dingell (D-Mich.) requiring all U.S. pharmaceutical deals with Chinese companies to be cleared by the Treasury Department.

Got that? A son flew to Shanghai to buy pharmaceuticals to extend his mother’s life and the House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party wants a Treasury lawyer monitoring the baggage claim. Yes, I am angry.

Dental implants can hijack your immune system, scientists discover

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For tens of millions of people, dental implants have provided a way to replace missing teeth with a full set of fixed, functional teeth rather than removable dentures. However, an estimated 10% to 20% of implant patients eventually develop peri-implantitis, a serious infection that damages the surrounding jawbone.

Antibiotics frequently fail to control this condition, and scientists have struggled to understand why. Now, researchers believe they have uncovered the mechanism responsible.

A new study published in PNAS Nexus by scientists at the Rutgers School of Dental Medicine reveals that bacteria can gradually corrode dental implants, releasing microscopic titanium particles into nearby tissues. These particles interfere with the immune cells responsible for fighting infection, trapping them in a state of excessive inflammation that progressively destroys jawbone.

The researchers examined human tissue samples, studied human immune cells in the laboratory, and conducted experiments using genetically engineered mice. Their investigation identified a calcium channel in macrophages, the immune cells that engulf bacteria, that becomes activated by titanium particles. When this channel was disabled in mice, the disease was prevented.

This discovery provides the first credible drug target for a condition that affects as many as one in five dental implant recipients and costs healthcare systems worldwide more than $1 billion annually.

"For the first time, we show why all the antibiotic treatments that work around teeth do not work around implants," said Georgios Kotsakis, senior author of the study and assistant dean for clinical research at Rutgers School of Dental Medicine. "Now that we know the cause, we can start developing therapeutics."

Why Dental Implant Infections Resist Antibiotics

One of the biggest mysteries surrounding peri-implantitis has been its similarity to periodontitis, the gum disease that develops around natural teeth. Both conditions begin with the same types of bacteria found in the mouth, yet they respond very differently to treatment.

For natural teeth, antibiotics combined with routine professional cleaning can resolve the infection. Around dental implants, however, the same medications are successful in fewer than half of cases. Even when treatment is attempted, the underlying jawbone may continue to deteriorate.

For the past two decades, scientists have largely concentrated on the bacteria responsible for these infections. Researchers in Kotsakis' laboratory decided to investigate another possible contributor: the implant material itself.

Their work showed that bacteria accumulating on implant surfaces create acidic biofilms that slowly damage the titanium. Over time, this corrosion can release billions of microscopic metal particles, each smaller than a red blood cell.

Titanium particles can also be dislodged during routine dental cleaning. This is particularly concerning when instruments designed for natural teeth are used on implants, because they can scrape and damage the metal surface.

How Titanium Particles Hijack the Immune System

Once released into the gums, the tiny titanium fragments attract a bacterial toxin known as lipopolysaccharide, which coats their surfaces. This combination creates a deceptive target for the immune system, making the particles resemble unusually large bacteria that cannot be broken down.

Macrophages, the white blood cells responsible for engulfing and destroying harmful microorganisms, attempt to consume these particles. But because the cells cannot digest titanium, they become stuck in a prolonged state of excessive inflammation.

As a result, the macrophages produce large quantities of inflammatory signaling molecules, including interleukin-1 beta. This protein is also associated with inflammatory processes involved in rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's disease.

The resulting immune response gradually breaks down the bone supporting the dental implant. At the same time, the macrophages become less effective at fighting the bacteria that triggered the infection in the first place.

Laboratory experiments revealed just how severely their defenses were disrupted. Macrophages exposed to titanium particles engulfed fewer than half as many bacteria as immune cells that had not encountered the particles.

"These particles are little magnets that attract the bacterial toxin, and they hijack the immune system, preventing it from clearing bacteria," said Kotsakis. "You have a perfect storm that defies antibiotics."

Scientists Identify a Potential New Treatment Target

After uncovering how titanium particles disrupt immune defenses, the researchers investigated the molecular pathway responsible for the damaging reaction.

They traced the process to TRPC1, a calcium channel (a specialized, pore-forming protein structure within cell membranes) found in macrophages. This channel plays a central role in the inflammatory response triggered by titanium particles and bacterial toxins.

To test its importance, the team used genetically engineered mice that lacked TRPC1. When these animals were exposed to the same combination of titanium particles and bacteria, their immune systems responded much more effectively.

The mice developed dramatically smaller abscesses, produced fewer inflammatory signaling molecules, and regained the ability to clear bacteria. Disabling the channel prevented the destructive disease process observed when TRPC1 was present.

The results point toward a potentially new approach to treating peri-implantitis by targeting the immune system response rather than relying exclusively on antibiotics.

With funding from the National Institutes of Health, Kotsakis and his colleagues are now evaluating potential drugs that act on the same molecular pathway in human cells.

Why Proper Dental Implant Cleaning Matters

Although the research opens the door to future therapies, it also reinforces an important preventive measure for people who already have dental implants.

Regular professional cleaning remains the strongest known protection against peri-implantitis. However, the instruments and techniques used during those appointments can make a significant difference.

Until approximately a decade ago, many dentists routinely cleaned implants with the same metal scalers used to remove deposits from natural teeth. Research by the Rutgers team and other scientists has since demonstrated that these tools can damage and corrode titanium surfaces, potentially releasing additional particles and accelerating the disease.

Today, nonabrasive cleaning methods have become standard practice, helping protect implant surfaces while reducing the risk of complications.

By revealing how corroded titanium can interfere with immune defenses, the findings offer a new explanation for why some dental implant infections are so difficult to treat and identify a promising direction for developing more effective therapies.

La IA podría hacer realidad las entrevistas de trabajo imposibles

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Preguntas desconcertantes, evaluadores virtuales y ningún humano al otro lado de la pantalla. Lo que comenzó como un concurso tecnológico viral refleja la nueva frontera del empleo: algoritmos que ponen a prueba nuestras habilidades en tiempo real, transformando la forma en la que demostramos nuestro talento. Los procesos de selección entran en un duelo entre máquinas y candidatos aumentados.

Una entrevista de trabajo con cinco preguntas imposibles, un entrevistador que se burla de las respuestas equivocadas y un premio de 10.000 dólares para quien consiga la mejor puntuación... No hay ningún puesto vacante esperando, ni un responsable de recursos humanos que vaya a recibir al candidato. El entrevistador es un avatar de inteligencia artificial y la compañía que ha organizado esta competición, Synthesia, quiere demostrar hasta dónde puede llegar una tecnología capaz de mantener conversaciones y evaluar las respuestas de sus interlocutores.

La compañía británica, especializada avatares generados mediante IA, ha bautizado la competición como The Impossible Job Interview. Se celebra por Internet y utiliza acertijos inspirados en los procesos de selección de empresas como Google o Tesla. Los concursantes reciben una calificación y pueden compararla con las de otros participantes. Y el mejor resultado obtiene el premio económico.

La más significativa es McKinsey. Según The Wall Street Journal, la consultora está probando este otoño una entrevista de diez minutos en la que determinados candidatos universitarios de Norteamérica deben conversar con un cliente ficticio generado mediante IA.

Una de las situaciones plantea negociar con un alcalde sobre la construcción de un recinto deportivo.

El objetivo es observar cómo razonan los aspirantes, cómo afrontan objeciones y de qué manera reaccionan ante situaciones inesperadas. La diferencia respecto de Synthesia es que el ejercicio se parece al trabajo que realizarían si fueran contratados. Además, son entrevistadores humanos quienes valoran su actuación. En su página de Careers, McKinsey confirma que está experimentando con nuevas modalidades de entrevistas asistidas por IA, pero mantiene las decisiones de contratación en manos de personas.

Otra compañía, Mercor, desarrolló Monty, un entrevistador virtual con el que, según sus propios datos, se iniciaban unas 10.000 entrevistas diarias de aproximadamente 15 minutos. Su sistema atiende a profesionales de centenares de categorías, desde ingenieros informáticos hasta especialistas financieros.

La escala representa una ruptura con el modelo tradicional. Una entrevista ya no necesita consumir necesariamente 15 minutos de un seleccionador por cada aspirante.

También podría permitir escuchar a profesionales descartados anteriormente por filtros de currículos, aunque todavía habría que demostrar que esa mayor cobertura mejora las contrataciones.

La ironía del concurso de Synthesia es que recupera precisamente una modalidad de entrevista que Google terminó desacreditando.

Durante años, sus aspirantes debían enfrentarse a preguntas extravagantes, como calcular cuántas pelotas de golf caben en un avión.

En 2013, Laszlo Bock –entonces responsable de recursos humanos de Google, y uno de los gurús mundiales de empleo que defiende contratar por talento y aptitudes más que por títulos académicos– reconoció que esos acertijos no servían para predecir el desempeño laboral. La compañía había comprobado que resultaban mucho menos útiles que las entrevistas estructuradas sobre experiencias y comportamientos concretos.

El episodio plantea la cuestión complicada que lleva a pensar que automatizar una mala entrevista no la convierte en una buena herramienta de selección.

La investigación tampoco respalda la idea de que las entrevistas de trabajo sean inútiles. Lo importante es cómo se hacen. Un estudio publicado en Journal of Applied Psychology, que revisó numerosas investigaciones anteriores, comparó hasta qué punto los diferentes métodos de selección permiten prever si una persona desempeñará bien su trabajo. Los autores concluyeron que se había exagerado la eficacia de muchas pruebas, pero que las entrevistas estructuradas eran las que ofrecían mejores resultados.

Consisten en hacer a todos los candidatos preguntas similares, relacionadas directamente con el trabajo que tendrán que realizar, y valorar sus respuestas con los mismos criterios. Así se reduce el peso de las impresiones personales y las preferencias del entrevistador.

La inteligencia artificial podría mejorar precisamente esta preparación. Una investigación presentada el pasado mes de julio en la Academy of Management, utilizó IA para elaborar preguntas de entrevistas correspondientes a 138 puestos de una multinacional. Un grupo de 129 expertos consideró que las preguntas generadas con IA eran mejores que las convencionales utilizadas como comparación.

Esto no demuestra que la IA permita contratar a mejores profesionales, pero sugiere que puede ayudar a formular preguntas más adecuadas para evaluar sus capacidades. Falta comprobar si esa mejora se traduce en decisiones de contratación más acertadas.

La experiencia de HireVue aporta otro antecedente. Esta compañía, pionera en entrevistas automatizadas, abandonó el análisis facial en sus evaluaciones después de concluir que apenas añadía capacidad predictiva. La decisión, anunciada en 2021, ilustra cómo una tecnología aparentemente sofisticada puede resultar irrelevante para contratar mejor.

La investigación también está mostrando que cambiar de entrevistador modifica el comportamiento de los candidatos.

Y otro experimento publicado en Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication utilizó precisamente avatares de Synthesia. Participaron 134 estudiantes en entrevistas simuladas, bajo distintas condiciones de evaluación humana o mediante IA. Los investigadores observaron que quienes creían ser evaluados por una máquina experimentaban mayor incertidumbre y menor sensación de presencia social. También cambiaban aspectos de su forma de hablar.

No es una cuestión menor. Si el entrevistador altera el comportamiento del entrevistado, sus respuestas pueden reflejar tanto sus competencias como su reacción ante la tecnología.

Otro estudio publicado en Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, basado en un experimento con 203 participantes, encontró una menor disposición a solicitar empleo cuando el proceso incluía entrevistas mediante IA. El efecto fue especialmente apreciable en el escenario de una empresa editorial, frente al de una tecnológica. Se trataba de situaciones hipotéticas, por lo que no demuestra una caída equivalente de candidaturas reales.

No parece que en el futuro se dé una sustitución completa del entrevistador sino más bien de sus funciones.

Las máquinas pueden encargarse de determinadas entrevistas preliminares, simular situaciones laborales y ayudar a aplicar criterios homogéneos. Los profesionales de selección tendrían que concentrarse en interpretar resultados, contrastar evidencias y valorar cuestiones que requieren conocimiento de la organización.

Hay límites importantes. La Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology insiste en que las evaluaciones mediante IA deben demostrar que predicen el desempeño, producen puntuaciones consistentes y no generan discriminación injustificada.

En Europa, además, los sistemas utilizados para evaluar candidatos y tomar decisiones de contratación pueden clasificarse como de alto riesgo conforme a la Ley de IA. Las obligaciones específicas aplicables a esos sistemas comenzarán el 2 de diciembre de 2027.

Existe incluso una paradoja en el caso que ha despertado todo este interés. La política de uso aceptable de Synthesia prohibe utilizar los resultados de sus servicios como fundamento exclusivo o parcial de decisiones laborales, incluidas las de selección.

Por ahora, su “entrevista imposible” demuestra una tecnología, no una metodología validada para contratar.

Sin embargo, el experimento apunta hacia una innovación de mayor alcance: sustituir las conversaciones genéricas sobre lo que un candidato asegura saber hacer por simulaciones donde tenga que demostrarlo. La inteligencia artificial permitiría multiplicar estas pruebas y adaptarlas a cada profesión.

La entrevista de trabajo podría ganar utilidad precisamente cuando deje de parecerse a una entrevista convencional. El verdadero avance no consistirá en que una máquina haga las mismas preguntas de siempre, sino en averiguar si puede observar mejor cómo trabajaría una persona antes de contratarla.

Es posible que el gran problema de las entrevistas del futuro no sea quién formula las preguntas, sino quién las responde realmente. Mientras las empresas introducen inteligencia artificial para evaluar candidatos, los aspirantes empiezan a utilizar la misma tecnología para superar sus pruebas. El resultado es una competición que amenaza con cuestionar la credibilidad de todo el proceso.

El último informe del Institute of Student Employers (ISE), publicado hace poco más de una semana, ofrece una señal: el 92% de los empleadores encuestados afirma haber detectado o sospechado conductas indebidas durante la selección, frente al 78% de 2024. Entre las más habituales figura la utilización no autorizada de IA durante las entrevistas y las evaluaciones.

El estudio, realizado entre 155 empleadores, comprende más de 1,7 millones de candidaturas, y sus resultados no significan que el 92% de los candidatos engañe: indican la proporción de empresas que declara sospechas o incidentes.

Las consecuencias ya están apareciendo. El 77% de los empleadores está revisando o rediseñando sus procesos de contratación. Algunos introducen preguntas aleatorias, límites de tiempo y cuestiones personales difíciles de responder utilizando soluciones preparadas.

La preocupación se extiende a la identidad de los aspirantes. Greenhouse anunció en 2025 su solución Real Talent, que incorpora mecanismos para identificar candidaturas fraudulentas. Su colaboración con CLEAR permite verificar la identidad con un procedimiento digital.

La paradoja es evidente: cuanto más fácil resulte generar respuestas convincentes con IA, mayor importancia adquirirán las pruebas de autenticidad.

Podría producirse incluso un regreso parcial a las entrevistas presenciales, especialmente cuando las empresas necesiten comprobar determinadas competencias sin asistencia tecnológica. Pero también aparecería un problema de equidad: no todos los profesionales pueden desplazarse ni disponen de las mismas condiciones para superar pruebas supervisadas.

La solución más prometedora quizá consista en distinguir las evaluaciones donde debe responder exclusivamente una persona de aquellas donde interesa observar cómo utiliza la IA.

Un programador que trabaja habitualmente con asistentes inteligentes puede ser más productivo utilizándolos que prescindiendo de ellos. Prohibirlos en todas las pruebas podría ocultar precisamente una de sus competencias profesionales.

La entrevista del futuro tendrá que decidir si pretende evaluar al candidato sin herramientas o comprobar cómo trabaja realmente con ellas. Son dos capacidades diferentes y confundirlas puede llevar a contratar a la persona equivocada.

Breakthrough PTSD treatment achieves 68% remission rate in clinical trial

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new approach to treating post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has received FDA clearance, offering a personalized alternative for people who have not found sufficient relief through existing treatments. Developed by California-based Wave Neuroscience in collaboration with researchers at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, the technology uses brain activity measurements to guide targeted magnetic stimulation.

Early clinical trial findings have been encouraging. According to Wave Neuroscience, patients experienced a 52% reduction in PTSD symptoms, while 68% achieved remission. The full findings are still being prepared for public release, but researchers believe the technology could significantly expand treatment options for millions of people.

The need for more effective PTSD care is substantial. In the United States, approximately 17.5 veterans die by suicide each day, and about 7% of veterans receive a PTSD diagnosis annually.

PTSD can develop after traumatic experiences such as military combat, serious accidents, natural disasters, or violence. An estimated 6% of Americans will experience PTSD during their lifetime, with higher rates among women and veterans.

Why Current PTSD Treatments Are Not Enough

Since PTSD was formally recognized as a diagnosis in 1980, treatment has largely centered on psychotherapy and medications known as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), including sertraline and fluoxetine.

Although these approaches can help, many patients continue to experience symptoms despite receiving treatment.

A 2020 review of PTSD medications found that patients reported improvements ranging from 6.8 to 10.1 points on symptom assessments. However, remission rates reached only 10.9% at most.

Wave Neuroscience reported substantially stronger preliminary outcomes in its clinical trial. Erik Won, DO, the company's chief medical officer, described the results as a major advance over previously reported medication outcomes.

“In comparison to that study, our FDA trial reported a 52% reduction in symptoms, so more than a four-fold increase in efficacy,” Won said. “And more importantly, we saw 68% remission in our clinical trial. So, it’s really a quantum change, an inflection point in what we can offer to patients. From a clinical care perspective, that’s really meaningful.”

These findings are promising, although the results come from different studies and should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of treatment effectiveness.

The newly cleared system uses Magnetic e-Resonance Therapy (MeRT), a technology that combines measurements of brain activity with targeted magnetic stimulation.

First, an electroencephalogram (EEG) records the brain's electrical signals. This information helps guide a personalized treatment plan using transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS.

During TMS, a coil positioned against the scalp delivers magnetic pulses that stimulate brain activity. The technique has already been used for other psychiatric conditions, including treatment-resistant depression.

What distinguishes MeRT is its use of an AI-assisted algorithm to customize stimulation protocols based on each patient's brain activity rather than relying on a standardized approach.

“Transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, was previously FDA cleared for treatment-resistant depression,” Won said. “By adding an augmented AI derived multivariate algorithm, we were able to personalize protocols that received FDA clearance for post-traumatic stress disorder, which should open up market access to about 13 million people who may be struggling with PTSD.”

Dr. Kenneth S. Ramos, a physician-scientist at Texas A&M Health with extensive experience in precision medicine, helped guide the research.

His work focuses on adapting medical care to the characteristics of individual patients instead of assuming that everyone will respond to the same treatment.

“Most clinical interventions for the treatment of PTSD do not work very well,” Ramos said. “So, this is a huge area where identifying interventions that could hopefully add quality of life to the people impacted by this disorder would be tremendously impactful.”

Wave Neuroscience CEO Fred Walke said Ramos was a natural fit for the project because of his expertise in personalized medicine.

Conventional TMS generally follows treatment protocols that are less individualized. MeRT builds on the same underlying technology but adjusts treatment according to the patient's specific brain activity.

Ramos emphasized that even small biological differences between people can have major implications for medical care. Humans are approximately 99.9% genetically alike, yet the remaining 0.1% contributes to differences that can influence how individuals respond to treatment.

By accounting for these differences and tailoring stimulation to each person's brain, researchers observed substantial reductions in PTSD symptom severity.

Ramos illustrated the distinction with an analogy involving a leaking roof.

He likened conventional TMS to covering a leak with a tarp to manage its effects. MeRT, in contrast, aims to provide a more targeted solution, comparable to identifying and replacing the damaged shingles responsible for the problem.

The broader goal is to move beyond treating patients with standardized protocols and toward interventions guided by measurable biological information.

From Breakthrough Designation to FDA Clearance

The regulatory process reached an important milestone in 2024, when MeRT received an FDA Breakthrough Device designation.

Dr. Rick Silva, executive director of clinical trials at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, explained that the designation helps promising technologies move through regulatory review more efficiently.

It does not eliminate scientific or medical requirements. Instead, it can streamline communication and reduce administrative delays while preserving the standards needed for FDA clearance.

Silva said the progress was particularly meaningful because the project began during the COVID-19 pandemic and advanced through the regulatory process unusually quickly.

According to Silva, medical device development and clinical evaluation can often take around 10 years before clearance is obtained. This project completed the relevant process in just over two years.

He credited the experience and dedication of the Wave Neuroscience team with helping make that accelerated progress possible.

“I’ve been involved in probably close to 1,000 projects in academic medical centers over the years at varying degrees of depth and detail,” Silva said. “Patients are why I do what I do. It’s hard, and the failure rate is high, and it’s a lot of work. But when you get to a point where something is going to benefit patients, it’s very professionally gratifying and personally gratifying in many ways as well.”

Dr. Israel Liberzon, distinguished professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at the Texas A&M Naresh K. Vashisht College of Medicine, served as the study's lead psychiatrist and PTSD specialist.

He believes the clearance represents an important expansion of the available treatments for PTSD.

According to Liberzon, MeRT will become the only FDA-cleared nonmedication treatment specifically for PTSD once it reaches patients.

“Right now, the FDA has approved only two medications for PTSD,” Liberzon said. “It doesn’t mean that only two of them are used, but both are SSRIs. This is the first time that in addition to the medication, we have a non-medication option. There is psychotherapy that people use for PTSD, but psychotherapies require a lot of effort. People must participate actively, and many drop out because it’s too anxiety provoking. So now we have a unique alternative.”

In the clinical trial, participants received five treatment sessions each week over a period of five to six weeks.

Researchers reported that the improvements appeared to persist after the treatment sessions ended, suggesting the benefits may extend beyond the active therapy period.

For patients who struggle with medication side effects or find psychotherapy difficult to complete, a treatment based on personalized brain stimulation could provide another option.

“People living with PTSD have been left with trial-and-error treatment, medications and their side effects, and limited outcomes—even when treated by the most dedicated and experienced physicians,” Won said. “Wave’s FDA clearance signals a fundamentally different future: one in which PTSD can be objectively evaluated, precisely treated and meaningfully healed.”

Won also believes the technology could eventually be adapted to other psychiatric conditions, including depression and anxiety.

Could Personalized Brain Therapy Transform Mental Healthcare?

Dr. Leslie S. Prichep, chief scientific officer at Wave Neuroscience, sees the FDA clearance as an important step toward treatment decisions guided by objective measurements of brain function.

“The FDA clearance of the MeRT System reflects decades of scientific research using electrophysiological biomarkers in identifying abnormal brain activity profiles and guiding personalized intervention strategies,” Prichep said. “We believe this represents an important advancement toward a more objective, data-informed model of mental healthcare.”

Walke believes that giving patients clearer information about their brain activity could also help change public attitudes toward mental illness.

He compared the idea to treating a broken bone with a cast. By making mental health conditions easier to understand through measurable physical information, the company hopes to reduce stigma and help patients better understand their experiences.

“One of the goals of the company is to create a platform for mental healthcare that uses objective imagery to help patients understand why they’re experiencing life the way that they are,” Walke said. “That insight, compounded with proven technologies, promises incredibly high efficacy when personalized.”

An important next step will be ensuring that patients can actually obtain the treatment. Wave Neuroscience and Texas A&M Health are working with other stakeholders to expand availability and make the therapy more affordable through insurance coverage.

“Our hope is that this research and technology can be a catalyst for more widespread development and innovation of more effective, personalized and precision-guided treatments,” Won said. “This is proof positive that population-scale personalized medicine is not just an aspirational goal but is an achievable reality today.”

The project combines years of work across medicine, neuroscience, clinical research, and technology, all directed toward improving the lives of people affected by PTSD.

For Ramos, the opportunity to help veterans carries particular significance because of Texas A&M's longstanding involvement in military and veteran initiatives.

“Texas A&M has a very rich history of military and veteran priorities,” Ramos said. “It’s a no-brainer that Texas A&M faculty would be interested in and supportive of an initiative that hopefully would bring positive solutions to a big problem. And anything we could do to help alleviate that problem is going to go a long way towards making a difference in the individual’s life as well as the people who are close to them. I feel very fortunate that we at Texas A&M were meaningfully involved in this project.”

Thousands of protesters took to the streets in Venezuela on Thursday afternoon to demand presidential elections and call for the return of exiled Nobel Prize-winning opposition leader María Corina Machado.

In the capital, Caracas, protesters gathered in Plaza Venezuela and called for presidential elections to be scheduled as early as 2027, as well as the release of all political prisoners in the country.

“We will have presidential elections very soon with María Corina as president,” said former congressman Piero Maroun, who attended the protests, according to independent local news organization Efecto Cocuyo. The Machado-led activist group Vente Venezuela spearheaded the protests, which led to the closure of some streets in Caracas. Others also staged protests in the states of Nueva Esparta, Táchira, and Zulia.

The ruling party that acting President Delcy Rodríguez belongs to, the United Socialist Party of Venezuela, also staged a gathering near the opposition demonstrators in Caracas, though for a women’s march for Breast Cancer Awareness Month.

Protesting has become common for disgruntled Venezuelans since the forced end of Nicolás Maduro’s presidency. Maduro and his predecessor Hugo Chávez heavily cracked down on political dissent. But since Maduro’s removal in January by U.S. forces, protesters have taken to the streets “with increasing frequency,” according to the international civil society alliance CIVICUS, citing data from the Venezuelan Observatory of Social Conflict, which recorded 1,101 protests during just the month of September, or about 37 demonstrations per day. These protests, however, have not necessarily been large.

Until this week. The Thursday protests, which Reuters described as “the first nationwide opposition protest in ​nearly two years,” coincided with the third anniversary of the opposition’s presidential primary elections, in which Machado emerged victorious.

Machado is still the opposition’s most prominent figurehead in the transition toward a democratic government: in an AtlasIntel survey conducted for Bloomberg News, Machado received 52% support, compared with 19% for Rodríguez. But Machado has her work cut out for her.

The protests also happened as Rodríguez met with Dinorah Figuera, the opposition representative backed by the U.S. State Department in negotiations for a political transition, inside the Venezuelan presidential palace. The three rounds of U.S.-backed talks between the Venezuelan government and Figuera’s faction have yet to yield an agreement on elections.

In a statement reported by El País after the meeting, Rodríguez appeared to reference the opposition protests, saying in Spanish: “There are still very, very small groups that resist and believe that trying to set the country ablaze and block streets is the way for Venezuela to move forward. I recognize the good Venezuelans who, through this dialogue, are promoting necessary changes.”

Since arresting Maduro in an extraordinary military operation in January, the U.S. has been working with an interim government led by Rodríguez, Maduro’s former deputy. Rodríguez and U.S. President Donald Trump both have said elections will eventually be held in the country, though Trump told TIME in late September: “It’s so raw now, you know that I just think it’s too soon.”

The post-Maduro government opened talks in August with the opposition faction led by Figuera, the president of Venezuela’s 2015 National Assembly, to help the country achieve a democratic transition. But the changes are expected to take time.

Domestic opposition and international observers have long slammed Maduro’s 2018 and 2024 re-elections for lacking transparency and fairness, disputing the results and arguing that Machado’s proxy, Edmundo González Urrutia, actually won in the latter. This raised questions about the independence of the Supreme Court and Venezuela’s National Electoral Council, which have been packed with loyalists of the regime.

For the transition, the establishment and the opposition are trying to iron out details of reforming the court and the council. Even if this happens, experts told Reuters that authorities will still need to update voter rolls and ensure that parties can reach a consensus on how subsequent elections would work.

The twin earthquakes that struck Venezuela in June and caused extensive damage also complicated efforts toward stability in the country.

But the U.S. has also set certain prerequisites that could affect how soon elections could happen. President Trump and Secretary of State Marco Rubio outlined that they seek stabilization, economic recovery, and national reconciliation in Venezuela, on top of a political transition. Axios reported that it’s a multi-step process: change the court makeup, reform the electoral commission, and then announce an election date. Rubio also announced other requirements, including a truly free press.

“What would threaten an enduring republic in Venezuela would be that someone is elected and they inherit a complete catastrophe, economic and social, and then the whole project is endangered because now anything can happen,” Rubio said on Sept. 23, following Trump’s meeting with Rodríguez in New York. “We want to do two things at the same time.”

Critics argue that the Trump Administration’s position sidelines the opposition faction led by Machado and instead prioritizes oil agreements and economic stabilization over Venezuela’s democratic transition. In August, Trump announced that Washington agreed to a deal with Caracas, saying it would give the U.S. majority control over more than 65 billion barrels or about a fifth of Venezuela’s oil reserves.

Dhayana Fernández-Matos, a professor of political science at the Federal University of Pelotas in Brazil, told Spain’s broadcaster RTVE last month that an election in Venezuela is not a priority for Trump, as his “primary interest lies in oil and the geopolitical power of controlling the world’s largest proven crude oil reserves.”

Split Tunneling Is Your VPN’s Best Feature. Here’s How to Make the Most of It

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

While you may well be familiar with virtual private network apps, you might not have come across one of the best features available inside these privacy protectors: a feature called split tunneling.

In simple terms, split tunneling lets you choose which apps or sites use the protection of a VPN and which don't. While VPNs are worth using for extra security and privacy online—foiling trackers, spoofing your location, and stopping your internet provider from seeing your browsing—they do by their nature come with some compromises in speed.

If, for example, you want to get the fastest possible speeds with your online games, you might want to leave your games outside of the VPN's remit. Likewise, since VPNs typically obfuscate your actual location, if you want to make sure you can always check movie showtimes in your local area, you can use split tunneling to keep those ticketing apps outside your VPN. Putting a VPN in place can also interfere with access to other devices on your local network, like smart home gadgets and printers.

For all these reasons, you might want the extra control that split tunneling gives you, and it's available in a lot of VPN packages. Here's what you need to know about it.

VPNs work by offering an encrypted bridge for data going to and coming from the web. This bridge means your internet provider, law enforcement agencies, and people connected to the same Wi-Fi can't see what you're doing online. Split tunneling lets you route some online traffic away from that bridge, without you having to constantly turn your VPN on and off.

App-based split tunneling is the most common type you'll come across, as it's the most straightforward and easiest to understand. Some of your apps use the VPN, and some don't. So maybe you want your banking app connected to a VPN for maximum protection, but you're less concerned about a weather app, and you don't want to see forecasts from whatever location the VPN is spoofing.

Depending on the VPN, you might also see URL-based split tunneling. This lets you set the rules based on domain names. It's the same idea as with apps: For websites that need fast internet access and where privacy is less important, there's no VPN. So you can keep the VPN turned on for a search engine page where you're typing information you want protected, but off for a local news site.

There are other types of VPN tunneling too, including route-based tunneling (based on specific IP addresses on the internet) and policy-based tunneling (essentially a set of overarching rules), but these are less common in consumer VPN software, and you're unlikely to come across them unless you're somehow managing other users.

It's not a complicated technology to understand, and it can be genuinely useful—but it's also a feature that users tend not to notice or think about when they're enabling and disabling VPNs on their devices.

The exact steps needed to enable split tunneling will vary depending on the VPN software you're using, but they shouldn't be too difficult to find. Most, but not all, VPN programs now offer some kind of split tunneling feature. If you're using a popular app, then you should find the option somewhere, especially if you're on a paid tier.

To give you some idea how it works: In ProtonVPN for Windows, on the main console there's a split tunneling link on the right, together with other features like a kill switch and port forwarding. Click Split tunneling, and once it's enabled, you can start picking your apps. You can either select a list of apps to be included by the VPN (with everything else excluded) or a list of apps to be excluded (with everything else included).

For NordVPN on Windows, you can access the feature by clicking the cog icon in the bottom left of the main interface, then choosing Split tunneling and enabling the Split tunneling toggle switch. Again, you'll be able to choose which apps are included in the VPN protections and which ones aren't.

On mobile, it's a similar process: Delve into the settings for whatever VPN app you're using, wherever those settings might be. Note, though, that iPhones don't allow as much freedom for VPNs here as Android devices and desktop platforms: You'll typically be limited to selecting websites and IP addresses to exclude, rather than apps.

Most of the time, it's going to be a set-it-and-forget-it type routine, and you're not going to need to keep diving back into the options screen to change around the apps that have VPN access—though you can if you want. It means VPN peace of mind where you need it, without the inconvenience of having a VPN applied where it's not useful.
