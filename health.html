Female hospital doctors linked to better outcomes for patients, study says

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Dr. Angela Jerath, the study’s lead author and an ICES scientist, says the ultimate goal of the work is to improve patient outcomes.Arlyn McAdorey/The Globe and Mail

Patients who are cared for by female physicians in hospital are less likely to die or to be readmitted compared with those treated by male physicians, says a newly released study co-authored by two Canadian researchers.

The findings were released on Friday and published in the October edition of JAMA Network Open, a peer-reviewed journal published by the American Medical Association.

The research was conducted by ICES, or the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences, a not-for-profit Ontario research and analytics institute that uses population-based health information for insights on various health care issues.

The ultimate goal of the work is to improve patient outcomes, said Angela Jerath, the study’s lead author and an ICES scientist.

The study analyzed ICES data on 958,150 adults who were hospitalized in the province for one of seven common medical diagnoses, including pneumonia, between Jan. 1, 2008, and Dec. 31, 2022.

It found that patients who were cared for by female physicians had a 30-day mortality rate of 12.3 per cent compared with 13.7 per cent for those cared for by male physicians.

Greater exposure to female physician care was associated with a 7-per-cent drop in 30-day mortality and the benefit persisted at 90 days and a year.

Additionally, it found a slightly lower hospital readmission rate of 18.6 per cent for those seen by female physicians compared with 18.9 per cent for those seen by male physicians.

Dr. Jerath said a possible explanation for the differences could be that female physicians may engage in more patient-centred communication and that they spend more time with their patients.

She also said female physicians may be more likely to order treatments for their patients that are directed by guidelines, and to consult their colleagues and share their decision-making.

The goal of the study is not to favour one group of physicians over another, but to help identify practices and behaviours to enrich patient care, said Dr. Jerath, who is also an associate professor in the department of anesthesiology and pain medicine at the University of Toronto’s Temerty Faculty of Medicine.

Dr. Jerath’s co-author, Christopher Wallis, said researching this topic as a male physician gave him an opportunity to be introspective on how he approaches his own surgical practice.

“This is about decision-making,” said Dr. Wallis, an assistant professor in the department of surgery at the University of Toronto.

“This is about how you interact with other human beings. None of this is finite or fixed in stone. We can all learn from each other.”

The findings contribute to a growing body of research on the relationship between physician sex and patient outcomes, Dr. Jerath said.

An additional factor affecting female physicians, which begins early in their careers and continues, is that there are gaps between earnings compared with male counterparts, she said.

Dr. Wallis warned that pay gaps should not be characterized on the basis that women often take on additional duties at home and therefore may work fewer hours in a professional capacity.

He said while this could be a factor, a number of analyses show on a per-hour worked basis, female physicians do not make as much.

“Even when you account in surgery for the number of hours you worked, the number of patients you’ve seen, women still get paid less,” he said.

Research also shows that physicians overall are putting in fewer hours.

In March, 2024, a study published in the Canadian Medical Association Journal found the decline was driven mostly by reductions among married male physicians rather than the fact there are more women in the profession.

Looking to the future, Dr. Jerath said it is important to ask questions about why female physicians may have a different approach and how these insights could be used to improve patient outcomes.

“That is what we are here for,” she said. “We don’t go to med school for any other reason. We all did it for a desire to serve.”

Russia, Central African Republic agree to expand cooperation in fight against epidemics

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

NAIROBI, October 30. /TASS/. Russia and the Central African Republic (CAR) have signed two memorandums on healthcare cooperation, including on prevention of infectious diseases and response to epidemiological threats on the sidelines of the third Russia-Africa summit, the Russian embassy in the CAR said on Telegram.

"The agreements create a solid foundation for coordinating efforts to strengthen health systems, share experiences, and implement joint prevention and response measures to epidemiological threats," the embassy said. "The cooperation will be carried out taking into account the national legislation of both countries."

The first document was signed by Russian Health Minister Mikhail Murashko and CAR Minister of Health and Population Pierre Somse. The Memorandum provides for the expansion of public health cooperation, including training of medical personnel, introduction of modern technologies and joint scientific research. Special attention is to be paid to the prevention of diseases, maternal and child health, control over the circulation of medicines and digitalization of healthcare.

The second document was signed by Russian sanitary watchdog Rospotrebnadzor head Anna Popova and Somse. The memorandum is aimed at ensuring the sanitary and epidemiological well-being of the people, exchanging experience, and coordinating efforts to prevent and counter epidemiological threats.

Russia and the CAR also signed an intergovernmental agreement on the mutual recognition of education, qualifications and academic degrees. The document is intended to simplify the recognition of diplomas, expand opportunities for continuing education and professional activity of people, as well as promote the development of scientific cooperation. It was signed by Russia’s Deputy Minister of Science and Higher Education Konstantin Mogilevsky, and CAR Minister of Higher Education, Scientific Research and Technological Innovation Jean-Laurent Syssa-Magale.

In the field of justice, an international document was signed by Russia’s Justice Minister Konstantin Chuichenko and CAR’s State Minister of Justice Arnaud Djoubaye Abazene. According to the Russian embassy, it lays the foundation for further development of legal cooperation between the two countries.

Earlier, on the sidelines of the summit, the parties also signed an agreement on the foundations of relations between Russia and the Central African Republic, as well as a memorandum of understanding in the field of transport and transit. The latter provides for the development of land and air, passenger and cargo transportation, including through international transport corridors.

US hospitals and nursing homes are breeding grounds for deadly superbugs – CDC

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

More than four in five US deaths linked to drug-resistant infections involve pathogens commonly associated with hospitals and nursing homes, according to a new report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The agency’s Antimicrobial Resistance Threats Report released this week draws on data from 2023 and estimates that 19 types of drug-resistant bacteria and fungi cause 9.7 million infections and 50,800 deaths annually in the US. Seven bacterial threats commonly associated with hospitals and nursing homes accounted for more than 42,000 of those deaths, along with nearly $12 billion in healthcare costs.

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when germs develop the ability to withstand drugs designed to kill them, making infections difficult or impossible to treat. Elderly people, those with weakened immune systems, and patients undergoing invasive medical procedures are particularly vulnerable.

Among the pathogens classified as urgent threats is Candida auris, a potentially deadly fungus that can resist multiple medications and survive on surfaces in healthcare facilities. It primarily affects seriously ill patients and can cause dangerous bloodstream infections.

A separate CDC study published in July found that reported cases of Candida auris more than doubled from 2022-2024, rising from 2,882 to 6,197. Most cases for which the type of medical facility was identified were detected in hospitals, including those that provide long-term treatment for seriously ill patients.

The CDC noted that many healthcare-associated infections are preventable, but much of the progress in combating drug resistance was lost during the Covid-19 pandemic, when staffing shortages, overcrowding, and disruptions to infection-control practices contributed to a rise in infections.

The warning comes as US hospitals face mounting financial and staffing pressures. A Harvard-led study published earlier this month found that 432 hospitals closed nationwide from 2010-2025, resulting in a net loss of 216 facilities and more than 24,000 beds.

Nursing homes are also struggling with bed and staffing shortages. An investigation in September by KFF Health News found that declining capacity forced some patients to remain hospitalized longer because suitable care facilities were unavailable.

Quebec plans to end twice-a-year time changes

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Quebec Premier says province hasn’t yet decided whether to keep daylight time or standard time year-round.Carlos Osorio/The Globe and Mail

Quebec Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon says his government intends to abolish the switch between daylight and standard time.

He says the province hasn’t yet decided whether to keep daylight time or standard time year-round, but wrote on social media on Wednesday that Quebeckers will most likely set their clocks back one hour for the final time this weekend.

He said studies have found the time change is counterproductive to health and is hard for children to adjust to.

St-Pierre Plamondon said he plans to consult with experts during a series of hearings in the coming months before making a final decision on whether to stay on permanent summer or winter time. He said there will be a short parliamentary commission on the topic.

“Dear parents who have to spend several weeks managing your children’s reaction to this time change, this is likely the last year we’ll be imposing such unnecessary constraints on ourselves. We invite the public to follow our work closely,” he wrote on social media.

Year-round daylight time has a dark side. Here’s how Canada can prepare for it

The previous Coalition Avenir Québec held an online consultation in 2024 that found 91 per cent of the approximately 214,000 respondents said they wanted to end the time change, and 72 per cent wanted to keep daylight time year-round.

But then-justice minister Simon Jolin-Barrette noted that the consultation had highlighted several issues, including the opinion of sleep experts and alignment with neighbouring jurisdictions.

He said in 2025 that further deliberation and consultation were necessary.

Justice Minister Marie-Claude Sarrazin said the government would follow all the necessary steps to make the change run smoothly.

“We wanted to announce our intentions at the start of the time change,” she said. “For the rest, we’ll prepare our work plans and we’ll consult with people so that things are done in an orderly way, including with our neighbours.”

St-Pierre Plamondon’s minority government could have the support of at least one opposition party to help pass any necessary legislation. Conservative Leader Éric Duhaime said he agreed with stopping the time change, but stressed the importance of collaborating with Ontario.

“I lived in the west when Saskatchewan decided to end the time change and the neighbouring provinces didn’t, and it caused all sorts of problems,” he said Wednesday in Quebec City.

“You know that the economies of Quebec and Ontario are interconnected.”

Manitoba to stick with daylight time year-round

By keeping daylight time year-round, Quebeckers would enjoy a later sunset during the winter. But this would mean the sun would rise an hour later during the short winter days, which could have negative consequences, according to several experts.

The analysis published in the Canadian Medical Association Journal a few weeks ago examined multiple studies and concluded that health evidence favours moving to permanent standard time – not daylight time – in part because it better aligns with the body’s rhythm.

If Quebec were to move forward with abolishing the time change, it would follow in the footsteps of several Canadian provinces. Those include British Columbia, Alberta, and Manitoba, which ended the practice this year.

These three provinces have chosen to keep daylight time year-round, as has the Northwest Territories.

In Ontario, certain regions in the northwestern part of the province will also maintain daylight time year-round to align with Manitoba.

In 2020, Ontario passed a law to make daylight time permanent, but it has not yet taken effect. The province noted that co-ordination with its neighbours, such as Quebec and New York, was essential.

Saskatchewan has not changed its time since 1966, while the Yukon ended the practice in 2020.

An Alberta Health Services sign outside the Rockyview General Hospital in Calgary.Jeff McIntosh/The Canadian Press

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith on Tuesday rejected renewed demands for a public inquiry into alleged procurement irregularities in the province’s health care system after The Globe and Mail reported that the RCMP are investigating potential fraud, breach of trust and money laundering in connection with the controversy.

Ms. Smith, speaking in the legislature during its first sitting since May, said a 2025 review conducted by Raymond Wyant, a retired judge from Manitoba, adequately probed the allegations, which were lodged by Alberta Health Services former chief executive Athana Mentzelopoulos earlier that year.

The Premier has leaned on Mr. Wyant’s report when defending her government against allegations of wrongdoing tied to the procurement affair since it was released in October, 2025.

“The best way to clear the air was to have a judge-led inquiry, which is what we did,” Ms. Smith told the legislature during Question Period on Tuesday. “We gave him literally millions of pages of documents.”

Mr. Wyant did not have the power to subpoena witnesses or hear testimony under oath. Some people declined to be interviewed or to answer certain questions, and he could not compel the production of records.

RCMP probe potential fraud, money laundering in Alberta health care procurement controversy

Naheed Nenshi, Alberta’s New Democratic Party Leader, argued the new information reported in The Globe adds to the need for a public inquiry.

“The net is tightening around this Premier and her government,” he said.

The RCMP confirmed in early 2025 that it was investigating the province’s health care procurement practices. The Globe’s report on Tuesday, based on an Oct. 15 letter filed in court, revealed for the first time the potential crimes police are pursuing.

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith speaks to media in Calgary, on Oct. 21.Jeff McIntosh/The Canadian Press

The procurement affair has dogged Ms. Smith since early 2025, after the government fired Ms. Mentzelopoulos as the head of the provincial health authority. The former executive alleges in court documents that the government fired her in part to shut down an investigation she ordered into inflated contracts and potential conflicts of interest in the health care system. The government, meanwhile, alleges it dismissed Ms. Mentzelopoulos because she failed at executing its strategy.

None of the allegations have been tested in court.

In September, Justice Michael Lema at the Court of King’s Bench of Alberta determined Ms. Mentzelopoulos kept records that were privileged or confidential after being fired. He ruled that she must return or destroy those records, and pay legal costs for the government and the health agency.

As part of their investigation, the RCMP seized documents from Ms. Mentzelopoulos, according to the October letter filed in court. Lawyers responsible for prosecuting potential charges stemming from the RCMP investigation want the Court of King’s Bench to provide direction around potential privileged and confidential records, the document says.

On Tuesday, Alberta Justice Minister Mickey Amery said the government will co-operate with the RCMP, but would not say whether the province would challenge the Mounties’ access to some of the same records the court ordered Ms. Mentzelopoulos to return or destroy.

“We argued that certain privileged and confidential documents should remain privileged and confidential, which is a legal right of every party involved in a civil action,” he told reporters at an unrelated press conference. “We don’t know what those documents are that the RCMP are relying on, nor can I speculate about that because we haven’t been made aware of what those documents are that they’re going to seek to rely on.

“If and when that issue arises, then we’ll have an answer for you.”

He added: “We will fully co-operate with the RCMP investigation and we’ll provide everything that we possibly can to the RCMP in supporting their investigation.”

Tom Andreopoulos, a Crown prosecutor with the Complex Prosecutions Bureau at Ontario’s Ministry of the Attorney-General, sent the Oct. 15 letter to lawyers involved in the civil dispute related to Ms. Mentzelopoulos.

“Our office is responsible for the provision of advisory support and the prosecution of any charges that may arise from the ongoing Royal Canadian Mounted Police criminal investigation into fraud, breach of trust, and money laundering arising out of procurement processes and practices at Alberta Health and Alberta Health Services,” Mr. Andreopoulos says in his letter.

The letter does not elaborate on the alleged potential offences or who is the subject of the investigation. Further, it does not specify why Ontario Crown prosecutors are involved in the RCMP’s probe in Alberta. (Provincial justice departments sometimes appoint prosecutors from other provinces when dealing with politically sensitive cases in which a potential conflict of interest may arise.)

One of Ms. Mentzelopoulos’ lawyers submitted the letter as part of a court filing disputing the costs requested by the government and Alberta Health Services in the wake of Justice Lema’s decision on privileged and confidential records.

Ms. Smith’s government had appointed Mr. Wyant, the retired judge, to examine Ms. Mentzelopoulos’ allegations. He determined Alberta Health Services did not follow its own procedure when it signed a $70-million deal for children’s medication with MHCare Medical Corp. in 2022 and that the government health ministry failed to follow its own rules regarding a private Edmonton surgical centre. MHCare denies any wrongdoing.

Mr. Wyant stressed his investigation and conclusions did not amount to clearing the government of wrongdoing.

“When I find that there was no wrongful interference by any government official in the matters concerning this report, that only means that I found no evidence of such, but I am not in a position to make a final and absolute determination,” Mr. Wyant stated.

Nearly 89% of Ontarians now have primary care, province says

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Ontario is making progress in connecting its residents with primary care services, with more than half a million additional people attached to a health care setting since January, 2025, the province reported on Monday.

The numbers were released two years after the provincial government appointed former federal health minister Jane Philpott to lead a drive to improve access to family doctors and other primary care providers.

There has been a net increase of 511,000 people connected to primary care since January, 2025, when the government launched its Primary Care Action Plan.

A total of 14,259,000 Ontarians, or 88.7 per cent of the population, now have a primary care provider, the province reported.

Officials wouldn’t confirm how many people are still without a doctor, but a calculation from the latest numbers shows that 1.8 million Ontarians remain without a health care provider.

Ontario touts drop in number of residents lacking primary-care providers

The government said it has cleared 470,000 names from the HealthCare Connect list, although there are still more than 24,000 registrants who have waited more than six months for a referral.

“I am incredibly proud of the progress we’ve made,” Ontario Health Minister Sylvia Jones said at a press conference.

Dr. Philpott said the province is on pace to meet its goal to connect every Ontarian to primary care by 2029.

Adil Shamji, health critic for the Ontario Liberals and a physician, cautioned that the improved numbers don’t reflect factors such as retiring doctors, population increase or the difficulties in retaining health care professionals.

“It’s great to finally see some action getting taken on this and some attention being paid to this issue, but a lot of the announcements today, I just don’t find credible,” he said.

Dr. Shamji said Ministry of Health data indicate that more than 2,100 Ontario family doctors with three million patients are over 60, and could be expected to retire in coming years.

Ontario to create electronic medical record system to streamline patient data sharing

The Ontario Medical Association released the results of an online survey last December, showing that half of the province’s doctors are planning to retire or considering that option in the next five years.

“This government prioritizes health care recruitment, but not health care worker retention, and so I am very concerned that this government will not be able to deliver on the promises that it is making,” Dr. Shamji said.

Dr. Philpott said the new 88.7-per-cent access rate cannot be compared to metrics from the Canadian Institute for Health Information, which tracks countrywide medical data. The institute’s numbers show that in 2024, Ontario was the province with the highest proportion of adults with a primary care provider, at 88.5 per cent.

The institute’s number is based on a survey sample, Dr. Philpott said, whereas Ontario’s Ministry of Health has developed a more accurate methodology in the last two years. “We can actually tell almost down to a person who is and isn’t connected to ongoing primary care rather than a survey sample,” she said.

Most of Ontario’s newly attached patients were put in touch with a family doctor, though in a minority of cases, the connection could have been made with a community health centre, an Indigenous primary health care organization, or a clinic led by nurse practitioners

When the province first launched the Primary Care Action Plan in January, 2025, it said it intended to connect approximately two million more people to primary care.

The province historically already had among the highest attachment rates in the country.

In Quebec, former premier François Legault pledged in 2018 that every Quebecker would have a family doctor. In British Columbia, outgoing Premier David Eby, whose NDP government was defeated on Saturday, promised that every resident who wanted a family doctor or nurse practitioner would be connected by the end of 2025.

Neither premier was successful: Primary care access in both provinces remained under 80 per cent, as per the most recent estimates available.

Canadian Blood Services says longtime plasma donor’s death not connected to donation

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A technician handles plasma bags at the production and distribution centre for Canadian Blood Services in Brampton, Ont., on Friday.Arlyn McAdorey/The Globe and Mail

Canadian Blood Services says a plasma donor recently died a day after making a donation, but the charity believes the death was not connected to the donation itself.

Blood collectors such as CBS are required under federal regulations to report to Health Canada if a donor has died within 72 hours of making a donation. The initial report must be made within 24 hours of the collector learning about the death.

It is the first time CBS has reported such an incident since at least 2018, the earliest that records are available.

The safety and oversight of plasma donation has come under scrutiny in the past year after the deaths of two donors who gave plasma at Grifols, a Spanish pharmaceutical company that operates collection facilities in Canada through a partnership with CBS. Health Canada found operational deficiencies at multiple Grifols locations and suspended the licence of one site in September. The company temporarily paused operations in Canada, but announced last week it was going to gradually reopen.

Plasma is a golden-coloured fluid found in blood that is used as a key ingredient of some medicines, such as immunoglobulin.

In depth: What went wrong with Canada’s paid-plasma experiment

The latest incident involved someone who died the day after giving plasma at a CBS facility.

CBS said it is withholding nearly all the details of the incident to protect the donor’s privacy. The charity, which runs the blood system everywhere in Canada but Quebec, would not provide any details about the donor, where the incident happened or precisely when it occurred, preferring to say it happened “earlier in the fall.”

CBS chief executive officer Graham Sher told The Globe and Mail on Friday that a long-time donor and volunteer died a day after making a plasma donation. Dr. Sher said CBS only found out three weeks later, when the donor’s family told the staff of a CBS clinic.

Dr. Sher said CBS quickly reported the death to Health Canada. He said CBS then reviewed the records of the donor and the donation. He said that nothing unusual happened during the donation and the donor appeared fine when they left. He said the donor’s family told CBS that doctors had described the death the following day as being due to natural causes.

“I just want to put it this way: Donors are normal people with normal lives, and donors will have things that happen to them in their lives: motor vehicle accidents, illnesses, falls, trips,” Dr. Sher said in an interview. “Anything can happen to a donor. The same is going to happen to anyone else. And this, an event of a natural cause death, just happened to be temporally related to a previous donation.”

Opinion: Plasma collection in Canada is a bloody mess that needs fixing

Health Canada said it received notice of the death from CBS on Sept. 25, which would suggest the death occurred in early September.

Health Canada said it could not provide any more information about the incident, such as the date and location it occurred, out of concern for the donor’s privacy.

Earlier this year, the regulator provided The Globe and other media outlets the date and location of two other such deaths, which happened in Winnipeg on Oct. 25, 2025, and on Jan. 30, 2026. Health Canada did not immediately answer a question Friday about why it provided the information in previous cases but not in this one. Health Canada also disclosed earlier this year that it had received a report of a death on June 4, 2018, that also came from Winnipeg.

Health Canada said that, under federal regulation, CBS holds the responsibility to conduct a thorough investigation of this incident.

The regulator said it reviewed documentation provided by CBS, which said the charity followed appropriate procedures and there was no suggestion of equipment failure or malfunction. Health Canada did not say it was taking any further actions.

As demand for plasma-derived immunoglobulin treatments soars, experts call for better oversight

Health Canada has previously told The Globe that as a regulator of blood and plasma products, it “does not determine the cause of death, including whether the donation process may have contributed to a death.”

CBS reports annual statistics on the number of “adverse reactions,” which is defined in federal regulations as an “undesirable response” associated with the collection of blood or plasma. CBS reported 28 adverse reactions in the 2025-26 year, which was lower than previous years.

Dr. Sher said the recent death is not classified as an adverse reaction because CBS believes it is unrelated to the donation.

Grifols announced in August that it had revised its own reports of serious adverse reactions, which involve incidents of injury or death, and believed there were only six such incidents that had occurred at its facilities since 2018, much lower than the 25 incidents the company had previously reported to Health Canada.

Hema-Québéc, the blood authority in Quebec, has reported three serious adverse reactions in that time.

None of CBS’s adverse reactions since 2018 have been classified as “serious.”

Globally, deaths connected to plasma donation are very rare. In the U.S., where millions of plasma donations are made annually, there are only a handful of deaths each year that may be connected to a donation, according to reports from the Food and Drug Administration.

B.C. Conservatives win majority government, Lorne Doerkson to become premier

BC Conservative interim Leader Lorne Doerkson speaks on stage during provincial election night in Vancouver, on Saturday. He pledges to boost the economy, cut red tape and freeze all provincial taxes.ETHAN CAIRNS/The Canadian Press

British Columbia has elected a Conservative majority government for the first time in nearly a century, delivering a remarkable reversal of fortunes to a party that entered the campaign after months of internal conflict and controversies that threatened to derail its path to power.

David Eby resigned as leader of the NDP, accepting responsibility for the outcome of an election he called two years early. His high-stakes gamble to take advantage of a fractured opposition backfired; with more than 99 per cent of polls reporting, the results showed 64 Conservatives elected, 27 for the NDP and two for the Greens.

In a sombre concession speech, Mr. Eby reflected on his government’s accomplishments in recruiting family doctors, helping to lower rents and raising the minimum wage.

“This is obviously something I do with a very heavy heart,” he said to supporters at his party’s gathering in Vancouver. “But it is time for new leadership within our party.”

The Conservatives appeared to be on the verge of collapse just weeks ago under the weight of scandal, defections and bitter internal rivalries, but regrouped just as Mr. Eby called the election.

The victory ends nine years of NDP rule – four under Mr. Eby – and puts the Conservatives in control of a province facing mounting economic uncertainty, persistent strains in health care services and a continuing trade war with the U.S.

Lorne Doerkson didn’t plan on leading the BC Conservatives, but he’s trying to meet the moment

Mr. Eby said in his concession speech that he had spoken with Conservative interim leader and premier-designate Lorne Doerkson, wishing him the best at a critical time for the province and country.

“The people of British Columbia have asked us to be the Official Opposition, which is obviously not the result any of us wanted,” he said. “A number of my colleagues, some of the hardest working MLAs I know for their communities, were not elected tonight. It breaks my heart, and I accept full responsibility for that outcome.”

In his victory speech, Mr. Doerkson thanked Mr. Eby and summarized his campaign pledges, which included boosting the economy, cutting red tape and freezing all provincial taxes.

“I want a B.C. where a hard-day’s work gets you a family home and a future right here in this beautiful province,” he said.

“And I want to say to those who aren’t in this room: There is a place for you here. Bring your ideas, bring your families, bring your ambitions and somebody will pick up the phone in British Columbia.”

As large sparks shot out of several small cannons behind him, Mr. Doerkson detailed how his government will jump-start the economy by tripling natural-gas production over the next nine years and holding an international investment summit in Vancouver.

He repeated his pledge to cut taxes and red tape and to support health care that is “fully and publicly funded.”

Mr. Doerkson has said that repealing the province’s Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act would be among his top priorities, arguing that it has created needless division. Indigenous leaders have been opposed to any changes to the legislation.

Mr. Doerkson addressed Indigenous people directly in his speech Saturday night.

“To our First Nations: This future is one we will build together as partners with the certainty all of us need,” he said.

“First Nations built lives and nations here for thousands of years.”

But he also told the crowd, to cheers, that he believed “in the protection of private-property rights and parks that are open to all British Columbians.”

Recent court rulings in favour of Indigenous groups have made those issues a centrepiece of Conservative commentary in the province.

“We may have a small caucus, but tonight we have inherited an enormous responsibility and that is to hold the majority Conservative government to account,” Ms. Lowan said in her victory speech.

“Over the next four years, our job is to be a voice for the many people who will be harmed by their policies – and that is going to require some serious movement building.”
