They were Federal Security Service (FSB) agents, arresting Imai on espionage charges likely related to his journalistic work. He is now awaiting trial in Moscow’s notorious Lefortovo Prison, where he faces up to 20 years in prison.

In Japan, his case is enveloped in secrecy. Few who knew Imai — including his employer, the Hokkaido Shimbun newspaper — are willing to talk publicly about his background, the kind of journalist he is and what motivated him to report on the ground in a contentious region, despite the mounting risks for foreign correspondents.

But a review of his limited online presence and published articles reveals a picture of someone who was deeply fascinated by Russian culture, had family connections to the Far East and was committed to explaining the complexities of his home country’s relations with Russia.

The Hokkaido Shimbun declined to comment for this story, saying that it was still “gathering information” about the criminal charges against its journalist. In an open letter signed by Editor-in-Chief Takeshi Yamamoto, the newspaper said it was “responding with our highest priority on ensuring our reporter’s safety.”

Foreign correspondents are often criticized for parachuting into unfamiliar territory, firing off a quick report and leaving without ever developing a deeper understanding of their subject. Imai wasn’t that kind of journalist.

An acquaintance of his told the exiled news outlet IStories that Imai first came to Russia in 2014 for Russian language studies at St. Petersburg State University. According to the report, he also took an interest in Russian politics and literature.

It doesn’t appear that interest waned, even after Imai reportedly returned to Japan to continue his studies.

In recent years, Imai channeled his curiosities through journalism. He started reporting for the Hokkaido Shimbun, a daily newspaper located in the capital of Japan’s immense northern island of the same name. Owing to its proximity to both the Russian mainland and the disputed Kuril Islands, the newspaper publishes a robust section focused on Russian affairs, with a particular emphasis on bilateral relations.

The Far East is a geopolitically complicated region. Both Russia and Japan claimed and contested large areas of territory there during the 19th and 20th centuries before World War II largely sealed the matter, handing the Soviets control over most of it.

The enduring disagreement over ownership of the four southernmost Kuril Islands, which are controlled by Russia, has prevented the two countries from formally signing a peace treaty to end World War II.

Imai’s articles interrogated the conflict’s legacy and Japan’s absence from the islands that were once its territory, reporting on everything from the construction of a controversial war monument to the recent demolition of a disused Japanese government-funded medical clinic.

He also covered developments in politics and defense, from Russia’s recent parliamentary elections and relations with North Korea to the rise in military drone production on Sakhalin.

It’s unclear if any of these stories were the reason for Imai’s arrest, but it’s easy to see how officials might have felt his coverage was prodding at delicate topics, even if it wasn’t particularly intrusive.

For Imai, Russia and Japan’s fractured relationship was not merely a subject of professional fascination. He had roots in Russia’s Far East, noting in an article published this summer that his grandfather had lived in the Sakhalin town of Kholmsk — then called Maoka — before World War II, something that drew Imai to the same place a decade ago.

“My grandfather had lived in Maoka before the war,” he wrote, “and I wanted to see the remnants of Japan that remained in the town.”

The “remnants” he referred to are the long-abandoned buildings of a former Japanese paper factory. In the same article, Imai writes about the melancholic experience of walking past this area in August of this year after the factory was demolished. Gazing at the lot where the factory used to stand, he felt “as if a part of the town’s history had been lost.”

Outside of his social media account and articles for the Hokkaido Shimbun, details about Imai’s background are scarce. That may come as a surprise to outsiders who remember the blitz of news coverage surrounding the arrest of Wall Street Journal reporter Evan Gershkovich in Russia on spying accusations in spring 2023.

In Imai’s case, there has so far been no public outcry and no media campaign spearheaded by colleagues or family, as happened with Gershkovich and other foreigners arrested in Russia on dubious charges in recent years.

Aleksandra Bielakowska, the advocacy manager of Reporters Without Borders’ Asia-Pacific Bureau, said this is a common tack the government and families take when a Japanese reporter is arrested overseas.

“Usually how it works in Japan is that the Japanese government takes the lead, and they very rarely allow anyone else to intervene with any type of engagement,” Bielakowska said. “They will be very strict about not sharing any information or insight into what’s going on.”

In the past, that’s extended to even naming the individual, she added. The only reason the Japanese public knows Imai’s name in this case is because it was released by the Russian side. Publicly, Japanese officials have still not acknowledged that he was the person arrested.

In a statement to The Moscow Times last week that did not name Imai as the detained individual, Japan’s Foreign Affairs Ministry called on the Russian government to release the journalist and treat him in a “humane, fair and impartial manner.” On Tuesday, the Hokkaido Shimbun announced that officials from the Japanese embassy had finally met with Imai after nearly two weeks of no contact.

Part of the reason for this secrecy comes down to deeply rooted cultural norms.

“It’s just sort of the Japanese way of doing everything. They always prefer to avoid any sort of loud confrontation. They always prefer to deal with things behind the scenes,” said James D.J. Brown, a professor of international relations at Temple University, Japan Campus.

But that quiet approach could backfire by encouraging Russia to target others, Brown argued.

“This poor guy is being locked up, and most of the population doesn’t know his name. I also feel that if you don’t publicize it, there’s more risk of other people falling into the same danger,” he told The Moscow Times.

The biggest unanswered question is what Russia wants from Japan. Japan doesn’t hold any Russian prisoners that the Kremlin would like to see returned home. Still, Brown speculated that Imai could end up as part of a multinational prisoner swap, similar to the 26-person exchange in 2024 that freed Gershkovich.

However, Brown thinks Russia may care less about a prisoner swap and more about punishing Japan over larger policy issues.

Authorities in Tokyo have made a series of recent decisions that have unsettled the Kremlin about security in the Far East, including hosting American Typhon intermediate-range missiles.

Japan has also helped pay for weapons for Ukraine through a NATO initiative, while Japanese companies, like the drone manufacturer Terra Drone, have started doing business with the Ukrainian military.

“I think that, for all of that, Russia has decided that they want to apply pressure on Japan,” Brown said. “And frankly they regard Japan as likely to concede, so they feel they probably have a good chance of forcing some concessions.”

“It’s almost certain they will,” he said, referring to Japan giving in to at least some of Russia’s demands, whatever those may end up being. “That also explains the low-key approach. They want to perhaps have the freedom away from the public eye to cut a deal.”

Toronto loves its mayoral incumbents. Since the city was amalgamated in 1998, every mayor who has chosen to stand for re-election has successfully won back the mayor’s chair, and by considerable margins. Mel Lastman won re-election in 2000 with a whopping 80 per cent of the popular vote. Incumbent mayor David Miller bested Jane Pitfield in 2006, winning by a margin of 25 percentage points. John Tory did better: he won his two bids for re-election in 2018 and 2022 by roughly 40 and 44 percentage points, respectively.

On Monday night, Olivia Chow carried on the tradition to earn a second term as Toronto mayor. She won handily, sweeping wards in downtown Toronto, all of Scarborough, and even eking out a win in Premier Doug Ford’s riding of Etobicoke North. But Ms. Chow’s victory was not the colossal blowout win enjoyed by previous incumbent Toronto mayors; her margin of victory over city councillor Brad Bradford was not 25, 40 or 44 points – but just 13. It’s a decisive win, of course, but hardly the thumping sweep Toronto has come to expect of its mayoral incumbents.

Toronto mayoral candidate and former councillor Brad Bradford concedes on stage on Oct. 26 after losing the race to Olivia Chow.Frank Gunn/The Canadian Press

At the outset of the campaign, Ms. Chow appeared to have believed she was headed toward that thumping return enjoyed by her predecessors. She was running a classic front-runner campaign: not saying all that much or promising too many ideas, and instead coasting on her name, her reputation, and her record. Ms. Chow’s campaign benefited by the fact that her two main opponents, Mr. Bradford (who got barely over 1 per cent of the vote in the previous race for mayor) and former Harper cabinet minister Chris Alexander were splitting the centre-right vote. Ms. Chow wasn’t performing all that well in early debates, but it didn’t seem to matter: she was going to win anyway.

That apparent inevitability started to look less certain a few weeks ago, after Mr. Alexander dropped out of the race and Mr. Bradford’s campaign picked up momentum. His message about Toronto being “stuck” was resonating – among people tired of sitting in traffic, frustrated with the ever-increasing cost of living, and worried about visible signs of social disorder such as open drug use on the TTC. Polling showed that more than a third of Torontonians don’t feel safe in their own neighbourhoods after dark, and a majority said that the city is headed in the wrong direction. But Ms. Chow wasn’t acknowledging that reality; when asked during one debate whether Toronto was living up to its full potential, she indicated that it was.

Five takeaways from the GTA’s municipal elections

So when the polls began to narrow, Ms. Chow’s campaign did what campaigns often do when they get cornered: it went negative. She attacked Mr. Bradford’s proposal to turn Toronto Water into a publicly owned arms-length corporation (akin to Hydro One) and labelled it an attempt to “privatize” Toronto’s “drinking water.” She warned he will “cut services you rely on,” and Progress Toronto, a group supporting her campaign, charged that Mr. Bradford was aligned with “questionable endorsers.” The attacks ranged from bad faith (by law, Toronto’s drinking water cannot be privatized) to ridiculous (one of the “questionable endorsers” Progress Toronto tried to portray as extreme is Conservative MP Scott Aitchison), but they clearly accomplished what they sought to do.

On Monday night, Ms. Chow achieved an unequivocal victory, but the result was also a rather respectable loss for runner-up Brad Bradford (with the exception of the walloping he was handed in his own ward). Mr. Bradford went from 1 per cent of the vote in 2023 to 41 per cent in just three years. He resonated with voters by acknowledging the reality they experience living in Toronto, and clearly spooked the Chow campaign by forcing a negative pivot in the 11th hour.

Ms. Chow came into this election with name recognition, the power of incumbency and her personal legacy as a public figure for the past four decades. She had the backing of major public sector unions, the loyalty of many incumbent city councillors, and an incredible get-out-the-vote machine fortified by her time as a city councillor and as a member of the federal NDP. Ms. Chow also came into this election without a full term behind her (since she was elected mayor in a by-election after Mr. Tory’s abrupt resignation in 2023), and thus could credibly make the case that she has not had the time to fully implement her plans for the city. Ms. Chow still managed to sail her way to victory, but it was on a swell – not a tsunami, as one might have expected. It’s clearly a win for the incumbent mayor, but a rather honourable defeat for her challenger, too.
