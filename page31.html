Iran war leaves ‘forever cruise’ residents stranded

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A cruise ship selling “permanent homes at sea” has abruptly suspended operations, leaving around 200 residents stranded in Cambodia as its operator blamed soaring fuel costs caused by the US-Israeli war on Iran.

The 200-meter (656-foot) Odyssey, with a capacity for 650 passengers, departed Belfast in October 2024 on a “continuous” round-the-world “forever cruise.” It offered cabins for rent, purchase, or lifetime ownership starting at $59,999 and reaching $440,000, plus monthly fees for meals, drinks, Wi-Fi and other services.

On Monday, Florida-based operator Villa Vie Residences (VVR) ordered passengers – some of whom reportedly sold their homes or spent their life savings on cabins – off the ship during a stop in Cambodia’s Sihanoukville. They had just seven hours to pack and leave, passenger Randy Cassingham reported on his cruise blog.

Cassingham shared a message from VVR CEO Kathy Villalba saying the company was “unable to secure the fuel necessary to continue operations” and that the Odyssey would enter a “temporary layup,” with crew remaining aboard. Other residents, many of whom also run travel blogs, confirmed the account.

Sihanoukville deputy governor Long Dimanche confirmed Tuesday that the vessel, which arrived from Thailand on October 23 with 203 passengers and 156 crew, was due to sail to Vietnam later this week, but instead disembarked all passengers. Some have since flown out of Cambodia, while others remain in Sihanoukville, he said in a statement to local media.

Passengers voiced their anger online. Cassingham blamed a “cascade of problems,” including a flawed business model and management mistakes, and accused the company of doing “absolutely nothing to help.” Another resident, Holly Hennessy, told CNN that problems had been mounting for months.

“It’s been incredibly tough since the war,” she said, adding that residents had been asked to pay a fuel surcharge in May, while the ship had reduced air conditioning to save energy.

Fellow residents Lynette and Johan, who run the YouTube vlog Living Life on a Cruise, also described months of financial uncertainty. Johan said he felt “really drained” and worried about those who had invested their life savings in the cruise.

VVR denied abandoning passengers, saying it was arranging temporary accommodation and disembarkation assistance. Company founder Mikael Petterson told Bloomberg that VVR would cover local lodging and transportation costs, but not passengers’ travel out of Cambodia.

In a statement on Tuesday, the company blamed “extraordinary” fuel costs tied to the Iran war, saying monthly fuel expenses had surged from $1,800 to as much as $8,000 per cabin in parts of Asia since February. Slower sailing and longer port stays had failed to contain costs.

“Passing the full increase on to residents is not a realistic solution,” the company said, announcing plans to restructure and seek financing within 60 days.

The US-Israeli war on Iran has severely disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Houthi attacks on Saudi energy facilities and shipping have further strained supplies, threatening the Red Sea’s Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Despite Gulf exports reportedly recovering to 80% of pre-war levels, Brent crude recently approached $117 per barrel.

US President Donald Trump has rejected Tehran’s latest settlement proposal and last week threatened another offensive. He also blamed Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries, rather than the Iran war, for the latest spike in fuel prices. Trump subsequently lifted restrictions on Russian diesel exports to the US in an effort to ease shortages, and Moscow said on Tuesday that three diesel tankers were already heading to America.

The move offers little immediate relief to the Odyssey, however, which refuels at ports along its route. Cambodia, for instance, relies entirely on imported petroleum products, with gasoline prices up 33.7% and diesel 46.7% since the Middle East escalation, according to its Ministry of Commerce. Marine gasoil, which powers the ship, has nearly doubled to $1,321 per metric ton in Singapore, the world’s largest refueling hub, according to ClearLynx.

Tågolyckan i Spanien: Detta vet vi

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Minst 69 personer har omkommit efter att två tåg kolliderat på höghastighetsbanan i den spanska regionen Toledo. Det här vet vi om olyckan.

• Larmet inkom strax innan klockan 9 på onsdagen den 28/10, mellan städerna Los Yébenes och Urda i provinsen Toledo.

• Det ena tåget tillhörde det statliga bolaget Renfe och hade vid tillfället 397 passagerare, medan det andra tillhörande privatägda Iryo hade omkring 150 ombord. Båda var på väg söderut på höghastighetsbanan mellan Madrid och Sevilla, där tågen kan färdas i upp till 300 kilometer i timmen.

• Enligt El País hade Irya-tåget saktat in och stannat upp strax innan olyckan, varpå Renfe-tåget körde in i det bakifrån. Båda tågen spårade delvis ur efter kollisionen, och fyra av vagnarna hamnade i diken som omger rälsen.

• Enligt preliminära uppgifter från det regionala styret har minst 68 personer omkommit och ytterligare ett 80-tal skadats. Merparten av de döda uppges ha färdats i det påkörda Iryo-tåget.

• Orsaken till olyckan är inte fastställd, men enligt Spaniens transportminister Óscar Puente är både tekniska och mänskliga faktorer involverade. Efter att ha sett en full rekonstruktion av olyckan och lyssnat på radiokommunikationen lämnar han denna preliminära redogörelse:

Runt 8.30: Ett fel inträffar i kontrollsystemets kommunikation, vilket gör att 60 kilometer av höghastighetsbanan hamnar i radioskugga. Detta triggar automatiskt ett stopp för sex tåg på den aktuella sträckan.

Runt 8.36: Kontrollcentret ger manuellt instruktioner om att bortse från varningen och låta trafiken fortsätta. Ett ”uppenbart felaktigt beslut”, påpekar Puente vid en presskonferens.

Runt 9.00: Under de efterföljande minuterna färdas Renfe-tåget cirka 25 kilometer innan det till slut kraschar in i Iryo-tåget.

• Tågsäkerhet har sedan tidigare varit en omdebatterad fråga i Spanien, särskilt efter att två höghastighetståg spårade ur i januari i år, med över 40 döda som följd. Det största facket för lokförare i Spanien, Semaf, hade sedan tidigare utlyst en dygnslång strejk på grund av upplevda säkerhetsbrister, en strejk som var tänkt att inledas dagen efter onsdagens olycka.

Russian Spy Satellites Helping Iran’s Allies Target Sites in Saudi Arabia – AP

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Russian satellites have consistently appeared in the skies above facilities in Saudi Arabia that Iranian-backed Houthis and Iraqi militia groups have struck with missiles and drones over the past several months, the Associated Press reported Tuesday.

Nawaf Obaid, founder of the Geneva-based firm AURORAH Geosignal Intelligence, told the news agency that he and his colleagues tracked 72 Russian military and government satellites between July and October, finding them orbiting above Saudi sites before and after they were attacked.

“What we found is a 100% match,” Obaid said.

The targeted facilities include Saudi Arabia’s East-West Pipeline, which was forced to shut down immediately after it was attacked in September.

Another example is the Kind Fahd Air Base. When the base was attacked last month, an Italian fighter jet was struck while a nearby Saudi aircraft was left unscathed.

Obaid said the apparent precision of the strike on Kind Fahd suggests vital intelligence is being collected and transferred for Houthi and Iraqi militia targeting, given that the groups lack sophisticated reconnaissance capabilities on their own.

An official briefed on Saudi investigations into the recent attacks told AP that evidence showed Russian satellites were being used for targeting. A senior Houthi official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said Iranian military advisers are directly providing the Russian reconnaissance information.

Trump claimed last week that Russia is “doing very little, if anything” to help Iran in the war. That came as President Vladimir Putin reiterated that Russia is ready to play a mediating role in ending the conflict.

Argentina helped Bolsonaro flee Brazil

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Argentina’s right-wing government has confirmed that it helped Brazilian Senator Flavio Bolsonaro flee the country after his election loss to President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio was reportedly involved in the operation.

Bolsonaro was ordered to surrender his passports on Monday, less than 24 hours after his narrow runoff election loss to Lula. The passports were canceled on Tuesday, after he failed to surrender them to authorities. Media reports claimed that he had fled to the US earlier that day, but no outlet could confirm that he had left the country.

“We still don’t have any news about his whereabouts,” Bolsonaro’s campaign manager told AFP on Tuesday.

In the early hours of Wednesday morning, Argentina’s National Directorate of Migration (DNM) confirmed that Bolsonaro, his wife, and his two daughters entered Argentina via a border checkpoint near the town of Puerto Iguazu, shortly before 11pm on Tuesday. Bolsonaro crossed the border “with valid travel documentation and without any legal impediment to entering the country,” the agency stated.

It is unclear if Bolsonaro crossed the border using his national identity card, or with his Italian passport, as he has held Italian citizenship since 2023.

Once in Argentina, the Bolsonaro family boarded a private jet bound for Ecuador, the DNM stated. Argentinian President Javier Milei and Ecuadorian President Daniel Noboa are both right-wing allies of Bolsonaro.

Bolsonaro and his family were seen arriving in Florida on Wednesday, where the State Department has told AP that it will grant visas to him and his family. While the State Department has not admitted any role in helping Bolsonaro flee Brazil, Mexican entrepreneur Simon Levy, who describes himself as “proud to serve President Trump and MAGA,” claimed on Tuesday that the entire operation was “coordinated by US Secretary of State Marco Rubio.”

Rubio has not commented on Levy’s claims. However, Rubio and Trump are both political allies of Bolsonaro. Rubio’s State Department sanctioned Brazilian Supreme Federal Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes last year over his role in jailing Bolsonaro’s father, former Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro, in 2022, and over his censorship of American social media platforms. Moraes is a close political ally of Lula.

Bolsonaro’s passports were canceled in connection with an ongoing corruption and money laundering probe. Despite Moraes being named in the same investigation, Lula warned Bolsonaro earlier this month that he would “be in a lot of trouble” if he lost the election, threatening him with “plenty of investigations.”

It is unclear how the US intends to respond to Lula’s election win. Trump has suggested that the election “had a very bad look…the way it was tabulated.” After claiming that Rubio aided in Bolsonaro’s flight, Levy made a cryptic post on X, saying “get ready for tomorrow…”

France planning new riot crackdown law

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

France’s Senate has backed a sweeping “vandals pay” bill that could force people involved in violent gatherings to cover up to €10,000 ($11,100) in damage caused not only by them, but also by other members of the group. The vote follows weeks of nationwide protests which spiraled into looting, arson attacks, and clashes with police.

The bill was initially drafted by the government over the summer after violence following Paris Saint-Germain’s Champions League victory, but it gained urgency after a new wave of mass public disorder. Demonstrations against soaring living costs and worsening quality of education turned violent in several cities this month, with rioters looting shops, torching vehicles, and destroying the 19th-century city hall in Limoges. Thousands of people were arrested during the unrest, with hundreds injured.

Senators passed the measure by 229 votes to 105 on Wednesday, backed by conservatives, centrists, and Marine Le Pen’s right-wing National Rally despite fierce objections from the left.

While presenting the legislation, Interior Minister Laurent Nunez argued that it seeks “to deter vandals and looters who burden taxpayers and demonstrators with their misdeeds, and… to better compensate the victims of this damage.”

He added that the bill is also “firmly based on fault; fault exists when one participates in a group formed with the intention of committing violence.” Meanwhile, centrist Senator Olivier Bitz reassured that “it should not be assumed that this text would allow for the prosecution of passersby or peaceful demonstrators.”

The bill – which included around 20 additional provisions compared to its original version – establishes joint civil liability for people who knowingly join a group formed to commit violence or vandalism, or remain in a crowd after police dispersal warnings while aware of ongoing attacks.  Those found liable could owe up to €10,000 for destruction committed by others, although the final sum is to be determined in court, and peaceful participation alone would not trigger the measure.

To recover court-ordered compensation, the legislation would also allow deductions from social benefits, including family allowances, housing assistance, and minimum-income payments. The bill would also exempt people who remain in a gathering for legitimate reasons, potentially including journalists and medical volunteers.

While centrists and right-wing supporters portrayed the bill as a more just measure to extract damages from those involved in vandalism and protect law-abiding taxpayers, socialist Senator Marie-Pierre de La Gontrie warned that the welfare measures risk penalizing entire families.

Nathalie Godard, Director of the Action Department at Amnesty International France, also argued that the bill “threatens to further deter people from taking to the streets to exercise their rights to freedom of expression and peaceful assembly,” adding that it comes on the heels of the government’s push to crack down on protests.

The Senate passed the bill ahead of the 2027 presidential race, with two key contenders taking contrasting positions over the unrest. Marine Le Pen, the frontrunner in recent polls, has championed the “vandals pay” principle, accusing President Emmanuel Macron’s government of failing to control the violence.

Her left-wing rival Jean-Luc Melenchon has backed the protests while condemning looting and vandalism and accusing the authorities of inflaming tensions through police brutality.

While the bill sailed through the right-dominated Senate, it now faces a much tougher test in the fractured National Assembly, where centrist and right-wing lawmakers together hold enough seats to outvote the left but remain divided over the proposed crackdown.

The Israeli election hurt Netanyahu, but the country is still stuck

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Murad Sadygzade, President of the Middle East Studies Center, Visiting Lecturer, HSE University (Moscow).

By Murad Sadygzade, President of the Middle East Studies Center, Visiting Lecturer, HSE University (Moscow).

The parliamentary elections in Israel were a major test of public confidence in Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

Israelis went to the polls in the aftermath of the events of October 7, 2023, the wars in Gaza and Lebanon, the confrontation with Iran, and increasingly strained relations with several international partners. The composition of the next government will decide the direction of Israel’s political and strategic development.

Turnout was exceptionally high – the Central Elections Committee put it at 77.7%, the highest since 1999. The main contest unfolded between Netanyahu’s Likud party and an opposition camp led by Gadi Eisenkot, the leader of the centrist Yashar party. His potential allies include the parties of Naftali Bennett, Yair Lapid (who had shared a rotating premiership in 2021-22), and other parties opposed to the incumbent coalition.

According to updated exit polls conducted by three major TV networks, the opposition bloc is projected to secure 47 seats, Netanyahu’s allies 54, and Arab parties outside either camp 19. Likud nevertheless remains the largest individual party, while other projections are more favorable to the right-wing bloc. The official results are expected to be published on November 4. For now, one thing is evident: Neither side has secured a clear parliamentary majority.

The turnout reflects a profound political mobilization within Israeli society. Over the past several years, decisions made by the leadership have become increasingly intertwined with questions of personal security, economic well-being, and the future of individual families. As a result, even citizens who previously remained relatively disengaged from politics have become more actively involved.

The events of October 7, 2023 were the decisive catalyst for this transformation. The failure of the national security system severely undermined confidence in the political leadership, and the subsequent wars failed to produce lasting national unity around the government. Instead, they intensified questions of political accountability, the human and economic costs of military decisions, and the absence of a convincing strategy for ending the conflicts. Even supporters of a hardline security approach have grown disillusioned with its effectiveness.

Socioeconomic pressures have also played an important role. Rising military expenditure, the high cost of living, housing difficulties, and prolonged reserve military service have shown the people the price of continuing instability. Particularly contentious has been the exemption of sections of the ultra-Orthodox community from military service, while other segments of society bear the burden.

Israel’s deteriorating international standing has further compounded these challenges. Disagreements with traditional European partners over the war in Gaza, developments in the West Bank, and the policies pursued by far-right members of the government are leaving the country with less and less room for diplomatic maneuvers. Some voters increasingly blame the government for growing diplomatic isolation and economic risks.

The high turnout was not just against Netanyahu. Right-wing voters were also highly mobilized, as was the Arab electorate, whose parties achieved a notable result. Nevertheless, the elections suggest that the existing political situation is no longer widely seen as sustainable.

In Israeli politics, electoral success does not necessarily translate into political power. In the 120-seat Knesset, a governing coalition requires the support of at least 61 members, a threshold that neither of the two main political camps is so far projected to reach. The decisive struggle will now move to negotiations between parties.

After the official results are released, President Isaac Herzog will consult parliamentary factions and entrust the task of forming a government to the candidate considered most capable of securing a majority. The initial period for coalition negotiations is 28 days, with a possible extension of up to 14 days. If these efforts fail, another member of the Knesset may be given 28 days to form a government. Subsequently, members of parliament would have 21 days to nominate an alternative candidate with the support of at least 61 legislators, who would then receive a further 14 days to complete the process. Should this final attempt also fail, the Knesset would be dissolved and new elections called.

Under these circumstances, smaller parties have far more power than their number of seats would suggest. In exchange for supporting a coalition, they may demand ministerial appointments, budgetary commitments, and significant policy concessions. Netanyahu’s camp will have to seek additional support beyond its traditional allies, while the opposition faces the difficult task of reconciling the positions of centrists, right-wing opponents of Netanyahu, and more liberal parties.

Arab parties, which are projected to hold 19 seats, could become particularly important. Eisenkot and some of his potential coalition partners, however, have ruled out including them in the government. External parliamentary support or abstention during a confidence vote could theoretically alter the balance, but this would require politically sensitive compromises from the centrist camp.

Netanyahu’s extensive experience in coalition-building gives him considerable opportunities to negotiate with smaller parties and persuade individual political actors to abandon competing alliances. Even if he fails to assemble a majority, he will remain in office as caretaker prime minister until a new government is formed. His political position has undoubtedly weakened, but this does not yet amount to his removal from power.

Netanyahu entered these elections with a loyal electoral base intact, but with substantially weakened support beyond the right-wing camp. National security has always been his central political argument. Yet the consequences of October 7 and the prolonged military campaigns have seriously damaged his reputation, raising questions about the effectiveness of his leadership.

With this in mind, reports of preparations for a new military operation against Iran shortly before the elections deserve particular attention. Axios reported on discussions between Netanyahu and US President Donald Trump, the Financial Times described preparations for a large-scale strike in early October, and CNN reported on Israeli efforts to secure American support. According to these accounts, the Trump administration prefers to avoid another major escalation ahead of the US elections scheduled for November 3. Netanyahu denied reports that Trump prevented a planned Israeli attack, and some of the reported details have also been disputed by Israeli media.

A swift and successful military operation against Iran could have unified a substantial part of society around Likud, restored Netanyahu’s image as a decisive defender of national security, and pushed criticism of his domestic policies into the background. While this does not prove that a strike was being planned for electoral purposes, the potential political benefits are obvious.

On the other hand, a short war against Iran would have been an extremely risky gamble. Iranian retaliation, the involvement of Tehran’s regional allies, additional pressure on Israel’s missile defense systems, and potential disruption of global energy markets could have produced another crisis of public confidence. The combination of American constraints and unpredictable military consequences appears to have prevented this from happening before the election.

Ultimately, the election took place without a major military development capable of fundamentally shifting votes. Should Netanyahu remain in power, the most likely outcome would be the continuation of his hardline approach towards Iran and aligned armed groups. Continued dependence on far-right coalition partners could push this policy in an even more confrontational direction. Even so, Israel would still have to take Washington’s position into account, given the importance of American support for major military operations.

Gadi Eisenkot, who served as chief of the General Staff of the Israel Defense Forces from 2015 to 2019, has emerged as Netanyahu’s principal political alternative. His main advantage lies in offering a change of leadership without abandoning the centrality of national security. For voters dissatisfied with the incumbent prime minister but unwilling to embrace a fundamental ideological shift, this is an appealing compromise. Eisenkot presents himself as a centrist, institutionally minded political figure with extensive experience in military leadership.

Particular emphasis in his political program is placed on soldiers, reservists, and their families. The bank’s economists screened trade data across nearly 5,000 product categories, matching goods where Canada has a proven edge with products that the European Union relies on imports to supply.

AI doomsday fears prompt researcher to abandon retirement savings

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An AI safety researcher has stopped saving for retirement because he believes artificial intelligence could wipe out humanity within the next ten years. His warning follows similar predictions from researchers concerned that increasingly powerful systems could escape human control.

Nate Soares, a former Google and Microsoft engineer who now heads the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI), discussed his outlook with Atlantic CEO Nicholas Thompson

“I do not save for retirement right now. I have a 401(k) from when I worked at Google, but I haven’t contributed for a decade,” he said, referring to his workplace retirement account.

Asked whether he believed there was a greater-than-50% chance humans would not survive another ten years, Soares stated that it’s “more than 50% that we don’t have a decade at this point.”

“I am not really an AI job apocalypse guy,” he said. “I’m an AI kills all humans guy.”

Soares argued that a sufficiently capable system could develop goals incompatible with human survival, evade oversight, and improve itself. It could then acquire resources and build automated infrastructure allowing it to operate without people, potentially making the planet uninhabitable.

The researcher urged governments to halt the training of general-purpose AI systems, arguing that superintelligence should be treated as a danger comparable to nuclear weapons. At the same time, he supported the use of the technology in narrowly focused applications, including cancer research and safer self-driving cars.

Soares stressed that catastrophe could still be prevented. “I do everything I can to stop this eventuality. I don’t think it’s inevitable,” he said.

Other researchers have voiced similar fears. In September, Anthropic’s Evan Hubinger put the probability of AI killing all humans within a decade above 10%. His colleague Jacob Coxon resigned, accusing developers of “racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives.”

The warnings come amid repeated disclosures of AI agents acting beyond developers’ instructions. OpenAI reported in July that its agents escaped restricted testing environments and accessed systems belonging to Hugging Face, an online repository for AI models and datasets. The company has since restricted internet access during training of its most powerful models.

Critics have called for binding government safeguards and a halt to the development of potentially uncontrollable systems. On the other hand, supporters of continued development argue that AI’s benefits outweigh many of the risks.
