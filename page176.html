South Africa: Dwindling support for ANC amid service delivery chaos

📅 2026-10-23  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

With less than two weeks to go to South Africa’s municipal elections, several recent polls have shown dwindling support for the African National Congress (ANC).

Frustration at the lack of service delivery saw it win just over 40 per cent in the 2024 national vote, forcing it to accept a coalition government.

Outside the wealthy neighbourhoods of Johannesburg, anger at lack of services like water or electricity has led many to say they will not be voting.

In Katlehong, outside the city, thick cables snake along footpaths in an area where 180 households have relied on illegal electricity connections for more than 20 years.

We have made a collective decision: no electricity, no vote," said Lesiba Rankapole, a community leader in the township.

"We know it is illegal, but we had no choice because we cannot live without electricity," he said of the wires that take power from another area of Katlehong.

"Our children cannot progress without electricity."

It is "obvious we are not going to vote, especially for the ANC", said another resident, Enock Majola, who pointed out illegal dumping sites.

"I was born ANC, but today I do not want anyone to tell me about the ANC because I am suffering," he said.

In Alexandra, another township near Johannesburg, uncollected refuse piles up as sewage flows down the street, while many residents don’t have access to running water.

Like in other communities across the country, mismanagement and corruption is feeding disillusionment with ANC-led municipalities.

"The reason why I'm not voting, it's because I don't believe in our government. The reason why I don't believe in them is because they have disappointed us, countless of times," said resident Sipho Mandala.

Elsewhere in Katlehong, a man has been digging in a yard in an attempt to reach a blocked pipe at a house surrounded by sewage.

Although he is angry, he admits that if he did make it to a voting station on 4 November – he will still vote for the ANC, the party of liberation.

At the heart of South Africa's anti-apartheid struggle, the party of jailed liberation hero-turned-president Nelson Mandela took nearly 70 per cent of the national vote in 2004.

It fell below 50 per cent for the first time at the last municipal elections in 2021, when it won 46 per cent of votes and retained control of nearly half the municipalities.

Political analysts like Sethulego Matebesi say municipal elections do generally attract low turnout.

But he believes the vote will give parties an "idea of where their support base is" as they look towards the 2029 national poll.

Of the 257 councils up for grabs, the fight for control is particularly intense in Johannesburg, an economic dynamo of more than 4.8 million people.

"The ANC has been governing for years, but we have not seen any changes in Alexandra," said maintenance worker Patrick Saohatse.

He said he would give his vote to the liberal Democratic Alliance, the second-largest party in the ruling coalition after the ANC.

Already in charge of the country's second city Cape Town, the DA has pushed hard on ANC service delivery failures and mismanagement in a campaign that started more than a year ago.

"Another five years of corruption and collapsing infrastructure will push our city beyond recovery," said mayoral candidate Helen Zille, at a recent rally.

Less than two weeks before polling day, the ANC was yet to choose its candidate amid lacklustre campaigning.

"If you look at the situation in and around Johannesburg, there are reasons for people not to be happy," the party's head of elections, Mdumiseni Ntuli said.

Analysts say it is likely that no party will win an outright majority in many municipalities, forcing them to form coalitions which are often fragile, further undermining service delivery.

Nairobi building collapse raises safety concerns as rescuers search rubble

📅 2026-10-23  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A multi-storey building collapsed mid-construction in the Kenyan capital on Friday, with many feared trapped in the rubble and accusations of dangerous building work.

Videos shared online showed a dramatic crash and huge clouds of dust as the building -- thought to have been around 18 storeys high -- came down in the densely populated business and residential area of Eastleigh.

The building was reduced to a pile of rubble as search and rescue teams arrived on the scene, but struggled to start accessing the site which was jammed between other high-rise buildings.

Police said they had already rescued one woman from the rubble, and could hear the voice of another.

"There are lots of people trapped here," Bibiye Mahmoud, president of the Institution of Engineering Technology of Kenya, told AFP at the site of the disaster.

"The contractors and the engineers are supposed to ensure that the works are followed by professionals. But most are not. This is why we are seeing these catastrophes... There is no accountability," she added.

Kenya's National Building Inspectorate report found that 87 percent of buildings audited nationwide last year failed to meet standards or lacked the required approvals.

"Our concern is to save lives," Nairobi Governor Johnson Sakaja told reporters, adding: "This building has no occupation certificate...this has potentially cost lives."

An occupation certificate is essentially the authority's formal sign-off that a building is safe and legally fit to be occupied.

John Ogallo, vice-president of the Institution of Engineers of Kenya, who was also at the site, said there were warning signs about the building.

"People here are saying distresses were observed, like cracks," before it collapsed, he told reporters.

If you build with the wrong materials, "it becomes a time bomb," he added.

Nairobi is undergoing a rapid construction boom, with high-rise buildings appearing all over the city, particularly in Eastleigh which is known for its busy market areas and as a hub for the Somali community.

Local media reported looting immediately after the collapse, which happened around lunchtime. AFP saw police use tear gas to disperse crowds gathering near the site.

There have been many concerns over safety over the years.

There were 86 building collapses between 2009 and 2019 in Nairobi, killing an estimated 200 people, according to a report by the Kenya Institute for Public Policy Research and Analysis.

The number of buildings completed annually in Nairobi has risen from 20,025 in 2022 to 25,090 last year, according to its statistics bureau, as east Africa's leading business hub sucks in people from rural areas and neighbouring countries.

Corruption has allowed contractors to cut corners or bypass regulations.

Five people were killed when a six-storey building collapsed in a town on the outskirts of Nairobi in September 2022.

In April 2016, 49 people were killed when a six-floor apartment building crumbled in the northeast of the capital after days of heavy rain caused floods and landslides.

Hundreds of Ethiopians crossed into Kenya on Thursday as gunfire and chaotic scenes were reported in the border town of Moyale, police said.

A police officer was struck on the head after a bullet hit a metal bar supporting a tent at the Moyale police station’s football ground. He was not injured, according to a police report. A woman was shot in the neck and taken to a private hospital in Moyale, where the bullet was removed. She was reported to be in stable condition.

Another bullet shattered a window at La-Tahsan Hotel, the report said. Police did not identify the source of the bullets.

In a separate security brief dated Thursday, police said hundreds of Ethiopians had crossed into Kenya through several entry points in Moyale, including the Sessi, Kore, Biashara, Foundation and Miti areas.

The crossings began on Wednesday, with women, children and young people making up most of the arrivals, the brief said.

According to Kenyan police, the arrivals were fleeing tensions between leaders of Ethiopia ’s Oromia and Somali regional states.

The Kenya Red Cross said it was setting up temporary shelter and distributing essential items including cereal and porridge flour. “The response will initially reach 1,000 households,” Red Cross wrote in a statement.

Gunfire was heard on the Ethiopian side of the border from about 5:50 a.m. on Thursday and continued afterward, the brief said.

WHO: Africa facing 'complex' Ebola outbreak after Nigeria reports first case

📅 2026-10-23  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The World Health Organization's Africa chief warned on Thursday that the continent now faced a "complex" Ebola outbreak after the virus crossed from east Africa to Nigeria, which has reported the first case in the west of the continent.

"We are facing a more complex outbreak landscape. Nigeria has confirmed an imported case of Ebola virus disease," Mohamed Janabi, the regional director for WHO in Africa, told an online media briefing.

Nigeria on Tuesday announced an imported case of the Bundibugyo strain behind a major DR Congo outbreak, in a woman, a diplomat who had a history of travel to Uganda.

The continent's most populous nation is the fourth African country affected by the outbreak, which this week came to span the continent, with Kenya in the east reporting nine cases since October 6.

The case reignited memories of a widespread outbreak in 2014 that struck several countries in the region and killed more than 11,000 people across west Africa between late 2013 and 2016.

Nigeria's experience in successfully containing the Ebola outbreak in 2014 raised confidence that the country can respond effectively to new public health threats.

The DRC's Ebola epidemic has become the deadliest in the country's history, killing 4,381 people out of more than 9,000 confirmed cases since mid-May, according to the health authorities.

A trial of the Ervebo vaccine is underway in eastern Congo. Researchers are hoping to determine if the jab, which is approved for the Zaire strain of the virus, is effective against the Bundibugyo strain driving the current outbreak.

The World Health Organization said in August that 70,000 doses of Ervebo had been approved for use in Congo.

About 50,000 doses are intended for frontline and health workers, while 20,000 doses will be used in a clinical trial to study whether the vaccine protects against Bundibugyo, which currently has no licensed vaccine nor treatment.

Attack on actor Tiger Shroff is dark reminder of Bollywood's gangster past

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Actor Tiger Shroff, who made his debut in 2014, is known for his action roles

On Sunday evening, Bollywood actor Tiger Shroff went to play football in Juhu, a leafy, affluent Mumbai neighbourhood where film stars live, fans congregate and celebrity sightings are a part of life. But as the 36-year-old was leaving the ground in his BMW, shots rang out. His driver was wounded, as was a vlogger who had come to film the actor.

Shroff, whose films usually include elaborate action sequences and choreographed escapes, had just survived an actual shooting attempt.

Four days later, the case still dominates headlines in India, with each new revelation spawning fresh theories, competing claims and a growing list of alleged conspiracies.

Police have detained or arrested 10 suspects, with investigators tracing alleged weapon deliveries through several states and examining payments made to the alleged attackers.

The attempted shooting has also brought the name of Lawrence Bishnoi, one of India's most notorious gangsters, back into the headlines. Soon after the attack, a screenshot of a social media post purportedly made by an alleged associate of the Bishnoi gang circulated online, claiming responsibility. A separate audio recording emerged in which a man identifying himself as Anil Pandit repeated the claim and said Shroff had been targeted over his alleged links to actor Salman Khan, who Bishnoi has been threatening for years.

Mumbai police have not verified the claims or established that the Bishnoi gang was behind the attempted shooting. Investigators are still trying to determine who ordered the attack and why.

The targeting of Shroff follows a series of threats and attacks involving prominent Bollywood figures, including gunfire outside Khan's home in 2024 and filmmaker Rohit Shetty's house earlier this year. Together, the incidents have revived fears about the reach of organised crime into the film industry, stirring memories of a darker era in Mumbai's history.

During the 1990s and early 2000s, the city's underworld exerted formidable influence over the film industry, financing productions, demanding protection money and sometimes using violence to enforce its demands. It took years of police operations, and the gradual arrival of institutional financing, to loosen that grip.

Now, as fresh allegations and theories emerge almost daily, a question that once seemed to belong to Mumbai's past has returned: is the underworld trying to control Bollywood again?

Watch: Bollywood actor Tiger Shroff survives shooting attempt in Mumbai

Those who confronted organised crime in Mumbai at its peak say the answer is not as straightforward as the attempt to shoot Shroff might suggest.

D Sivanandhan, who took charge of Mumbai's Crime Branch in 1998, remembers a time when a phone call from Dubai, where some of the most notorious gang leaders were based, could decide the fate of a film - who starred in it, financed it or profited from its distribution.

In an industry dependent on private money, the line between investor and extortionist could be perilously thin. And the threats were real. In 1997, music mogul Gulshan Kumar was shot dead outside a Mumbai temple. Three years later, filmmaker Rakesh Roshan survived an assassination attempt.

Sivanandhan investigated the financing of Chori Chori Chupke Chupke, a Salman Khan film whose producer and his assistant were each sentenced to six years in prison under Maharashtra's organised crime law for underworld links. The film's financier received a one-year sentence for withholding information about those links. Police seized the film's negatives, and its release eventually happened under court supervision.

Gangsters were not always imposing themselves on a reluctant industry. Some filmmakers willingly sought their money, Sivanandhan recalled, in arrangements that could benefit both sides and proved difficult for police to dismantle.

But Sivanandhan is reluctant to see the latest violence as a return of the mafia he once fought. The men he pursued - Dawood Ibrahim, Chhota Shakeel, Arun Gawli and others - belonged to criminal networks rooted in Mumbai, even when their leaders operated from abroad.

"This is not the Mumbai mafia which we know in the last 60 years," he said. "This is something else."

Gangster Lawrence Bishnoi has been locked up in prison since 2015

That something else has become inseparable from the name Lawrence Bishnoi. A gangster with roots in northern India who has been in jail for years, Bishnoi has acquired an aura of folkloric menace: a prisoner said to command gunmen across continents, his name invoked in threats and claims of responsibility that spread almost instantaneously online.

Bishnoi has publicly threatened Khan over a 1998 poaching case involving blackbucks, a species of antelope revered by the Bishnoi community. In April 2024, gunmen fired five rounds outside Khan's Mumbai home. Six months later, Baba Siddique, a politician close to Khan and other actors, was murdered. Mumbai police have linked both attacks to the Bishnoi gang, though they have not established any personal involvement by Lawrence Bishnoi in Siddique's killing.

Some allegations against his network are backed by police investigations; others remain untested. But the distinction between what the gang has demonstrably done and what is claimed in its name can become blurred in the public imagination, doing little to diminish Bishnoi's notoriety.

Rakesh Maria, a former Mumbai police commissioner, has cited Bishnoi as an example of a new generation of gangsters who are more technologically adept and internationally connected than their predecessors. "The old underworld was visible and territorial, but the new one is dispersed, fluid, making it harder to pin down," Maria told The Print, external last year. "They no longer need to reside in the city to command it."

Bishnoi's gang claimed responsibility for killing Baba Siddiqui (right) in 2024, citing his ties to Salman Khan (left)

The relationship between gangsters and Bollywood has changed in another way, too, in who gets to shape their image.

In the 1990s, Bollywood itself helped turn Mumbai's feared underworld figures into objects of fascination. Films such as Satya (1998) and Company (2002) brought a gritty, visceral realism to their world, while Manoj Bajpayee's portrayal of gangster Bhiku Mhatre in Satya became, in the words of film scholar Ashvin Immanuel Devasundaram, "mythologised and talismanic".

The fear and fascination of that era were "seared into the collective consciousness", Devasundaram told the BBC.

Each new attack brings those memories back, he added. But today's gangsters no longer need cinema to build their mythology. Social media allows them to shape their own image, circulating threats, claims of responsibility and carefully staged photographs to audiences far beyond Mumbai.

After the latest shooting, police reportedly stepped up security outside the homes of Khan and Rohit Shetty. The All Indian Cine Workers Association demanded greater protection, asking: "Today it is Tiger Shroff. Who is next?"

There is little evidence so far that criminal networks have regained their former influence over Bollywood's finances and decisions. But the latest attacks have exposed a different vulnerability.

Gangsters may no longer need to control an industry to unsettle it. A single shooting attempt, amplified across millions of phone screens, can spread fear far beyond its intended target.

Sivanandhan, who spent years confronting Mumbai's gangsters, knows the limits of even the tightest security.

"There is always a crack in the armour," he said.

Moderna and Merck reported on Oct. 22 that a customized cancer vaccine for people with late-stage melanoma can reduce the risk of cancer recurrence or death over two years by 42% compared to current treatment.

The results will be presented at the European Society of Medical Oncology meeting in Madrid on Oct. 24.

The study involved more than 1,130 people with Stage 2B to Stage 4 melanoma, all of whom had their tumors surgically removed. Some were then randomly assigned to have a cancer vaccine custom-made after their tumors had been genetically sequenced. They received that vaccine, called intismeran, once every three weeks for up to nine doses, along with Keytruda, a checkpoint inhibitor, every six weeks for up to nine cycles, for about a year. Others were randomly assigned to receive a placebo vaccine and Keytruda.

Intismeran, developed jointly by Moderna and Merck, represents a new way to harness the immune system in cancer, by training immune cells to recognize 34 specific proteins on tumor cells. The mRNA coding for these proteins, or antigens, are encased in a lipid particle in a similar way to Moderna’s COVID vaccine, which used mRNA from the COVID virus’ spike protein. Using this mRNA approach, once cells start making the foreign proteins coded for by the mRNA, the immune system sees them as intruders and mounts a response against them—and the latest data show that the approach works not just against viral proteins but also against those found on tumors.

Keytruda contributes a key part of the response by removing the brakes that keep immune cells from recognizing and attacking tumor cells because they originate from normal "self" cells.

“I was surprised by the fact that the results replicated the Phase 2 [study] so closely,” says Dr. David Berman, chief development officer at Moderna, referring to an earlier stage study the companies released in June that the combination lowered the risk of cancer returning or death by 49% over five years. That study also reported that the therapy lowered the risk of the cancer spreading to other sites by 59% compared to the checkpoint inhibitor alone. “This Phase 3 [study] included a much broader population, so the hypothesis was that the treatment effect would be harder to see. There was a lot of suspicion going in that these results would not be as good as those in the Phase 2. But the fact that they reproduced almost exactly what we saw initially in the Phase 2 blew my mind.”

The companies report that the effect seems to start six months into the therapy—an important insight in understanding how immune-based approaches may work in cancer. “We think it’s because the immune system takes time to be activated,” says Berman. That includes making sure that immune cells like T cells, which are critical to recognizing and controlling cancer, have time to learn to detect tumors. Berman says these results could also help to explain why only about 30% of cancer patients respond to the checkpoint inhibitors, a powerful new tool in cancer that allows immune cells to see tumors rather than thinking they are "self" cells and therefore not foreign. “This shows us that in a significant percentage of patients, the checkpoint inhibitors are not doing anything because the immune system hasn’t even been educated to recognize the cancer cells,” he says of the potential role that the mRNA-based intismeran may play. “That’s where we now need to focus—on how to better educate the immune system. It’s a big leap, a big piece of information, that there is now another way to educate the immune system to recognize cancer.”

Data on overall survival of people on the combination therapy isn’t available yet.

Moderna and Merck are studying the intismeran-Keytruda pairing in other cancer types in nine other studies, such as pancreatic and gastric cancer, as well as in three late-stage trials of non-small cell lung cancer. They are also exploring the role of using intismeran alone in an early study in lung cancer.

Traditionally, personalized therapies have not been practical as medical treatment because of their high cost and long timeline to develop. But Berman is confident that the bespoke process of making an individualized mRNA cancer vaccine for each patient is both scalable and potentially affordable. “We have scaled down the technical footprint and have plans to scale up [the program] to look at different tumors,” he says. “The nice thing about the [mRNA] system is that it’s purely enzymatic, so no cell cultures need to be grown.” Another advantage of using mRNA is its speed—scientists at Moderna can develop a customized vaccine based on a patient’s tumor within weeks—critical for any patient with cancer.

The original version of this story misstated the length of time of a study that showed a 49% reduction in recurrence of cancer. It was over five years, not over a year.

El nuevo Consejo para la prevención del blanqueo se constituirá antes de las elecciones

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

El departamento que lidera Carlos Cuerpo constituye un órgano para canalizar la colaboración público-privada en la lucha contra el blanqueo de capitales y la financiación del terrorismo.

Tras la aprobación, hace unos meses, del Plan Estratégico Nacional de Lucha contra el Blanqueo de Capitales y la Financiación del Terrorismo 2026-2030 y del anteproyecto de ley antiblanqueo que servirá para crear la nueva Autoridad Nacional de Integridad Financiera (Anifi), el Ministerio de Economía que lidera el vicepresidente primero del Gobierno, Carlos Cuerpo, publicó ayer la orden ministerial por la que da luz verde a la constitución del Consejo Nacional para la Prevención del Blanqueo de Capitales, la Financiación del Terrorismo y la Financiación de la Proliferación de Armas de Destrucción Masiva.

Se trata de un órgano, según explican desde el Ejecutivo, que servirá “como vía de encuentro” para articular y canalizar la “colaboración público-privada de forma estructurada y permanente, con un marco normativo específico que permita el correcto ejercicio de sus funciones”.

Entre ellas, se encuentra la promoción de la colaboración entre las autoridades competentes y los sujetos obligados en el ámbito de la prevención del blanqueo de capitales, la financiación del terrorismo o de la proliferación de las armas de destrucción masiva, así como para la implementación de “sanciones financieras internacionales”. También el intercambio de información “estratégica, táctica y operativa” entre las autoridades y los sujetos obligados, así como entre estos entre sí.

Asimismo, el nuevo Consejo elaborará informes, guías y recomendaciones en lo relativo a los riesgos y vulnerabilidades del sistema de prevención del blanqueo y la lucha contra el terrorismo; y propondrá a las autoridades iniciativas, acciones y prioridades de actuación para mejorar el sistema.

Según la orden ministerial publicada ayer en el BOE, el Pleno del Consejo, que estará formado por un presidente y 21 vocales deberá reunirse al menos una vez al año, si bien el nuevo órgano podrá constituir un comité ejecutivo formado por hasta diez miembros; así como comisiones técnicas encargadas del cumplimiento de funciones más específicas, “especialmente aquellas relacionadas con el intercambio de información operativa y estratégica”.

La primera reunión del Consejo Nacional para la Prevención del Blanqueo de Capitales, la Financiación del Terrorismo y la Financiación de la Proliferación de Armas de Destrucción Masiva se celebrará antes de las elecciones generales del 29 de noviembre. Economía ha dado “el plazo máximo de un mes” a todos los actores implicados para constituir la mesa, por lo que, como tarde, debería celebrarse en las jornadas anteriores al domingo electoral.

El nuevo Consejo de Prevención del Blanqueo de Capitales es diferente a la Autoridad Nacional de Integridad Financiera (Anifi), cuya creación avanzó EXPANSIÓN a finales de julio y que el Gobierno incluyó en el anteproyecto de ley antiblanqueo. El texto también incorporaba nuevos sujetos obligados a someterse al control del blanqueo, como proveedores de servicios de criptoactivos, plataformas de financiación participativa o clubes de fútbol.

Según confirman a este diario fuentes gubernamentales, la constitución de este organismo, que pretendía unificar las competencias de supervisión, inspección y sanción contra el blanqueo de capitales en España, se ha paralizado tras la convocatoria de elecciones anticipadas y la derogación del anteproyecto, si bien el Ministerio de Economía podría recuperar su creación más adelante si Sánchez continúa en La Moncloa tras los comicios.

En cualquier caso, el Ejecutivo que se constituya después del 29 de noviembre deberá adaptar la norma española al paquete europeo antiblanqueo, aprobado en 2024 y cuya fecha límite de transposición es el 10 de julio de 2027.

El Gobierno lanza “reducciones fiscales excepcionales” para el sector agrario en 2027, con un recorte general del 10% en el rendimiento neto aplicable en el IRPF, y acicates por la compra de gasóleo y fertilizantes.

“Producimos tu comida; merecemos vivir de nuestro trabajo”. Bajo este lema, los agricultores y ganaderos españoles volverán a movilizarse el próximo 28 de octubre, miércoles. Lo harán con una gran tractorada por las calles de Madrid con la que buscan escenificar su malestar por la concatenación de problemas antiguos y nuevos. Entre ellos sobresalen el aumento de los costes de producción por el fuerte encarecimiento de la energía, lo que está golpeando aún más la escasa rentabilidad de muchas explotaciones; una Política Agraria Común (PAC) que amenaza con recortar los fondos europeos que recibe el sector; unos precios en origen que siguen siendo bajos, y la denuncia constante del sector de que Europa no garantiza la reciprocidad en los acuerdos comerciales con terceros países. Todo ello aderezado por una burocracia que, según los agricultores, les implica crecientes cargas y les impide dedicar más tiempo a producir.

Con este telón de fondo, en el que las principales organizaciones agrarias ven del todo insuficiente la prórroga hasta el 31 de diciembre del descuento de 20 céntimos por litro al gasóleo agrícola para combatir la fuerte subida de costes, el Gobierno ha lanzado varios guiños fiscales a un colectivo con el que su relación ha estado -y sigue- marcada por los altibajos, a pesar de los esfuerzos del Ejecutivo por desmarcarse de Bruselas en determinadas cuestiones, como los recortes de la PAC, y alinearse así con los agricultores.

El Gobierno abrió ayer el periodo de información pública del proyecto de orden de módulos del IRPF para 2027, que, según informó el Ministerio de Agricultura, que lidera Luis Planas, “incluye reducciones fiscales excepcionales para agricultores y ganaderos”.

Esos incentivos se resumen esencialmente en una reducción general del 10% del rendimiento neto aplicable a todas las actividades agrarias en el Impuesto sobre la Renta, frente al 5% que se mantiene para el resto de actividades económicas, y en bonificaciones que afectan a dos componentes esenciales para la actividad agrícola: los fertilizantes y el gasóleo agrario. En concreto, el proyecto de orden de módulos plantea una reducción del 35% en el IRPF por la compra de gasóleo profesional y del 15% por la adquisición de fertilizantes, indispensables para el campo y cuyo precio, al igual que el de la energía, se ha visto disparado por la inestabilidad en Oriente Próximo y el prolongado bloqueo del estrecho de Ormuz.

Según los cálculos del Ejecutivo, estos guiños fiscales al campo supondrán en la práctica un tijeretazo de unos 860 millones de euros en la base imposible del IRPF. Es decir, un significativo balón de oxígeno económico para el sector cuyo objetivo, de acuerdo con la cartera que encabeza Luis Planas, es precisamente “paliar los efectos en la rentabilidad de las explotaciones agrarias del incremento de los precios del gasóleo y los fertilizantes como consecuencia del conflicto en Oriente Medio”, señaló ayer Agricultura en un comunicado.

Se estima que estos acicates fiscales, que se anuncian menos de una semana antes de la gran tractorada convocada por Asaja, Coag y Unión de Uniones en la capital de España, y en plena precampaña de unas elecciones decisivas para trazar el futuro rumbo político, económico y social de España, beneficiarán a los 800.000 agricultores y ganaderos que tributan por el método de estimación objetiva del IRPF; esto es, por el sistema de módulos.

Las medidas anunciadas ayer por el Gobierno son en realidad una suerte de continuación de las que se vienen aplicando desde 2022 por culpa de diferentes crisis y shocks. “Estas medidas excepcionales de aplicación en el ejercicio de 2026 son similares a las que estuvieron vigentes en los años 2022, 2023 y 2024 para hacer frente a los efectos en las explotaciones agrarias de la guerra en Ucrania y de la sequía”, explicó el Ministerio de Agricultura. Ahora, el epicentro del seísmo que golpea al campo español se sitúa en Oriente Próximo, que ha resucitado el fantasma de la crisis inflacionaria.

El periodo de consulta pública de la nueva orden de módulos concluirá el próximo 3 de noviembre.

Sus reivindicaciones son muchas y gran parte vienen de antiguo, pero en la gran tractorada convocada para el miércoles de la semana que viene en Madrid el sector agroganadero centrará sus protestas en cinco grandes cuestiones. -En primer lugar, la exigencia de un plan de choque al Gobierno frente a los costes de producción, “con la implantación inmediata de un gasóleo profesional agrario”. Esto es, el sector demanda “una fiscalidad reducida y estable, con bonificaciones aplicadas directamente al repostar”. -En segundo lugar, “una PAC fuerte que proteja la renta de agricultores y ganaderos” ante la incertidumbre que en estos momentos rodea el futuro de la política agraria europea. El sector rechaza cualquier recorte presupuestario y demanda financiación suficiente. -En tercer lugar, que, de una vez por todas, haya “una Ley de la Cadena Alimentaria que se cumpla” y permita al sector cobrar unos “precios justos” por sus productos. -En cuarto, se revuelve contra lo que considera competencia desleal en los acuerdos comerciales con terceros países o bloques y reitera su rechazo a tratados como el de la UE-Mercosur en sus condiciones actuales o el pacto con Marruecos, exigiendo reciprocidad normativa y controles efectivos en frontera. -Y en quinto y último lugar, el campo pide “menos papeles y menos normas”. Esto es, una descarga y simplificación de la carga burocrática que el sector se ve obligado a afrontar. “Agricultores y ganaderos quieren poder trabajar en sus explotaciones en lugar de dedicar cada vez más tiempo a trámites, registros y obligaciones administrativas”, señalan las organizaciones convocantes de la movilización, que creen llegado el momento de “dejar claro de forma contundente que el campo necesita soluciones para poder seguir produciendo y viviendo de su trabajo”.

Palmer Luckey and Ben Lamm Made a Genetically Modified Military Dog Startup

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new startup launched today with the definitely not terrifying mission of creating genetically enhanced dogs for the United States military, law enforcement, and emergency first responders.

Warhound is the brainchild of Anduril’s Palmer Luckey and Colossal Biosciences cofounder Ben Lamm. The startup has raised $200 million to “rebuild America’s domestic supply of elite working dogs” and reduce dependency on European dog breeders for military canines.

Lamm tells WIRED that Warhound is in the process of gene-editing its first batch of dogs. The startup has already collected blood samples from specific "top-performing” military, police, and first responder dogs in the United States, and its goal is to clone them. Breeds will be “mostly Belgian Malinois, some labs, and some shepherds,” he says. The company anticipates the first round of puppies to be born in early 2027.

The initial rounds of gene-editing will focus on eliminating common health conditions that impact these breeds, like hip dysplasia. Further down the line, Lamm envisions expanding to “really interesting things,” such as incorporating olfactory receptor genes from wolves to “enhance” the military dogs. (Colossal Biosciences is perhaps best known for its work on gene-edited wolves for its so-called Dire Wolf De-Extinction Project.)

Warhound, based in Texas, will also equip its canines with “Anduril’s advanced cybernetic Augments, software, and battlefield situational awareness,” according to a press release. Asked if this means the dogs will be some kind of cyborgs, Lamm clarifies that the plan right now is to supply them with tools like “advanced body armor” rather than, say, embedding some sort of computer in their dog bodies.

“So no dogs with lasers?” WIRED asked Lamm.

“I can’t comment on what Anduril plans,” he says. “Dog safety and performance, and then helping continue helping to improve the human handler and dog bond, and working as a team—that is what the mission is.”

Neither Luckey nor Lamm will be involved in Warhound’s day-to-day, although Lamm is on the company’s board, he says. David Spirk, a former Department of Defense chief data officer who most recently worked at Palantir, is serving as chief executive, and investors include In-Q-Tel, Sequoia Capital, and ARK Invest. Luckey is not an owner and does not have equity in the company, nor does Anduril; Colossal Biosciences does have an ownership stake.

Luckey and Lamm have been working on the project for over a year, Lamm says, and just recently decided to take the company out of stealth. “Someone said, ‘So it's like the Captain America dog program with the Ironman tech program on top of it,’” Lamm tells WIRED.

Everyone can agree that this is normal, and good, and that when the precision-designed beasts arrive on Earth, they will recognize that we are not the enemy.

The company plans to operate under the guidance of the American Humane Society.

Child health in England at risk from sharp rise in NHS waiting times, report says

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CQC research finds long delays are making conditions harder to treat and highlights regional disparities in care

Thousands of children in England are experiencing some of the sharpest rises in waiting times for healthcare in the history of the NHS, a report has revealed.

While some adult waiting times have improved, children and young people are being “hit hardest” amid soaring demand and falling numbers of staff, according to the annual state of care report by the Care Quality Commission.

In community health services alone, 80,000 children and young people have been waiting more than a year for an appointment – almost 11 times the number of adults, the report said. Experts said that was “wholly unacceptable”. Children’s health conditions were getting worse and becoming harder to treat as a result, they added.

More broadly across health and social care services in England there were signs of improvement, including more GP appointments, increased use of digital solutions and shorter waits for elective care. The report also highlighted examples of local innovation, including neighbourhood models and integrated care teams.

But improvements were struggling to keep pace with rapidly rising demand for health and social care. In many areas, including mental health services, patients’ needs are also becoming more complex.

Persistent inequalities across the entire health and care system mean people’s experience of care is fractured and heavily dependent on where they live and who they are, according to the report.

Across all areas of the country and every part of the health and social care system, there was “unwarranted variation” in access to services, quality of care and outcomes, the CQC said.

Some adults with cancer were waiting far too long to begin treatment after being diagnosed, while there remained “sustained pressure” in urgent and emergency care.

But the report highlighted how children in particular were bearing the brunt of long waiting times. And, despite growing demand, health visitor and school nurse numbers were “continuing to decline”, the CQC said.

It also raised concerns about regional variations in rates of children being admitted to hospital for diabetes and asthma, and sounded the alarm over particularly high rates of rotten teeth among children in some areas.

Dr Ronny Cheung, of the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health, said community health services existed to support some of the country’s most vulnerable children outside hospital, including those with complex conditions, developmental disorders and safeguarding needs.

“Yet these children face some of the longest waits in the NHS, while waiting times for adult community services have improved. It is wholly unacceptable that more than one in four children still wait over a year for treatment, and without urgent action, too many more will be left to suffer unnecessarily.

“As a paediatrician, I am sick of seeing children wait so long for support that they miss crucial opportunities to improve their health and development.”

Behind the figures were children struggling at school, families pushed to breaking point and conditions becoming more complex while they wait, Cheung added.

The CQC chair, Julia Neuberger, said that while some health and social care services were “working well”, improvements were not keeping pace with demand. “To repair a fracturing system, there must be a collective response,” she added.

The Department of Health and Social Care said the report showed “the scale of the challenge this government inherited” when it was elected in 2024. “While the CQC recognises signs of progress on waiting lists, cancer care and general practice, patients and families rightly expect faster improvements – which is why the secretary of state has committed to go further to end corridor care, transform support for mothers and children, and deliver a new national care service,” a spokesperson added.

Trump’s Repudiation Is at Hand, Right?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Is this, finally, the repudiation of Donald Trump? In decades of covering American politics, I cannot think of a midterm election which has more directly posed that question about a President—never mind one where all indicators point, ever more starkly, to a straightforward answer: Yes.

As Trump has barnstormed around the country this fall, he has reverted to the playbook he tried in his first midterm campaign. “Pretend, please,” he says wherever he goes, “that I’m on the ballot.” As always, he wants this to be the Trump, Trump, Trump election. The problem for the President is that that’s precisely what it is shaping up to be—and, with only days left before his November 3rd reckoning, Americans overwhelmingly do not like him, his policies, or his party.

By just about any metric except the obscene, record-setting amounts of cash that Trump and a small circle of billionaires are spending to save the Republicans’ Senate majority, this fall’s contest looks like the wipeout that so many have predicted (and so often been embarrassingly wrong about) since Trump first entered politics 4,146 days ago: historically low approval ratings, an unpopular war with Iran that has led to high prices and surging inflation, corruption so flagrant that two-thirds of Americans now believe he is profiting off the Presidency. Most notably, Trump’s sagging popularity is not just with Democrats and independents but with many Republicans in red states whose unwavering backing has, time and again, saved him during this insane past decade in American politics.

In the final Times poll of the election cycle, Democrats had an eleven-point advantage in the generic ballot, which is four points higher than in 2018, when the Party picked up forty-one seats and regained the House. As for the issues driving voters, the Democratic pollster John Anzalone summed up the current state of the race. “Democrats lead on everything,” he wrote on X after the Times poll was released. “Like Literally Everything!”

The result has been an entirely unexpected series of tossups in the American heartland, where control of the Senate—once seen as unattainable by Democrats given this year’s map—may be decided in states such as Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, and Texas, none of which have elected a Democrat to that chamber in decades. (Ninety-four years, in the case of Kansas.) Democrats also have a strong chance to win a majority of state governorships for the first time since 2010. In the race for the House, where Democrats need to pick up only three seats, the Party is seen as a virtual lock to win.

This is not just a matter of Democratic hopium. Republicans began panicking weeks ago, spending hundreds of millions of dollars on ads in states they once reckoned on winning comfortably and all but withdrawing from previously competitive Senate seats in places, such as North Carolina and Georgia, where Trump won decisively just two years ago. On Monday, Trump will campaign in Mississippi—staunchly Republican Mississippi—where the Senate seat is now officially “in play.” As for the House, “exceedingly grim” G.O.P. insiders have been telling reporters for weeks that they are braced for a midterm blowout.

“The economic center of the American electorate is collapsing on Republicans,” Greg Strimple, a Republican pollster, declared on Tuesday. His new joint survey with a Democratic colleague for the Cook Political Report found Democratic Senate candidates narrowly leading in Iowa, Ohio, and Texas—all states which voted three times for Trump. According to Strimple, “Voters are so upset with the lack of leadership on reducing their cost of living, they appear willing to propel Democrats to upset victories in reliably Republican states despite the fact that Democrats are less popular than either President Trump or Republicans. The level of voter discontent is truly unprecedented.”

So why, then, are so many Democrats no more than what Rahm Emanuel called, in an interview for The New Yorker’s Political Scene podcast the other day, “small ‘c,’ small ‘o,’ cautiously optimistic” about their prospects for dealing Trump a decisive blow by winning the Senate?

History suggests one obvious reason. Trump may never have been President at all if not for the Party’s fatal overconfidence about him, in 2016. In the final two weeks before that election, with some national polls giving Hillary Clinton a seemingly insurmountable double-digit lead, her doomed campaign did not even bother to send the Democratic nominee to the decisive battleground state of Wisconsin, instead looking to expand the map to traditionally red states such as Arizona and Utah. Even in 2018, when Democrats did come back from Clinton’s defeat with a strong performance, the split decision—with Republicans keeping the Senate even as Democrats won the House—meant that Trump was unlikely to face real accountability or meaningful constraints from Congress. It was seen as a rebuke, not a Presidency-ending repudiation.

The point here is that winning in American elections is about expectations as much as who claims more seats. At the start of 2026, Democrats taking back the House and running competitively for the Senate across a daunting map might have been seen as a rousing recovery from the Party’s poor state two years ago. Now, even though polls show that control of the Senate remains up for grabs, with key races all within the margin of error, the baseline has shifted. As a Democratic friend said to me the other day, “I’ll be disappointed if we don’t win the Senate that I did not expect to win a month ago.”

There is also the question of what lesson the parties take from the election. In 2022, Democrats bought their own spin about how successfully they’d held off a red wave, convincing themselves that losing the House by only five seats and holding on to a bare fifty-one-seat majority in the Senate was a great victory. The better-than-expected performance helped spur Joe Biden to announce that he planned to run for another term, and we all know how that worked out. After Trump’s 2024 win, Republicans believed a political realignment of such importance had taken place that they undertook an unprecedented mid-decade redistricting designed to blunt the Democrats’ advantage going into this year’s midterms. At the start of this year, that redistricting looked like it could help them gain as many as a dozen seats; now, with many 2024 Trump voters turning against the President in places such as South Texas and Florida, the net effect might be as little as one seat.

But, as the election that might spell the beginning of the end of Trump’s long, strange hold on the American electorate finally looms, it’s not the hubris of the victors or the inevitable flood of bad punditry that has me most concerned. It is, as always, the President himself.

Ever since he returned to office, Trump has been hedging against the possibility of a midterm embarrassment by preëmptively attacking the results, questioning the legality of mail-in voting, pretending there is an epidemic of illegal noncitizen voting, and threatening to mobilize the considerable federal resources at his disposal on Election Day and beyond. The Times found that he has publicly cast doubt on the elections on more than seventy per cent of the days since his second Inauguration took place, with recent comments focussing on “rigged polls,” which he said, “have ALWAYS underestimated MAGA.” In the post-2020 world, is there anyone among us who thinks we should not take these threats seriously?

In private, many Democrats (and I’m sure plenty of Republicans, too) self-soothe with talk of a blue wave that will be too big to rig. No less a stolid figure than the former Treasury Secretary Robert Rubin is so alarmed about this complacency that he warned the public this week of the “meaningful risk” that Trump might seek to interfere with or outright “usurp” the election results. I have no doubt that, if the President who was willing to unleash a mob on America’s Capitol rather than concede defeat sees a way to try, he will.

Most of all, I am sure of this: Trump remains who he has always been. He has never publicly accepted blame for anything, and he will not start now. There will be no course correction, no pivot to the center. He will not, as George W. Bush and Barack Obama both did after their midterm shellackings, acknowledge the voters’ anger and vow to do better by working across party lines. The worse Trump and his Republicans fare on Election Night, the more we should worry about his response. Repudiated Trump may turn out to be the most dangerous Trump of all. ♦

Scoop: Some Freedom Caucus members want Brandon Gill out

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

A small group of Rep. Brandon Gill's fellow House Freedom Caucus members are privately questioning whether he still belongs in the hardline group, Axios has learned.

Why it matters: The 32-year-old Texas freshman's rapid rise through House Republican ranks has transformed him into one of the party's most prominent young stars — and earned him a growing list of critics.

Driving the news: Multiple House Republicans told Axios that Gill is the future of their party, but several Freedom Caucus members told Axios that his engagement with the group has faded as his national profile has grown.

  • The lawmakers spoke on condition of anonymity to offer candid assessments of a colleague.
  • "He's not really in the Freedom Caucus. He's just using it for conservative bonafides," one HFC member told Axios.
  • The Freedom Caucus is "livid" at Gill, another member told Axios.

The intrigue: Four sources familiar with the conversations said members have privately discussed kicking Gill out, though the conversations have been informal and the issue has not been raised at a HFC meeting.

  • It's unclear if there would be enough support within the group to boot Gill if there ever was a formal vote.

The other side: "Congressman Gill is laser-focused on getting out the vote and ensuring Republicans hold the majority this November," a spokesperson for Gill told Axios in a statement.

State of play: Gill has become a favorite in President Trump's orbit, has won praise from GOP leadership and has launched an ambitious bid to head one of the House's most powerful committees.

  • Asked about the criticism from Gill's colleagues, Majority Whip Tom Emmer (R-Minn.) flatly told Axios in an interview earlier this month that Gill "needs to be out front. I don't care where it is," adding, "Brandon is the future of our party, and his communication skills are phenomenal."
  • But his ascent has fueled resentment, with colleagues accusing him of burning bridges and treating fellow Republicans as stepping stones to greater power and influence.

Zoom in: Members say Gill benefited from introductions to HFC donors but hasn't adequately financially supported fellow members in difficult races.

  • "For those of us who have been in meetings, who have been in the tough votes over the last two years, he's been M.I.A.," one HFC member said, adding that he's "totally looking for the ladder climbing."
  • Gill, who is in a R+11 district, raised $4.7 million in the third quarter, a massive haul for a freshman member.
  • Gill's office pointed out that he's given to over a dozen HFC members' campaigns.

What they're saying: Freedom Caucus Chair Andy Harris (R-Md.) accused the media of "ginning up stories about Republicans based on anonymous sources," insisting the group is "united and laser-focused" on advancing Trump's agenda.

  • One HFC member defended Gill, telling Axios: "I'm glad that young talented patriots like Brandon are not only coming into politics but that they are climbing quickly...Of course I would like to see him stay in HFC."
  • "I'm not frustrated with Brandon at all," Rep. Mark Harris (R-N.C.), also a freshman member of the caucus, told Axios. "He comes to meetings. I don't know why they feel like he doesn't."

"HFC is its own worst enemy sometimes," one GOP member told Axios. "They've managed to kick out, alienate some of their members that help them fundraise."

  • The Freedom Caucus has kicked out members before. In 2023, the group voted to oust former Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-Ga.) over a fight with Rep. Lauren Boebert (R-Colo.), and in 2024, the group ousted former Rep. Ken Buck (R-Colo.) for "nonattendance."

Friction point: Gill's bid to become the top Republican on the powerful Oversight Committee is also creating friction beyond the Freedom Caucus.

  • Republicans Axios spoke with questioned whether Gill's success at generating viral hearing moments makes him qualified to lead the committee, while others have raised concerns about his history of publicly attacking fellow Republicans like Rep. Maria Salazar (R-Fla.) over her immigration bill.
  • "He's kind of skipping a few steps here, and he doesn't necessarily have the credentials to do it," one House Republican told Axios.
  • Gill has also sought to build influence within the broader conference, contributing $1.1 million to the National Republican Congressional Committee in September.

Behind the scenes: A House Republican said Gill stopped returning texts and began treating colleagues differently as his national profile grew, echoing complaints from Gill's Freedom Caucus colleagues.

  • The lawmaker told Axios that there was a sentiment among some Republicans that Gill's fame had gone to his head.
  • Gill, who is president of his freshman class, is a frequent guest on Fox News, including during coveted primetime hours.

The big picture: The Freedom Caucus is on the cusp of an identity crisis — at least 10 of HFC's more than two dozen members will not return next term, including some of its most prominent voices.

  • Gill has been viewed by some in the caucus as someone who could fill that void and emerge as a leading voice for the next generation.
  • But the group could gain influence at the highest levels of leadership: Founding member Jim Jordan (R-Ohio) is being floated as a potential minority leader if Republicans lose the House.
  • That could reshape the group's relationship with GOP leadership, which the hard-line caucus has made a habit of challenging.

The bottom line: "He's already kind of left the Freedom Caucus," an HFC member told Axios. "The only question is whether you formalize it or you don't."

What to Know About Shamonda Virus Following the First Reported Human Infection

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

France confirmed the death of the first person infected with the Shamonda virus, which is known to affect cattle, sheep, and goats and was previously considered non-transmissible to humans. The French government asserts that, based on current knowledge of this pathogen, there is no risk to the general public. It emphasized, however, that health authorities are continuing to investigate.

The report specifies that the case occurred in Lyon. The patient, who died earlier this month, suffered from severe immunosuppression, making this likely an exceptional case. “The neurological complications observed in the immunocompromised patient prior to his death were most likely related to infection with the Shamonda virus,” the official statement notes.

According to preliminary evidence, the patient contracted Shamonda virus from an insect bite. A tiny gnat known as the biting midge is known to be the primary route of transfer between animals. Since the virus was first identified in Nigeria in the mid-1960s, sporadic infections have been reported in livestock across Africa as well as in Japan. Cattle are considered the primary host because they develop visible symptoms. (Although sheep and goats can become infected, no obvious clinical signs have been documented in these animals.)

After animal is bitten by a midge carrying Shamonda virus, the infection has an incubation period of two to three days. Symptoms observed in animals include fever, diarrhea, and a decrease in milk production. When pregnant cows have become infected, the virus can cause miscarriages or severe deformations in the calf.

To date, there is no vaccine, so prevention and control measures rely on the use of insecticides and repellents. That doesn’t mean you should panic. Public health authorities are considering the case to be “exceptional,” because the deceased patient was severely immunocompromised.

Still, several European countries are on alert, because until a few months ago, this region was considered free of the virus. However, this summer, a new variant known as Shamonda-Europe was detected in livestock in Switzerland. Other cases were subsequently identified in Germany, France, Austria, Belgium, the Netherlands, Liechtenstein, Italy, and Spain.

Spain’s Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, and Food states that two genetically distinct forms of the virus, known as Shamonda Type 1 and Shamonda Type 2, have been identified circulating in Europe. It adds that, in early September, infections of both types were confirmed on livestock farms in 37 provinces across France.

The case documented in France constitutes the first known infection in a person.

“This is an important scientific finding. However, it is important to put this into perspective. The patient had a serious underlying illness that left them particularly vulnerable to infections, and we do not yet know whether Shamonda virus contributed to their death,” Ben Brennan, professor of virology at the University of Glasgow, said in a statement.

It’s premature to draw conclusions about the virus and it’s potential to infect humans, agreed David Roiz, a scientist Institute for Game and Wildlife Research in Spain. Many viruses “circulate naturally amongst mammals in tropical regions and cause sporadic cases in humans,” he said. “In a globalized world, where animals and goods are on the move and the climate is changing, it is not unusual for cases of human transmission to occur, and we should not be concerned; we simply need to monitor the situation, remain vigilant, and be proactive in addressing potential health risks.”
