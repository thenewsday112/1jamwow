France: Protesters clash with police in nationwide protests, amid rising social polarization

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Unrest broke out in parts of France as students, trade unionists and Yellow Vest activists took to the streets to protest high prices and inequality. Police arrested over 1,000 people amid chaotic scenes.

Police and protesters clashed amid mass rallies across France on Saturday, over high prices and social inequalities and the state of the education system.

France's Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu said some 1,200 people had been arrested across the country and that 150 police officers had been injured.

"It's a miracle the toll wasn't higher," Lecornu said while visiting officers deployed in Paris on Saturday evening.

Interior Minister Laurent Nunez said 110,000 people took part in the demonstrations in Paris alone, while 110 marches had taken place across the country.

The Interior Ministry said some 550,000 people turned out for the nationwide demonstrations.

The ministry said the arrests were made following violent incidents in Paris, Marseilles, Rennes, Toulouse, Angers and other cities across the country.

"Such actions include attempted looting, confrontations with law enforcement, the setting of fires to street furniture and the targeting of seats of power and media outlets," the ministry said, blaming the unrest on what it described ​as the "hard-left."

In Paris and Marseille, police fired tear gas at protesters as violent clashes took place.

AFP reported that dozens of shops were looted in Paris, Marseille, Nice, Bordeaux and Lille.

The windows of the offices of French broadcasters CNews and Europe 1 in western Paris were also broken by protesters, and AFP journalists reported seeing several parked cars torched.

French President Emmanuel Macron condemned the violence in a post on X and warned: "Those who have broken, attacked the prefectures as well as businesses, looted even our town halls, will be prosecuted and punished."

In Lyon, situated in central France, the city's tourism office was set alight with flames and black smoke seen billowing from the building.

In the city of Limoges, the town hall was set alight as unrest broke out. The blaze spread from a wing of the building and eventually reached the roof, where it continued burning as crowds stood watching.

The historic building was largely destroyed by the blaze, despite attempts by firefighters to douse the flames.

Meanwhile, in the northern city of Lille, protesters smashed their way into the city hall and vandalized the premises before leaving, according to an AFP photographer.

Riot police within minutes confirmed that all the protesters had left.

The protests come on the heels of an initial wave of demonstrations that began last month over the situation at schools in the country, with pupils complaining about teacher absences and run-down facilities.

In the French capital, police had banned rallies around the Elysee presidential palace, the prime minister's office and the Champs-Elysees avenue, along with other key locations.

A march through the center of the city was also banned.

Reports began emerging late on Saturday morning of riot police clashing with protesters in the Saint-Denis area on the outskirts of the capital, as well as near the Gare du Nord train station, among other areas.

In Paris, DW correspondent Jack Parrock said unrest was playing out in small pockets of the city.

"The videos circulating on social media of the violence in France don’t paint the full picture," Parrock said.

"The significant percentage of the people we met out on the streets of Paris were teachers, nurses, students, and workers, who want no part in the unrest, but do also seem to understand why the outbreaks are happening," Parrock explained.

"Amongst those people, we did still witness a collective rage against a government they don’t feel is listening to them," said Parrock.

On the matter of how police were responding, Parrock said they had seen police "seemingly try to show some restraint — clearly intending to de-escalate some of the recent tension."

Students have been joined by trade unionists and remnants of the Yellow Vests ("Gilets jaunes") movement, which staged mass weekly protests from November 2018 to June 2020, initially against rising fuel prices. They later morphed into protests against the high cost of living and economic inequality in general.

The student protests over the past weeks have been backed by Jean-Luc Melenchon, the leader of the hard-left France Unbowed ("La France insoumise", or LFI), who marched in one demonstration on Tuesday.

The government has blamed the LFI for pouring fuel on the fire of widespread public discontent, an allegation the party has denied.

France: Young voters blase about Glucksmann's Socialists

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Raphael Glucksmann wants to lead France's Socialists to power without working with the left-populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, who is particularly popular with young voters. How can the center-left candidate win them over?

The historically working-class Parisian neighborhood of Belleville has gentrified in recent years. It was here that Raphael Glucksmann, a member of the European Parliament for France's center-left Socialists, celebrated his victory in the party's presidential primary on October 11.

Two rounds of voting had been anticipated, but the 46-year-old son of philosopher and essayist Andre Glucksmann triumphed in the first round with a convincing 66% of the vote and will be the Socialist candidate in France's 2027 presidential election.

As he left the Cantine de Belleville restaurant, Glucksmann was flour-bombed by an unidentified assailant. The following morning, he called on his rival, the left-wing French populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, to condemn attacks carried out by his supporters, though it is not clear that the attacker was indeed a supporter of Melenchon's La France Insoumise (LFI).

The incident has highlighted the issue set to dog Glucksmann's presidential candidacy over the coming weeks: How can he win over Melenchon's supporters if he refuses to work with their candidate?

"Where do you stand on Melenchon?" That was the question that dominated much of the Socialists' primary campaign. Party leader Olivier Faure spoke in favor of leaving the door open for cooperation with the LFI, but Glucksmann called for a clear break with the far left. His decisive victory is therefore also seen as a vote in favor of a clear demarcation.

The other primary candidates questioned Glucksmann's political experience. Segolene Royal, a previous presidential candidate, compared his bid for the Elysee with that of an "intern" wanting to take charge of a company. She said Glucksmann had no experience in government or even running a local authority.

Despite Glucksmann's victory in the primary, his starting position is a difficult one. A poll by Toluna Harris Interactive, published the day after his win, projects that he would win 10–11% of the vote. The same poll put Melenchon on 18%, meaning the far-left man would be much more likely to reach the runoff. But the survey was conducted before Glucksmann won the primary, and so does not take into account any potential boost the nomination may give him. Still, 10% would be an improvement on the Socialists' performance in the last presidential election in 2022, when their candidate Anne Hidalgo won just 1.75% of the national vote.

There is also the complication that the Socialists are banking on a man who is not even a party member to lead them from irrelevance. Raphael Glucksmann actually belongs to a small center-left party called Place Publique.

Glucksmann has his work cut out for him — especially with young people. In an Ipsos survey in September, which posited Glucksmann as the social-democratic candidate and Edouard Philippe as the only candidate from the political center, Melenchon scored 41% among the under-25s. Meanwhile, 20% of this age group supported the far-right nationalist Marine Le Pen. France's young electorate is politically divided, but Melenchon still dominates on the left.

The 75-year-old is currently rallying alongside the students whose protests have drawn international attention. At a rally near Clermont-Ferrand in central France, he expressed solidarity with their movement and reiterated his calls for lowering the voting age to 16 and abolishing Parcoursup, France's national admissions procedure for higher education.

In an analysis for the Fondation Jean-Jaures, a Socialist-aligned think tank, the political scientist Antoine Bristielle distinguishes between a core group of committed LFI voters and the broader left. Melenchon's most loyal supporters, he says, are often young, educated city-dwellers, and many are under financial pressure. Four in 10 households in this group live on a net monthly income of less than €2,000 ($2,200).

Bristielle estimates that just under a third of the left-wing electorate in his study care more about a left-wing victory than their allegiance to any particular party. Almost half of the people in this group are younger than 35 and would consider voting for the Socialists, the Greens or LFI. Redistribution and an active government are important to them, but most would prefer politicians who are willing to compromise.

The pattern recently observed in Germany, where young women are more likely to vote for the left and young men for the right, does not apply in France. Political scientists Anja Durovic, Nonna Mayer and Noemie Piolat found that in the 2024 European and parliamentary elections, young French women were not significantly more likely to vote for the left, or less likely to vote for the far right, than men of the same age. It remains to be seen whether this pattern will be replicated in the 2027 presidential election.

Glucksmann says he would ease the burden on earned income by reducing France's CSG social security tax while increasing taxes on inheritances worth more than €2 million. He also presents himself as a committed supporter of European integration, while Melenchon has called the EU a neoliberal institution. Glucksmann's political profile combines social policy demands with foreign policy positions that could also appeal to voters from the camp of the current president, Emmanuel Macron.

France's Ukraine policy is one such issue. Glucksmann favors decisive support for Kyiv. Manon Aubry, a member of the European Parliament for Melenchon's LFI, says her party supports military assistance only under certain conditions, namely: The provision of aid to Ukraine must first be subject to democratic debate; it must not enable attacks deep inside Russian territory; and it must not weaken France's own defense capabilities. In 2024, LFI MPs voted against the French–Ukrainian security agreement.

The tension between the parties of the left was once again apparent in the aftermath of the flour attack in Belleville. The LFI MP Sebastien Delogu posted a declaration of support on X for the taxi driver who was going to have to clean his car.

Glucksmann must win over a lot of voters from Melenchon's camp if he is to have a chance of winning the presidency. This is likely to prove a much more difficult task than simply distancing himself from Melenchon.

STEPHEN GLOVER: Burnham is ducking all the big decisions while wasting time on the trivial and ridiculous. He has no idea how to save a country heading for bankruptcy

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Published: 01:00 GMT, 26 October 2026 | Updated: 01:25 GMT, 26 October 2026

Defence Secretary Wes Streeting tells us we must be ready for war in 1,000 days. He’s thinking of a Russian attack. Scary stuff.

His warning comes days after he launched a ‘national resilience conversation,’ when he asked us to prepare for disruption caused by natural disasters or cyber attacks by hostile states. He said we should all have three days of emergency supplies.

The Government is trying to make the hairs stand up on the back of our necks. War with Russia in less than three years! That isn’t just serious. It is apocalyptic.

Why, then, doesn’t the Government prepare for the life-or-death struggles it claims lie ahead? Why does it concentrate on the trivial or the ridiculous – or both – instead of rearming our depleted Armed Forces so that we can defend ourselves?

Ministers’ reactions to Streeting’s alarums make my point. When asked a few weeks ago how she would react to the Government’s ‘Be Ready’ campaign, Housing Secretary Angela Rayner said her disaster box would include mascara, concealer, baked beans and a bottle of Sauvignon Blanc.

I very much doubt that a single bottle of white wine would last Ms Rayner three whole days. She also forgot to mention an adequate supply of vapes.

Andy Burnham’s response to a similar question last week was even more absurd. He said he would stock up on baked beans, as well as soup. He then recommended Twiglets, without which he wasn’t sure he could survive for long.

I happen to share the Prime Minister’s fondness for Twiglets, but they are hardly vital for survival. No one asked Wes Streeting to invoke societal breakdown but, given that he has done so in such a heavy-handed manner, Burnham’s answer was inexcusably frivolous.

'The PM immerses himself in gimmicks', writes Stephen Glover, as the Government announces plans to clamp down on 'headphone dodgers' and fill in potholes

Since the ‘King of the North’ came down from Manchester to run the Government, it has seemed to me that his approach is similar to a guest at the tail end of a long dinner party who has drunk too much.

He loves to muse aloud. Wouldn’t it be nice if we did this? Or that? His latest thought is that it might be a good idea to have open borders again, though he isn’t categoric. Let’s have a conversation about it. Another glass, Andy? Oh, all right, just the one.

We have the highest taxes in peacetime history and a welfare budget spiralling out of control, with 1,000 new people signing on for disability benefits every day. And, as I’ve said, we have tragically weakened Armed Forces, even though Wes Streeting thinks we could be at war in 1,000 days.

These are huge problems, which would be challenging for the most determined and coherent of governments. But Andy Burnham and his crew have no intention of tackling them.

Instead, the PM immerses himself in gimmicks. In the past week or so, he has pledged to clamp down on ‘headphone dodgers’ who make a racket on public transport, bear down on private car park fines, and fill in potholes on 7,000 miles of roads.

Of course it would be wonderful if Andy dealt with these irritations. But we all know that louts won’t stop playing loud music on trains. Rogues will still slap tickets on our windscreens in car parks. Councils will probably go on spending money on things other than filling potholes.

The Prime Minister may realise deep down that he won’t deal with these issues. He’s trying to convey that he cares about the daily frustrations that drive us mad. He wants us to think he is on our wavelength. That he’s one of us – which is why he appeared last week on Channel 4’s Celebrity Gogglebox.

This is a dangerous policy. People will soon see that he has failed to solve the nuisances he identified. As prime minister, John Major introduced the ‘cones hotline’ in 1992. Thousands of calls were made but little or nothing was done, and unsightly traffic cones continued to litter our roads.

The big decisions are being ducked while Andy Burnham talks about Twiglets and appears on Gogglebox. I suppose he’s planning an early election

There’s an even more apposite comparison. In 1974, Labour prime minister Harold Wilson faced problems comparable in seriousness with those confronting Andy Burnham. Inflation was running at 26 per cent and the trade unions were doing their utmost to destroy British industry.

Wilson – who had just become prime minister for the second time, and was to last only until April 1976 – devoted much energy to a programme he described as ‘Little Things Mean a Lot’.

He had innumerable bees in his bonnet, which were doubtless shared by many people. He wanted to preserve local breweries. He disliked empty office blocks and juggernauts (which have grown much larger since 1974). He championed protection for caravan owners.

Not all of his causes were hopeless or cranky. Despite metrification, he defended the British pint of beer. We should thank him for that.

But most of what he did was fiddling while Rome burnt. He couldn’t solve the big problems that were literally ruining Britain, so he turned his attention to smaller matters, which occupied a great deal of his time and that of ministers.

So it is with Andy Burnham who, unlike Wilson in 1974, may – I stress ‘may’ – be at the beginning of his prime ministership rather than towards the end. He shirks the big challenges.

On Wednesday John Healey will deliver his first Budget. Since its contents have been widely leaked, it’s a reasonable bet that it will be a ‘steady as you go’ Budget that won’t tackle the terrifying problems threatening to bring the country down.

Despite largely sharing Wes Streeting’s fear of war when he was himself Defence Secretary, Healey is unlikely to increase front-line defence spending on Wednesday.

According to yesterday’s Sunday Times, the Chancellor will announce new investment in producing armaments such as drones. That is welcome, but it won’t address the critical shortage of Royal Navy ships, the smallest British Army for 200 years, and a much reduced RAF.

Both Burnham and Healey have already said there won’t be a decision to increase defence expenditure to 3 per cent of GDP until the completion of yet another spending review, which probably won’t happen until next autumn. By that time, a third of Streeting’s 1,000 days will have passed.

Difficult decisions are also being kicked into the long grass over welfare reform. This awaits the outcome of a report, in this case from former Labour Cabinet minister Alan Milburn, into young people not in education, employment or training. Will Burnham ever dare to cut welfare?

Nor is there any prospect of public spending – which stands at 45 per cent of GDP, compared with 39 per cent just before the pandemic – being reduced in the Budget. Our gargantuan debt will go on rising, whatever assurances Healey may give.

The big decisions are being ducked while Andy Burnham talks about Twiglets and appears on Gogglebox. I suppose he’s planning an early election, and hopes that being seen to care about everyday things will endear him to a cynical electorate.

God help us if this ploy works. Our essentially trivial Prime Minister may understand what irritates ordinary people, but he has no idea how to save a country heading for bankruptcy, or any plan to defend it from the Russian threat.

Poll finds Democrats still boosted by voters' economic concerns

Updated on: October 25, 2026 / 8:13 PM EDT / CBS News

Call it the "avoidance" election, with many on each side more motivated by trying to prevent or stop things than by what they'll gain.

Against a backdrop of continued concern about inflation and the economy, Democrats maintain their lead in a bid for a House majority. They're currently on pace for 229 seats in our CBS News House model estimate, which translates voters' congressional preferences into seats.

The potential range of outcomes could reach into the 230s in our model, but Republicans do still have a very narrow path to hold the majority, too.

While that Democratic lead has been durable over recent weeks, it hasn't expanded, either.

In other words, there isn't a growing wave, at least not as of today.

If anything, the still undecided or uncertain voters tend to profile as more Republican.

Whether they decide to show up is perhaps the key question of 2026.

Either way, voters describe a dynamic in which they're more motivated in opposition to things they think might happen. For instance:

More think gas prices will go up if Republicans win than if Democrats do.

(Though they don't think Democrats will make gas prices go down.)

Most voters don't think Democrats have a plan and won't make people financially better off, either...But more voters think they'll be worse off if Republicans win.

The president remains a central factor. It would be a high number voting either to support or oppose a president in a midterm if this sentiment holds through Election Day.

Most of the Democrats' voters are trying to oppose either President Trump or Republicans, even more than to help Democrats.

(Whether that rationale ultimately limits Democrats' gains or not is another key question.)

But he's motivating for most Republicans, especially MAGA.

Some of this lead is attributed to more Democrats saying they'll either definitely vote, or have already voted early.

But this is not over: The voters who describe themselves as only "probably" or "maybe" going to vote are more Republican. If they become motivated, this can all change.

Few voters think gas prices will go down no matter who wins.

But the GOP is now relatively more associated in voters' minds with rising gas prices: More think they'll go up if Republicans win than if Democrats do.

Most voters don't think they'll be better off financially, no matter who wins. That cynicism does permeate the electorate.

But as a choice, relatively more think they'll do better with Democrats.

Most voters think there will be more corruption in government if Republicans maintain control of Congress.

Both Democrats and Republicans — at about similar large majorities — see government corruption as a factor in their votes.

Mr. Trump looms large over the midterms, particularly for Democrats. More than seven in 10 voting for Democrats say their vote for Congress is to oppose him.

Mr. Trump is also a factor for those backing Republicans. Most say their vote is to support him, but to a lesser extent than Democrats are voting to oppose him.

For most who are backing Democrats, their motivation is more about stopping Mr. Trump and GOP policies than advancing the Democrats' own policies.

Here's how prevalent opposition to either party is as a motivating force this year.

Neither Democrats nor Republicans are perceived by a majority of voters as having a clear plan, should either win control of Congress.

There are four in 10 voters supporting Democrats who don't believe the party has a clear plan, should they win. They say opposition to Mr. Trump is the main motivator for them.

Meanwhile, there are four in 10 voting Republican who say their vote isn't about the president. They're less likely to consider themselves MAGA, tend to just "somewhat," rather than "strongly," approve of Mr. Trump. But a big majority of them have an unfavorable view of the Democratic Party – so they remain in the Republican camp.

Many voters feel a win by the other side is a threat to their way of life. Democrats think a Republican win would be a threat to theirs.

MAGA Republicans in particular, more so than non-MAGA, think the Democrats would be a threat.

Although the issue ranks behind others as a factor in voters' decisions, Republicans lead Democrats among those who consider transgender issues a major factor in their vote.

There are a third of voters who think the Democratic Party puts the interests of recent immigrants ahead of those of U.S. citizens, and voters who hold this view are backing Republicans in big numbers.

(Another third does think the Democratic Party prioritizes both groups, and these voters are supporting Democrats.)

Americans' overall ratings of the economy continue to be negative, as they have been for years. They've even dipped a bit lower now, to where they were last spring.

And most likely voters feel the president's policies are making them financially worse off.

The economy and inflation remain top concerns. Those calling inflation a very important issue mostly back Democrats, as do those who feel prices have been difficult or a hardship.

Against that backdrop, voters voice broader concern about whose financial interests they think the president prioritizes.

More feel he prioritizes his own financial interests and those of his family over the financial interests of the people in the country. And those who hold this view are backing Democrats.

Republicans mostly feel Mr. Trump prioritizes the financial interests of people in the country over his own.

The president's numerous appearances on the campaign trail may help to rally the base behind Republican candidates, but those events may not have as much impact with the rest of the party.

Most MAGA Republicans say that when they see Trump campaigning for political candidates, it makes them more likely to vote for Republicans.

For many non-MAGA Republicans, these campaign events do not do that.

Most voters express at least some confidence in their own local and county elections administration to conduct the election process, including determining eligibility and correctly counting ballots.

In turn, most tend to voice confidence that their own vote will be recorded and counted accurately in this election.

Scientists reveal the hidden danger of missing blood pressure medication

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Missing doses of blood pressure medication could significantly reduce its ability to protect against heart attacks, strokes, and other serious cardiovascular problems, according to findings presented at ESC Congress 2026.

An estimated 1.4 billion adults worldwide between the ages of 30 and 79 have high blood pressure (hypertension), a major contributor to illness and death. Although medications that lower blood pressure are proven to reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications, approximately half of patients do not take their prescriptions as directed.

"We have evidence that poor adherence to antihypertensive medication is associated with worse outcomes," explained presenter, Miss Qianqian Yang from the University of Oxford, UK. "However, previous data has come from observational studies and could be confounded by other factors. We undertook an analysis of randomized trial data to assess the true impact of adherence on the benefits of antihypertensive treatment."

Major Study Examines the Impact of Missed Blood Pressure Medication

To better understand how consistently taking medication affects its benefits, researchers analyzed individual patient data from 91,339 participants across nine clinical trials. Each trial compared a blood pressure treatment (intervention group) with an alternative approach (comparator group: either placebo or a less-intensive regimen).

The researchers divided participants into two categories based on how closely they followed their prescribed treatment. Those who took at least 80% of their assigned medication were considered to have higher adherence, while those who took less than 80% were placed in the lower adherence group.

Even with the regular monitoring and structured conditions of clinical trials, patients became less consistent about taking their medication as time passed.

"A decline in adherence over time was apparent, even in the structured setting of trials," noted Miss Yang. Overall, 88% of participants met the higher adherence threshold during the first year, but that figure had dropped to just 79% by the fifth year.

Skipping Blood Pressure Pills Reduces Treatment Benefits

The difference in medication adherence was reflected in how much patients' blood pressure improved.

Among participants with higher adherence, systolic blood pressure, the top number in a blood pressure reading, was 5.2 mmHg lower in the intervention group than in the comparator group. For those with lower adherence, the difference was considerably smaller at just 3.0 mmHg.

Researchers then examined whether these differences translated into protection against major cardiovascular problems. The outcomes included strokes, heart attacks (myocardial infarction), ischemic heart disease, and heart failure that resulted in hospitalization or death.

The findings revealed a striking contrast. Among patients who consistently followed their treatment plans, the intervention group experienced an 11% lower incidence of major cardiovascular events compared with the comparator group. However, among patients with lower adherence, there was no statistically significant reduction in these events.

"Notably, our results reinforce the saying that 'drugs don't work in patients who don't take them.' Patients with lower adherence derived little or no cardiovascular benefit, whereas those with higher adherence had greater reductions in blood pressure and prevention of cardiovascular events," said Miss Yang.

Helping Patients Get Lifesaving Heart Protection

The researchers believe that helping people take their medication consistently should become a greater priority in the treatment of high blood pressure. They recommend more routine assessments of medication adherence, along with better patient education and practical strategies to make treatment easier to follow.

Possible approaches include simplifying medication schedules, combining multiple treatments into a single pill, and prescribing longer-acting drugs that can help limit the consequences of an occasional missed dose.

Professor Felix Mahfoud, Chair of the ESC Communication Committee, emphasized that missed medication has received too little attention as a contributor to poorly controlled blood pressure.

"For years, we have neglected non-adherence to medication as a cause for uncontrolled hypertension. Assessment of adherence should become a routine part of hypertension care so patients do not miss out on life-saving benefits. As healthcare providers, we are here to have open, non-judgmental conversations with our patients about any barriers they may have to taking medication, to help improve adherence."

Und doch gibt es ihn noch, den Fachkräftemangel. Und zwar in zahlreichen Branchen. Er wird in den nächsten Jahren auch nicht weniger werden, wenn die Wirtschaft wieder kräftiger anzieht und sich die geburtenstarken Babyboomer mehr und mehr in die Pension zurückziehen.

Die Fachkräfte von morgen sitzen heute in Österreichs Klassenzimmern. Zumindest viele potenzielle Fachkräfte. Denn längst nicht alle Kinder brennen für technische oder naturwissenschaftliche Karrieren. Aber viele, die sich dafür begeistern ließen, wissen vielleicht gar nicht, welche Möglichkeiten es in der österreichischen Industrie für sie gäbe. Das gilt zum Beispiel für viele Mädchen. Den Unternehmen entgehen sonst womöglich helle Köpfe, die tolle Karrieren machen könnten. Und den Kindern entgehen womöglich berufliche Perspektiven, für die sie Talent und Begeisterung mitbringen würden. Auch deshalb hat Therese Niss, die aus der Eigentümerfamilie des oberösterreichischen Industriekonzerns Miba stammt und von 2017 bis 2024 für die ÖVP im Nationalrat saß, die Initiative MINTality ins Leben gerufen. Ziel ist, Mädchen für technische und naturwissenschaftliche Ausbildung zu begeistern, ihr Selbstvertrauen zu stärken und sie am Weg von der Schule bis zum Beruf zu begleiten. Der Frauenanteil in technischen Ausbildungswegen liegt bei weniger als einem Viertel.

Zum Nationalfeiertag schlagen wir Lösungen für Herausforderungen vor, die unser Land zu bewältigen hat.

Unternehmen und Schulen sollten enger zusammenarbeiten, um verborgene Talente zu entdecken, Begeisterung zu wecken und jungen Menschen neue Perspektiven zu eröffnen.

Welche Möglichkeiten es gibt und was sich hinter einem Beruf verbirgt, lässt sich allerdings oft nur schwer allein im Klassenzimmer vermitteln. MINTality versucht deshalb auch, Unternehmen und Schulen zusammenzubringen. Ein Beispiel ist das Unternehmensschuljahr. Die Jugendlichen erhalten wiederholt Einblicke in technische Berufe und lernen Menschen kennen, die dort arbeiten. Am Ende erarbeiten die Schülerinnen und Schüler ein Projekt, das sie in der Schule oder im Unternehmen präsentieren – und vor ihren Eltern.

Während der Fokus MINTality auf Schülerinnen und weiblichen Vorbilder aus der Wirtschaft liegt, profitieren freilich auch Buben davon, wenn sich Schulen und Unternehmen vernetzen. „Die Zusammenarbeit zwischen Schulen und Unternehmen ist ein wichtiger Bestandteil der Bildungs- und Berufsorientierung“, so etwa das Bildungsministerium. Schülerinnen und Schüler können ab dem achten Jahr der allgemeinen Schulpflicht bis zu fünf Tage im Unterrichtsjahr für die individuelle Berufs- und Bildungsorientierung vom Unterricht freigestellt werden.

Georg Stockinger, Vorsitzender der AHS-Gewerkschaft, sieht allerdings auch die Firmen in der Pflicht. Gerade für Industriebetriebe seien berufsbildende Schulen oft interessanter als allgemeinbildende Schulen. So könnten Firmen etwa proaktiver auch auf AHS zugehen und etwa Ferialjobs für Schülerinnen und Schüler anbieten. „Das wäre eine Win-Win-Situation“, sagt Stockinger zur „Presse am Sonntag“. Lehrer für Fort- und Weiterbildungen in Unternehmen schicken sei grundsätzlich sinnvoll, man müsse aber auch bedenken, dass auch Lehrer nur endlich Ressourcen zur Verfügung hätten.

Einig sind sich Niss und Stockinger bei der Rolle der Schulleitung. Letztlich ist es ihre Aufgabe, sich mit der lokalen Wirtschaft zu vernetzen und Kooperationen einzugehen. „Die Qualität einer Schule hängt auch wesentlich von ihrer Führung ab“, sagt Niss. Viele Direktoren und Direktorinnen bringen langjährige pädagogische Erfahrung mit, übernehmen mit der Schulleitung jedoch auch Führungs- und Managementaufgaben, auf die sie in ihrer bisherigen Laufbahn nur begrenzt vorbereitet wurden. Niss hat deshalb ein Cross-Mentoring-Programm lanciert, das Direktorinnen und Direktoren mit erfahrenen Führungskräften aus der Privatwirtschaft zusammenbringt. Die Schulleitungen erhalten dabei praktische Einblicke in Führungs- und Managementthemen, während die Manager und Managerinnen den Schulalltag aus nächster Nähe besser kennenlernen. So werden auf beiden Seiten auch Berührungsängste abgebaut.

Je mehr Möglichkeiten Kinder kennenlernen, desto leichter können sie eigene Interessen und Talente entdecken. Doch Berufsorientierung ist nur eine Aufgabe der Schule. Sie soll auch Eigeninitiative und Gestaltungskompetenz vermitteln. Denn nicht nur die Fachkräfte, auch die Entrepreneure von morgen – Menschen, die Ideen entwickeln und in wertschaffende Lösungen umsetzen – gehen heute in die Schule. Kinder und Jugendliche sollen erfahren, dass sie selbst etwas bewegen können, sagt Johannes Lindner, Bundeskoordinator für Entrepreneurship Education und Gründer der Bildungsinitiative IFTE #Entrepreneurship4Youth, zur „Presse am Sonntag“. Kinder brauchen nicht nur Schutz und Unterstützung, sondern auch Freiräume, um eigene Erfahrungen zu sammeln, Fehler zu machen und Selbstwirksamkeit zu erleben, so Lindner.

Le Burkina Faso est « un petit pays », « pauvre » et « enclavé » : ces expressions que vous ne trouverez plus dans les manuels scolaires burkinabè.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Depuis la rentrée scolaire le 1er octobre 2026, les autorités burkinabè ont amorcé un processus de « décolonisation » de l'histoire enseignée dans son système éducatif.

Au Burkina Faso, la lutte contre l'impérialisme prônée par les autorités depuis la proclamation, en avril 2025, de la Révolution progressiste populaire, par le Capitaine Ibrahim Traoré, passe aussi par une révolution mémorielle, foi des autorités.

Dans une déclaration le 7 septembre 2026, le ministre de l'Enseignement secondaire et de la formation professionnelle et technique, Pr Moumouni Zoungrana a indiqué qu'il y avait dans les cours d'histoire et de géographie « des expressions qui ne nous honorent pas ».

« Ce qui veut dire que dans nos manuels aussi, il y a des contrevérités qui sont consignées pour nous infantiliser, pour nous diminuer, pour nous abêtir. Et nous avons passé notre temps à faire la promotion de ces antivaleurs que nous avons transmises de génération en génération », avait-il commenté avant d'inviter le monde éducatif burkinabè à avoir « le courage de casser totalement la table et de la reverser ».

Le professeur Zoungrana a alors annoncé une révision des curricula notamment les cours d'Histoire, de Géographie et d'Education civique, afin dit-il, d'extirper les expressions « incohérentes ».

Ces trois matières sont considérées comme les plus urgentes alors que le pays préparait une refondation générale de l'école burkinabè baptisée « Pacte Neekre » (réveil en français) lancé le 17 octobre dernier.

En attendant la réécriture complète de ces manuels d'histoire, géographie et d'éducation civique, une note contenant quelques changements immédiats a été disséminée aux enseignants.

Ces changements vont du remplacement de certaines expressions, à l'introduction de nouveaux chapitres en passant par le bannissement de certaines expressions et chapitres

Voici quelques expressions et cours qui ont changé au Burkina Faso depuis le 1er octobre dernier :

Des informations vérifiées à portée de main

En Histoire vingt-sept mots et expressions disparaissent du vocabulaire des enseignants et élèves.

Les envahisseurs et les espions : Parmi les concepts remplacés, la colonisation devient l'invasion impérialiste, le colon ou le colonisateur est appelé envahisseur et les explorateurs deviennent des espions puis les colonies, territoires envahis.

L'invasion impérialiste française du Burkina Faso est désormais l'expression utilisée pour qualifier la Colonisation française du Burkina Faso de

La libération de l'Afrique : La décolonisation et les indépendances sont désormais rappelée Libération de l'Afrique. De même, on distingue deux époques : pré-invasion et post-invasion pour designer respectivement les ères précoloniale et postcoloniale.

La guerre de ré-invasion : Désormais le terrorisme des qualifié de guerre de ré-invasion. L'appellation tirailleurs sénégalais fait place à combattants africains de la guerre et anciens combattants devient vétérans africains de la guerre.

Des leçons en histoire comme la traite orientale et la traite négrière changent d'appellation pour devenir les déportations orientale et transatlantique des Africains.

En géographie, une dizaine de concepts jugés « déshonorants » disparaissent également.

Le Burkina Faso présenté comme pays enclavé est désigné par l'appellation pays continental, le terme ethnies est remplacé par Groupe socioculturel tandis que les faiblesses et limites du pays sont désormais présentées comme des défis et opportunités.

La notion de développement est tropicalisée et devient notion de développement endogène.

Le cours d'Education civique et morale (ECM) lui change carrément d'appellation devenant Education à la Citoyenneté et au Patriotisme (ECP).

Plusieurs expressions particulièrement descriptives et notamment en Géographie, sont désormais bannies du lexique scolaire. Les qualificatifs pays pauvre, pays endetté, petit pays, climat défavorable, etc. sont interdits.

Il est également interdit de quantifier les groupes socioculturels en pourcentage des ethnies ou en pourcentage des religions.

Certaines leçons dispensées en géographie notamment en classe de 4e disparaissent. On note la suppression de la leçon sur "le commerce triangulaire".

Le chapitre également consacré à l'étude « de la géographie physique de l'Europe a été purement et simplement supprimé », confie un enseignant joint par BBC Afrique.

« L'accent est plutôt mis sur les conditions naturelles et développement de l'Afrique », explique-t-il.

Dans le même temps, de nouvelles leçons d'Histoire et Géographie sont introduites aussi bien au post-primaire qu'au secondaire.

En 6e, une nouvelle leçon intitulée la notion de révolution voit le jour. Un thème sur lequel les autorités mise pour impulser un changement majeur. S'adressant à des jeunes burkinabè à l'occasion de la Journée internationale de la jeunesse en août dernier, le ministre Moumouni Zoungrana a déclaré que « la révolution a été la seule option possible pour créer une rupture, (…) pour assurer la souveraineté totale, économique, financière mais aussi politique » du Burkina Faso.

Deux nouvelles leçons font leur apparition dans les cours d'histoire de la classe de 5e. Il s'agit des titres les savoirs et les savoir- faire africains et les révolutions burkinabè. Ce dernier revient sur les idéaux de la Révolution démocratique et populaire ( RDP) du Capitaine Thomas Sankara qui a dirigé le pays entre 1983 et 1987 et la révolution progressiste populaire (RPP) du Capitaine Ibrahim Traoré en cours depuis fin 2022.

Chez leurs ainés de la classe de 4e on note l'introduction d'une nouvelle leçon intitulée la révolution haïtienne. Elle revient sur les mouvements de lutte pour la libération des peuples haïtiens, leurs succès et leurs erreurs. Dans son dernier entretien avec la presse, diffusé fin septembre le président burkinabè Ibrahim Traoré appelait d'ailleurs ses compatriotes à « lire l'histoire de Haïti et comprendre qu'il ne faut pas s'amuser avec ces gens-là », faisant références aux anciennes puissances colonisatrices.

Natural compound could fight rheumatoid arthritis at its source

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a natural plant compound that could offer a surprising new approach to fighting rheumatoid arthritis. In experiments with rats, the substance reduced joint swelling, protected cartilage, and calmed harmful immune activity. Researchers also discovered that it works through an unexpected mechanism involving the way the body processes fats.

The findings, published in the journal Engineering, center on a compound called obakulactone (OL), a naturally occurring tetracyclic triterpenoid extracted from Phellodendri cortex, a type of plant bark. Tetracyclic triterpenoids are plant chemicals characterized by a structure containing four interconnected rings.

The researchers found that obakulactone helps restore balance to unsaturated fatty acids, which play important roles in cellular function and inflammation. It does this by promoting the breakdown of a protein called acyl coenzyme A thioesterase 1 (ACOT1), using the cell's natural protein disposal system, known as the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway.

By identifying this previously underexplored molecular target, the study suggests that changing how cells process fatty acids could become a new strategy for treating rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Natural Compound Reduces Arthritis Inflammation and Joint Damage

To investigate the compound's effects, scientists created a rat model of rheumatoid arthritis using complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA), a substance that triggers a strong inflammatory response resembling certain features of the disease.

The animals received obakulactone for 21 days at three different daily doses: low (50 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), medium (100 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), and high (200 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹). These amounts were calculated according to body weight.

The results were encouraging. Rats treated with the compound experienced significantly less joint swelling, while the structure of their cartilage and synovium returned toward normal. The synovium is the thin membrane lining joints that produces lubricating fluid and can become severely inflamed in rheumatoid arthritis.

The treatment also improved disease-related changes in the thymus and spleen, two organs that play important roles in the immune system.

Beyond these physical improvements, researchers observed major changes in immune activity. Obakulactone reduced elevated levels of CD3⁺ T cells and CD68⁺ macrophages in joint tissues. These immune cells normally help defend the body, but they can contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue damage when the immune system becomes overactive.

The compound also shifted macrophages away from an inflammation-promoting M1 (CD86) state toward a more inflammation-resolving M2 (CD206) state. At the same time, it suppressed the development of CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells, a type of immune cell associated with autoimmune inflammation.

Blood tests provided further evidence of these effects. Obakulactone reduced several inflammatory signaling proteins, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α. These proteins, known as cytokines, help immune cells communicate but can also intensify inflammation when produced in excessive amounts.

The treatment also lowered rheumatoid arthritis related blood markers, including rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (CCP-Ab), C-reactive protein (CRP), and matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3). The reductions became more pronounced as the dose increased.

Scientists Uncover a Surprising Link Between Fat Metabolism and Arthritis

One of the study's most intriguing findings involved fatty acids, molecules that are commonly associated with nutrition and energy storage but also influence inflammation throughout the body.

Using several advanced analytical methods, including metabolomics, MALDI mass spectrometry imaging, and proteomics, the researchers examined how obakulactone affected the chemical processes inside tissues.

These techniques allowed them to investigate changes in small molecules, map their distribution within tissues, and measure differences in protein levels.

The analysis revealed that the compound helped correct abnormalities in the production and breakdown of unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid).

These fatty acids are important components of cell membranes and can serve as raw materials for molecules that regulate inflammation. Arachidonic acid, in particular, is involved in producing several powerful inflammatory signals.

In the rats with arthritis, obakulactone helped bring these disrupted metabolic processes back toward a healthier balance. This suggested that its benefits extended beyond simply suppressing immune activity.

A Hidden Protein Emerges as a Potential Treatment Target

To understand exactly how the compound worked, the scientists conducted additional laboratory experiments involving rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (SFs).

These cells help maintain the tissue lining the joints. However, in rheumatoid arthritis, they can become abnormally aggressive, multiplying excessively, releasing inflammatory substances, and contributing to cartilage damage and tissue scarring.

Obakulactone slowed the growth of these cells, reduced their production of inflammatory cytokines, and encouraged apoptosis, the controlled process through which the body eliminates unwanted or damaged cells.

The researchers then investigated which protein the compound was acting on directly.

Using cellular thermal shift assays, microscale thermophoresis, and surface plasmon resonance, they identified ACOT1 as a direct molecular target of obakulactone.

The binding measurements produced a dissociation constant (Kd) of (6.18 ± 0.26) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by microscale thermophoresis (MST)) and (6.34 ± 0.38) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR)).

These closely matching results provided evidence that the compound physically interacts with ACOT1. A dissociation constant describes how strongly two molecules bind to one another, with lower values generally indicating tighter binding.

Importantly, obakulactone did more than bind to the protein. It promoted ACOT1's destruction through the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, a cellular mechanism that marks proteins for removal and breaks them down.

This process reduced the activity of several downstream mechanisms associated with inflammation and abnormal cell behavior.

How Targeting One Protein Can Disrupt Inflammation

When ACOT1 was broken down, levels of another enzyme called stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) also decreased. This enzyme helps regulate the production of certain unsaturated fatty acids.

The changes subsequently suppressed two major cellular signaling networks: Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT).

These pathways act like internal communication systems, carrying signals that influence how cells grow, survive, and respond to their surroundings. When their activity becomes abnormal, they can contribute to inflammation and excessive tissue growth.

By interfering with these signals, obakulactone reduced inflammation and fibrosis, a process involving excessive buildup of scar-like connective tissue, in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts.

Additional experiments, including rescue tests and studies using molecular inhibitors, strengthened the evidence that ACOT1 was central to these effects.

The researchers confirmed that the compound's ability to reduce inflammation, slow abnormal cell proliferation, and encourage apoptosis depended on its influence over ACOT1, the arachidonic acid pathway, and the downstream JAK-STAT/PI3K-AKT signaling pathways.

Together, the findings point to a potential way of disrupting the biological processes that sustain arthritis inflammation rather than focusing exclusively on suppressing immune responses.

A Potential New Direction for Rheumatoid Arthritis Treatment

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, particularly the joints. It affects approximately 1% of people worldwide and can cause persistent pain, swelling, stiffness, and progressive joint damage.

Because it is a systemic disease, rheumatoid arthritis can also affect organs and tissues beyond the joints.

Although existing medications can reduce symptoms and slow disease progression, they do not work equally well for everyone, and some can cause serious side effects. This makes the search for additional treatment approaches especially important.

The new findings suggest that obakulactone could become a candidate for future rheumatoid arthritis drug development. They also identify ACOT1 as a potentially valuable treatment target and highlight the importance of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism in controlling inflammation.

However, the results remain preclinical. The compound has been tested in rats and laboratory cells, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether it is safe and effective in humans.

Even with that limitation, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility: targeting the metabolic machinery that helps drive inflammation could provide another way to combat rheumatoid arthritis and potentially protect joints from lasting damage.
