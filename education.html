Putin rounds off diplomatic week in talks with African leaders (PHOTOS)

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Russian President Vladimir Putin is holding a series of bilateral meetings with African leaders at the Kremlin on Friday, following the third Russia-Africa Summit.

The talks involve African heads of state, including from Sudan, Madagascar, Ghana, Mali, South Sudan, the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Eritrea, as well as the prime minister of Togo.

The meetings follow the two-day summit, held in Moscow on October 28-29, which focused on expanding trade, investment, energy, security, and humanitarian cooperation between Russia and African nations.

During Friday’s talks with Sudan’s Sovereign Council Chairman Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, the Sudanese leader proposed establishing a free economic zone on the Red Sea for Russian fuel and wheat storage facilities, as well as cooperation on a railway linking Port Sudan to Chad.

Burhan also offered Russian investors access to Sudan’s gold and copper deposits.

Putin presented a roadmap for bilateral cooperation and said Russia-Sudan trade had more than doubled in 2025, rising by a further 25% in January-August 2026.

Addressing the ongoing conflict in Sudan, Putin reiterated Moscow’s support for a political settlement and proposed discussing additional steps Russia could take to help resolve the crisis.

In a separate meeting with Madagascar President Michael Randrianirina, Putin highlighted expanding educational ties, noting that around 5,000 Malagasy students have studied in Russia and that MGIMO University signed a cooperation memorandum with Madagascar’s Foreign Ministry in June.

Putin also welcomed the Russian Orthodox Church’s recognition as a legal entity in Madagascar in April, noting that it has more than 1,500 parishioners on the island.

Randrianirina called for stronger practical cooperation and urged Moscow to accelerate work on debt conversion.

During talks with Ghanaian President John Dramani Mahama, Putin said bilateral trade had grown by 6% between January and August. He added that Ghana ranks 12th among Russia’s African trading partners. The Russian leader also praised Ghana’s independent foreign policy and its opposition to neocolonialism, thanking Accra for its support for Russian resolutions at the UN.

Mahama expressed interest in expanding economic and investment cooperation with Moscow. The leader praised Russia for having “always stood by Africa” during the struggle against apartheid and for supporting the continent during the Covid-19 pandemic through supplies of Sputnik V vaccines.

At a meeting with Mali’s transitional president, Assimi Goita, Putin said Russia had increased its scholarship quota for Malian students to 300 places for the 2026-27 academic year, citing growing interest in Russian education and language studies. He added that six Russian language and cultural centers are operating in the West African country.

During the summit, Putin also held at least nine more bilateral meetings with other African leaders, including the presidents of Egypt, Equatorial Guinea, Mozambique, Namibia, the Republic of the Congo, Burundi, and Kenya, as well as Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed and Libya’s Presidential Council Chairman Mohamed al-Menfi.

Russia-Africa ties can help continent overcome economic dependence — Ugandan educator

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

MOSCOW, October 30. /TASS/. Closer cooperation between Russia and African countries could bring economic opportunities to Uganda and the whole continent, Ugandan educator and founder of the Chunga-Changa interactive Russian-language school Daniel Omara told TASS.

"Russian people and Africans have been tied in this sort of collaboration before. The Russian people, together with other peoples of the Soviet Union, helped Africans fight for and gain their independence," said Omara, who won the Grand Prize in the Art of Teaching category of the International Eurasia Award.

He said that Africa had continued to face economic dependence even after gaining independence, with some Western and European partners maintaining relationships that, in his view, benefited them at the expense of African countries.

"Unfortunately, after getting independence, we almost found ourselves in a new season called neocolonialism. These colonial powers did not actually let go of African peoples. They kept taking advantage of them, with deals that are almost parasitic in nature," he said.

Omara believes closer ties with Russia could help African countries build partnerships on different terms, with greater respect for their independence and sovereignty.

Speaking about the outcome of the Russia-Africa summit, which ended on Thursday, he said: "This forum is bringing us back to a new reality, where we respect ourselves, respect each country's sovereignty and are able to meet on equal terms. We are not taking advantage of anyone."

He also welcomed the growing trade between Russia and African countries and the expansion of Russian businesses on the continent.

Omara singled out plans for local manufacturing and pharmaceutical production, saying that producing vaccines and medicines in Africa could open up new possibilities for the continent.

"These kinds of economic collaborations that we are seeing announced at this forum this year are just the beginning of the future that Africa is contributing to create together with Russia and countries of BRICS," he said.

The third Russia-Africa Summit was held in Moscow on October 28-29, bringing together delegations from 48 African countries, including 22 represented by heads of state. Participants discussed expanding cooperation in trade, investment, education, healthcare, technology and other areas.

Quebec Premier fires opening salvo at Ottawa with refusal to attend Canada-EU summit

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Quebec Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon called the federal government’s perceived snub a rupture with the Gérin-Lajoie Doctrine, a policy in which Quebec conducts its own foreign policy on matters touching its areas of jurisdiction.Christinne Muschi/The Canadian Press

By refusing to attend a Canada-European Union summit and declaring that his province was being turned into a “vassal,” Quebec Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon has fired an opening salvo in what promises to be a combative relationship with the federal government.

The Premier’s comments marked a new tone of conflict between Quebec and Ottawa weeks after the election of the separatist Parti Québécois, which is seeking to abolish the monarchy in the province and hold a referendum on independence.

After days of back-and-forth between the Premier and Prime Minister Mark Carney, Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon refused to attend the Canada-EU summit in Montreal on Thursday in protest of Ottawa’s refusal to let him speak publicly at the event.

The federal government denied the province’s request to have its leader deliver a speech and hold its own reception, instead offering a seat at the head table for the opening supper and a role greeting visiting dignitaries at the airport.

“The federal government would have us believe that the Premier of Quebec being granted permission to serve shrimp cocktails at a reception constitutes an acceptable and sufficient form of influence for representing Quebec’s interests,” said Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon in a social-media post on Tuesday.

The PQ government, which has promised to hold a referendum on Quebec independence, was elected with a minority of seats and just 28 per cent of the popular vote on Oct. 5. Though Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon has said he will not organize a vote on secession before U.S. President Donald Trump leaves the White House in 2029, he has already taken steps to demonstrate his continued commitment to sovereignty, including by naming a minister responsible for independence to his cabinet.

This week, the Premier called the federal government’s perceived snub a rupture with the Gérin-Lajoie Doctrine, a policy in which Quebec conducts its own foreign policy on matters touching its areas of jurisdiction, such as education and culture.

Announcing his decision to pull out of the summit on Wednesday, Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon said on social media that the invitation to sit at the head table was an effective request for Quebec “to get back in line, with the vassalization and invisibilization of Quebec which that implies, in exchange for obtaining better seats to watch the party.”

Mr. Carney said earlier this week that it was the Premier’s decision to leave an “empty chair.”

“He was invited to the dinner and he refused,” the Prime Minister said.

Konrad Yakabuski: Quebec’s new PQ cabinet is shaped with a referendum in mind

Asked about the prospect of a more challenging relationship with Quebec under a PQ government, Mr. Carney said Thursday that he would continue to practise “co-operative federalism” with the provinces.

“That means working together on the priorities of Canadians,” he told reporters at a news conference in Montreal. “I’ll continue to practise that and be available to work with Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon for the interests of Quebeckers.”

Quebec Liberal Leader Charles Milliard criticized the Premier’s absence from the summit and the provincial government’s choice of replacement, newly appointed chief diplomat and former PQ deputy Pascal Paradis. Sending someone unelected to represent Quebec is insufficient, Mr. Milliard told reporters Thursday.

Every opposition party agreed that Quebec should have been allowed to speak at the summit, however. Quebec Conservative Leader Éric Duhaime said Ottawa’s refusal constituted “toxic federalism.”

Even Quebec Liberal luminaries such as former deputy premier Nathalie Normandeau and the party’s former parliamentary leader Marwah Rizqy called Mr. Carney’s decision “a serious mistake” and an “enormous lack of respect,” respectively, this week.

There are precedents for inviting Quebec premiers to speak at international events held in Quebec, said the public policy scholar Stéphane Paquin, such as the UN biodiversity conference in 2022.

Many of the subjects likely to be discussed between Canada and the EU this week fall under provincial jurisdiction, such as the Erasmus+ program and critical minerals, said Prof. Paquin. Quebec could still refuse to implement aspects of any treaty touching on its constitutional powers, he added.

Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon seemed to threaten as much this week when he alluded to the “risks” involved in negotiating an international agreement without Quebec’s participation.

“Politically, it would have been easier to let him speak,” Prof. Paquin said.

The Quebec Premier gave another indication of his posture toward the federal government earlier this week, when he chose not to attend a first ministers’ meeting with First Nations leaders in Ottawa.

Simon-Pierre Savard-Tremblay, Quebec’s Minister for First Nations and Inuit Relations, said in a social-media statement that the Quebec government meets Indigenous nations “on a nation-to-nation” basis and “not at a table organized by Ottawa.”

Sophie Villeneuve, a political analyst and former Parti Québécois staffer, said Mr. St-Pierre Plamondon is setting the tone for future discussions with the federal government.

“Few premiers in the last 20 years have taken such a firm and clear position,” she said.

“Paul St-Pierre Plamondon intends to speak out more forcefully and to do so in a different way. We’ll see what kind of results that yields.”

Albania takes anti-riot crackdown cue from France

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Albania has imposed steep fines on people who take part in violent protests, citing a recent law imposed in France, in an attempt to quell weeks of clashes between police and protesters demanding the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama.

Under measures adopted Wednesday, remaining at a demonstration that turns violent will be a criminal offense, even for those not directly involved in clashes. Anyone who “chooses to remain in a crowd” engaged in violence or looting, or is found “concealing their identity,” faces fines of up to 500,000 leks ($6,130), over six-times the Balkan country’s average monthly salary.

Initially sparked by student anger over education costs and deteriorating facilities, the demonstrations have evolved into nationwide protests against inequality, soaring living costs, and cuts to public services.

Citing “urban anarchy” when announcing the crackdown, Rama acknowledged France’s controversial ‘casseurs-payeurs’ (rioters pay) bill, which would hold demonstrators liable for up to €10,000 ($11,160) in damages. The bill passed the French Senate on Wednesday and is now headed to the National Assembly, following weeks of protests and riots in which over 1,300 protesters were arrested in a single day.

Italy has also followed suit. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni’s government approved measures last week that criminalize “violent participation in a demonstration,” punishable by up to five years in prison and fines of up to €20,000.

In Albania, a “special electronic register” will track suspected offenders, while police gain powers to restrict gatherings near schools, hospitals, and government buildings over potential violence. The measures – which also provide compensation for businesses damaged in the unrest – are already in place and require parliamentary approval within 45 days to remain in force.

Anti-government protests in the country began peacefully in May over plans for a luxury resort in the Zvernec nature reserve, a project linked to US President Donald Trump’s son-in-law, Jared Kushner.

By mid-October, the protests escalated into daily clashes with police amid growing anger over corruption, economic mismanagement, unemployment, and failing public services. Demands for Rama’s resignation took center stage, but the prime minister, who has been in power since 2013 and was elected to a fourth term last year, has refused to budge.

The unrest has caused over €2 million in damage, according to official estimates, as rioters torched ministries and attacked police stations, public buildings, and businesses. Clashes flared up again over the weekend, with protesters chanting “Revolution” and “Rama in prison” and police using tear gas, rubber bullets, and water cannons in Tirana.

Despite the protests being driven by grievances over domestic matters, Rama blamed the violence on a “hybrid war” by foreign actors, specifically Russia and Iran.

Russia has dismissed the accusations. Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova suggested last week that Rama should look closer to home. “These are citizens of your own country who took to the streets to protest. Don’t shift your responsibility onto others. Deal with your own domestic problems.”

Quebec plans to ban religious symbols in schools, Education Minister says

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Parti Québécois Leader Paul St-Pierre Plamondon and current Education Minister Joel Arseneau, right, at the legislature in Quebec City in April, 2023.Jacques Boissinot/The Canadian Press

The president of the National Council of Canadian Muslims is accusing the Quebec government of attempting to take away the rights of children by expanding secularism laws.

“We’re talking about going after the rights of children to have a spirituality and go to school,” said Stephen Brown in an interview.

Quebec’s new education minister, Joël Arseneau, told journalists on Wednesday that he plans to ban schoolchildren from wearing religious symbols. He added that doing so is “non-negotiable.”

The Parti Québécois had promised the ban during the election campaign that resulted in a PQ minority government. In its platform, the party wrote that secularism “embodies the desire of the vast majority of Quebeckers to live in a society where religion is practised in the private sphere.”

But Brown said the government is already breaking its promise to be the “the government of all Quebeckers.”

“What we’re seeing now is anything but that,” said Brown. “It’s ridiculous and a missed opportunity.”

Trump celebrates Quebec separatist, B.C. Conservative election wins in social media posts

Brown said the government had pledged to reach out to communities and find balanced policy solutions to difficult questions like secularism. He believes that believes preventing children from wearing religious symbols is “blatant discrimination against religious minorities.”

He said the rules would mainly apply to those who wish to wear the kippa, hijab, crucifix and turban.

“We should not live in a country where politicians are able to literally trade away the rights of Canadian citizens in an election or political game,” said Brown. “The Charter rights of Canadian citizens have to be worth more than the paper they’re written on.”

Québec solidaire’s education and secularism critic, Roxane Milot, said the leftist party will vote against the proposed ban.

“Leave the kids alone! The priorities for the education system are clear: address the staff shortage, repair our schools, put an end to the three-tiered school system and reduce the dropout rate,” she said in a news release.

In the same release, Québec solidaire said it is concerning to see the new administration get bogged down by “identity-driven one-upmanship.”

It is unclear when the PQ government intends to table a bill to this effect, though the legislature is scheduled to resume sitting on Nov. 17.

The Quebec government has been strongly criticized for its law banning employees of the public sector, including teachers, from wearing religious symbols.

A legal challenge of that law, known as Bill 21, has made it to the Supreme Court of Canada. Lawyers argued it raised serious concerns involving several Charter rights, including freedom of expression, conscience and religion.

Brown said he is prepared to mount a legal challenge if the PQ’s proposal becomes law.

Jean-Christophe Jasmin, the Quebec director of Christian think tank Cardus, also opposes expanding secularism laws.

In an interview, he said the government’s rationale to ban religious symbols worn by government employees was “presented in the name of freedom of conscience.” The previous Coalition Avenir Québec administration had argued that children are subject to an authority figure, and that that figure must be religiously neutral as to not impose their beliefs on their students.

“I don’t see how that logic applies to children today. Children do not represent the state, they are part of civil society,” said Jasmin. “There is no duty of neutrality on the part of children. The argument doesn’t hold up.”

Brown agreed, saying the scope of secularism laws banning religious symbols has continued to expand since the CAQ took power in 2018.

At first, the government targeted those in positions of authority. It was extended to teachers, daycare workers, all school staff and parents who volunteer in schools.

The CAQ then expanded the scope of the law to the public sphere by banning praying outside, in universities and at workplaces.

“And now of course, we’re going after the rights of children. So where does this end?” said Brown.

Frédéric Bérard, a constitutional lawyer who took part in the legal challenge against Bill 21, did not want to comment on Arseneau’s comments but said it was to be expected.

He pointed out that former premier François Legault had openly said in a 2019 radio interview with Radio-Canada that Bill 21 was a concession to “people who are a little racist and don’t want to see religious symbols anywhere in public.”

“It’s never enough,” said Bérard. “It’s a slippery slope.”

France planning new riot crackdown law

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

France’s Senate has backed a sweeping “vandals pay” bill that could force people involved in violent gatherings to cover up to €10,000 ($11,100) in damage caused not only by them, but also by other members of the group. The vote follows weeks of nationwide protests which spiraled into looting, arson attacks, and clashes with police.

The bill was initially drafted by the government over the summer after violence following Paris Saint-Germain’s Champions League victory, but it gained urgency after a new wave of mass public disorder. Demonstrations against soaring living costs and worsening quality of education turned violent in several cities this month, with rioters looting shops, torching vehicles, and destroying the 19th-century city hall in Limoges. Thousands of people were arrested during the unrest, with hundreds injured.

Senators passed the measure by 229 votes to 105 on Wednesday, backed by conservatives, centrists, and Marine Le Pen’s right-wing National Rally despite fierce objections from the left.

While presenting the legislation, Interior Minister Laurent Nunez argued that it seeks “to deter vandals and looters who burden taxpayers and demonstrators with their misdeeds, and… to better compensate the victims of this damage.”

He added that the bill is also “firmly based on fault; fault exists when one participates in a group formed with the intention of committing violence.” Meanwhile, centrist Senator Olivier Bitz reassured that “it should not be assumed that this text would allow for the prosecution of passersby or peaceful demonstrators.”

The bill – which included around 20 additional provisions compared to its original version – establishes joint civil liability for people who knowingly join a group formed to commit violence or vandalism, or remain in a crowd after police dispersal warnings while aware of ongoing attacks.  Those found liable could owe up to €10,000 for destruction committed by others, although the final sum is to be determined in court, and peaceful participation alone would not trigger the measure.

To recover court-ordered compensation, the legislation would also allow deductions from social benefits, including family allowances, housing assistance, and minimum-income payments. The bill would also exempt people who remain in a gathering for legitimate reasons, potentially including journalists and medical volunteers.

While centrists and right-wing supporters portrayed the bill as a more just measure to extract damages from those involved in vandalism and protect law-abiding taxpayers, socialist Senator Marie-Pierre de La Gontrie warned that the welfare measures risk penalizing entire families.

Nathalie Godard, Director of the Action Department at Amnesty International France, also argued that the bill “threatens to further deter people from taking to the streets to exercise their rights to freedom of expression and peaceful assembly,” adding that it comes on the heels of the government’s push to crack down on protests.

The Senate passed the bill ahead of the 2027 presidential race, with two key contenders taking contrasting positions over the unrest. Marine Le Pen, the frontrunner in recent polls, has championed the “vandals pay” principle, accusing President Emmanuel Macron’s government of failing to control the violence.

Her left-wing rival Jean-Luc Melenchon has backed the protests while condemning looting and vandalism and accusing the authorities of inflaming tensions through police brutality.

While the bill sailed through the right-dominated Senate, it now faces a much tougher test in the fractured National Assembly, where centrist and right-wing lawmakers together hold enough seats to outvote the left but remain divided over the proposed crackdown.

A look at Ontario’s current negotiations with its education unions

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

All five education unions in the province have set dates for members to vote on giving their leadership a mandate to strike in November.Chris Young/The Canadian Press

The Ontario government is hoping to reach deals with five major education unions, all of which have been without a contract since the end of August.

Bargaining has been stalled over a dispute regarding which issues should be addressed centrally with the government and which should be handled at the local table with school boards.

Usually, large issues such as salaries, class sizes and sick days are negotiated centrally, while school board-specific issues such as operational and administrative policies are negotiated locally.

The matter will be decided by Ontario’s Labour Relations Board. Its ruling is expected in early November.

In the meantime, all five unions have set dates for members to vote on giving their leadership a mandate to strike in November.

Here is where each union stands in its negotiations with the province and school boards.

Represents: 84,000 members, which include elementary teachers, occasional teachers, education-support personnel, professional-support personnel and early-childhood educators.

Latest: The union will vote on a strike mandate from Nov. 9 to 18.

Represents: About 60,000 members, which include high-school teachers, occasional teachers, educational assistants, psychologists and speech-language pathologists.

Latest: The union has scheduled its strike vote for Nov. 6 to 16.

“Our members want fair, negotiated contracts that address the realities they are seeing in schools every day,” OSSTF president Martha Hradowy said in a press release.

“A strike vote gives us an opportunity to strengthen our bargaining position and sends a clear message to the Ford government that OSSTF/FEESO members are united behind the bargaining priorities we will bring to the table.”
