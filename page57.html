Brazil’s President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva pulled off a dramatic comeback on Sunday as he clinched a re-election victory with a lead of nearly 4.4 million votes over opponent Flávio Bolsonaro, giving him 51.8% of the total vote.

The 81-year-old leftist defied projections to win an unprecedented fourth term, staving off a conservative wave that has seen numerous South American countries, such as Argentina, Chile, Colombia, and Peru, all shift right, backed by US President Donald Trump.

Bolsonaro quickly refused to concede defeat and asked for the election results to be annulled after an evening of chaos at some polling places that followed a last-minute decision to extend voting by two hours.

The senator is the son of former far-right president Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year jail term for attempting a coup after his loss to Lula in the country's last election in 2022.

Lula had requested the extension after he complained that buses had been pulled from circulation in various cities, barring citizens from voting.

Flavio Bolsonaro also requested an extension in some regions due to heavy rains, but he described the decision as "unacceptable" and a "dirty trick".

"It is impossible to recognise the results. I am going to request the annulment of these elections," he said in a brief speech in Brasília.

After a first-round defeat, the left swung into action with massive rallies, declaring "Never Again Bolsonaro" and revamped their social media strategy.

No candidate who lost the first round has gone on to win the second in Brazil since 1989, the first direct election after the end of the country's military dictatorship.

The abstention rate decreased from the first round, falling from 21 percent to 19 percent.

"People went to vote because we managed to convince them that voting was very important for ensuring democracy in the country," Lula said in his first comments after the win.

Fireworks and vuvuzelas rang out in Rio de Janeiro, while thousands of people dressed in red, the color of Lula's Workers' Party, streamed into the streets of Sao Paulo to celebrate.

Lula's campaign leaned heavily on the theme of sovereignty in the face of commercial and diplomatic pressure from Trump's United States.

“Either the homeland remains free, or we die for Brazil,” he said. “This country will not accept being anyone’s backyard," he said as he addressed his supporters.

In Rio de Janeiro, cars beeped their horns and a woman dressed in red — the color of Lula supporters — shouted “You lost! Cry harder!” to four men in yellow and green sipping beers, Bolsonaro's signature colors. A huge roar echoed in the street as the result was confirmed.

Lula heads into a fourth term with almost half of the nation having rejected him, desperate for change.

He rose from poverty to become president in 2003 and was re-elected in 2006.

Lula made a dramatic return to office in 2022 elections after a corruption scandal saw him jailed for over 18 months. The charges were annulled on procedural grounds.

In his third term, Lula oversaw a drop in inflation and unemployment, and lifted millions out of hunger, but many voters felt gloomy about the high cost of living and crime.

Already one of the world's oldest leaders, Lula will be 85 by the end of his new term in 2030.

On the professional networking site LinkedIn, NHS doctor Abdul Qader Ismail describes himself as a 'human being, Muslim, Son, Brother, Husband, Father and Humanitarian'.

That last term is a bit rich coming from a man who promotes anti-Semitic conspiracy theories about Israelis 'harvesting Palestinian organs', describes Hamas's October 7 terror attacks as legal 'armed resistance' and justifies the medieval practice of stoning.

The Oxford-educated paediatrician, who has worked in hospitals across the East Midlands, has spread his controversial views in talks, social media posts and writings over recent years, including books aimed at teenagers.

Dr Ismail's troubling positions only emerged last week after he designed a controversial 'Palestine poppy', which he sells for £1.78 on eBay, with the profits donated to charities working to 'end the occupation' in Gaza.

Military veterans have accused him of 'hijacking' the symbol of remembrance, while others object to the fact that it is sold on a website featuring a video carrying the slogan 'From the river to the sea, Palestine will be free', which effectively calls for the destruction of Israel.

But as the Daily Mail discovered this week, such views are now becoming commonplace in the NHS, a disturbing trend that has been branded 'hate in healthcare'.

The febrile atmosphere has led to Jewish medics being called 'baby-killers' and even being greeted with a Nazi salute by a colleague.

Meanwhile, Jewish patients have reported being berated about Israeli actions, including a cancer patient while he was being operated on, making some scared to attend appointments.

Campaigners and medics accuse the General Medical Council (GMC) of turning a 'blind eye' to the problem and, at worst, introducing policies that have helped it flourish.

A campaign group has compiled a disturbing list of around 40 doctors – the majority working in the health service – who publicly spout deeply disturbing views.

They include an NHS consultant and senior trauma surgeon at a major London hospital who spread a conspiracy theory that Jews celebrated the 9/11 attacks – on the anniversary of the atrocities in 2024 they reposted an AI-generated image of Orthodox Jewish men celebrating in front of the Twin Towers as a plane approaches.

He has also shared a cartoon showing Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu pushing a coffin draped in a Palestinian flag into a Nazi concentration camp oven.

The image, which accentuates the Jewish leader's ears and nose, includes an inscription above the oven that reads 'Arbeit Macht Frei' – 'work makes you free' – the slogan that hung above the entrances to concentration camps such as Auschwitz.

The consultant had previously responded to reports of a rocket hitting a house in Israel on social media by saying: 'Great news, thanks for sharing. May all the Palestinian rockets hit their target, amen.'

Another case involves an Iraq-born surgeon working as an NHS consultant at a north London hospital who has shared a series of anti-Semitic conspiracy theories online, including claims that the Israeli military killed most Jewish victims on October 7 and that Mossad was behind the assassination of John F Kennedy and the 9/11 attacks.

He has also reposted videos of other people spouting an off-the-wall conspiracy that Jews are orchestrating the rape of little girls by US politicians so they can be videoed by Mossad and then blackmailed 'for the benefit of Israel'.

Algeria’s parliament approved a resolution Sunday to send troops to Niger, making it the first time Algerian soldiers will be deployed abroad since 1973, when they were sent to support Egypt during the Arab-Israeli war.

Authorities have not released any information about when and where exactly the Algerian army detachment will be deployed, or how many soldiers will be involved.

Algerian Sen. Belkacem Boulghiti said troops would be sent “to protect Algeria’s economic and strategic interests, particularly its oil facilities in Niger.”

“We are not sending our soldiers to wage war in Niger, but for a mission to secure economic infrastructure," he told reporters as he left the chamber after the vote. "It is simply an adaptation of our doctrine to evolving circumstances so we can rise to the new challenges imposed by the situation in the Sahel region.”

Algeria’s state-owned energy giant and the largest company in Africa, Sonatrach, has been working with the Sahelian country on the Trans-Saharan Gas Pipeline, which is designed to transport natural gas from Nigeria, through Niger to Algeria for export to European markets.

The western Sahel region comprising Niger, Mali and Burkina Faso, has been shaken by violent extremism due to deadly attacks by armed groups linked to al-Qaida and the Islamic State group and the governments fighting them. All three countries have seen coups in recent years and struggled with overstretched security forces.

The move on Sunday emphasizes the thawing relations between Algeria and Niger.

In August, Algeria was the first country to express support for Niger after aggrieved soldiers staged a mutiny against the military junta under Gen. Abdourahmane Tchiani, which was repelled by loyalist forces aided by Russian troops.

After its foreign ministry assured Niger of its “full solidarity and firm support," Algeria then sent a delegation to Niger's capital, Niamey, with four combat fighter jets and a military transport aircraft in what Niger’s ruling council described as “the demonstration of the strategic support.”

The diplomatic rift between the two countries plunged to a historic low last year.

Last April, the countries of the Alliance of Sahel States — Niger, Mali and Burkina Faso — recalled their ambassadors from Algeria following an incident in which a Malian drone was shot down by Algeria's army near the border.

Algeria's foreign ministry also condemned the 2023 coup which brought Niger’s junta leader Tchiani to power.

Appeals court hears arguments in Trump admin's voting suit against California

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

The three-judge panel included two Trump appointees and a Biden appointee.

A rare Sunday federal appeals court hearing for the Justice Department's voting lawsuit against California closed without a ruling.

The department requested that the court issue one by Tuesday prior to the scheduled 1 p.m. PDT hearing before the district court.

The arguments followed a surprise appeal by the Trump administration, which claims a district judge did not act quickly enough to address the administration's allegations of roughly 20,000 potential noncitizen voters on the state's voter rolls.

A three-judge panel that includes two appointees of President Donald Trump and one appointee of former President Joe Biden heard arguments on DOJ's efforts to segregate ballots from the alleged noncitizens.

California officials have countered that the list is unverified and includes the names of lawful voters, despite recent charges announced by DOJ of roughly 28 individuals from the list accused of voting and immigration-related crimes.

Earlier the DOJ sent letters last week to every county in California asking local elections officials to help identify potential voters who may be in the U.S. illegally, according to a letter described to ABC News.

"Within 48 hours, we ask that you commit to working with DOJ and DHS to identify who on the accompanying list is a citizen and who is not," according to a letter sent by Justice Department officials to every county in California and described to ABC News. "The Department strongly urges you and your agency to work with the Department of Justice to ensure the integrity of California's federal elections before taking actions that risk significant federal scrutiny and legal exposure."

Earlier this month, the DOJ filed suit against California accusing the state of failing to review a list of alleged noncitizens that the Trump administration claimed were sent mail-in ballots for next month's midterm election.

In court papers filed earlier this week, Los Angeles officials said a preliminary analysis of 200 of the names on the 20,000-person list, included a "high percentage" of individuals who say they were born in the U.S. and many more who have no voting history at all.

Of those 200 individuals, officials said, nearly 40% "indicate that they were born in the United States, which is strong evidence of citizenship; and 71.2% of the records have no voting history," according to a filing.

The Justice Department's rushed efforts to have an appeals court quickly rule on its lawsuit faced considerable skepticism from the three-judge panel in Sunday's emergency hearing.

Circuit Judge Mark Bennett, a Trump appointee, repeatedly questioned whether the appeals court had jurisdiction to step in at this stage in the litigation, and expressed considerable constitutional concerns over the lawsuit itself.

"I don't see how we have jurisdiction," Bennett said to DOJ attorney Matthew Donnelly after pressing him over the timeline of the government's decision to seeking emergency appeal when a hearing before the district judge is already set for this Tuesday.

Donnelly claimed that the DOJ's decision to seek relief from the appeals court came after disclosures by the state of the numbers of mail-in ballots already being processed in advance of the election -- which number in the hundreds of thousands.

But Bennett's most significant concerns appeared to be the nature of the lawsuit and it appearing to directly contradict the Constitution's Elections Clause -- which mandates that states have jurisdictions over the manner their elections are run.

"How can it preempt something that is expressly authorized by a provision of the Constitution?" Bennett asked. "How can the legislature making laws with regard to the manner of holding elections be preempted by a criminal statute passed by the Congress, especially when both the Congress and the California Legislature have made it illegal for non-citizens to vote?"

Donnelly repeatedly pointed to DOJ's position that the way California is administering its elections -- and not acting on the hard drive of potential 20,000 likely non-citizens -- infringes on the federal government's ability to enforce the federal statute barring voting by illegal immigrants.

That explanation didn't appear to sit well with Bennett, however, who pointed out that the Constitution specifically tasks Congress with jurisdiction over how states administer their elections and not the Judiciary.

"How can we, a federal court, do what is reserved to Congress, which Congress, in my view, hasn't done?" Bennett asked.

"Your Honor, I would just simply -- unfortunately -- disagree with you that 18 USC 611 is not an election law that fits in the power of Congress," Donnelly replied.

The judges also appeared to express concern about last-minute intervention to force California to segregate certain mail-in ballots given the state has provided a declaration saying if such an order was implemented it could have a "catastrophic" impact on tabulating the upcoming election.

Circuit Judge Patrick Bumatay, a Trump appointee, repeatedly pressed California's attorney Samuel Siegel over why an injunction wouldn't be appropriate to ensure non-citizens votes are being actively cast in the ongoing election.

"Without an injunction, you will never know, correct?" Bumatay asked. "Because if -- if the ballots are cast and separated, then they're going to be tabulated, and there's no way of knowing."

"Your Honor, we would respectfully disagree with that because of the number of steps that people have to take to attest to their citizenship, to declare consistent with what is required by both federal and state law that they are citizens when they register to vote," Siegel responded.

Even as Bumatay's questioning suggested he had more sympathy to granting the government some kind of relief, both Bennett and Circuit Judge Gabriel Sanchez, a Biden appointee, appeared unified that the extraordinary relief being sought by DOJ was not appropriate at this juncture.

"That's why I have a problem with the relief that's being sought here because I just don't see -- Congress and the California legislature have said non-citizens can't vote, and this lawsuit is about 'we don't like the way you're sorting citizens from non-citizens, and I just don't see 611 as telling California how to do the sorting," Bennett said.

"That's absolutely right, Your Honor, and to reiterate my earlier point, if the federal government, if the Executive Branch wants that authority in the first instance, they need to go ask Congress for it, they haven't done that," Siegel said. "They ask this court to short circuit the democratic process and issue an order, not just on the eve of an election, but as an election is underway."

In closing remarks, Bennett said the panel "will try to decide this case as quickly as possible."

The Israeli military continued a massive operation in the occupied West Bank on Sunday, placing major restrictions on movement ahead of Israel's upcoming elections.

In Hebron, more than 2,200 people, including nearly 200 children under the age of 18, have been displaced, the municipality said.
