Your guide to Proposition 5: Big changes to recall elections in California

Proposition 5 would change the way recall elections are conducted in the state by prioritizing voters’ right to oust an official before selecting a replacement. The ballot measure also would make a major change to gubernatorial recall elections by automatically designating the lieutenant governor as the replacement.

California’s general election takes place on Novemer 3. Learn about L.A.’s city and county races as well races for state office.

Under current law, when an elected official is recalled in California, the election for their replacement occurs at the same time. This ballot measure would amend the state constitution to nix that system by first asking voters to choose whether or not to oust an official. Then, in the event of a recall, the official would be replaced by appointment, succession or special election at a later date. In that case, the former officeholder could run again.

The measure also would designate California’s lieutenant governor as the de facto replacement for a recalled governor until the next term.

Those in support include Secretary of State Shirley Weber, California Common Cause, the League of Women Voters of California and Lt. Gov. Eleni Kounalakis.

Opponents include nonprofit Election Integrity Project California, Inc.

The state Legislature was tasked with developing a better recall system after Gov. Gavin Newsom faced a gubernatorial challenge in 2021, costing state and local governments more than $200 million.

Newsom defeated the recall attempt after nearly 62% voted to keep him in office. Had he been ousted, conservative talk radio host Larry Elder was the leading candidate to replace him with less than 49% of the vote.

Former state Sen. Josh Newman, who was recalled in 2018 and later reelected to office, co-authored the measure that made it to the ballot. He believes that the elimination of a second simultaneous vote for a replacement removes “the political incentive for gamesmanship around recalls.”

“My solution is really simple: It’s to eliminate that second question and maintain the recall as a valid, powerful tool of accountability,” he said.

It’s not uncommon for someone to get appointed to fill an official’s term if they leave office before their time is up. Newman said that slotting the lieutenant governor into the role of governor in the event of a recall would follow a precedent.

The lieutenant governor currently serves as acting governor when the governor leaves the state.

Your guide to Proposition 4: Allowing public campaign financing in California

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Proposition 4 would grant all state and local governments the ability to establish public campaign financing for candidates seeking office.

California’s general election takes place on Novemer 3. Learn about L.A.’s city and county races as well races for state office.

Proposition 4 would repeal a 1988 ban on the public funding of campaigns and allow all local and state governments to offer public financing programs with certain conditions.

Candidates would have to abide by certain expenditure limits and follow strict criteria. Funds could not be used for legal or defense purposes or to repay personal loans, nor could they be pulled from education, transportation or public safety buckets. The ballot measure also prohibits discrimination based on party or whether a candidate is an incumbent or running in opposition.

Gov. Gavin Newsom and the state Legislature placed this measure on the ballot.

Supporters include California Clean Money Campaign, California Common Cause, the League of Women Voters of California, ACLU California Action, the California Federation of Labor Unions AFL-CIO and Oakland Rising.

Opponents include the California Taxpayers Assn. and Reform California.

The measure aims to tackle the cost of what it takes to run a campaign and to broaden the pool of people who could run for office without being tethered to any individual, group or special interest.

“The increasing costs of political campaigns can force candidates to rely on large contributions from wealthy donors and special interests, which can give those wealthy donors and special interests disproportionate influence over governmental decisions,” the measure states.

There have been several attempts to allow for public campaign financing in California. Five charter cities, including Los Angeles, and at least 14 other states already allow some version of it.

“This bill will restore control to local governments and the state by giving counties, districts, general law cities, and the state the same option that charter cities currently have to enact public financing of campaigns,” a Senate rules committee analysis states.

Proposition 3 would make an expiring state income tax on high earners permanent, which supporters say is critical to funding schools and healthcare.

California’s general election takes place on Novemer 3. Learn about L.A.’s city and county races as well races for state office.

Proposition 3 stems from a decision voters made in 2012, amid a budget crisis, to temporarily increase the income tax rate on the top 2% of California earners. In 2016, voters approved another extension through 2030.

Proposition 3 would make the additional levy permanent.

If passed, tax rates for the top 2% of earners would remain as they are. Income brackets rise with inflation, but last year the top 2% of taxpayers earned at least $371,000 for single filers and $743,000 for joint filers.

If rejected, tax rates for the top 2% would fall in 2031 from at least 10.3% to 9.3%.

Money generated by Proposition 3, like the current tax, would flow to education, with 89% going to K-12 schools and 11% to community colleges.

Then, because the state constitution requires minimum levels of spending on education, more dollars are freed up to pay for services including healthcare and the University of California and California State University systems, said Kayla Kitson, a senior analyst with the California Budget and Policy Center.

Proposition 3, like the current high-earners tax, has the added effect of raising the minimum levels of funding required to flow to K-12 schools and community colleges, because of the complicated formulas California uses to set its budget, Kitson said.

If Proposition 3 fails, the state could expect to lose between $5 billion and $15 billion annually in tax revenue starting in 2031, according to estimates from the Legislative Analyst’s Office.

Supporters of the measure include the California Teachers Assn., the California School Nurses Organization, the California State PTA and Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California.

The California Teachers Assn., whose members gathered signatures to put Proposition 3 on the ballot, says if voters reject the measure, public schools could face a 15% budget cut, with 1 in 6 educators at risk of losing their jobs.

“Prop. 3 keeps the wealthiest Californians paying their share so our public schools get the funding our students deserve,” the union says on its website.

The California Taxpayers Assn., the Family Business Assn. of California and the California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce have come out against the measure.

Opponents say the state already has the nation’s highest income, sales and gas taxes and Sacramento needs to stop wasteful spending, rather than make taxes that were supposed to be temporary permanent.

“Californians shouldn’t have to bail out politicians and special interests’ mismanagement,” an opposition website sponsored by the California Taxpayers Assn. says. “Waste, fraud and overspending by the state government is not an excuse to raise our taxes even higher.”

Your guide to Proposition 2: Lets leaders fatten ‘rainy day’ fund, reduces likelihood of taxpayer rebates

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Gov. Gavin Newsom and Democrats in the state Legislature placed Proposition 2 on the November ballot to give government the ability to put more money into savings and create another option for leaders to avoid triggering taxpayer rebates during future budget surpluses.

The measure exempts deposits into state savings accounts from a spending limit that voters previously adopted through a series of ballot measures dating to the late 1970s, and increases the share of general fund tax revenue that can be put aside in reserves.

California’s general election takes place on Novemer 3. Learn about L.A.’s city and county races as well races for state office.

Under an existing state appropriations restraint, also known as the Gann Limit, lawmakers cannot spend more in the state budget than an amount determined by a formula that takes annual tax proceeds, changes to the population and cost of living into consideration.

Tax revenue above the limit must be divided between schools and refunds to taxpayers. More recent carve-outs in the law also allow leaders to direct excess funds to infrastructure and local government.

The limit applies to most other appropriations of tax revenue, including money that lawmakers tuck away into the “rainy day” fund and other reserves.

California voters also have capped the amount of money lawmakers can set aside in the rainy day fund at 10% of general fund proceeds in a given year.

The proposed changes would exempt deposits into the rainy day fund and a short-term reserve, called the Projected Surplus Temporary Holding Account, from the state appropriations limit. The cap on the rainy day fund would grow from 10% of general fund tax revenue to 20%.

In effect, the measure would allow the governor and the Legislature to put more money into budget reserves, creating another option to avoid triggering rebates.

For example, if the spending limit is $400 billion for the year, and revenue hits $410 billion, the measure would give lawmakers the option to deposit the $10 billion above the spending limit into the rainy day fund, direct the money to infrastructure or give it to local governments, instead of returning the funding to taxpayers and increasing funding for schools.

Newsom, legislative Democrats and the California Democratic Party say the changes will allow the state to save more when the economy is growing and use the funds to lessen cuts to vital programs during a recession or stock market crash.

Reform California, a political action committee started by state Sen. Carl DeMaio (R-San Diego), and legislative Republicans argue the proposal will allow Democrats to spend more money and hold on to revenue that otherwise would go back to taxpayers.

Since taking office, Newsom has argued that it doesn’t make sense for deposits into a savings account to count as spending under state law.

California’s budget revenue also is vulnerable to dramatic and at times unpredictable swings from year to year based on stock market activity. Newsom has said the state should have more flexibility during times of surplus to deposit money into the reserve accounts that lawmakers could then use during deficits to prevent big cuts to social services.

Statewide voters originally adopted the constitutional spending limit during an antitax wave in the late 1970s. Subsequent changes weakened the measure with exemptions to the limit for different kinds of appropriations, such as spending on infrastructure, and made it easier for lawmakers to avoid exceeding the limit and triggering rebates.

Revenue last exceeded the Gann Limit in 2021 when the pandemic left California with a predicted tax surplus of about $75 billion. As he campaigned against a failed recall, the governor set aside about $11.8 billion in cash rebates for middle class and low-income residents under a program he called the “Golden State Stimulus.” The Newsom administration predicted a historic surplus of nearly $100 billion the next year.

But the boom ended up smaller than anticipated and was followed by a predictable bust when federal stimulus dried up. Democrats have continued a pattern of spending more than the state brings in, leaving California with perennial budget shortfalls.

Proposition 2, Democrats say, seeks to even out the ups and downs.

By one measure, California is short nearly 1.4 million homes for people earning very low incomes. At the same time, middle-income families also struggle to afford housing in the state, where single-family homes sold for a median price of $887,680 in July.

In response, lawmakers are asking voters to let the state borrow $11.25 billion through the sale of bonds to build and maintain housing that low- and middle-income residents can afford.

It’s called Proposition 1, or the Veterans and Affordable Housing Bond Act of 2026.

California’s general election takes place on Novemer 3. Learn about L.A.’s city and county races as well races for state office.

The largest chunk of this money — $7.2 billion — would be doled out as loans to builders who agree to rent some of the homes to low-income families; in Los Angeles County, a family of four earning about $133,000 a year qualifies as low-income.

Researchers recently found that the construction of nearly 40,000 low-income homes was in limbo because of a lack of funding.

The second-largest portion of these funds — $1.25 billion — would be set aside for housing for veterans, in the form of homeownership loans. Voters last approved this type of statewide bond for veteran home loans in 2018.

“Through this historic bond, we’re giving voters the opportunity to help more Californians achieve homeownership, expand access to affordable housing, and give more families a real shot at the California Dream,” Gov. Gavin Newsom said about the proposition in June.

The bill to put this item on the ballot also was widely supported by organizations that provide low-income housing, plus various municipal governments, including San Diego, Orange and Santa Barbara counties.

Your guide to the California state schools superintendent race: Barrera vs. Shaw

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The office of state superintendent of public instruction is classified as nonpartisan, but that’s not how the candidates line up: Sonja Shaw is a conservative Republican whose views align closely with those of the Trump administration; Richard Barrera is a liberal Democrat backed by the California Teachers Assn. and closely aligned with its views.

They are vying for an office with little clear authority and an uncertain future. In June, the Legislature signed off on Gov. Gavin Newsom’s plan to reshape the position. By nearly all accounts, the state superintendent’s power has been reduced — and the authority of the office already was somewhat limited. Many of the new duties have yet to be worked out.

But that doesn’t mean the two candidates are any less motivated. And the California Teachers Assn., as the main source of campaign spending, also is invested in the outcome.

Leaving the office is two-term incumbent Tony Thurmond, who was barred by term limits from serving again and ran unsuccessfully for governor.

California’s general election takes place on Novemer 3. Learn about L.A.’s city and county races as well races for state office.

Although the contest is defined as nonpartisan — and party affiliations won’t be listed on the ballot — the candidates’ affiliations are likely to decide the race, in the view of political analysts.

In the primary, voters appeared to hew closely to political party lines. Shaw finished first with 22.6% of the vote — apparently consolidating the support of conservatives and Republicans. Barrera, who had 20.3%, split a much larger swath of more liberal votes with six other competitive Democrats. Together the Democrats in the primary garnered 67.6% of the vote.

Democratic voters generally are expected to consolidate behind Barrera in the general election and sweep past the smaller number of Republicans. Shaw hopes to beat the odds, however, by appealing to independents and centrist Democrats, especially with positions on culture-war issues that she thinks will resonate in her favor with parents.

The state superintendent always has had a challenging role — with limited authority over school districts, which are locally managed. Instead, the officeholder managed the California Department of Education, which guides local school districts and also provides partial oversight. In that capacity, the state superintendent also worked in close conjunction with the state board of education — appointed by the governor — and often in concert with the governor and Legislature.

But under a new distribution of powers, the next state superintendent will lose control over the education department. Newsom has consolidated authority over education under the governor, and the education department now will be run by an appointee of the governor. The elected superintendent is reimagined as a student “champion” who would analyze and report on the effectiveness of the education system and also take on an advocacy role.

Shaw, 44, is the president of the Chino Valley Unified Board of Education, overseeing a relatively large school system of 26,000 students in San Bernardino County.

Barrera, 59, is the president of the San Diego Unified Board of Education, which governs the state’s second-largest school system with about 113,000 students. Barrera also serves as a senior adviser to state Supt. Thurmond, whom Shaw has sharply criticized.

Both are parents. Barrera’s children are grown; Shaw’s still are in school.

Shaw broadly characterizes the public education system as failing because of liberal Democratic policies, although she praises the quality of the public school system over which she presides. On the culture-war front, she supports flag bans at schools — which were conceived to target gay pride flags — and book restrictions that target content deemed as sexually obscene.

She became politically activated by the pandemic’s prolonged school closures, COVID vaccine mandates and mask mandates — all of which she opposed.

Despite a consistent policy alignment with the Trump administration, Shaw, a longtime religious conservative and a small business owner, objects to any implication that she is falling into line behind Trump or that she is subservient to his policies.

“I don’t put loyalty behind a person,” Shaw said. “I put my my effort and my love and my everyday sacrifice behind protecting kids, whether it’s this president, the next president or whatever. If they’re on the right side of protecting kids, then, of course, when it comes to the issues, I’m all in. I really honestly hate the division. In my heart, if we just speak on our issues, it’ll bring people together. We could see more eye to eye. We can actually have decent conversations, right?”

At times, Shaw presents a sharp rhetorical style, as when she characterized a September court ruling that placed limits on when school officials would have to notify parents about their child’s gender issues. Shaw believes such notifications should be mandatory. Her first emphatic comment, in an interview and a social media post after the hearing, which she attended, was: “Kangaroo courts. Dishonorable judges.”

Shaw sidesteps discussions about Trump’s immigration enforcement. “Districts should follow the law, communicate clearly with families and keep their attention on the classroom ... That’s where our energy belongs,” she said.

Barrera, in contrast, calls Trump’s immigration raids harmful.

“This assault on our immigrant families by the Trump administration absolutely is a real issue that affects students and affects educators,” he said. “And I’m going to stand up to those assaults, and Californians will continue to stand up to those assaults.”

Barrera defends the quality of public schools and advocates for increased funding and expanding what works from successful campuses to those with poorer academic results.

Barrera sees the superintendent as a convener and organizer to advance initiatives that benefit students — with or without state funding or direction from the governor and Legislature.

As an example, he cited the education department’s effort to promote affordable workforce housing projects on school district property. He also cited efforts to reduce chronic absenteeism, for which the department gathered a working group that has produced guidance for schools.

Barrera has a long career in community and labor organizing and worked as a senior official in two unions. That background, combined with his 18 years on the San Diego school board, where he has been a union ally, made him an ideological fit with CTA. Neither Barrera nor CTA had to compromise values or shift positions to join forces for this campaign.

“I have absolute respect and … a long demonstrated partnership with the unions that represent our educators,” Barrera said, “and believe strongly that unions are democratically elected, governed institutions where the educator voice comes out most powerfully, and so for me, the unions are a partner and will always be a core voice in influencing the way that I make decisions.”

“On his watch, academic outcomes have declined while the state focused on everything but the basics. California law allows sports participation based on gender identity, meaning transgender athletes have a right to participate in girls’ and women’s sports. “Girls are losing roster spots, medals and scholarships. Some don’t even feel comfortable using their own locker rooms, choosing to change in their cars before games rather than be forced to undress in front of boys. That’s not fairness. That’s failure,” she said.

Shaw demonstrated at a track meet to oppose the participation of a trans athlete — which won her plaudits from supporters who felt she was standing up for girl athletes, but criticism from those who felt she was publicly targeting a child for harassment.

Your guide to the California insurance commissioner race: Allen vs. Kim

The race to succeed Ricardo Lara as California insurance commissioner following the devastating January 2025 wildfires is a classic study in contrasts.

It pits a veteran legislator, state Sen. Ben Allen (D-Santa Monica), serving his third and final term in the Senate, against an upstart progressive candidate, Jane Kim, a former San Francisco supervisor. They topped a packed field of candidates in the June 2 primary.

Kim was the leading vote-getter with 26.8% of the vote to Allen’s 19.6%, amid a progressive wave nationally that has led to Zohran Mamdani being elected mayor of New York and Abdul El-Sayed winning the Democratic nomination for senator in Michigan.

With widespread anger over how some insurers have handled claims stemming from wildfires in the state, Kim has stood out with a proposal to establish a nonprofit “single payer” insurance plan run by the state to cover catastrophic fire damage — something that never has been done.

Allen has pointed to a long legislative record seeking to reform the industry and make the home insurance market more attractive to insurers — something heavy on the details but hard to condense into a campaign slogan.

Allen, 48, represents the Palisades fire zone and has a lengthy history of insurance-related legislation, including leading the effort to put a successful $10-billion climate bond on the 2024 ballot, which he calls a key step in reducing fire risk and lowering premiums. A native and resident of Santa Monica, Allen attended Harvard and has a law degree from UC Berkeley. He previously served on the Santa Monica-Malibu Unified School District Board. He is endorsed by the California Democratic Party and leading elected party officials, including U.S. Sens. Adam Schiff and Alex Padilla.

Kim, 49, served as a San Francisco supervisor from 2011 to 2019. Her progressive credentials include leading a campaign to make the city’s community college tuition free and serving as California political director for Bernie Sanders’ 2020 presidential campaign. The daughter of Korean immigrants, she attended Stanford and also has a law degree from Berkeley. Though she ran as a Democrat, she is a former director of the Working Families Party and is endorsed by Sanders (I-Vt.) and the California Federation of Labor Unions.

PTBS in der Ukraine: Wie der Krieg die Psyche belastet

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Fast ein Drittel der ukrainischen Bevölkerung erfüllt die Kriterien für eine Posttraumatische Belastungsstörung. Ein zentraler Auslöser sind Russlands Angriffskrieg und seine Folgen – auch jenseits der Front.

Mehr als vier Jahre dauert Russlands umfassender Angriffskrieg gegen die Ukraine bereits an. Er wird nicht nur an der Front geführt. Immer wieder zerstören Raketen auch Wohnhäuser und zivile Infrastruktur, wie etwa Anlagen zur Stromversorgung.

Tote, Verwundete, Zerstörung, Flucht – all das hinterlässt auch Spuren, die erst auf den zweiten Blick sichtbar werden: In der ukrainischen Zivilbevölkerung erfüllt fast jede und jeder Dritte (31,2 Prozent) die Kriterien für eine Posttraumatische Belastungsstörung (PTBS) oder sogar für eine sogenannte komplexe PTBS, eine noch schwerere Form.

Zu diesem Ergebnis kommt eine repräsentative Umfrage, die das internationale Forschungsnetzwerk "Kooperation und Konflikt im östlichen Europa" (KonKoop) durchgeführt hat. Die Forschenden aus den Bereichen psychische Gesundheit und Sozialwissenschaften befragten mehr als 2500 Ukrainerinnen und Ukrainer, die in den Regionen leben, die weiterhin oder wieder unter Kontrolle der Regierung in Kyjiw sind. Die Studie wurde vom deutschen Forschungsministerium (BMFTR) gefördert und in der Fachzeitschrift European Journal of Psychotraumatology veröffentlicht.

Von einer Posttraumatischen Belastungsstörung (PTBS) spricht man, wenn Menschen ein Trauma erleben – also eine potenzielle oder reale Bedrohung der körperlichen Unversehrtheit oder des Lebens bei sich oder anderen – und sich dieses Erlebnis in der Psyche festsetzt. Betroffene durchleben das traumatisierende Ereignis in sogenannten Flashbacks immer wieder, leiden unter Angstzuständen, Reizbarkeit und Schlafstörungen. In der Regel treten die Symptome erst einige Zeit nach dem Erlebten auf.

Weltweit finden sich bei weniger als zehn Prozent der Allgemeinbevölkerung die Kriterien einer solchen Belastungsstörung, so schätzen Fachleute. In der ukrainischen Bevölkerung ist der Wert laut der neuen Studie also gut dreimal so hoch.

Die Studie beruht auf selbst berichteten Symptomen. Das Ergebnis könnte also davon beeinflusst sein, wie die Teilnehmenden die Fragen interpretierten, so die Forschenden.

"Besonders auffällig an den Ergebnissen ist das enorme Ausmaß der Traumatisierung", so Studienleiterin Deirdre MacManus, Dozentin für Forensische Psychiatrie am King's College London (KCL) in einer Pressemitteilung.

Mehr als 90 Prozent der Befragten gaben an, dass sie mindestens ein traumatisches Erlebnis im Erwachsenenalter hatten. Nicht alle, aber doch viele dieser Erlebnisse hatten mit dem Krieg zu tun. Die Hälfte gab an, einen Angehörigen im Fronteinsatz zu haben. Mehr als 70 Prozent haben Beschuss in ihren Wohngebieten erlebt und 65 Prozent generelle Zerstörungen durch den Krieg.

"Es gibt in der Ukraine keine Familie mehr, die nicht unter dem Krieg gelitten hat, in der jemand entweder gestorben oder im besetzten Gebiet zurückgeblieben ist", berichtet Ruslana Lutsyk-Andrushko der DW. Sie arbeitet als psychosoziale Beraterin für die Hilfsorganisation Ärzte ohne Grenzen in der ukrainischen Stadt Winnyzja.

"Wir können die Ursache nicht allein der Invasion zuschreiben, da es andere Traumata gegeben haben mag, die zur Entwicklung einer PTBS beigetragen haben", sagt die Hauptautorin der Studie, Emma Facer-Irwin. Die Wissenschaftlerin am KCL ist auch als klinische Psychologin bei der britischen Gesundheitsbehörde mit den Schwerpunkten Trauma und PTBS tätig.

Je mehr traumatische Ereignisse eine Person im Laufe ihres Lebens erlebe, desto höher sei die Wahrscheinlichkeit, dass sie eine Posttraumatische Belastungsstörung entwickelt, erklärt Facer-Irwin im DW-Gespräch. Aber: "Wenn man bedenkt, dass es sich hier um die Zivilbevölkerung handelt, nicht um Militärangehörige oder ausschließlich um Vertriebene oder Flüchtlinge, dann können wir schon vermuten, dass der Krieg ein wesentlicher Faktor für die Entwicklung und Verbreitung von PTBS ist."

Viele Menschen in der Ukraine seien täglich der Bedrohung durch Beschuss, Vertreibung, Trauer und Ungewissheit ausgesetzt, betont Studienleiterin MacManus. "Trotz dieser Umstände gelingt es vielen weiterhin, unter außerordentlich schwierigen Bedingungen zu funktionieren und sich anzupassen."

So berichteten zwar viele Menschen von traumatischen Erlebnissen, aber nicht alle von ihnen entwickelten anhaltende Belastungsstörungen, so Facer-Irwin. "Das sagt uns, dass zwei Drittel der Bevölkerung traumatische Ereignisse anders verarbeiten."

Möglicherweise spiele dabei auch das starke ukrainische Nationalbewusstsein eine Rolle, meint die Psychologin: "Dieses Gefühl einer starken kollektiven Kultur, in der man gemeinsam gegen die Invasion vorgeht." Die Forschung zeige, dass das Gefühl, sozial aufgefangen und verbunden zu sein, vor Posttraumatischen Belastungsstörungen schützen kann.

"Deshalb geht es auch darum, unterstützende Umgebungen zu schaffen. Viele Menschen leben in Situationen, denen sie aufgrund des andauernden Krieges nicht einfach entfliehen können. Wir brauchen also PTBS-Traumatherapien, aber auch soziale Hilfsangebote, die einer größeren Bevölkerungsgruppe angeboten werden können."

Einen solchen Ansatz verfolgt das Rehabilitationszentrum von Ärzte ohne Grenzen im ukrainischen Winnyzja. "Wenn Menschen zu uns kommen, sind Stabilisierung und Ressourcenaufbau der erste Schritt", erklärt Ruslana Lutsyk-Andrushko. Erst wenn sich die Menschen besser fühlten, könnten die traumatischen Erinnerungen bearbeitet werden. In einer dritten Phase folge dann die Reintegration der gewonnenen Erfahrungen.

"Dieser Plan zielt darauf ab, Selbsthilfe zu vermitteln, also was man in einer Stresssituation tun kann. Nach dem Aufenthalt im Zentrum verfügen die Betroffenen dann über eine Art Erste-Hilfe-Set, das sie für sich selbst, ihre Kinder, ihre Freunde und in schwierigen Situationen nutzen können."

Derzeit verstärkt Russland seine Angriffe auf die Ukraine, und das belaste die psychische Gesundheit vieler Ukrainerinnen und Ukrainer, berichtet Lutsyk-Andrushko. "Wenn ein Mensch unter Stress lebt und keine Hoffnung auf Besserung sieht, dann ist das natürlich zermürbend. Die Menschen brauchen Zuversicht, Vertrauen in eine bessere Zukunft."

Und so sei ihre Studie vor allem eine Momentaufnahme, sagt Psychologin Facer-Irwin. Die gesamten Auswirkungen des Krieges auf die psychische Gesundheit ließen sich noch gar nicht abschätzen. "Die langfristigen Folgen werden vermutlich erst deutlich, wenn die Menschen die Möglichkeit hatten, sich in Sicherheit zu bringen und sich vollständig vom Krieg zu erholen."

Fifty Billion Seedlings: Can Ethiopia Replant Its Ravaged Forests?

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Women water seedlings at a nursery near Buee, Ethiopia. Michael Tewelde / AFP via Getty Images

A legacy of deforestation has left Ethiopia struggling with more intense flooding and drought. The country is trying to restore its native forests through one of the world’s most ambitious and well-funded tree-planting campaigns, but such projects in other nations have often fallen short.

By Samuel Abate and Freddie Clayton • October 1, 2026

Niku Argane’s farm sits on a steep hillside in central Ethiopia’s semi-mountainous Gurage Zone, where years of flooding have carved deep gullies through the land. Soil that once produced enough barley and potatoes to feed his wife and four children — and left a surplus to sell in the market — has eroded, while drought has made what land remains harder to cultivate.

“We have never stopped farming, but our production declined because of land degradation,” the 32-year-old said from his farm in Ambele Kebele.

Over the last century, Ethiopia nearly eradicated its forests as trees were cleared for farming, grazing, and fuel. The loss of tree cover and tree roots allowed wind and rain to wash away fertile topsoil and contributed to declining crop yields. That vulnerability is now being compounded by climate change. Ethiopia is among the countries most vulnerable to its effects: Heavier downpours are sluicing away depleted soils and triggering landslides, while more intense droughts, combined with the loss of shade that trees once provided, rapidly dry out the land.

Land degradation costs the country an estimated $4.3 billion a year through smaller harvests and lost income to farmers, which also means there is less food to eat. With more than 100 million Ethiopians dependent on agriculture for their livelihoods, the consequences reach far beyond the environment. Climate shocks and crop losses are among the biggest drivers of acute hunger; in 2024, 15.8 million Ethiopians were estimated to need food assistance, roughly 12 percent of the population.

Now the country is trying to reverse this trajectory. Argane planted 80 tree seedlings on his farmland and another 50 in a communal area — a small contribution to a vastly larger effort. In just the last seven years, tens of millions of Ethiopians have planted more than 47 billion seedlings across the country in one of the world’s largest environmental restoration campaigns. Volunteers for the program, which aims to plant 50 billion seedlings by 2030, are planting trees to rebuild native forests and integrating them into farmland. In the process, they are sequestering carbon to help protect future generations from ever more extreme weather.

Such programs often fall short, but Ethiopians are finding hope in the scale of their ambitions. “Reforestation is one of the most difficult things you can imagine,” said Aad Kessler, a researcher at the Netherlands’ Wageningen University. “It almost always fails, but this is so big that even if 30 percent of the trees survive, it’s still huge.”

In the early 20th century, forests covered an estimated 35 to 40 percent of Ethiopia. Its highlands were greener, with forests spanning much of the country’s cool, fertile uplands. But as populations grew and settlements spread, trees were cleared for agriculture, pasture, fuel, and construction. As soils lost their fertility, cultivation moved onto increasingly marginal land, including steep slopes, where tillage and the loss of protective vegetation led to the erosion of fertile topsoil, which washed into rivers and reservoirs. By 1989, forest cover was estimated at less than 3 percent, while Ethiopia’s highlands were losing around 1 billion metric tons of topsoil every year.

Farms in North Gondar in the Ethiopian highlands. The spread of agriculture has stripped the region of trees. Danita Delimont Creative

After the devastating droughts and famines of the 1970s and 1980s, Ethiopia’s government and international donor organizations began investing heavily in soil and water conservation, excluding livestock from worn-out land, planting trees across the country, and building terraces and check dams — small, low barriers of rocks and logs placed across gullies — to slow the flow of rainwater. By the 2000s, restoration programs were being implemented at increasing scale.

The government supercharged that effort in 2019 by turning tree-planting into a nationwide campaign with a scheme known as the Green Legacy Initiative, which mobilized millions of individuals and groups, including farmers, public servants, schools, and religious organizations, and distributed free seedlings across the country. In 2024, the government established the Green Legacy and Landscape Restoration Special Fund, committing 0.5 percent to 1 percent of the annual federal budget — roughly $73 million to $145 million — to restoring landscapes. It was an unusually large commitment for a low-income country, and the program’s success and ambition, according to development experts, contributed to the selection of Ethiopia as the host of next year’s U.N. climate conference — a prestigious honor that will help the nation showcase its greening efforts.

In August, 26.6 million Ethiopians planted 800 million tree seedlings in a single day, according to government figures, beating the previous year’s tally of 714.7 million. One of the volunteers, Yeshihareg Teferi, traveled at sunrise from her home in Addis Ababa to Tulu Dimtu so she could hunch in the rain to dig holes in the mud. It was Teferi’s fifth year participating in the annual event. Previously she’d planted indigenous species, including African wild olive and African fern pine. This time, she chose juniper. “We have a responsibility to ensure future generations inherit a green and fertile environment, rather than barren land,” she said.

There are already signs the work is having an impact.

The government reports that national forest cover rose from 17.2 percent in 2019 to 23.6 percent in 2023, while studies of restored areas have found substantial reductions in soil erosion. Between 2013 and 2023, the annual deforestation rate fell from more than 350 square miles to around 100 square miles.

Volunteers plant seedlings on the outskirts of Addis Ababa as part of a nationwide tree-planting drive in early August.

Such efforts can also benefit wildlife. In Abijata-Shalla National Park, in the Ethiopian highlands, workers replanted degraded areas and built terraces and contour bunds (small earthen ridges that run across hillsides to slow runoff and allow water to soak into the soil). As trees and native grasses returned, more water infiltrated into the ground, and water levels on lakes in the park began to rise, said Kumera Wakjira, director-general of the Ethiopian Wildlife Conservation Authority. More water spurred the return of fish and fish-eating birds like cormorants and African darters. Grant’s gazelles and wild dogs are also expanding into areas where they were previously scarce, Wakjira said.

But planted trees are not guaranteed to survive. A 2022 study of 176 reforestation projects in Asia found that around half of planted trees did not live five years. And trees alone do not make a forest, said Endalkachew Wolde-Meskel, a soil scientist and senior researcher at the Center for International Forestry Research: That process usually requires the regeneration of grasses and other vegetation, in addition to soil- and water-conservation measures and management by local communities. “The objective is not really ‘plant trees,’” he said. “It is to restore the functioning of the entire landscape.”

Emiru Birhane Hizikias, an Ethiopian restoration ecologist at Norway’s Nord University, said it was a “blessing, politically speaking,” to have the prime minister lead on tree planting but that the scale of the Green Legacy Initiative had outpaced the systems needed to make it work effectively on the ground. Too often, he said, the focus is on getting huge numbers of seedlings into the earth rather than ensuring their survival. You can’t expect a baby to grow if you don’t care for it, he said, but “that’s how plantation in Ethiopia is being done.”

Abreham Berta Aneseyee, an environmental scientist at Ethiopia’s Wolkite University and an adviser to the Ministry of Agriculture, disputes that characterization, saying the planting campaign is accompanied by practical advice from government experts in forestry and farming. The experts go from farm to farm, advising smallholders on how to plant and care for different types of seedlings, he said, including how deeply to place them in soil and how to conserve soil with structures like bunds. They also advise farmers on managing forests and protecting trees rather than cutting them down.

A 2026 study of more than 44,500 seedlings planted in southern Ethiopia under the Green Legacy Initiative in 2023 found that about 80 percent had survived, with rates significantly higher among older seedlings, wet-season plantings, and seedlings whose planters had received training in best “plantation practices.” In Humbo and Boloso Sore districts in Ethiopia’s southwestern Wolaita Zone, the Green Legacy Initiative has been layered onto longer-running efforts to sustainably manage land, with communities building check dams to stabilize gullies, digging half-moon ditches to trap water and let it soak into the soil, and planting trees alongside grasses and other vegetation.

Left: Half-moon ditches trap runoff, helping to nourish plants and combat erosion. Right: A check dam in a gully stems erosion by slowing the flow of water. Courtesy of the Alliance of Bioversity International and CIAT

Ethiopia’s approach has few direct parallels in Africa for its combination of political leadership, funding, public mobilization, and scale, said Kessler of Wageningen University. He compared the effort with China’s restoration of the Loess Plateau, one of the world’s most highly eroded regions. In the 1990s, China launched major restoration efforts on the plateau, including a World Bank-backed watershed rehabilitation project and, starting in 1999, the Grain for Green program, which paid farmers to restrict cultivation and plant trees, shrubs, and grasses. By 2016, more than 11,500 square miles of rain-fed cropland had been converted to more dense forest or grassland, contributing to a 25 percent increase in vegetative cover in a decade.

The comparison has limits, though. China paid farmers to take degraded slopes out of cultivation, while millions of Ethiopian smallholders still depend on the land for food and livelihoods. But Kessler said the two efforts shared a fundamental approach: “Do it massively, and then see where you end up.”

Kessler’s work, which includes a government-backed field project in Ethiopia, takes a more participatory approach, involving farmers and communities in decisions about what to restore and how. “Of course it is better [than top-down action],” he said, “but it takes more time.”

Even as Ethiopia invests in rebuilding forests and soils, climate change is altering the conditions landscapes must withstand. In the arid pastoral regions of Afar and Somali, the Green Legacy Initiative focuses on drought-tolerant species, but it is unclear whether the trees planted today will be able to withstand the conditions they face decades from now.

Volunteers search for bodies in the aftermath of a 2024 landslide in the Geze Gofa district in southern Ethiopia. Michele Spatari / AFP via Getty Images

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change projects more frequent and intense rainfall in East Africa as global temperatures rise. In July 2024, exceptionally heavy rainfall in Ethiopia’s Geze Gofa district, in the southern highlands, triggered two landslides that killed at least 257 people. Recent studies also suggest that climate change is making El Niños more frequent and severe, with consequences for East Africa. This summer, the Famine Early Warning Systems Network linked El Niño to “historically poor” summer rains in parts of Ethiopia, leaving some areas with their driest June and July on record.

For some farmers, the changing climate has already complicated the most basic requirement of restoration: getting seedlings to survive their first season. Belayneh Mamo, 65, a farmer from Ethiopia’s northern and drought-prone Amhara region, relies on July rainfall for his teff, sorghum, maize, and wheat for bulgur. He also receives government-provided coffee, neem, and gesho seeds, but was unable to plant any crop until August, when the season’s first rains arrived.

“In previous years, September was a period when crops were already developing and food such as bulgur and maize were becoming available for consumption,” he said. “The delays affected the seedlings we plant, our crops, and the availability of animal feed.”

On Argane’s hillside in the Gurage Zone, however, signs of restoration are already visible. Since 2019, he has planted grass, fodder, fenugreek, and fruit trees along the edges of his fields, helping to hold soil in place while providing feed for the four cows, six goats, and three sheep that he keeps for milk and meat. His barley harvest has risen from about 1,800 pounds per acre to roughly 3,200, while the trees and grasses have reduced erosion and slowed the flow of water across his land during heavy rains.

“Restoring degraded land isn’t just about protecting the environment,” Argane said. “It’s also about making life better for my family and community.”
