Six Canadians injured, one ‘unaccounted for’ in fatal Spanish train crash

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Emergency services work at the scene of a collision between two trains in Urda, Spain, on Wednesday.Manu Fernandez/The Associated Press

Six Canadians are among the injured and a seventh remained unaccounted for following a high-speed rail crash in central Spain Wednesday morning.

The deadly accident occurred just before 9 a.m. local time, when one train slammed into another that had stopped on the tracks. Spanish officials reported at least 68 people killed, and another 58 were receiving treatment at local hospitals.

Global Affairs Canada spokesperson Thida Ith said Wednesday the department had confirmation six Canadians injured in the crash, and one more was still unaccounted for by Wednesday evening. Ith said Canadian officials are providing consular assistance and are in contact with local authorities to gather more information.

“Our thoughts are with their families and loved ones as well as all of those affected,” said Ith.

At least 68 dead after two high-speed trains collide in Spain

Earlier in the day, Prime Minister Mark Carney expressed Canada’s solidarity with victims and family members and indicated Ottawa would help as needed. Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon said on social media he had reached out to his Spanish counterpart.

“I also thank the emergency responders and medical teams working around the clock to help those impacted by this tragedy,” McKinnon said.

Spain’s rail system operator ADIF said the crash occurred Wednesday morning in the province of Toledo. It involved two high-speed trains carrying hundreds of passengers and heading in the same direction from the capital Madrid to Seville in southwestern Spain.

A train operated by the state-owned RENFE company struck the rear of another train operated by private company IRYO that was stopped. The collision derailed two carriages on the tail of the IRYO train and the front of the RENFE train.

Authorities said 397 passengers were in the RENFE train and 147 passengers in the IRYO train.

Rail service was suspended between Madrid, Ciudad Real and Andalusia.

Spain’s Transport Minister Oscar Puente said the crash was caused by what he called an “inexplicable” order from a central command centre for Spain’s trains after a security system had failed. He said the train engineers were not to blame.

Puente said at 8:30 a.m., local time, the security system on a stretch of 63 kilometres stopped working due to a computer malfunction, causing all trains on that stretch to stop. But the central command told the RENFE train to start running again without any speed restrictions, and it ended up colliding with the stopped IRYO train at 8:53 a.m.

Puente said the train never should have been told to start moving again, since there was still a “stop signal” active on that line, and that it should at least have been told to go at a reduced speed.

Authorities were still trying to determine Wednesday how fast the train was going, but it could have been at 200 kph for that stretch of the track, Puente said.

The crash, which occurred in a flat stretch surrounded by olive fields in between the towns of Urda and Los Yebenes, left carriages shredded into bits of metal and other material, scattering debris down a gravel slope.

Until this year, there had been no fatal crash on Spain’s dedicated high-speed lines since the network opened in 1992. Now there have been two in just 10 months.

In January, 46 people died in southern Spain when an IRYO train jumped the track and hit an oncoming RENFE train.

“Today all of Spain is grieving,” Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez said from the crash site, where he announced that his government would declare three days of national mourning.

Sánchez called for increased safety measures to restore confidence in a high-speed rail system that was once a source of national pride.

“It is unacceptable for a country like Spain to suffer two high-speed rail accidents in the same year,” said Sánchez, who faces a national election on Nov. 29.

Russian Bookseller Chitai-Gorod Spent Millions Destroying Banned Books, CEO Says

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Russia’s largest bookstore chain, Chitai-Gorod-Bukvoed, has spent around 100 million rubles ($1.2 million) in recent years removing and destroying books that violate the country’s increasingly strict censorship laws, its CEO told the RBC news outlet on Tuesday.

CEO Alexander Brychkin said the expenses were connected to storing, transporting and paying specialized services to shred the censored books, “all of which adds up to a huge volume of work and significant costs.”

“We cannot just throw these books in the trash. They’re toxic waste. Accordingly, they must be disposed of, and this is also at our expense. A contractor destroys the books on camera using an industrial shredder,” he said.

“No one is reimbursing our expenses,” Brychkin told RBC.

Since the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Russian stores and libraries have had to remove scores of books from their shelves as the government increasingly curtails freedom of speech and the press.

Brychkin said Chitai-Gorod has spent “tens of millions” of additional rubles placing anti-drug warning stickers on 2 million books.

He argued that the government also denies booksellers a fair opportunity to bring their inventories into compliance before facing legal prosecution.

“A book at the moment of its release complied with the spirit and letter of Russian law. And then at some point an expert evaluation arises,” Brychkin said.

“And at the exact moment this evaluation appears, they begin to hold us liable. We do not even have a chance to meet the requirements of the law,” he told RBC.

Brychkin proposed the creation of a system that warns booksellers beforehand when a specific title enters state review so that they can reduce their legal risks.

Russia-Africa Summit: Putin’s key proposals on trade, technology and Western finance alternatives

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Russia and Africa can expand trade and share technology while reducing dependence on Western financial systems, President Vladimir Putin said at the Russia-Africa Economic and Humanitarian Forum in Moscow on Wednesday.

The event forms part of the third Russia-Africa summit, bringing delegations from 48 African countries to Moscow on October 28–29.

Speaking alongside Burundian President and African Union chair Evariste Ndayishimiye, Putin proposed closer banking links, called for bilateral trade to at least double, and offered Russian expertise in energy, agriculture, healthcare, and artificial intelligence.

Here are the key takeaways from the speeches and opening-day announcements.

Putin urged Russian and African banks to expand direct ties, open branches in each other’s markets and strengthen payments in national currencies.

He proposed greater use of alternatives for exchanging payment information, including the Bank of Russia’s financial messaging system and the pan-African payments system. These arrangements would lower transaction costs and protect settlements from “politicized interference” by unfriendly countries, Putin said.

He argued that countries that don’t have their own financial systems end up relying on Western infrastructure, which ultimately limits their sovereignty.

Western countries are resisting the emergence of a multipolar world because they remain accustomed to colonial thinking and “growing fabulously rich at others’ expense,” Putin argued.

He accused them of using sanctions, tariffs, economic pressure, and interference to preserve their influence, including attempts to push African countries back into “debt bondage” through predatory lending.

Servicing such debts diverts resources from long-term development, Putin argued, calling for reforms of the International Monetary Fund and World Bank and a stronger African role in their decisions.

Russia-Africa trade exceeded $27 billion last year, compared with $18 billion in 2022, Putin said, calling for the figure to at least double and surpass $50 billion in the near future.

He added that neither unprecedented restrictions against Russia nor Western pressure on African partners have been able to stop economic relations between the two from developing and expressed appreciation for this “firm and principled position of our African friends.”

Putin stated that Russian businesses support local processing, employment and tax revenues rather than engaging in “predatory, neocolonial exploitation” of Africa’s natural resources and people. Their investments also support healthcare, education, and environmental projects, he said.

Russia and African partners are implementing more than 30 energy projects in 16 countries, Putin said, describing affordable and reliable power as essential to industrial development and better living standards.

He offered cooperation on hydropower, including modernizing existing plants and supplying remote communities, alongside other energy technologies.

Russia’s Rosatom and Togo’s Energy Ministry signed a memorandum on nuclear energy cooperation. Rosatom chief Aleksey Likhachev said this could include floating nuclear power plants to supply electricity to the West African country.

Putin also stated that Russia had exported 13 million tons of agricultural products to Africa in the first nine months of 2026 with more than 40 African countries receiving Russian grain and fertilizers.

However, he stressed that Africa has the potential to feed itself, noting that Moscow is offering seeds, machinery, agricultural technology, storage, and processing infrastructure to help develop that capacity.

Transport proposals also include railways, roads, shipping routes, and logistics hubs, Putin said, inviting African airlines to begin flights to Russia.

Russian and Mozambican ministers also signed a road cooperation memorandum and discussed a possible direct shipping route.

Technological independence has become an essential part of sovereignty, Putin argued, pointing to AI, cybersecurity, personal data, and digital infrastructure. Russia is ready to share its expertise, including digital public services and medical technologies, he added.

Putin also announced that Russia’s epidemic-control assistance program for African countries would be extended through 2029.

Under the program, eight countries have already received 15 mobile laboratories and around 450 African specialists have been trained. Russian doctors continue to assist against infections including cholera and the outbreak of a new Ebola strain that began in the spring, he said.

Further cooperation could include local production of medicines and vaccines, diagnostic centers, and digital healthcare services.

Around 40,000 African citizens are studying at Russian universities, Putin said, with enrollment increasing by half since 2020. Scholarship places have nearly tripled over the same period, reaching 5,500 for the 2026–2027 academic year.

The president proposed to establish a Russia-African Youth Union to coordinate further exchanges between youth organizations. He also called for Russian news agencies and broadcasters to expand their presence across Africa and said that Moscow would welcome more African correspondents working in Russia.

In his closing remarks, Putin stressed that discussions between senior officials alone are insufficient, which is why this year’s forum has been designed to be more accessible and focused on practical opportunities.

He invited participants to an exhibition presenting Russian technologies and proposals for joint projects, saying Moscow is ready to share its achievements with African partners.

Putin said that leaders also intend to adopt a Russia-Africa partnership action plan for 2027–2029 when the summit concludes on Thursday,

During the plenary session, Burundian President Evariste Ndayishimiye said that Africa wants a greater role in the global economy and partnerships that help transform its resources into prosperity for its population.

“We do not want to remain merely exporters of natural resources and then import finished goods,” he said, calling for African participation throughout production and trade.

The African Union chair urged investment in local processing, technology transfers, and training, alongside energy, agriculture, transport, and infrastructure.

He also called for diversified trade, payment systems, and logistical routes connecting Africa with Russia and BRICS.

Canadian diver Erik Brown helped save boys trapped in a flooded Thai cave

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Erik Brown, centre, with a group of foreign divers and U.S. soldiers, gather at the Tham Luang cave area on July 6, 2018.LILLIAN SUWANRUMPHA/Getty Images

Canadian scuba diver Erik Brown was not about to call himself a hero after risking his life to help rescue 12 boy soccer players and their coach, who became trapped in a cave in Thailand as it flooded during a monsoon in 2018. But many people around the world view him as one.

Going in with a team of rescuers after the monsoon ended and before another torrential rainstorm would strike, Mr. Brown spent 63 hours over nine days and seven missions in the Tham Luang Nang Non cave complex in the country’s northernmost province. In what he described as “zero visibility,” he navigated a treacherous four-kilometre underwater route while laying cables, carrying equipment and fighting rushing currents and other obstacles. Working with a hastily assembled team of international divers and two Australian doctor-divers, he helped relay the boys and their coach, who could not swim and were anesthetized to prevent them from panicking and removing their full face masks and breathing gear. Tethered along an overhead guide rope, all 13 of those who were trapped were transported face-down to safety. Their survival prompted rejoicing among a worldwide audience who had followed the rescue through news stories.

“I won’t lie. In my head, I was [thinking] there [was] very little chance it was going to work. … But it did,” Mr. Brown told CBC.

Mr. Brown died on Sept. 15 at the age of 44, his brother, Kirk Brown, told The Globe and Mail.

For his contribution to the Thai rescue, Mr. Brown received several honours, including Canada’s Medal of Bravery (2019); the Royal Canadian Humane Association’s Bronze Medal for Bravery (2018) and admission to Thailand’s Most Admirable Order of the Direkgunabhorn, First Class (2020). He and the other rescuers were fêted at official celebrations in Thailand. But Mr. Brown, who lived in Thailand at the time and felt obligated to help, took the praise in stride, according to Kirk.

“He didn’t feel what he did was heroic,” Kirk said. “He was really good at what he did. He was probably one of the only people that could do it. … So, he went and did it.”

Divers including Brown, second from right, head to Tham Luang Nang Non cave to continue the rescue operation on July 3, 2018.Linh Pham/Getty Images

Erik Richard Brown was born in Vancouver on July 13, 1982, and grew up on a hobby farm in its suburb of Langley, B.C., where his family raised cows and chickens. He was the younger of two sons of Richard Brown and Dorothy Brown (née Butchart). His father, a Vancouver firefighter, died of cancer when Erik was nine. Dorothy Brown, now retired, worked as a flight attendant and took the family on dozens of international trips.

“[Erik] always had that exploration bug,” Kirk said.

While growing up in Langley, Erik developed a love for sports and competed for his high-school volleyball and basketball teams, while also playing minor hockey. He went on to earn a business degree from the University of Victoria and then ventured to Australia, where he worked odd jobs and surfed. During a trip to the Great Barrier Reef in 2004, he completed an entry-level recreational scuba-diving course on a whim and, as he told Diver magazine in 2020, “got hooked.” Three months later, he became a certified divemaster, qualified to lead and guide other divers on tours and assist instructors.

“At the end of that trip, I ended up dislocating my shoulder and having to come back to Canada for surgery,” he told Diver. “Surfing was no longer an option, so I tried to think of ways I could stay travelling and stay in the water, and that led me back into diving. I flew to the Philippines after my surgery and became an instructor.”

Mr. Brown went on to train technical and recreational divers, instructors and their trainers, while co-owning dive centres in Thailand and Egypt. He also explored the waters off Iceland, South Africa and elsewhere, produced and appeared in underwater photos and videos, and promoted Hollis-branded scuba-diving equipment internationally.

“For many years, Erik was literally the face of Hollis,” said fellow diver and instructor Greg DeSatnick, who co-owned an internationally renowned dive centre in Egypt with him.

Before the Thai cave rescue, Mr. Brown had undertaken notable rescue missions and shipwreck explorations. During the 2010s, he saved a client trapped in a narrow section of an underwater canyon off the coast of Egypt by removing the diver’s other equipment while keeping his breathing apparatus in his mouth, Mr. DeSatnick said. The manoeuvre was “essentially unheard of” and Mr. Brown “did it flawlessly,” Mr. DeSatnick added.

In 2013, Mr. Brown joined a team of technical divers who placed a memorial plaque on the wreck of the Second World War U.S. Coast Guard cutter USCGC Alexander Hamilton off Iceland.

As his career unfolded, Mr. Brown developed a passion for cave diving, taking annual trips to Mexico and Florida.

“Caves are where I find myself most relaxed,” he told Diver. “It is the diving I reserve for myself.”

In 2018, however, he put his skills to work for others, flying from his base on the Thai island of Koh Tao to the flooded cave complex. Initially expecting to play a supporting role in an operation involving 9,000 international volunteers, he soon joined the evacuation effort because of his cave-diving expertise.

Mr. Brown headed a transfer station and administered a ketamine-based general anesthetic to the players and coach. The anesthetic lasted about 45 minutes and had to be readministered periodically along the route, he told Diver in 2020 and attendees at the Diving Equipment Marketing Association conference and trade show in Las Vegas later in 2018.

“We had small medical stations set up and would inject the kids if needed,” Mr. Brown told Diver. He and the other divers involved in the rescue had received a crash course on how to do the injections.

“I don’t think I need to explain that this is outside my normal skill set,” Mr. Brown added, indicating that he had been nervous about administering the anesthetic.

Mr. Brown played himself in the 2019 film The Cave, a Thai drama about the rescue, and he became a public speaker, discussing his role in the complex operation at diving conferences, trade shows and other events in various countries. Publicity surrounding Mr. Brown’s exploits boosted his profile considerably.

Brown played himself in the movie The Cave.FREDRIK DIVALL/AFP/Getty Images

But Mr. Brown was already well-known in the global diving community before the Thai rescue as a respected diver, instructor and dive-centre operator, Mr. DeSatnick said. Teaching under the Technical Diving International certification program, he helped develop the next generation of professional divers.

Technical diving extends beyond recreational diving, enabling longer, deeper and more complex dives, Mr. DeSatnick said. It requires specialized training, careful gas management and knowledge of sophisticated equipment such as closed-circuit rebreathers, which recycle breathing gas to extend dive times. These skills allow divers to explore underwater cave systems and deep shipwrecks extensively. Mr. Brown developed and tested innovative techniques, procedures and philosophies before introducing them to his students, Mr. DeSatnick said.

“He was just extremely epic,” Mr. DeSatnick said. “He was a game-changer for the technical-diving world.”

Mr. Brown was also a life-changer according to Kirk Brown, Mr. DeSatnick, other colleagues, friends and family members, who paid tribute to him on Facebook.

“He was pretty much everything to everyone,” said Kirk, who was one of his brother’s technical-diving students. “If he was your friend, he was your best friend. If he was your roommate, he was your favourite roommate with the most stories. If he was your teacher, he’s probably the one that taught you the most.

Mr. Brown’s family has not disclosed the cause of his death or where he died. He leaves his mother, brother and a godson, Erik Ratchinsky, who was named after him.

Journalist Andrea Warner ‘changed the way we write about music’

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Vancouver music journalist Andrea Warner died on Sept. 27 from cancer.Chloe Krause/Supplied

While Andrea Warner was waiting for her first appointment to see an oncologist after her cancer diagnosis in March, 2024, the first song she and her husband, Carlos Hernandez Fisher, heard was Michael Bolton’s How Am I Supposed To Live Without You. While she felt it should be “illegal” to play that song in the cancer agency waiting room, she also found it very funny.

It was the beginning of her waiting room playlists, which she shared on Instagram alongside updates about her treatment for endometrial cancer. The posts reflected the music journalist’s honesty, gratitude and what she would describe to her editor, Jen Sookfong Lee, as “pathological optimism.”

“Yesterday’s waiting room playlist was as aggressively mid as this news. At least there’s always the music. Long live this wild, cursed hilarious algo,” Ms. Warner wrote on April 23, after learning that some of her tumours had spread.

On Oct. 2, her husband announced that Ms. Warner died on Sept. 27 from the cancer she had fought for nearly three years. She was 47. Surrounded by family, friends and some of her favourite music in her final days, she left behind one last waiting room playlist.

Ms. Warner was a feminist music journalist, author and social justice advocate who championed overlooked artists and challenged how people thought about pop culture. She examined misogyny and questioned why some artists were celebrated while others were dismissed.

“I think she really honestly shows us how ethical, generous, but still entertaining and funny that cultural criticism can be,” Ms. Lee said. “She fundamentally changed the way we write about music in this country.”

Ms. Warner wrote four books, We Oughta Know: How Four Women Ruled the ’90s and Changed Canadian Music; Buffy Sainte-Marie: The Authorized Biography; Rise Up And Sing! Power, Protest, and Activism in Music; and The Time of My Life: Dirty Dancing. Alongside Susan Aglukark, she also co-authored Kihiani: A Memoir of Healing. Ms. Warner was with CBC Music since 2012, eventually becoming an associate producer and a co-host of the weekly podcast Pop This! for a decade.

Andrea Warner was born on Jan. 25, 1979, in East Vancouver to Bob Warner and Elsie Marie Warner (née Allen). Her father was 21 and her mother was 18 when they walked down the aisle, a month before Andrea’s birth. They later had another daughter, whom they named Jennifer.

While growing up, Andrea spent many Sundays in her father’s secondhand shop. When she was in Grade 5, her parents separated shortly before Christmas. Living in a two-bedroom apartment in East Vancouver above a drugstore with her sister, father and grandmother, she received a precious electronic keyboard, a big-ticket item considering the humbleness of the family’s circumstances.

The first time she can remember writing, it was a poem in the shape of a keyboard, letting the keys give voice to the breakup.

By Grade 6, Andrea was writing long novels by hand every night, and poetry when, she joked, she should have been paying attention in class. Her love of reading started in her early teens, with the Babysitters Club series and books by Judy Blume. Eventually, she devoured Shakespeare and cracked open the works of Maya Angelou and bell hooks.

One of the first places she was published was her high school newspaper although the first issue she contributed to was banned, partly because of an editorial Andrea wrote criticizing a new provincewide mandatory career-preparation curriculum that overlooked students who already had jobs.

Ms. Warner worked part-time after school, and with her earnings she would buy Soap Opera Weekly, Soap Opera Digest, and when she was really flush, Entertainment Weekly. It was how she learned about people; the magazines took TV, film and music criticism seriously.

After her father died suddenly when she was in Grade 12, she became the first person in her immediate family to attend university. At the University of Victoria, she accidentally enrolled in professional writing courses instead of the creative writing ones she had intended; she eventually left to pursue journalism studies at Langara College.

When she secured an internship at Soap Opera Weekly between her first and second year, her professors weren’t impressed. They didn’t care that she was living in New York, or living at the same place Leonard Cohen stayed, or that she was getting bylines. Ms. Warner admired soap operas for tackling taboo topics like abortion, racism, homophobia and mental health, and believed the fans – who were predominantly female – deserved respect.

After briefly running her own magazine devoted to arts, culture, politics and feminism, she eventually found herself back at the university she once left, taking the creative writing classes she always meant to take.

It was around then that she met Mr. Hernandez Fisher, whom she married in 2009. Together, they went to Seattle’s Bumbershoot arts and music festival, where Ms. Warner heard Miranda July speak about equity and injustice, power and risk.

The experience inspired her to quit her job and move to Vancouver to pursue writing full-time.

Holly Gordon, a digital producer at CBC Music who worked with Ms. Warner for 14 years, remembers her arriving at pitch meetings overflowing with ideas. She wrote about the greats she admired, from Leonard Cohen to the Tragically Hip, without being starstruck.

“She was really good at bringing a lot of love to a project, even if she was fully examining something,” Ms. Gordon said. “It’s so easy to be judgmental or a bit cutting about something, but it’s a lot harder to be kinder, but still be very to the point.”

She made conscious decisions to focus on the stories of women, women-identifying people and gender-nonconforming people.

“I prioritize telling their stories, asking about their art, celebrating and reassessing their historical contribution to our culture, framing their brilliance with purpose and intention because all too often, a woman’s work goes unnoticed, undervalued or dismissed entirely,” Ms. Warner said in a public talk.

At one point in her career, she started looking closely at everything she loved and everything she thought she hated. As a teenager, she believed Alanis Morissette and Sarah McLachlan were the best, and Celine Dion and Shania Twain were the worst. Confronting her internalized misogyny became the basis of her first book, We Oughta Know.

That thoughtfulness shaped Ms. Warner’s relationships with the people she worked with.

At one point after Ms. Warner’s cancer diagnosis in 2024, Ms. Aglukark travelled to Vancouver to ensure that Ms. Warner wasn’t pushing herself too hard to finish the book. She says in the three days they spent together, she saw her commitment to relationships and writing first-hand.

“I left Vancouver having a deeper respect for the humanity of industry. We forget that because we have deadlines, we have stories. But she was the job. She was a professional writer to the core, and respected the story so much that there was a humanness and a deeper connection,” Ms. Aglukark said.

At the end of an interview with Buffy Sainte-Marie in 2018, while Ms. Warner was working on her authorized biography of the singer, the two women shared that they wished they didn’t have to get off the phone. In her research for the interview, Ms. Warner was “struck” by the lack of a historical record about Ms. Sainte-Marie, she later said, compared with her musical peers.

Ms. Warner wrote the book, which involved about 60 hours of interviews, with the musician based in Hawaii and Ms. Warner in Vancouver. Five years after its publication, Ms. Sainte-Marie came under fire after an investigation by the CBC’s The Fifth Estate into her claims of Indigenous identity. The show’s investigation found evidence that contradicted the account of Ms. Sainte-Marie’s family background that is published in the biography. Ms. Warner never spoke publicly to address those revelations.

Whether it was a collaborator on a book or a stranger at a party, Ms. Lee says she “walked into a room expecting to love everybody.”

Mr. Hernandez Fisher says Ms. Warner was constantly introducing people she thought should know one another and finding ways to support those around her.

Looking back on his time with her, he remembers “a series of adventures.” In the past few years alone, they travelled to San Luis Obispo, Calif., to stay at the Madonna Inn, visited Mr. Hernandez Fisher’s family outside Madrid, often renting a car to explore different areas, and went to London.

He also thinks about their quieter moments, like lying in bed together or watching British crime shows with her grandmother.

Mr. Hernandez Fisher says that through her illness, Ms. Warner never stopped wanting to keep writing – even when she couldn’t. Until the end, she was going through her notes and e-mails for different book ideas, such as a young adult fiction and a memoir filled with her waiting room playlists, he says.

By the early summer of 2026, her latest chemotherapy had stopped working and the cancer had spread. While she never felt well, her husband says, she felt better than she had in a long time.

Mr. Hernandez Fisher remembers her final weeks at home and days at Vancouver General Hospital, as a time that was both sad and joyful. As Ms. Warner’s community streamed in to say their goodbyes, he says, they turned the 10th floor of the hospital “into a real party zone.” That’s what he is holding on to.

Her sense of care for her direct community was inseparable from the one for the larger world, which she was always striving to change.

“She showed us by example how you can be a good person and still be critical of the culture. I don’t think anyone else did it before, and I’m sure many of us will try to emulate [it] going forward,” Ms. Lee said.

In April, 2025, Ms. Warner posted that while she was in the waiting room at BC Cancer, the Carole King song It’s Too Late came on the sound system. The song’s lyrics took on a new, unintended meaning. Even during one of the most difficult periods of her life, she found herself inspired to collect and reflect on the songs she was “subjected to” while waiting for her appointments.

It was another way for Ms. Warner to let people into her life and make them laugh a little more, and think a little deeper.

“It is hard and laughter helps,” Ms. Warner wrote on Instagram, “but so does thinking of ways I can turn toward small acts of kindness, love, resistance and advocacy.”

Ms. Warner leaves her husband, Mr. Hernandez Fisher; mother, Elsie Warner; sister, Jennifer Warner; nephew, Alex Warner-Smith; cousin, Kamiko Allen; and grandmothers, Lena Warner and Donna Allen.
