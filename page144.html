Scientists discover a hidden switch that burns fat and blocks new fat cells

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a remarkable protein switch that influences whether cells burn fat or store it. Disabling this protein increases fat consumption and interferes with the formation of new fat cells, raising the possibility of a different approach to treating obesity. Subsequent research has strengthened the discovery, revealing that the same protein helps regulate how much energy the body burns.

What if one of the keys to fighting obesity lies not in suppressing appetite, but in changing how the body's cells use energy?

Researchers at the Weizmann Institute of Science have identified a protein called MTCH2, nicknamed "Mitch," that plays an important role in controlling metabolism. When scientists disabled this protein in laboratory experiments, cells began consuming more fuel, including fats and carbohydrates. They also found that removing Mitch could interfere with the process through which immature cells develop into fat cells.

The discovery builds on earlier experiments in mice, in which switching off Mitch in muscle tissue increased energy expenditure, improved endurance, and protected the animals from diet-related obesity.

The findings, published in The EMBO Journal in January 2025, suggest that manipulating this protein could eventually offer a way to influence both fat burning and fat accumulation. Additional research published later that year has provided further evidence of its importance in regulating body fat and metabolism.

Why Scientists Are Searching for Better Obesity Treatments

Over the past decade, medications designed to promote weight loss have transformed obesity treatment. Drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have helped many patients achieve substantial reductions in body weight, largely by influencing appetite and the body's responses to food.

But losing weight does not necessarily mean losing only fat.

Weight reduction can also involve a decline in lean body mass, which includes muscle and other tissues. Preserving muscle is especially important because it supports strength, mobility, and metabolic health.

A 2026 analysis of 20 clinical trials involving 15,782 participants found that lean mass accounted for approximately 25% to 39% of the weight lost with several widely used weight-loss medications. Importantly, the researchers found that comparable proportional losses also occurred with lifestyle interventions, while adding resistance training helped preserve more lean tissue.

These concerns have encouraged scientists to investigate ways to reduce body fat while protecting the muscles needed for long-term health.

Professor Atan Gross and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute arrived at a possible avenue through an unexpected discovery involving Mitch.

Switching Off One Protein Protected Mice From Obesity

In earlier experiments, Gross's team reduced the activity of MTCH2 in the muscles of mice. The effects were striking.

The genetically modified animals showed increased metabolic activity and were resistant to obesity caused by their diet. Their muscles also developed more fibers with a high capacity for using oxygen, helping improve physical endurance.

The mice performed better in exercise and stress tests and showed improvements in heart function. In addition to resisting excess fat accumulation, they experienced favorable changes in their overall body composition.

The results suggested that Mitch was doing much more than controlling a single metabolic reaction. Somehow, this protein appeared to influence the body's ability to use energy, store fat, and sustain physical activity.

That raised an intriguing question: How could disabling one protein produce such widespread effects?

To investigate, the researchers turned their attention to mitochondria, the microscopic structures responsible for supplying much of the energy that cells need to function.

The Mitochondria Connection Behind Fat Burning

Mitochondria are frequently described as the powerhouses of cells because they convert energy from food into a usable form called ATP, or adenosine triphosphate.

ATP acts as a cellular energy currency. Muscles use it to contract, cells need it to grow and repair themselves, and countless biological processes depend on a steady supply.

However, mitochondria are not static structures. They constantly change shape, joining together into larger networks or separating into smaller units.

When mitochondria fuse, they can form interconnected systems that help coordinate energy production. When they divide, the resulting structures may operate differently, altering how efficiently cells produce and consume energy.

Gross's team had previously established that Mitch plays a major role in mitochondrial fusion, the process that allows separate mitochondria to merge.

Without this protein, the mitochondrial network becomes fragmented. Although the cells can still generate energy, they experience changes in energy efficiency and demand that can force them to consume more fuel.

This offered a possible explanation for the unusual metabolism observed in the mice. Instead of conserving energy efficiently, their cells appeared to be operating in a more energy-intensive state.

But would disabling Mitch have a similar effect in human cells?

Human Cells Begin Burning More Fat Without Mitch

To answer that question, researchers led by doctoral student Sabita Chourasia used genetic engineering to remove MTCH2 from human cells grown in the laboratory.

The change disrupted the normal structure of their mitochondrial networks. Individual mitochondria became more separated, and the balance between energy production and energy demand shifted.

The cells developed a persistent shortage of readily available energy relative to their needs, even as their mitochondria increased their oxygen-consuming activity.

To compensate, they began consuming more nutrients.

"After deleting Mitch, we examined, every few hours, the effect that had on more than 100 substances taking part in metabolism in human cells," Chourasia explains. "We saw an increase in cellular respiration, the process in which the cell produces energy from nutrients, such as carbohydrates and fats, using oxygen. This explains the increase in muscular endurance in previous experiments using mice."

Cellular respiration is the series of chemical reactions that allows cells to extract usable energy from nutrients. Increasing this activity generally requires a greater supply of fuel.

The researchers found that cells lacking Mitch consumed more fats, carbohydrates, and amino acids, the building blocks of proteins.

Fat metabolism was particularly revealing.

Rather than relying on their usual mix of energy sources, the altered cells increased their use of fatty acids for fuel. This indicated that MTCH2 influences not only how much energy cells consume, but also which nutrients they use to meet that demand.

"We discovered that deleting Mitch led to a major drop in fats in membranes," Gross explains. "At the same time, we saw an increase in fatty substances used to produce energy, and we realized that the fat was being broken down from the membrane to be used as fuel. In other words, we showed that Mitch determines the fate of fat in human cells."

Cell membranes are thin, flexible barriers made largely from fatty molecules called lipids. They protect cells and organize many of their essential functions.

The findings suggested that cells deprived of Mitch were changing how they distributed and used these lipids, drawing on some of their available fatty resources to support increased energy consumption.

Detailed analysis also revealed changes in stored lipids, demonstrating that the effect was more complex than simply eliminating cellular fat. Overall, the results showed that MTCH2 is a powerful regulator of how cells allocate nutrients between energy production and other biological needs.

The researchers then investigated whether Mitch also influences how fat cells develop.

This question was particularly interesting because previous studies had found elevated MTCH2 levels in fat tissue from women with obesity.

Fat cells, also known as adipocytes, do not appear fully formed. They develop from immature precursor cells through a process called adipogenesis, or fat cell differentiation.

As these precursor cells mature, they must build new cellular structures, produce fats, and activate genes that allow them to specialize in storing energy.

All of this requires considerable energy and raw materials.

Gross's team suspected that removing Mitch might disrupt the process by creating an environment in which cells were too busy consuming fuel to build the components needed for fat storage.

Experiments using mouse precursor fat cells supported this idea. When the researchers deleted MTCH2, far fewer cells successfully developed into mature fat cells.

In the published experiments, approximately 80% to 90% of normal precursor cells differentiated within six days, compared with only 5% to 10% of cells lacking MTCH2. The altered cells also accumulated substantially fewer lipid droplets, the structures used to store fat.

"When we deleted Mitch from the progenitor cells, we discovered that the environment created in these cells was not conducive to the synthesis of new fats," Gross explains. "Reducing the ability to synthesize membranes prevents the cells from growing, developing and reaching the point where differentiation is possible. The process of fat accumulation requires a large amount of available energy, but in cells without Mitch, there is a shortage of energy. In addition, the expression of genes necessary for differentiation is suppressed, and there is a shortage of the substances vital for this process to occur. As a result, differentiation of new fat cells is reduced, along with fat accumulation."

In other words, disabling Mitch appeared to affect fat metabolism in two distinct ways. It increased the consumption of energy-rich nutrients and interfered with the cellular machinery needed to develop new fat-storing cells.

Importantly, the fat cell differentiation experiments used a mouse-derived cell line, while the detailed analysis of nutrient consumption was conducted in human cells. The combined results suggest a broader role for the protein, but they do not establish that blocking MTCH2 would prevent new fat cells from forming in people.

Follow-Up Research Reveals Another Fat-Burning Mechanism

The importance of MTCH2 became even clearer in research published on October 3, 2025, in Nature Communications.

An international research team, which included Gross and Chourasia, investigated how MTCH2 influences metabolism in fat tissue. By analyzing human and mouse fat samples, studying cultured fat cells, and conducting experiments in genetically modified mice, the scientists identified another mechanism linking this protein to energy expenditure.

They found that MTCH2 helps regulate the movement of fatty acids into mitochondria, where these molecules can be broken down to produce energy.

The process involves an enzyme called carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, or CPT1. This enzyme acts as an important checkpoint, helping determine how much fat can enter mitochondria for use as fuel.

Normally, CPT1 activity is regulated by a molecule called malonyl-CoA, which signals when cells should limit fatty acid consumption.

The researchers discovered that MTCH2 physically interacts with CPT1 and influences how strongly this regulatory signal affects the enzyme.

Removing MTCH2 altered that control system, allowing mitochondria to increase fatty acid utilization and energy expenditure.

This provided a more detailed molecular explanation for why disabling the protein could encourage cells to burn additional fat.

Mice Burned More Energy Without Eating Less

The follow-up study also produced an especially promising result.

When researchers removed MTCH2 specifically from the fat cells of mice, the animals burned more energy without increasing their physical activity or changing how much food they consumed.

Under a regular diet, the genetically modified mice gained weight at a rate similar to that of normal mice.

However, when the animals were fed a high-fat diet, a difference emerged.

Mice lacking MTCH2 in their fat tissue gained less weight and accumulated less body fat than untreated mice, despite consuming similar amounts of food.

Their lean body mass remained comparable to that of the control animals.

The genetically modified mice also demonstrated improved glucose tolerance, indicating that their bodies were better able to manage blood sugar after receiving glucose. In addition, their cholesterol levels were lower.

These findings strengthened the evidence that MTCH2 influences whole-body metabolism, rather than simply changing the behavior of isolated cells.

The scientists also found that its metabolic effects could occur independently of UCP1, a protein commonly associated with heat production in brown fat. Brown fat is a specialized form of body fat that helps generate heat by burning energy, whereas white fat primarily stores excess energy.

Discovering an additional pathway for increasing energy expenditure could be important because it offers researchers another potential target for treating obesity and related metabolic disorders.

Could This Protein Lead to Better Weight-Loss Treatments?

Together, the findings suggest that MTCH2 may be an important molecular switch linking fat storage, energy consumption, and the development of fat cells.

The original experiments showed that removing the protein could increase nutrient consumption in human cells and interfere with fat cell formation in mouse cells. The later research demonstrated that selectively removing MTCH2 from fat tissue could increase energy expenditure and protect mice against excessive weight gain on a high-fat diet.

That combination raises the possibility of developing treatments that change how the body handles energy, potentially complementing medications that primarily work by reducing appetite.

However, several major questions remain unanswered.

Scientists have not yet demonstrated that inhibiting MTCH2 produces safe, sustained weight loss in humans. The protein also participates in other important biological activities, including mitochondrial regulation and pathways involved in cell survival. Blocking it throughout the body could therefore produce unwanted consequences.

Preventing fat cells from forming is not necessarily beneficial in every situation, either. Healthy fat tissue plays an essential role in storing excess energy, maintaining blood sugar balance, and producing hormones. Disrupting its function could potentially redirect fats into organs where they may cause harm.

Researchers will need to determine whether MTCH2 can be targeted safely, which tissues would benefit most, and whether reducing its activity would preserve muscle health while promoting fat loss.

For now, established strategies such as resistance exercise and adequate protein intake remain important for protecting muscle during weight reduction. A 2026 review of clinical trials found that combining lifestyle changes with resistance training produced a more favorable pattern of lean mass preservation than weight-loss interventions without such exercise.

The research nevertheless points toward a compelling possibility: Rather than focusing exclusively on how much food the body takes in, future obesity treatments might also influence how cells decide whether to burn energy or store it.

And one protein, Mitch, appears to be deeply involved in that decision.

The original study, titled "MTCH2 controls energy demand and expenditure to fuel anabolism during adipogenesis," was published in The EMBO Journal on January 3, 2025.

It involved an international collaboration, including researchers affiliated with ETH Zurich, Weizmann Institute of Science, and other institutions.

Teure Energie, hohe Inflation: Prognosen machen dennoch Hoffnung [premium]

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Die Energiepreise ziehen wieder an und befeuern die Teuerung. Wie es weitergehen könnte, analysiert Volkswirtin Ombretta Signori. Sie nennt Gründe, die sie zuversichtlich stimmen.

Die US-Zwischenwahlen am 3. November nahen mit großen Schritten. US-Präsident Donald Trump hatte gehofft, bis dahin die Energiepreise senken zu können, um bei den Wählern zu punkten. Doch die Friedensverhandlungen mit dem Iran sind vorerst gescheitert. Nun weitet sich der Nahostkonflikt auf Saudi-Arabien und den Jemen aus. Und auch der Dieselpreis steigt trotz des Lieferabkommens mit Russland weiter an.

Die Folgen für die Inflation sind längst sichtbar: Im September stieg die Teuerung in den USA um 3,7 Prozent, in der Eurozone betrug das Plus sogar 3,8 Prozent. Doch wird sich der Anstieg noch weiter beschleunigen?

Ombretta Signori, Ökonomin und Leiterin „Macroeconomic Research and Strategy“ bei Ofi Invest Asset Management, beobachtet die Entwicklungen genau. Sie verweist im „Presse“-Gespräch auf jüngste Prognosen aus ihrem Haus: Aus aktueller Sicht dürfte demnach die Inflationsrate für das Gesamtjahr 2026 in den USA bei 3,5 Prozent und in der Eurozone bei drei Prozent liegen. Für 2027 deuten die Prognosen auf einen weiteren Rückgang hin. So dürfte diesseits des Atlantiks die Teuerung für das Gesamtjahr gut 2,4 Prozent betragen.

Die Zuversicht beruht vor allem auf Marktprognosen zu den künftigen Ölpreisen. Entwicklungen an den internationalen Terminmärkten deuten auf einen allmählichen Preisrückgang im kommenden Jahr im Vergleich zur aktuellen Notierung hin – sofern sich die geopolitischen Spannungen nicht weiter verschärfen. Zuletzt notierte die Nordseemarke Brent bei rund 111 Dollar je Fass.

Signori begründet die aktuell vorsichtig positive Einschätzung an den Terminmärkten damit, dass der Ölhandel sehr anpassungsfähig ist, wie jüngste Entwicklungen zeigen. So finden etwa Transportfirmen neue Routen, um die Straße von Hormus möglichst zu umschiffen. Je mehr sich die Marktteilnehmer jedoch anpassen und nötigenfalls auch alternative Wege befahren, desto mehr können hohe Transportkosten, zu denen neben den Frachtraten z. B. auch Versicherungsbeiträge zählen, wieder schrittweise sinken. „Zudem haben die USA ihre Ölexporte erhöht.“

Für Schlagzeilen sorgt aber auch der starke Preisanstieg auf Europas Gasmarkt. Dies dürfte die Teuerung stärker anfachen, zumal die Speicher teils immer noch geringer gefüllt sind als in den vergangenen Jahren zur selben Jahreszeit.

Die Expertin will das jedoch nicht überbewerten: Etwa in Italien seien die Speicher bereits gut gefüllt. „Sorgenkinder sind die Niederlande und Deutschland“, räumt sie ein – dort hatten Unternehmen auf günstigere Preise gehofft und müssen nun zu höheren Notierungen einkaufen.

Auf längere Sicht gebe es aber auch dieser Region Pläne, die das Problem entschärfen sollen. So stellte das deutsche Wirtschaftsministerium Mitte Oktober einen Gesetzentwurf zum Aufbau einer nationalen Gasreserve vor, die über eine Umlage für Gaskunden finanziert werden soll.

Neue Entwicklungen gab es zuletzt auch bei Europas Handelspolitik. Vergangene Woche einigte sich die EU mit China auf eine Importbeschränkung von jährlich 350.000 Hybridautos nach Europa. Die Einfuhren werden damit mehr als halbiert.

Ob das vor allem deutschen Automobilherstellern eine nachhaltige Entlastung bieten wird, bleibt abzuwarten. Denn solche Maßnahmen werden Europas Wettbewerbsnachteile kaum beheben können, und auch der Handelskonflikt mit China könnte erneut eskalieren. Signori kann dem Abkommen dennoch Positives abgewinnen. „Europa ist entschlossen und proaktiv nach außen hin aufgetreten, um eigene Interessen zu vertreten, anstatt den wachsenden Druck durch Billigkonkurrenz noch weiter hinzunehmen.“

Dass es dabei nicht nur eine Lösung gibt, das wisse Europa. Beispielsweise gebe es ernsthafte Bestrebungen für eine Reindustrialisierung. Signori verweist dazu auf den am 4. März 2026 von der Europäischen Kommission vorgelegten Verordnungsvorschlag für den „Industrial Accelerator Act“.

Dieses Gesetz, das 2028 in Kraft treten könnte, enthält unter anderem Maßnahmen zur Stärkung der lokalen Wertschöpfung. Beispielsweise sollen bei öffentlichen Ausschreibungen und Förderprogrammen die europäische Produktion und CO₂-arme Produkte bevorzugt werden.

Das Ziel dabei ist klar: Strategische Sektoren in Europa, wie die Stahl- und Automobilproduktion, sowie umweltfreundliche Technologien sollen gefördert werden.

Zuvor war die Ökonomin unter anderem bei den Vermögensverwaltern Aviva Investors, AXA Investment Managers und Amundi tätig. Signori hat einen Doktortitel der Universität Paris Ouest und einen Master-Abschluss in Ökonomie von der Universität Bocconi in Mailand.

Erfolg als Falle: „Wer an Innovation spart, steht langfristig ohne Hose da“ [premium]

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Gute Ideen sind keine reine Glückssache. Unternehmen können gezielt die Voraussetzungen schaffen, um innovativer zu werden, sagt WU-Professor Nikolaus Franke. Was es dafür braucht, warum Fehler dazugehören und weshalb gerade der Erfolg zum Hindernis werden kann.

Ist Österreich ein innovatives Land oder einfach nur eines, das sehr viel Geld für Forschung ausgibt?

Nikolaus Franke: Österreich hat eine ausgezeichnete wissenschaftliche und technische Basis und investiert sehr viel in Forschung und Entwicklung. Kurz gesagt: Wir sind stark beim Input, aber schwächer beim Output. Zu wenige Erkenntnisse und Erfindungen werden zu erfolgreichen Innovationen am Markt. Ein Grund dafür ist unsere defensive Haltung: Wir diskutieren häufig zuerst über Risiken und erst danach über Chancen.

Der Anteil österreichischer Firmen, die neue Produkte entwickeln, ist zuletzt gesunken. Ein Grund zur Sorge?

Das ist ein Warnsignal. Früher hatte man im globalen Wettbewerb die Wahl zwischen einem strategischen Schwerpunkt auf niedrige Kosten oder hohe Qualität. Heute ist die Innovationsführerschaft der Wettbewerbsfaktor Nummer eins. Andere Länder haben das erkannt. Wenn wir uns nicht weiterentwickeln, werden wir überholt.

Welche Standortmaßnahmen würden der Innovationskraft Österreichs helfen?

Einzelne Maßnahmen reichen nicht, man muss das systematisch angehen und das Innovationsökosystem nachhaltig stärken. Dazu gehören klassische Standortfaktoren wie Steuern und Bürokratie. Wir müssen in den Köpfen der Menschen verankern, dass man Innovationen hervorbringen und auch Unternehmen gründen kann. Und wir müssen Forschungseinrichtungen stärken. Gerade die Debatte über die Kürzung der Universitätsbudgets ist fatal für den Innovationsstandort. Hier zu sparen ist so wie wenn ein Bauer nach einer schlechten Ernte sagt, dass er sich die Aussaat nicht mehr leisten kann.

Österreichs Unternehmen klagen über hohe Standortkosten. Wer auf der Kostenseite nicht wettbewerbsfähig ist, wird sich vermutlich auch mit Innovation nicht leicht tun.

Das stimmt. Aber zwischen den Unternehmenszielen von niedrigen Kosten, hoher Qualität und Innovation gibt es Zielkonflikte. Sie sind unvermeidlich: Wer Kostenführer sein will, muss sparen. Und wer an Innovation spart, der verbessert zwar kurzfristig das Ergebnis, steht aber im Innovationswettbewerb langfristig ohne Hose da. Das Gleiche gilt für Qualitätsführerschaft. Wer unbarmherzig jeden möglichen Fehler in Prozessen und Ergebnissen bestraft, treibt dem System die Innovationsfähigkeit aus. Innovationen sind offene Experimente. Irrtümer, Rückschläge und Probleme sind unvermeidbar. Dies bedeutet: Innovation gibt es nicht zum Nulltarif. Es muss eine grundsätzliche Zielsetzung des Unternehmens sein.

Sie haben mit Rudolf Dömötör das Buch „Innovation ist kein Zufall“ geschrieben und dafür Tausende Betriebe analysiert. Wie entsteht Innovation?

Innovative Unternehmen behandeln Innovation nicht als Aufgabe einer einzelnen Abteilung, sondern als Fähigkeit der gesamten Organisation. Vier Elemente greifen dabei ineinander. Ein Topmanagement, das Innovation zur eigenen Aufgabe macht, Ressourcen bereitstellt und auch unbequeme Ideen schützt. Klare Verantwortlichkeiten und schnelle Prozesse, die trotzdem Raum zum Experimentieren lassen. Eine Kultur, in der Mitarbeiter Probleme ansprechen, Ideen einbringen und aus Fehlschlägen lernen können. Und Offenheit nach außen – gegenüber Kunden, Nutzern, Partnern, Universitäten oder Start-ups. Entscheidend ist das Zusammenspiel.

Das klingt nicht wie etwas, das sich einfach so über Nacht umsetzen lässt.

Man kann morgen damit beginnen, aber man ist nicht morgen fertig. Es reicht eben nicht, das Forschungsbudget zu verdoppeln oder eine Innovationsabteilung einzurichten. Am Anfang steht eine klare Entscheidung des Topmanagements: Welche Bedeutung soll Innovation haben, welche Ressourcen stellen wir bereit, und welche unbequemen Projekte sind wir bereit zu schützen? Wie groß der Unterschied ist, zeigen unsere Daten: Bei Innovationsführern beschäftigt sich das Topmanagement im Durchschnitt rund ein Drittel seiner Arbeitszeit mit Innovation – etwa doppelt so viel wie in anderen Betrieben. Danach müssen Strukturen, Anreize und Verhaltensweisen schrittweise angepasst werden. Oft sind das große Änderungen. Diese Transformation erfordert Zeit, Konsequenz und die Bereitschaft, erhebliche Widerstände auszuhalten.

Innovation bedeutet eine schöpferische Zerstörung. Zwangsläufig werden Kompetenzen, gewachsene Strukturen und Wissen entwertet. Und wer ist schon gern Verlierer? Und je größer und erfolgreicher eine Organisation ist, desto stärker sind diese Beharrungskräfte. Und desto schwerer tut sie sich, wirklich Neues zu ermöglichen.

Wie lassen sich diese Widerstände überwinden?

Widerstand muss man ernst nehmen, aber man darf vor ihm nicht kapitulieren. Sachliche Einwände können helfen, eine Idee zu verbessern oder Fehlentscheidungen zu vermeiden. Wo es dagegen vor allem um Besitzstände, Zuständigkeiten oder persönliche Interessen geht, muss das Topmanagement notfalls Widerstände überwinden. Ein gutes Beispiel aus unserem Buch ist Mawa, ein Hersteller von Kleiderbügeln – also wirklich keine Branche, die man spontan mit Innovation verbindet. Kleiderbügel galten als austauschbare Commodity, bei der fast nur der Preis zählt. Das Unternehmen geriet durch den Preiswettbewerb mit chinesischen Anbietern in die Insolvenz. 2007 übernahm Michaela Schenk das Unternehmen und richtete Produktion, Produkte, Vertriebswege und Markenauftritt auf Innovation aus. Mawa sollte bei Kleiderbügeln werden, was Tempo bei Taschentüchern ist – eine Marke, die für eine ganze Produktkategorie steht. Die Krise schuf den nötigen Handlungsdruck. Entscheidend war, dass Schenk selbst als glaubwürdiges Vorbild voranging. So ist über Jahre eine Innovationskultur entstanden. Heute erzielt Mawa fast die Hälfte seines Umsatzes und Gewinns mit Innovationen der vergangenen drei Jahre und die Mitarbeiterzahl hat sich mehr als verdoppelt.

»Ich kenne ein Start-up mit einem hervorragenden Produkt, das pleiteging, weil niemand daran gedacht hatte, Rechnungen zu stellen. «

Und wenn das Unternehmen nicht gerade vor dem Konkurs steht?

Das ist die eigentliche Führungsleistung: Dringlichkeit zu erzeugen, bevor eine Krise sie erzwingt. Solange die Dinge gut laufen, ist es wie bei dem Mann, der von einem hundertstöckigen Hochhaus fällt und bei jedem Stockwerk sagt: Na ja, bis jetzt ist es gut gegangen. Aber man kann Handlungsbedarf frühzeitig sichtbar machen. Wir haben im Buch systematische Checklisten, mit denen Unternehmen ihre Innovationsfähigkeit nüchtern prüfen und Schwachstellen erkennen können. Die beste Zeit, Innovationsfähigkeit aufzubauen, ist dann, wenn der Betrieb noch erfolgreich ist und über Zeit, Geld und Handlungsspielraum verfügt.

Bei Start-ups geht es anfangs oft fast nur um Innovation und schnelles Wachstum. Wie können sie diesen Innovationsgeist erhalten, wenn sie einmal größer werden?

Die Herausforderung ist paradox: Start-ups müssen sich professionalisieren, ohne zu verbürokratisieren. Am Anfang sind sie wie ein Jetski, der sich auf der Stelle drehen kann. Wenn das Produkt oder Geschäftsmodell erfolgreich ist und das Unternehmen wächst, braucht es Strukturen – sonst entsteht Chaos. Daraus entstehen aber schnell Bürokratie und Erstarrung. Organisationen haben eine natürliche Gravitation zum Stillstand. Die Balance zwischen Kreativität und Stabilität muss man sehr aktiv managen.

From zeitgeist to zeitghost: How Netflix kept a hold on viewers—but lost its spot in pop culture

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Netflix has been pumping out original content for so long and at such an immense scale that it recently released its second original movie entitled War Machine.

Although the Brad Pitt-starring first iteration sank like a stone in 2017, the unrelated new War Machine, led by Reacher’s Alan Ritchson, was reportedly a ratings smash, hitting 10th on Netflix’s all-time most-watched movies list, with 140 million views. So far, it’s the platform’s biggest movie this year.

If the dearth of online chatter around it is any indication, though, it’s gotten all too easy to have zero interest in or opinions about Netflix’s biggest projects.

Presumably, very few people got yelled at on social media for sharing War Machine spoilers, or felt slighted by a significant other for watching it without them. By all accounts, it’s a pretty good movie, but far from what could be considered an “event,” despite a sheen of event-ness being a reasonable expectation for any studio’s biggest film of the year.

Instead, it feels more like a movie designed with the goal of capturing a Super Bowl number of eyeballs. That’s the main problem with 2020s Netflix: It gets eyeballs watching far more than it gets tongues wagging. 

If a Super Bowl-size event takes place and nobody talks about it, does it even matter?

To be clear, Netflix is still an apex predator in the streaming wars (although it may be dwarfed by the world’s true streaming leader, YouTube). At the end of last year, Netflix topped 325 million subscribers, and in the first half of 2026, it logged 97.7 billion hours watched, landing 11 titles on Nielsen’s top 20 original series.

But just because a lot of people still watch Netflix doesn’t mean they like what they’re seeing.

While revenue was up 12% during its third quarter, engagement is estimated to be down. According to a Jefferies analysis cited by Reuters, average monthly viewing time per member declined from 66 hours in the first half of 2023 to 49 hours in the first half of 2026.

It’s as clear an indication as any that Netflix has drifted away from the center of pop culture.

Between an overabundance of streaming options—and the fact that they’re all competing against the likes of TikTok and other attention-seekers—it’s tempting to posit there’s no longer any such thing as a zeitgeist hit. But only sometimes.

Eventually, something like HBO’s heart-palpitating The Pitt or Apple TV’s horror-comedy Widow’s Bay emerges as a word-of-mouth sensation that sweeps the Emmys, proving it’s still entirely possible to make movies and shows that put pop culture in a choke hold. Netflix is just no longer as efficient as it once was at developing them.

While the zeitgeist remains intact, Netflix is now in danger of becoming a zeitghost.

Quantity over quality

One of the major issues affecting the platform is too much signal and not enough noise. 

Just last year, Netflix released the anime film K-Pop Demon Hunters, a bona fide phenomenon, along with the timely chiller Adolescence, a must-see limited series, but those were only 2 of the 597 original movies and series that Netflix reportedly released in 2025—and it’s hard to think of something comparatively popular that the streamer has put out since.

The quirky melodrama Remarkably Bright Creatures won some Emmys, and a just-released, Florence Pugh-led adaptation of East of Eden made a splash.

But they’re sharing crowded Netflix menu space with Sacha Baron Cohen’s dud romcom Ladies First, two adaptations of Harlan Coben novels, and star vehicles for each of Adam Sandler’s daughters, all of which will be joined later this year by reality game show Squid Game: The VIP Challenge, featuring Zac Efron’s younger brother, Dylan.

It’s as if Netflix has taken a little too literally the idea of making something for everybody.

It wasn’t always this way. When Netflix first emerged as a player and innovator in original content in 2013, it came out swinging.

Not only were House of Cards and Orange Is the New Black both powerhouses—the kinds of shows people ask whether you’ve seen yet—but they also comprised two of only three new series Netflix released that year. (Eli Roth’s long-forgotten Hemlock Grove was the third.)

It may not be fair to expect Netflix to retain the same 66% hit rate when it puts out 597 new projects in a year; however, Netflix was under no legal obligation to churn out that many new projects.

At what was arguably its cultural peak in 2019, Netflix released a relatively slight 371 new originals, among them The Witcher, Sex Education, and The Umbrella Academy, not to mention Ava DuVernay’s When They See Us, Martin Scorsese’s The Irishman, and Noah Baumbach’s Marriage Story.

Back then, that level of quantity was a dramatic escalation.

While Netflix has slowed down on original output in the years since its peak, its closest competitors dabble in the dozens instead of hundreds. Their market share may be nowhere near Netflix’s, but their hit-to-miss ratios are comparatively off the charts.

Breadth over depth

Following reports of a subscriber drop in April 2022, Netflix posted its largest one-day plunge in stocks in the company’s entire history, shedding $54 billion in market cap.

To right the ship, Netflix cracked down on password-sharing, introduced ad-supported tiers, and stopped pushing the limits of how much original content one streamer can possibly produce in a calendar year.  

As it began stabilizing subscriber growth and accelerating profits, though, Netflix also embarked on a different form of expansion—instead of more original content, more kinds of content.

Starting in 2023, Netflix branched out into live events with Chris Rock’s special Selective Outrage.

Later that year, it got into the live sports business, with The Netflix Cup, followed by a series of similar events and, eventually, a $5 billion deal to become the home of WWE Raw through 2035.

Finally, earlier this year the streamer began airing a wide range of well-established video podcasts, or “talk shows” as they were once known.

With all this expansion, Netflix increased the breadth of reasons to subscribe—while weakening its earlier promise as a hub for buzzy movies and TV shows.

It feels almost like a slap in the face to scroll through a vast menu of seemingly interchangeable new Netflix movies and shows, only for those options to be surrounded by a blizzard of podcasts, sports events, and The Roast of Theo Von. (That last one is not a real thing. Yet.) 

Throwing a bunch of stuff at the wall to see what sticks mostly just leaves the thrower with a ridiculously messy wall, and that’s what Netflix looks like now. Instead of reinventing the maximalism of linear cable, perhaps the streamer should try to reinvent the hit-rate of 2013 Netflix.

It certainly seems to have worked for Apple TV, whose tightly curated slate keeps producing prestigious smashes like Pluribus—and exponentially less filler.

Not only did Apple outperform Netflix at the last two Emmys, Apple led all network and streaming services for the first time this year. It scored 29 total wins, including 14 for Widow’s Bay, breaking the record for most-winning freshman comedy, which Apple previously broke in 2025 with 13 wins for The Studio.

Whether Apple’s success is a direct result of its less-is-more strategy or not, it reflects poorly on Netflix-style excess.

Burn and churn

It’s unfortunate enough that Netflix now encompasses everything, but it also feels permanently on the edge of becoming anything.

Of all the explanations for the recent phenomenon of viewers not returning for a second seasons of hit Netflix shows, one of the more plausible ones is that Netflix’s itchy trigger finger for cancellations has trained viewers to not get too attached to new series.

Barring vanishingly rare exceptions like the juggernaut of Bridgerton, most shows on Netflix feel ephemeral and thus skippable. That transient nature is starting to bleed over to the talent behind its programming, along with Netflix’s staff and its C-suite.

Over the last few years, Netflix has reportedly lost out on renewals for its deals with Dahmer creator Ryan Murphy, Stranger Things’s Duffer Brothers, and, most recently, director David Fincher—who made three movies with Netflix over the past six years.

It must also sting that after The Haunting of Hill House creator Mike Flanagan left in 2022, his first post-Netflix series, the just-released and much-discussed Carrie, already appears to be a sizable hit for Prime Video.

As for in-house turnover, Netflix laid off 1,000 employees last week, its largest workforce reduction yet, and this week the company fired film chief Dan Lin, director of original series Carolina Garcia, and content chief Bela Bajaria.

Perhaps these exits could be read as a sign that Netflix is aware of having missed the mark with its movies and TV shows recently. On the surface, though, it just scans as instability, suggesting these executives’ replacements have an impossible job ahead.

It’s anyone’s guess what the next iteration of Netflix will look like, but the 2026 version is overstuffed, strategically unmoored, and completely illegible from a branding perspective.

What has long been considered the most essential streamer is starting to feel increasingly optional—a membership to dip in and out of when one of its hundreds of annual original projects merits it.

If Netflix wants to be more than just a profitable quagmire again, it will have to go back to winning viewers’ hearts instead of capturing their eyeballs.

Wales is one of the main development hotspots outside London. It already has the largest datacentre capacity beyond the capital, with new hyperscale facilities planned for former industrial and urban sites. The Keir Starmer-led Labour government viewed this as an inevitable revolution, which the country must embrace or be left behind. It established five AI growth zones to push more datacentres through the planning system and speed up connections to the national grid. The new prime minister, Andy Burnham, has signalled little will change, turning down calls for a moratorium and insisting datacentres bring economic benefits. But anti-datacentre groups springing up in villages, towns and cities across the country see it rather differently: as the imposition of a resource-hungry, polluting industry that increases profits for often US big tech, with little tangible benefit to local people or the country as a whole.

Digital storage is nothing new. The first home PC users saved files on floppy discs and hard drives; businesses used their own server rooms in the 80s and 90s, then moved to off-site facilities as their needs grew. But the development of cloud computing services in the 2010s, such as Google Drive, and AI chatbots in the 2020s, like ChatGPT, led to even greater information and processing demands, which the tech industry insists can only be met by larger and larger datacentres. This has driven huge speculative bets on hyperscale hubs, which investors and tech firms hope will one day reap the rewards of an AI-led transformation of the economy. Globally, it is estimated spending on new facilities could reach $7tn by 2030 – more than double the cost of the New Deal, the Marshall Plan to rebuild Europe after the second world war and the Apollo space programme combined, even allowing for inflation.

The US is at the forefront of the boom, already hosting roughly half of the world’s hyperscale capacity. But the industry is facing a furious backlash, with three-quarters of Americans now opposed to datacentre development and blaming it for increasing energy bills, threatening water supplies in drought-hit areas and spewing out toxic fumes. More than 500 counties and municipalities have passed permanent or temporary datacentre bans.

Loudoun County in northern Virginia sits in what is known as Data Center Alley because it is home to more facilities than anywhere else in the world. Greg Pirio’s life was turned upside down when a gas-powered datacentre opened 150 metres from his home last year. “This neighbourhood was calm and peaceful, and it’s turned into this nightmare,” he says on a video call. “Our quality of life has been degraded so much.”

The noise from the centre’s eight gas turbines is “like being at an airport with all these engines roaring,” Pirio says. “It has given many of my neighbours headaches. It has kept children up at night.” Pirio, who used to work for the Voice of America radio station, has also developed health problems since it opened, including dry eyes and chest tightness. Once he had to leave his house because he was struggling to breathe: “I know whenever the diesel generators are on, because I feel it in my lungs.”

The UK has not yet seen the same level of opposition as the US. But anti-datacentre groups are gathering momentum. Residents in east London are waging a high-profile campaign against a new datacentre on Brick Lane, including protesting outside the offices of Angela Rayner, the communities secretary, who overturned a council decision blocking the scheme.

Seasoned campaigner Owen Espley, who works for environmental charity Global Action Plan, is helping scores of residents’ groups organise against planned datacentres. “This summer has been wild. Every other week a new group has popped up. Petitions are getting thousands of signatures in days,” he says. “Communities are in uproar, and most politicians are playing catch-up.”

Espley has been taken aback by the gulf between the government’s conviction that there is no alternative and ordinary people’s deepening understanding of the complex issues at stake. “There has been a real grassroots questioning of datacentres that connects British Bangladeshis in Brick Lane with Hertfordshire villagers and communities in Northumberland, who want good jobs to come back,” he says. “It is astounding.”

In fact, there are many alternative pathways to market-­led expansion – from tougher regulation to prioritising publicly-owned data infrastructure – which serve communal goals, such as the heart disease and dementia research powered by Bristol University’s new Isambard-AI facility. “First, we need strong standards for private datacentres,” Espley says. “Second, we need industrial strategy to deliver our own public computer, rather than an assumption that US big tech building and owning everything is the only way forward.”

On the deprived eastern edges of Cardiff, the early morning sunshine is warming a car park dotted with scraggly weeds. The perimeter fence is overgrown with brambles, with patches of singed, blackened grass from a recent wildfire visible just beyond. It looks abandoned but soon cars carrying doctors, nurses and other NHS staff start arriving. They are here to catch free buses to Cardiff’s Heath hospital, the largest in Wales.

Hundreds are said to use the park and ride every day, but it is under threat because Labour-controlled Cardiff council wants to sell the land to a property developer, Curtis Hall, to build a hyperscale data facility. The centre will use up to 150 megawatts (MW) of electricity a year, more than triple the demand of a city the size of Cardiff.

The development is one of the most controversial in Wales. Council meetings have been disrupted and more than 1,200 planning objections lodged. Last month, the new Plaid Cymru-led government ordered the council to put the application on hold while it considered whether to take the decision out of councillors’ hands.

Campaigners are determined to keep up the pressure on the Welsh government. Sue Elias, a retired librarian with seemingly endless reserves of energy, dashes back and forth reattaching her “Ditch the datacentre” banner to the car park fence and taping leaflets to the bus stop glass. “What is going to happen to all the people who use the park and ride? It’s bonkers,” she says.

Elias has leafleted the park and ride every Wednesday morning for six weeks. Today she is collecting signatures for an open letter to prove it is well used – so far she has added the names of 1,300 staff and patients. “I’ve not done anything like this before but I’m told I’m a natural,” she says with a mischievous smile, before breaking off to speak to staff arriving for the next bus.

She belongs to community-based union Acorn, which has campaigned against the scheme, and has taken part in protests, including bursting into a council planning meeting in July. “We’d sent emails, done petitions, but we’d had nothing back – tumbleweed. They’re our elected representatives … They should be talking with us.”

It is hard to find anyone in support of the plan in the queue for the bus, which runs three times an hour. “I use it every day. Without it, I probably wouldn’t continue working at the hospital,” says Ami George, a midwife. “Parking there causes that much stress.”

Across Cardiff’s Rhymney River, which curves around the proposed datacentre development, the area’s newest residents have only recently moved into 100 homes built under the city council’s flagship house-building programme. Nick Richards, a freelance filmmaker and skateboarder, and his wife arrived in November. “It felt like the stars had aligned because there is a brand new skate park up the road … the best in Cardiff. It’s amazing,” he says. “I never pictured myself living in suburbia but when we came we saw kids playing outside and thought, this is a really nice area.”

Like many other residents on the estate, Richards had no idea the council was planning to sell the park and ride to a datacentre developer. They moved in two months before the planning application was submitted. Instead of enjoying their new life, they now fear they will never be able to sell. Their home is 300 metres from what the plans describe as the temporary energy centre, which includes seven giant gas turbines. They are expected to power the facility around the clock for five years, according to documents filed by the developer. “I feel trapped,” Richards says, pacing his kitchen. “I couldn’t look prospective buyers in the eye and not say there’s going to be seven gas turbines right next to them.”

Richards has pored over the planning documents and leafleted every house on the new estate, including a care home, which will be just 150 metres from the turbines. “This datacentre will be powered by gas turbines,” he says. “They will pump nitrogen dioxide into our atmosphere. There will also be fine particulate matter and formaldehyde. All these toxins cause health conditions, such as asthma, COPD and heart disease.”

Air quality assessments commissioned by the developer estimate the emissions will be within safe levels. Curtis Hall says the scheme cannot proceed without 100% compliance with all environmental and public health regulatory requirements.

There are more than 100 other planned UK datacentres aiming to use gas turbines due to the long wait to be connected to the grid. Richards doesn’t want his estate to be a test case. “Within a mile of this datacentre are 30,000 people. But the people building this centre don’t live here ... A stone’s throw away is Llanrumney Hall, once the home of privateer and slave trader Sir Henry Morgan, who gave his name to Captain Morgan rum. It is now a thriving community centre, with a cafe, recording studio and creche. Labour councillor Lee Bridgeman is vaping on an outdoor table, dressed in a hoodie and baseball cap. He ran the local youth centre until it was lost to austerity-era cuts, and though he’s a high-ranking councillor, jointly responsible for housing and communities, he does regular youth work shifts in the area, one of the poorest in Cardiff.

He is one of the few public figures in the area who has spoken out in support of the development, sometimes clashing with anti-datacentre campaigners on local Facebook groups. “Some of the information being spread is scaremongering,” he says. “They are telling residents their electricity bills will go up … the river and reservoir will be drained … It’s false information.”

Many of these fears stem from the US, where the rapid expansion of hyperscale facilities has been linked to rising electricity bills, water shortages and toxic air pollution. Bridgeman argues that “planning processes are totally different in the UK. So whereas [US authorities] turn a blind eye to some of the environmental impacts of those monstrosities, that would never happen in the UK.” He “wouldn’t be comfortable” if the gas turbines were running for five years but thinks they might not be needed if the datacentre can connect to the grid sooner.

The backlash has caught him by surprise, but he blames outside activists. “I didn’t see it coming. I didn’t think there would be this much interference,” he says.

Emotions are running high in the city. Some residents say they have been blocked by councillors when trying to question them on Facebook. Planning committee members have received threatening emails, Bridgeman says. “Threats like ‘I will come after you ... we will make your life hell.’ That shouldn’t happen.”

Although the datacentre will employ only up to 125 people when it is built, Bridgeman believes it could encourage other big companies to east Cardiff: “I’m told the knock-on effect could lead to thousands of jobs.”

Later that day, residents squeeze into the local boxing club for a public meeting about the datacentre plan. It is standing room only, with people lining the walls. Bridgeman repeats his claim that few locals have complained: “I have hundreds of emails from residents ... So far I’ve had two emails with regards to the datacentre.”

Some in the audience react angrily. There are shouts of, “We don’t know you, that’s why” and, “Have you heard of social media?” One woman stands up and says, “Don’t tell me you haven’t been privy to the meetings … or things online.” In a show of hands, roughly three-quarters of the 80 to 100 people in the room indicate they are from Llanrumney, almost all opposed. There are cries of, “There you are … you don’t need emails!” and, “That’s every person!” Almost three weeks later, Bridgeman performs a sudden about-turn, submitting a formal letter of objection to the scheme, noting that he has heard from a large number of residents.

The UK hyperscale buildout has been focused on London, but datacentre developers are increasingly targeting areas outside the south-east, to escape severe electrical grid capacity limits. Wales already has the largest datacentre capacity of anywhere outside the capital. Many more could be coming: five of Wales’s largest new and proposed datacentres will consume more electricity than all Welsh homes put together. While the previous Welsh Labour administration welcomed the building spree, claiming giant tech firms’ investment would generate thousands of jobs, the Plaid Cymru-led government has been more circumspect, with ministers emphasising it must benefit Wales.

In the light and airy Welsh parliament building, which overlooks Cardiff Bay, opposition is growing. Plaid Cymru’s Nick Carter, who is the Senedd member for Caerdydd Ffynnon Taf, which includes Cardiff East, worries that the surging market will benefit developers and US tech firms rather than the public. “As a party, we’re not going to want to see a proliferation of datacentres, haphazardly being built across the country, on what may be an opportunistic basis,” he says.

Carter, who has a background in commercial property, has examined the Cardiff plans in detail. He says the developer, Curtis Hall, has no track record in datacentres; “They could be a front for someone else: Google, Microsoft, whoever. I can understand why these operators need datacentres, but I’m not sure members of the public share the need.” In response, Curtis Hall says it never claimed it would operate the facility itself, adding it had been working on datacentre projects for five years.

Carter is glad the Welsh government has intervened in Cardiff. But he doesn’t believe Wales needs to follow Scotland by imposing a moratorium on new datacentres: “The Welsh government is developing a strategy for datacentres nationally. We’ll see what comes out of that.”

However, it is not clear how the Welsh government can meet its goal to reach net zero by 2040 without limiting datacentre expansion. Carter admits, “It’s massively problematic.” In Westminster, similar questions have been asked. The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology tells me the government will enable the development of datacentres by generating unprecedented amounts of clean power.

A soft autumnal dusk descends on the residents living beside the new datacentre in Newport. Its sleek industrial-grey walls cast shadows across the closest gardens and homes. Much of the anger and frustration that met the original proposals has given way to weariness and resignation. Some residents talk of moving away, while others are mollified by meetings with the US-based data campus operator, Vantage.

Yet their troubles may only just be beginning – right now, only one of the three Vantage datacentres is operational. Microsoft will be opening its own nearby soon. When all are running, there could be upwards of 500 air handling units working at the same time, with back-up diesel generators needing regular testing, so they can immediately fire up if there is a power cut.

Newport council’s chief scientific officer warned during the planning process that the fumes from all the generators had the “potential to cause unacceptable air pollution levels under use, and testing needs to be understood and mitigation proposed, if needed”.

The council tells me it doesn’t have a duty to monitor emissions from generators, but says its officers check air quality management plans, and ask managers to minimise the impact of generator tests. Vantage says all its facilities operate in accordance with noise and air quality controls. It says it will monitor impacts and investigate concerns when the new centre opens.

Some residents have already given up on democratic process. Johnston fought the proposals through the planning process but felt he was ignored. “We’ve been let down by all of them. I’ve had electoral roll cards come through my door. But I’m voting for none of them … I can’t trust them. They don’t care about any of us.”

Over the road are older homes, where staff working on a nearby wealthy estate once lived. Sonia Thomas, a retired teacher, has lived in the same housekeeper’s cottage since 1969. She can remember when orchards and fields fanned out in front of their gardens but now she doesn’t even know if the sun is shining. “From inside you see grey, not blue,” she says, glancing at the datacentre filling the sky. “It’s horrible.”

In A Fine Balance, one of the finest novels about modern India set in the 1960s and 1970s, Rohinton Mistry powerfully conveys a sense of the social meaning of the vote for the Indians at the bottom of the ladder. Narayan, a Dalit tailor, insists on marking his own ballot in an upcoming election instead of allowing an upper-caste landlord’s men to vote on his behalf.

Dukhi, his father, warns him against it.  “It is still my right,” Narayan replies. “And I will exercise it in the next election, I promise you.”

“Life without dignity is worthless,” Narayan replies.

I have been rereading Mistry’s novel over the past few weeks as a series of protests has turned October into a month of fierce battles over the integrity of India’s elections, the accountability of its institutions, the right to protest, and the soul of its democracy.

A nascent Gen-Z movement, Cockroach Janta Party, which organized protests over education and unemployment in Delhi in June and July and forced the resignation of India’s higher education minister, gave the call for the October protests. Young protestors answered with an insurrectionary energy in the summer and soon found support from a broader coalition comprised of opposition parties, social movements and other pillars of Indian civil society.

These assorted protesters see the stakes of the current moment as existential: the sanctity of the vote in India, the essential ingredient, in other words, of democracy.  Throughout October thousands joined the cause in Delhi and other Indian cities, their marches spurred by anger directed against an ongoing national exercise to overhaul the country’s electoral register.

Known as the Special Intensive Revision, the exercise has, according to the protestors, resulted in more than 130 million voters being removed from electoral lists. Although electoral rolls have been revised periodically, this is the first time such an exercise has placed the burden on citizens to establish their eligibility, raising fears that millions of legitimate voters, particularly the poor and marginalized, could be disenfranchised.

Seeking accountability, they have been calling for the resignation of Gyanesh Kumar, the country’s chief election commissioner, who oversees the Election Commission of India, the body charged with conducting and safeguarding the sanctity of elections across the nation. The protestors accuse Kumar of abandoning his constitutional obligation to act impartially. In their telling, the mandarin has set the table in a way to favor the interests of Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s Bharatiya Janata Party, a charge denied by those in power.

Already shaken by the summer protests over education, this time the Indian government responded with coercive force, effectively turning central Delhi, the political and administrative heart of the capital, and the place where India goes to protest, into a garrison. On Oct. 10, it deployed tens of thousands of police and paramilitary personnel, restricted internet access, detained thousands of protesters, and sought to deny the protesters access to public spaces across the capital.

The crackdown was intense. On the night of Oct. 16, there was a palpable sense of panic on social media in India as an urgent question was asked repeatedly: Where is Neha Bora? A 29-year-old doctoral student and left-wing activist, Bora has emerged as one of the most articulate and formidable leaders of these protests, which have rattled Modi’s government and—despite valiant attempts to construct a counter narrative by BJP leaders and other party apparatchiks—become a significant challenge to his popularity and legitimacy.

Bora was detained by the police on their way to join the thousands of protesters who had converged on Connaught Place, a prominent commercial district in the capital, waving the national flag and reiterating demands to protect the right to vote and the sanctity of electoral democracy in India. For several hours after sunset, there was no news of the whereabouts of Bora, who uses they/them pronouns. Their lawyers spoke about the police refusing to reveal their location. Eventually, around midnight, their lawyers located them at a police station.

When Bora was released on Saturday morning, they tweeted, “Fighting for democracy and the Constitution cannot be criminalized.” It was a neat summation of the sentiment moving so many Indians to take to the streets, namely a sense of being robbed of something of existential proportions. “Now if they take away our right to vote,” a protester told a reporter, “what is left with us?”

Members of the Cockroach Janta Party take part in a protest march on October 16, 2026 in New Delhi, India. Ritesh Shukla—Getty Images

The emotional charge behind the protests has its roots in the birth of the modern Indian nation-state itself. India always took great pride in adopting universal franchise rights as the Constitution of India came into effect on Jan. 26, 1950. In its historic first general elections in 1952, every Indian above the age of 21, irrespective of caste, religion, gender, race or wealth, had their constitutionally guaranteed right to vote. It was, as many legal and constitutional scholars have since remarked, a colossal feat, and one that became a touchstone for the modern idea of India.

Hence the current upheaval in the country. The Modi government may be reversing decades of democratic gains by recasting the act of voting as a privilege, conferred at the discretion of bureaucrats or as a favor from the ruling party, rather than a right guaranteed by the constitutional order. Now, every prospective voter must first complete an enumeration form, and many may subsequently be required to produce onerous documentary evidence, such as birth certificates and educational records to establish their eligibility to vote. Tens of millions of Indians from marginalized communities either lack documentary records or possess documents that are riddled with mistakes such as spelling errors, making it harder for them to navigate any process of bureaucratic verification.

The fear is that India is—to put it bluntly—being yanked back to a darker time, one from which it emerged in no small part because of the work done over decades to firmly establish the credibility and broaden the depth of its democratic franchise. In the 1950s, India was an overwhelmingly rural country, with around 83% of its population living in the villages and only about 17% in urban areas. Its nascent democratic system confronted entrenched social hierarchies of caste and power. Early accounts of grassroots democracy in India describe how higher-caste landowners controlled the votes of their lower-caste dependents, sometimes even marking ballots that the voters had to authenticate with their thumbprints.

The pioneering Indian sociologist M.N. Srinivas showed how politicians campaigning for votes treated villagers not as individual voters with agency but as members of “vote banks.” Politicians approached landlords or community leaders to deliver these blocs of votes in return for favors from the government, from “licenses for buses and rice-mills to seats in medical and technological colleges for their kinsfolk.”

And well into the 1960s, millions of Indians were still unable to exercise their constitutional right to vote. In the eastern Indian state of Bihar, landlords used club-wielding enforcers known as lathaits to keep Dalits, members of the most oppressed caste, out of polling booths. “I remember watching members of my caste being dragged out of a polling booth,” an elderly Dalit man in Bihar’s Siwan district recalled in an interview with The Print, an Indian news site.

During the state elections in 1967, Jagdish Master, a teacher turned activist, challenged the veto of the landlords over Dalit suffrage by escorting a group of Dalits to a polling booth in Ekwari, a village in Bihar’s Bhojpur district. The landlords’ henchmen brutally beat him and drove him out. He returned to the village as an armed guerrilla, recruiting Dalit laborers and other poor peasants in a Maoist-style rural insurgency that would flare across the region for decades.

The power of the vote in Indian consciousness transformed radically after the “Emergency,” the period between June 1975 and March 1977 when Prime Minister Indira Gandhi suspended constitutional rights, assumed untrammeled powers, imprisoned opposition leaders and silenced the press. Much of India’s elite—bureaucrats, businessmen, newspaper editors and movie stars—bent the knee and welcomed Mrs. Gandhi’s promise of “order” and “discipline.” Wealth and connections offered protection from the state. India’s poor, stripped of their electoral power, suffered terrible abuses of an unaccountable bureaucracy and police from slum demolitions to forced sterilization of men.

In the general elections held in March 1977 after the end of the Emergency, Mrs. Gandhi’s Congress Party was decisively defeated by the people and tossed out of power for the first time since Indian independence in 1947. The principal victims of abuses of the Emergency—Dalits, Muslims, and the poor of the populous northern Indian states—voted against the Congress party for the first time and contributed significantly to its removal from power

The power of the vote has gradually forced India’s high-handed bureaucracy to recognize the human dignity and political citizenship of ordinary people. The electorate delivered its verdict on Mrs. Gandhi’s breach of the sacred covenant between the Indian state and its people: the people confer legitimacy on the state in exchange for their rights, including its protection of the sanctity of the vote. Having asserted their power, voters proved difficult to subdue again. As the political theorist Partha Chatterjee put it, it was the 1977 defeat of Mrs. Gandhi and the Congress party that established the “capacity of the vote.”

India’s poor and marginalized took proper control of their voting choices three decades after independence. In the years after the Emergency, they leveraged the vote as a means to a secure livelihood or as a tool for ensuring justice. Politicians could no longer rely on local patrons to intimidate people into voting for them and had to compete on representing their interests. A new competitive politics emerged in the 1980s and led to an upsurge, as Chatterjee wrote, “of popular demands of a kind that had never before been allowed to disturb the order and tranquility of the proverbial corridors of power.”

By the 1990s, political leaders from socially disenfranchised castes and Dalit communities were taking power in the populous, northern Indian states of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. Political scientist Christophe Jaffrelot memorably described it as “the silent revolution.” This revolution, ushered in by the votes of the historically oppressed communities, did not quite transform the economic and social structures of the region. But it changed the everyday relations of power. Indrajit Roy, a scholar of development studies, recalled a refrain he heard frequently during his fieldwork among Dalits in Bihar in the late 2000s: “There has been development, change has come. Today, we can look the landlord in the eye and speak as equals.”

Vote banks had thus turned into vehicles of collective political assertion. Marginalized communities had become adept at pooling their votes to secure political favors and advance their shared interests. They chose their own patrons, preferring their own caste leaders to represent their interests and negotiate with the state on their behalf.

Over time, the clientelist politics of vote banks became associated with an entrenched culture of corruption. This helped pave the way for Modi’s successful national campaign in 2014, in which he cast himself as a populist strongman who embodied the aspirations for “development” of all Indians while portraying the Congress and regional parties as beholden to their respective vote banks. Modi’s attacks on vote bank politics also carried a more sinister charge: that successive governments had appeased India’s Muslims minority, which accounts for around 14% of the population, for their votes at the expense of the national interest.

Bangladesh borders the Indian state of West Bengal and Modi reserved his sharpest, xenophobic barbs for Bengal’s Muslims, whom he tarred during his 2014 campaign as “Bangladeshi” and “illegal immigrants,” establishing a template for their vilification that persists to this day. In an inflammatory campaign speech, Modi issued an ominous warning: “After May 16 [the day of the results of the 2014 elections], these Bangladeshis better be prepared with their bags packed.”

The Modi government invoked that very specter of the “illegal, Bangladeshi Muslim” to push through revisions to electoral rolls across most Indian states. India’s home minister, Amit Shah, who had earlier likened Bangladeshi immigrants to “termites,” responded to the ongoing protests by calling them an “action plan to protect infiltrators.”

There are no reliable estimates of how many Bangladeshi immigrants live in India. In 2016, a minister of the Modi government told the parliament, without citing a source, that the country was home to some 20 million “illegal immigrants from Bangladesh.” The government later acknowledged that it had no accurate data on the number of illegal immigrants in India.

Yet the Indian government has used unfounded speculation about “illegal” Bangladeshis to overturn the fundamental logic of the country’s electoral registration system, shifting the burden of ensuring registration from the Election Commission to the voter herself. The commission was responsible for ensuring that every citizen made it onto the electoral rolls.

The exercise has already disproportionately disenfranchised the poor, migrants, religious minorities, women—and is bound to continue doing so. “The majority of voters in Bihar who were excluded from the list were in rural areas,” said retired Maj. Gen. Anil Verma, who heads the Association for Democratic Reforms, a nonprofit group that challenged the electoral rolls revisions in the Supreme Court. “Those worst affected were the poor and the marginalized—they had no documents.”

Opposition parties have also alleged that the electoral roll revisions tilted the electoral field in the BJP’s favor in the West Bengal election held earlier this year, helping the party win power in the state for the first time. An analysis by the SABAR Institute, a public policy research institute, lent some credence to that charge: a disproportionate share of the 9.08 million voters removed from West Bengal’s electoral rolls came from constituencies held by the then-ruling Trinamool Congress or those with large Muslim populations.

The Election Commission has denied the charge that the deletions were politically motivated, arguing that it applied uniform criteria to remove entries for voters who had died, were registered more than once or had permanently relocated. But that claim is difficult to reconcile with the disproportionate representation  of certain  groups among those deleted from supplementary lists issued for some constituencies after an adjudication process. For instance, although Muslims make up around 23% of the population of Nandigram, a constituency in West Bengal, the SABAR Institute study found that they accounted for 95.5% of the deletions across seven supplementary lists released by the Election Commission for the constituency.

Voting carries an almost existential significance for Indians whose equal citizenship has not been fully recognized by the institutions of the state. Indians see the vote as an affirmation of their existence. If the vote is no longer a gift freely given or withdrawn by the people but a resource controlled by the state, it reduces ordinary voters once again to supplicants dependent on the favor of political elites.

The electoral revisions and mass deletion of voters have provoked such widespread revulsion because many Indians see it as an assault on their identity as citizens and a threat to the social recognition they have won through a long, arduous struggle. Many Indians might have forgiven Modi for his numerous missteps but they are determined not to let him devalue their voting power.

The young protesters moving through Delhi’s barricaded streets carry a palpable sense that they are living through a historical moment. References to the Quit India movement against the country’s erstwhile British colonizers and the protests around the Emergency have become commonplace. Memories of seminal events of the past offer a lens through which to consider the present. The future seems open to change through collective action. This sense of a turning point imbues individual actions with a larger purpose and drives protesters to confront a police force intent on clearing the streets by whatever means necessary.

On Oct. 9, a day before Delhi was placed under a lockdown, Bora invoked India’s struggle against British imperialism to denounce the pre-emptive detention of protest organizers. “History is repeating itself. Just like in 1942, the arrest of leaders could not stop the Quit India movement; this movement will also not stop,” Bora said. “This is nothing but a state of open emergency in the country.”

The convergence of young people’s anger over education and employment, fears of authoritarianism, and concern for the sanctity of the vote poses a serious challenge to the Modi government. It could forge a broad popular coalition of unemployed young people, urban poor, Dalits and religious minorities, united in challenging the legitimacy of the government. Unless the government retreats on electoral roll revisions, sustained protests could drain its political capital.

One of Indian democracy’s great achievements is that even its poorest citizens have proved adept at recognizing when the power of their vote is being wrested away, gauging the consequences and organizing to resist. A government in India can retain its legitimacy despite failing to provide functioning schools or decent employment. It cannot do so if it also violates the sanctity of the vote.

Nvidia's role as AI financier faces first big test as neoclouds take market hit

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia's emergence as financier for the global AI buildout is facing its first major stress test as credit markets tighten for key partners.

After creating the chips that powered the early stages of the generative artificial intelligence era, Nvidia turned to using its financial prowess to fund AI model developers, data center companies and a host of so-called neoclouds. CEO Jensen Huang's strategy has been to not only build the most advanced technology but to provide customers with investment as well as help them take out loans so his systems can reach many more hands.

That's meant taking full advantage of Nvidia's investment grade credit rating and providing backstop guarantees on mega-projects for OpenAI so that buildouts can get funded at lower interest rates.

As of its last quarterly financial filing in July, Nvidia had $36 billion in off-balance-sheet AI cloud agreements, or backstops, for neoclouds, in addition to its guarantee on up to $105 billion for chips, land and power at a proposed 10 gigawatt OpenAI data center in Ohio. The company signed as a tenant for $20 billion in future data center leases, which will later be assigned to third parties.

For the vast majority of these deals, Nvidia won't have to pay up unless participants in the project default.

"If the demand for compute doesn't meet these really super lofty expectations, we're going to have a problem," said David Trainer, founder and CEO of financial research firm New Constructs, in an interview.

Nvidia declined to comment for this story.

Market conditions may soon test Nvidia's mettle. Shares of neoclouds CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN, all cash-burning companies in Nvidia's portfolio, are down at least 30% this month. CoreWeave, the biggest of the group, has seen the spread on its credit default swaps soar past 11%, as investors are forced to pay up for insurance against default.

Nvidia has backed other neoclouds that have run into challenges.

The company is an investor in Australian AI cloud provider Firmus, which abruptly withdrew its IPO plans this month, as well as Nscale out of the U.K. and SoftBank-backed SB Energy. Both of those companies filed to go public in the U.S. in September but haven't provided updates on their plans in weeks.

Nvidia wrapped up a third straight down week on Wall Street on Friday, with its stock now off 7.6% in October, on pace for its worst month since November of last year.

Huang reiterated his confidence in the market this week, telling CNBC on Thursday that demand for neoclouds is "off the charts."

"What is missing in the middle is the working capital to fuel that growth," Huang told CNBC's Arjun Kharpal on the sidelines of the company's GTC conference in Berlin. "Because there's just so much demand across so many sectors of AI, there's a scarcity of capital."

Nvidia has taken equity stakes in dozens of companies, including big investments in OpenAI and Anthropic. Its portfolio is worth $99 billion as of the second quarter.

Investors told CNBC that a big question surrounding Nvidia is how the company can use its cash and $5 trillion market cap to get more chips into customers' hands, and how much risk it will assume in the process.

Nvidia graphics processing units are critical for training and deploying AI models. Revenue in the latest fiscal year hit $216 billion, up roughly eightfold since OpenAI launched ChatGPT in late 2022.

Before the AI boom, Nvidia sold graphics cards that could fit into a standard server rack or PC. Its newest systems are much larger and more expensive. Nvidia's flagship Vera Rubin product is a seven-foot rack containing 72 GPUs and weighing as much as a pickup truck. It costs more than $7 million, according to a Morgan Stanley estimate.

While Nvidia still dominates the AI chip market, competition is emerging from the likes of Google, which rents out tensor processing units (TPUs) inside its massive cloud offering, and Advanced Micro Devices, which launched its first AI rack called Helios in July.

Cash is Nvidia's other great advantage. The company generated $70 billion in free cash flow in the first half of this year, while AMD totaled just over $4 billion and Google parent Alphabet went cash flow negative in the second quarter to fund its buildout.

That's a big edge for Nvidia in building out its ecosystem. The company is now offering a full-service platform complete with software, services, integrated hardware and, for many customers, financing assistance.

Nvidia's most recent quarterly financial filing in August outlined over $500 billion in off-balance-sheet commitments over the coming years, with a majority maturing through fiscal 2029. That same month, Huang announced a partnership with six large asset managers on a $500 billion financing push designed to treat compute infrastructure as an asset class. That agreement, which promised financial firms up to $125 billion in residual value guarantees on GPU financings, was in addition to the stakes Nvidia has taken as a limited partner in private equity funds. The company seeded $2 billion in Brookfield's inaugural AI Infrastructure Partners Fund, which aims to raise $10 billion in total capital, as well as other unconfirmed sums in KKR's Helix Digital Infrastructure (an $11 billion vehicle) and Valor Equity Partners' $5.4 billion dedicated compute fund.

"We used to build chips that we sell, and these are technology components that people buy and use," Huang told CNBC, in announcing the agreement in August. "But now, Nvidia's AI factory platform is really an investable asset."

Huang has adopted a marketing mantra to reinforce the unique value of its equipment.

"We're the most rentable. We're fungible, and we're durable," he said on a podcast with investor Brad Gerstner published last week.

While Nvidia waits for Wall Street firms to pull in investors to expand the GPU loan pool, the chipmaker is already actively fostering record-sized deals in the AI infrastructure space.

Nvidia's biggest single guarantee is for OpenAI's forthcoming facility in Pike County, Ohio, which could be the largest data center in the world. That deal requires use of Nvidia hardware for at least 20 years.

The shift from chips to capital carries significant risks should OpenAI run into trouble.

If OpenAI misses payments or faces insolvency, Nvidia can take over the lease, find a new tenant, or ask the landlord, SB Energy, to sell the facility, guaranteeing to pay the difference for the latter two arrangements up to $105 billion if the release or sale falls below a minimum price. Nvidia could also pay SB Energy a lump sum. On the other side, if OpenAI posts its own collateral, or achieves a high enough credit rating, Nvidia's guarantee ends.

At CoreWeave, Nvidia's investment includes equity and a commitment to buy up to $6.3 billion of its compute if it can't find another taker.

Nvidia's backstops can take many forms, such as guaranteeing a minimum resale price or a minimum hourly offtake price for the chips in the event the lender has to recoup the assets. With Sharon AI and other neoclouds, Nvidia's deals have come with revenue-sharing agreements.

"We believe these AI cloud partners have strong customer demand and robust sales pipelines but are constrained by the large-scale infrastructure that is required to meet that demand," Nvidia said in its quarterly filing.

Huang said this week that CoreWeave doesn't need Nvidia's backstop and that the neocloud's cost of capital is decreasing. CoreWeave has roughly $51.6 billion in total debt, including about $35 billion in notes, bonds and loans. Yields on its bonds maturing in 2031 and 2032 topped 15%.

Brian Stutland, portfolio manager at Equity Armor Investments, said the market jitters reflect concerns about the stability of the business.

CoreWeave and other neoclouds "will sign contracts into the future, and that all looks fine and dandy in their earnings reports, but is that contract going to be stable?" Stutland said. "Is it going to break at some point?"

Nvidia says its financial support can help customers build the infrastructure needed to meet strong demand for its chips. More computing capacity could also help AI labs develop new models and services, creating further demand for Nvidia products.

"They'll do $700 billion in revenue next year," Deepwater Asset Management founder Gene Munster told CNBC last month. "That affords them the ability to finance some of their customers."

Nvidia's credit backstops have mostly kicked in over the past six to 12 months. Its last annual filing, published in February, makes no mention of AI cloud agreements.

Warren Hosseinion, co-founder of AI infrastructure startup Compute Labs, said Nvidia doesn't want to be in the position of insurer, "but they're willing to play that role while the market and the industry catches up."

"They kind of control how things move," said Hosseinion, whose company tokenizes GPUs in order to open up pools of capital for neocloud operators. "They have to be very careful about how they release their technology at what pace, because a lot of that will dictate how the market moves around it."

Nvidia is actively looking for insurers to bear some of the risk associated with the potential loss in value of GPUs.

"Insurers are not yet a complete panacea to this problem," said Tom Hoad, executive director of product innovation at global insurance group Howden. "But they are probably going to turn up and be helpful in the right situations, and therefore open up some commercial transactions."

According to research from New Constructs, Nvidia's net present value of off-balance-sheet liabilities in fiscal 2026 totals over $153 billion, an increase of over $100 billion from last year.

Such liabilities have been growing across the entire AI trade, mainly in the form of lease obligations. Ratings agencies like Moody's have been sounding the alarm. In 2026, guarantees are the fastest-growing type of off-balance-sheet financing arrangements, the agency said.

Moody's doesn't count guarantees as debt when rating corporate buyers, but it's "something we're constantly monitoring," said David Gonzales, a vice president at the agency.

"These things are advanced structures that are unique, and so there is no blanket real approach you can take," he said. Gonzales said it's likely that chips will retain "values in excess of contractual residual value amounts" in the near future, which would mitigate risk in many of Nvidia's financing structures.

Still, how much exposure investment grade AI players can handle is "the question we're evaluating at all times every day," Gonzales said.

"It's the center of all of our discussions right now," he said.

For its 2026 After Dark Tour, a nighttime race series of 10Ks and half-marathons for women, Nike designed a multipurpose medal that’s meant to be worn long after race day as a necklace, bracelet, keychain loop, or belt chain.

Each comes with a city-specific carabiner for the places where the races take place: London; Shanghai; Los Angeles; Mexico City; Sydney; Manila, Philippines; and Mumbai, India. Most finisher medals end up in a drawer, the thinking goes, but this medal was designed to leave the house.

“The Nike After Dark Tour isn’t a traditional race, so the medal didn’t need to be traditional either,” Elizabeth Brock-Jones, Nike’s footwear development director, tells Fast Company.

The new medal was designed by an all-woman team, which helped get the details just right.

“Because women led every stage of the process, we designed for things we’d experience ourselves: how it hangs when you move, whether it catches in your hair, whether you can open the clasp with longer nails,” Brock-Jones says. “We wanted something women would actually reach for, not just something that looked good on paper.”

The carabiner took about seven rounds to perfect and the chain system at least five. To cut down on the design timeline, the team traveled to Italy to work directly with its manufacturing partner to make decisions in real time instead of waiting for samples.

For Nike, the new medal is the latest example of the sportswear brand’s outreach to women, a demographic group it’s courting through partnerships like NikeSkims, advertising, and events, like competitions. Nike hosted a series of women’s races in major cities from 2005 to 2015. Then last year it launched the After Dark Tour, a race series it said was “designed to be the ultimate girls’ weekend.”

The 2025 medal was unconventional, too. It featured a shoelace-inspired chain that worked like a reflective, sweat-proof bolo tie on one side with a magnetic closure on the other and a textured Nike Swoosh. After seeing how runners actually wore it, the design team was inspired to make this year’s medals intentionally as something that could be worn as jewelry.

“We saw women styling the 2025 medal in ways we never intended,” Brock-Jones says. “They put it on bags, layered it with their own jewelry, and made it part of their everyday look.”

Nike also designed a small, matching accessories collection including hoop and stud earrings featuring swooshes. The products are available to After Dark runners as a gift with purchase at select race locations.

All race medals are mementos, but by turning this medal into a multipurpose jewelry piece, Nike hopes it will keep the race—not to mention everything that led up to it—much more front of mind.

“Every time she sees it, we hope it brings her back to the early mornings, the long training runs, and the setbacks she pushed through to get there, while reminding her what she’s capable of next,” Brock-Jones says.
