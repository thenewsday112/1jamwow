Who was Eva Mamlok? Berlin square renamed after Jewish anti-fascist resistance fighter

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

She started taking action against Hitler as a teenager and never stopped until her death in a concentration camp. Berlin's Blücherplatz is now being renamed after her.

In the history of German resistance to Nazism, some names have become symbols: Sophie Scholl, Claus von Stauffenberg, Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Georg Elser. Yet many others have remained largely forgotten.

Among them is Eva Mamlok, a young Jewish woman from Berlin whose acts of defiance began in her teenage years and continued until her murder in a Nazi concentration camp.

Now more people will get to know her story as Berlin renames Blücherplatz, a square in the district of Kreuzberg, Eva-Mamlok-Platz on October 16.

"Eva Mamlok was a very remarkable person. As a young Jewish woman from Berlin, her spirit of resistance was particularly striking," said cultural researcher Alexandra Weltz-Rombach, who has worked extensively with the historians' network "Gruppe Eva Mamlok."

Mamlok was born on May 6, 1918, into a Jewish family in Berlin. They ran a wholesale wine business, and the family belonged to the vibrant Jewish middle class that formed an important part of Berlin's social and economic life during the Weimar Republic.

But Eva came of age just as Germany was descending into dictatorship. When Hitler became chancellor in 1933, she was only 14 years old. She was also a member of the Socialist Workers' Youth (SAJ). Following the suppression of the left-wing political youth organization later that year, she became active in the underground resistance.

According to testimony from Inge Berner (1922-2012), the sole survivor of Mamlok's later resistance group, Eva climbed onto the roof of a department store on Blücherplatz as a teenager and painted the slogan "Down with Hitler."

"She was caught doing that, though she wasn't arrested at that particular moment — that didn't happen until a year later," noted Weltz-Rombach.

Mamlok first appeared in official records in November 1934, after she was arrested for taking part in commemorations for socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht at Berlin's Friedrichsfelde Cemetery.

Authorities determined that the 16-year-old held "hostile views toward the state" and placed her in "protective custody until further notice" — a euphemism the regime used to justify imprisonment without trial. She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

Several places, including streets and parks, have been named after him in Germany.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

The Bundestag has debated the genocide Germany carried out in Namibia a century ago. But will it spur progress on addressing colonial crimes?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Opposition parties have forced a debate in the Bundestag on the early-20th-century genocide Germany carried out in Namibia. Will this finally spur progress in addressing colonial crimes?

Gaob Daniel Timotheus Frederick was sitting in the front row of the visitors' gallery in the Bundestag this week. Wearing a suit and hat, he had traveled all the way from Namibia to see this parliamentary debate unfold. For almost 120 years, his family had been waiting for the return of human remains of their ancestor, Cornelius Frederick (sometimes spelled Fredericks), who had led the Nama resistance against German colonial rule and died on Shark Island in Germany's first concentration camp.

In the name of his great-grandfather and as a member of the Nama Traditional Leaders Association (NTLA), Frederick was there to demand an "unconditioned apology" from Germany.

Sitting next to him, in uniform and white gloves, with a carved lion on the handle of his walking stick, was the head of the Ovaherero Traditional Authority, Paramount Chief Mutjinde Katjiua. Speaking before the Bundestag debate, the chief said: "We call upon the Bundestag to recognize unequivocally the genocides committed against the Ovaherero and Nama peoples."

Many people wonder why the German parliament hasn't already done this — and why the government has not yet issued an official apology. There is a draft joint declaration with Namibia in which the German government acknowledges that "from today's perspective, [it] would be called genocide." But there is no German-Namibian reconciliation agreement. The evidence is overwhelming: From 1904 to 1908, the German Empire waged a war of extermination against the Indigenous people of southwestern Africa, including the Ovaherero (also known as the Herero), the Nama, the Damara and the San. At least 100,000 people were killed, and just as many displaced. Many were subjected to forced labor and forced prostitution. The land seizures and expropriation carried out at the time continue to affect their descendants today.

Jürgen Zimmerer, a professor of history at the University of Hamburg whose research focuses on Africa, told DW that the Bundestag and government lack the will to confront this era in German history. "There are Bundestag resolutions on the Armenian genocide, one on the Holodomor [in Ukraine], and one on the genocide against the Yazidis [by the "Islamic State" in Iraq]. But there is no recognition of the genocide against the Herero and Nama by the German Bundestag." If there were any political will to do so, Zimmerer said, it could happen very quickly.

The socialist Left Party initiated the Bundestag debate by drafting a resolution calling for full recognition and compensation in collaboration with representatives of the descendants. The goal: "restorative justice." Paramount Chief Katjiua has rejected the existing agreement between the Namibian and German governments. "Development aid cannot substitute for comprehensive reparations," he said.

Since 2021, the governments have been negotiating a joint declaration that pledges €1.1 billion ($1.24 billion) in reconstruction aid to Namibia over 30 years. Once the agreement is signed, Germany's president is set to issue a formal apology.

But the Foreign Ministry warned: "Legal claims to compensation cannot be derived from the recognition of genocide. With these negotiations — which are unique worldwide — Namibia and Germany have broken new ground and are pioneers in confronting their colonial past."

Political scientist Andreas Mehler, the director of the Anton Bergstraesser Institute at the University of Freiburg, said the government's plan sounded like "checkbook diplomacy."

Zimmerer also said there was a financial calculation: "If we were to recognize an obligation to provide reparations to the families of those killed — given the millions of victims of World War II, the victims of the Germans — it would ruin us."

Four lines in the 144 pages of the coalition agreement of the current German government — between the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU)/Christian Social Union (CSU) and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) — summarize the extent of the government's commitment to reconciliation with Namibia.

"We will intensify our efforts to come to terms with colonialism," the text reads. "This includes transnational research into artifacts and the restitution of cultural property in dialogue with the countries of origin. Particular attention will be paid to establishing a dignified memorial site and the repatriation of human remains."

Compared with the efforts to address colonialism led by the SPD-led government from 2021 to 2025, "the issue has lost visibility," said Alexander Stroh-Steckelberg, a professor of political scientist at the University of Bayreuth who specializes in Africa.

Zimmerer has an even more critical take: "I wouldn't even call it a stalemate anymore. I believe this government is rolling back the progress that has been made in terms of raising awareness of colonial injustices."

Much remains to be done, as far the descendants of the Ovaherero are concerned: The remains of tens of thousands of people are believed to still be in Germany. Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick and other descendants of the victims have been fighting to heal these wounds for generations.

In March, Germany established a coordination council, headed by the Foreign Ministry, which stated that the government did not wish to "dictate to the countries and societies of origin which cultural artifacts and human remains they should reclaim, what priorities they should set or how restitutions should be handled."

Serap Güler, a state minister in the Foreign Ministry and the chair of the coordination council, defends the government's actions so far. Recounting her own official trips — she has already been to Togo, and next week she plans to travel to Rwanda and Burundi — she said: "Everywhere I go, I receive recognition for our sincere and trusting approach to the past — as well as for our determined commitment to a future of freedom, security and prosperity."

Zimmerer said Germany "has earned a reputation as a country that takes a critical view of its own history and learns from its mistakes" — which gives it "enormous cultural capital" on the world stage. But he is concerned that this capital is currently being squandered.

The government's memorial policy does not appear to address colonialism. Zimmerer even detects an attempt to "marginalize German colonial crimes" in the recent proposal by Minister of State for Culture Wolfram Weimer to create a dignified memorial site in Bremen. He notes that victim groups have not been included in Weimer's proposal and said the colonial capitals of the German Empire — Berlin and Hamburg — must also become sites of remembrance.

Ninety-five percent of the colonial troops set sail from Hamburg's Baakenhafen, including Commander Lothar von Trotha, who issued the orders to exterminate the Ovaherero and Nama. According to Zimmerer, the fact that a new opera house — rather than a memorial site — is being built at Baakenhafen constitutes a staggering case of "art-washing of history." Mehler also hopes that a productive debate on memorial sites will still take place and that, overall, "many more places will come to terms with colonial history."

Meanwhile, Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick continues to call for a collective effort to come to terms with the past: "Let us give opportunity to the affected communities that are being represented by us to have a seat on the table to discuss with them the rest of the way."

Hijab ban at schools in Azerbaijan: What's behind the rare wave of protests?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Girls wearing headscarves have been turned away from schools in Azerbaijan, triggering unusual protests involving children. The government rules do not mention hijabs. Why are schools enforcing unwritten rules?

A dispute over Islamic headscarves in Azerbaijani schools has triggered protests at home and an unusual exchange of criticism between politicians in Azerbaijan and Turkey, two countries whose leaders routinely describe their relationship as that of "one nation, two states". The row has raised questions about religious freedom, sovereignty and the limits of Turkish and Iranian influence in the south Caucasian country.

The unprecedented protests have been taking place across Azerbaijan for a nearly week. Children have demonstrated against the headscarf ban introduced at schools. Among those rallying outside of schools are girls who wear Islamic headscarves; others, including boys, have joined classmates in solidarity.

Rallies in general are rare in a country where police routinely disperse any public protests. But demonstrations involving children are unprecedented in Azerbaijan. As the videos showing the protests started spreading on social media, access to Instagram and TikTok was disrupted in the country, indicating how seriously the authorities are taking the issue. Internet monitors documented blocks; the authorities have not explained their cause.

The dispute follows a series of legal changes. On July 7, Azerbaijan amended its general education law, reinforcing the principle of secular education and requiring pupils and teachers to comply with dress codes. On September 21, the government adopted Resolution No. 309, tightening school uniform rules.

The resolution prohibits additional clothing that disrupts the standard uniform or conflicts with secular education. It does not explicitly mention the hijab ― a Muslim headscarf covering the hair and neck ― or instruct schools to exclude pupils wearing one. But some school administrators have interpreted the rule as applying to hijabs and turned girls covering their heads away. The regulation gives each school's pedagogical council responsibility for resolving compliance issues.

A separate decision on October 6 set dress requirements for teachers. Reports followed of women wearing headscarves being prevented from teaching.

Azerbaijan is a predominantly Muslim country, but Article 18 of its constitution separates religion from the state and requires state education to be secular. Articles 42 and 48 protect the right to education and freedom of conscience.

Supporters of the schools' actions say the same dress rules should apply to all pupils. Critics argue that excluding girls because of religious clothing violates their rights. The dispute cuts across conventional political divisions: some government critics support the restrictions, while some non-religious Azerbaijanis oppose them. School headscarf restrictions also prompted protests in 2010 and subsequent years.

A Baku literature teacher with 40 years of experience, said that girls wearing headscarves were not admitted to classes at the teacher's school. Some removed their scarves; others went home or wanted to study at home. The teacher, who didn't want to be identified by name and provided only the initials M.C., supported enforcing the uniform rules, arguing that children were too young to make decisions about religious dress.

A parent identified as D.A. told DW this was not a nationwide hijab ban, but a matter of school uniforms. Another parent who opposed the restrictions withdrew an interview before publication.

Facebook user Sarraf Cabbarov from Baku posted on his social media account that his daughter Sevil, a primary school teacher in Azerbaijan's capital, was dismissed for refusing to remove her headscarf. He also published a school order issuing her a written warning under the new dress regulations.

On October 12, the Interior Ministry said 37 people had been detained in connection with the protests. According to the ministry's statement, the group had confronted school staff, damaged property and attempted to obstruct school activities and traffic. A criminal case was opened under Article 233, concerning disruption of public order. The ministry did not identify those detained or say whether they included children.

Artist and activist Ilkin Huseynov went missing after launching a project linking volunteer tutors with pupils affected by the restrictions. Local media later reported, citing people close to him, that he had been given 30 days of administrative detention for allegedly disobeying police orders.

Some observers linked the introduction of the new rules for school uniform to the attempts by the Azerbaijani government to counter the influence of neighboring Iran and the growing strength of religious groups within the country. This interpretation is, in essence, echoed by government officials as well, although they stop short of explicitly naming Iran.

Fazil Mustafa, who chairs the Committee for Public Associations and Religious Institutions at Azerbaijan's parliament, said the authorities were concerned about religious groups influenced from abroad and defended the school rules. Allahshukur Pashazade, head of the Caucasus Muslims Office, also warned of outside interference but called for a peaceful solution. The alleged foreign involvement has not been independently established.

Amnesty International condemned the exclusion of girls wearing hijabs and called for the release of anyone detained solely for protesting peacefully.

The controversy has also drawn criticism in Turkey, Azerbaijan's close ally. Protesters gathered outside Azerbaijan's consulate in Istanbul on October 3. Oktay Saral, an adviser to Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan, criticized the restrictions,saying it's unacceptable to deny education to students wearing headscarves. Azerbaijan's ruling New Azerbaijan Party replied that close relations did not justify interference in its internal affairs.

For affected families, the immediate question remains whether children can attend school without removing their headscarves.

Ukrainian President Zelenskyy speaks at EU summit amid spat with Donald Trump

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

EU leaders reaffirmed support for Ukraine at the Brussels summit. But souring relations with the US over Russia and Starlink and a big hole in the budget will make for a difficult winter for Ukraine.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy was applauded by EU leaders when he appeared at this week's summit in Brussels. But getting what he needs from Europe will be tough, and a public dispute with US President Donald Trump has put him in a very difficult position.

Speaking to press at the summit, he said that "Putin wants to destroy us" and that Ukraine must prepare for a hard winter.

Experts view the upcoming cold season as a rough one for Ukraine as well.

"Ukraine is arguably entering the most difficult winter since [Russia's] full scale invasion," Olena Prokopenko, a senior fellow at the German Marshall Fund of the United States, a nonpartisan think-tank, told DW.

Three painful topics were on the table at the summit for discussion between Zelenskyy and EU leaders: US relations with Russia, Starlink ― and money.

Zelenskyy's visit to Brussels comes at a time when he needs European support more than ever. That's because his relationship with the US is worsening. Worries are growing in the EU and Ukraine that the US is growing more sympathetic to Russia.

President Trump told US news channel Newsmax: "I think I find Zelenskyy to be more difficult to deal with than [Russian President Vladimir] Putin."

Trump struck a deal to buy Russian diesel on October 12, which signifies a step back from sanctions placed on Russia and a blow for Ukraine.

When Zelenskyy ordered Ukrainian forces to conduct fresh strikes on Russia's oil refineries, Trump labelled the Ukrainian leader's decision as "a disgrace."

At the press conference at the summit on Friday, Zelenskyy told reporters that he knows that US-built missiles "are being sent to the Middle East, so it is no secret that we are not a priority."

He is asking Europe instead to supply the military power Ukraine needs to get through the winter.

Since the beginning of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the US has been the biggest military donor to Ukraine. But the relationship between the two countries has become more difficult since the start of Donald Trump's second term in the White House.

Ukraine uses Starlink, a satellite Internet service developed by tech billionaire Elon Musk. The country also uses Starshield, the military-grade version, for battlefield communications, mid-range strikes on Russian-occupied territory and interceptor drones, though not for long-range strikes in Russia.

At the summit, Zelenskyy spoke of his "deep and unpleasant" surprise upon finding out that Ukraine was experiencing disruptions to the systems. He said this has hobbled Ukraine's ability to defend itself.

Both Elon Musk and the White House say they are not responsible for the slowdown.

The EU's foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas told the press at the summit before the talks began: "Starlink is really necessary for Ukraine and unfortunately in [the] short term Europe can't replace this capability."

Prokopenko said that this was the reason "why Ukraine's most credible medium-term solution here is to continue relying on the US. And the EU is critical in this context in terms of backing Ukraine's requests for the US administration to keep those technologies on offer" for Kyiv.

Speaking to journalists at the summit, Zelenskyy said the speed of Starlink had still not been fully restored but had improved. He said Ukraine is cooperating with various countries, including France, to search for a partial solution.

Zelenskyy urged EU leaders Friday to keep backing Kyiv financially as well, asking for nearly $80 billion (€70 billion) more next year.

"This is a realistic amount and I ask you to find this support," Zelenskyy said. "It will help us hold Russia back."

The EU gave Kyiv an approximately $100 billion (€90 billion) loan last year, making the EU Ukraine's biggest international backer. But the Ukrainian government is saying it needs more.

"The war is becoming objectively more expensive, and the Ukrainian economy is increasingly affected by the Russian strikes, leading to a drastic decline in tax and customs revenues," Prokopenko said. "So the needs will continue growing."

So will Ukraine get what it needs? Closing the summit, President of the European Council Antonio Costa said that Ukraine "remains a priority" and asked the European Commission to come up with a proposal to pull together more funds for the country in 2027.

Essentially, it looks as though the EU will try to find a way to meet Ukraine's cash demands ― though it is not yet clear how.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen promised the EU will "continue to lead" but urged international partners to step up as well.

Zelenskyy will leave Brussels without concrete promises or numbers, but with renewed commitments for support from Europe.

The Ukrainian president concluded his press conference with an emotional statement: "We will keep living and fighting with dignity."

France: Protesters clash with police in nationwide protests, amid rising social polarization

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Unrest broke out in parts of France as students, trade unionists and Yellow Vest activists took to the streets to protest high prices and inequality. Police arrested over 1,000 people amid chaotic scenes.

Police and protesters clashed amid mass rallies across France on Saturday, over high prices and social inequalities and the state of the education system.

France's Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu said some 1,200 people had been arrested across the country and that 150 police officers had been injured.

"It's a miracle the toll wasn't higher," Lecornu said while visiting officers deployed in Paris on Saturday evening.

Interior Minister Laurent Nunez said 110,000 people took part in the demonstrations in Paris alone, while 110 marches had taken place across the country.

The Interior Ministry said some 550,000 people turned out for the nationwide demonstrations.

The ministry said the arrests were made following violent incidents in Paris, Marseilles, Rennes, Toulouse, Angers and other cities across the country.

"Such actions include attempted looting, confrontations with law enforcement, the setting of fires to street furniture and the targeting of seats of power and media outlets," the ministry said, blaming the unrest on what it described ​as the "hard-left."

In Paris and Marseille, police fired tear gas at protesters as violent clashes took place.

AFP reported that dozens of shops were looted in Paris, Marseille, Nice, Bordeaux and Lille.

The windows of the offices of French broadcasters CNews and Europe 1 in western Paris were also broken by protesters, and AFP journalists reported seeing several parked cars torched.

French President Emmanuel Macron condemned the violence in a post on X and warned: "Those who have broken, attacked the prefectures as well as businesses, looted even our town halls, will be prosecuted and punished."

In Lyon, situated in central France, the city's tourism office was set alight with flames and black smoke seen billowing from the building.

In the city of Limoges, the town hall was set alight as unrest broke out. The blaze spread from a wing of the building and eventually reached the roof, where it continued burning as crowds stood watching.

The historic building was largely destroyed by the blaze, despite attempts by firefighters to douse the flames.

Meanwhile, in the northern city of Lille, protesters smashed their way into the city hall and vandalized the premises before leaving, according to an AFP photographer.

Riot police within minutes confirmed that all the protesters had left.

The protests come on the heels of an initial wave of demonstrations that began last month over the situation at schools in the country, with pupils complaining about teacher absences and run-down facilities.

Such a scene could fit in a new installment of "Call of Duty," the popular first-person shooter game that transports players to battlefields past, present, and future. But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

More than 700 million people in China regularly play video games. According to market research company Newzoo, most consider gaming an important part of their lives. Many use games to stay in touch with friends.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Anyone who wants to play must register using their real name and a personal identification number. That effectively allows the state to track who plays what and when, as well as whom they communicate with.

There is also a gray area that the Chinese government appears to tolerate. Some players log in using VPNs, giving them access to games that are not officially available in China. Some younger players use their parents' accounts to get around the limits on gaming time.

Despite its strict controls on underage gamers, China has become increasingly receptive to video games in general, having recognized the industry's enormous economic potential. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.

How reliant is Ukraine on the US-controlled Starlink internet service?

Kyiv keeps issuing warnings that the satellite internet system Starlink is being restricted in Ukraine. How dependent is the country on the system, and what would happen if it went down?

Few technological systems have played such an important role in the war in Ukraine as Starlink, central to the communications, reconnaissance and drone operations of the Ukrainian armed forces. There are currently widespread concerns that Elon Musk's satellite internet service in Ukraine may be restricted or even switched off. In recent days, Kyiv has repeatedly reported disruptions and restrictions affecting its use.

Since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, Ukraine has made extensive use of the satellite internet service, which is operated by the US company SpaceX. With it, military personell can continue to communicate and exchange data, even when infrastructure has been destroyed or cellphone networks are down.

According to the Center for Security Studies (CSS) at ETH Zurich, around 50,000 Starlink terminals were in use in Ukraine in 2025. These are the system's specialised receiving and transmitting devices, which enable communications on the battlefield. They are also used to relay orders between command levels and receive drone footage.

The importance of the Starlink network can in part be explained by events at the beginning of the war. According to a CSS analysis, the KA-SAT satellite system previously favored by Ukraine was targeted in a cyberattack just hours before Russia's invasion. Starlink quickly became a vital alternative.

When discussing current affairs, the terms "Starlink" and "Starshield" are often used interchangeably.

However, Starlink is SpaceX's commercial satellite internet service, whereas Starshield is specifically designed for governmental and military use. While based on the same technological infrastructure, it is designed for security and defense applications.

This distinction matters, because the latest reports of restrictions seem to primarily concern systems used for military purposes. Elon Musk recently stated that Starshield systems are controlled by the US government, whereas Starlink is a commercial service operated by SpaceX.

Alongside communications, Starlink is mainly used for drone operations. The system provides an internet connection for ground, naval and aerial drones.

This connection means video footage can be transmitted almost in real time, while drones can be controlled over long distances. Ukraine uses these systems for reconnaissance and attacks deep behind enemy lines.

According to ETH Zurich, images and GPS coordinates of Russian positions are also transmitted via these communication channels. The data is sent to artillery units and used to coordinate operations.

According to Ukrainian media reports, on Tuesday night, SpaceX temporarily introduced a speed restriction for Starlink terminals in Ukraine, limiting terminals in motion to a speed of about 160 kilometers per hour (100 mph).

Such a restriction has direct consequences for fast-moving Ukrainian drones, some of which travel at speeds of up to 300 kilometers per hour (186 mph). If the connection to the drone is lost, live video footage can no longer be transmitted, and the systems can no longer be controlled in real time. The drones effectively have to continue their mission while flying blind.

Business associations and the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU) parties have long called for these rules to be made more flexible.

They argued that it is not "in the spirit of a modern reform of working time law" if only employers can determine when employees finish work.

From a political perspective, the dispute over reforming work hours has long become more than just a debate over legal provisions. It is a fundamental dispute over how much flexibility workers should be expected to tolerate in the future and to what extent the state should protect them. And it is yet another stumbling block for the already beleaguered government in Berlin.

"It is really an abstract concept, but we experience it absolutely every day," said Rebecca Shaw, chief scientist at conservation group WWF. "And we almost never think about it when nature is functioning."

But as biodiversity declines, Shaw and other scientists are calling on the world to really think about it. Because biodiversity is struggling as humans push ecosystems to the brink through intensive farming, burning fossil fuels and pollution from sources like plastic and chemicals.

Over the past 52 years, monitored vertebrate populations have collapsed by an average of 73%, while freshwater ecosystems are faring worse with a drop of 85%, according to WWF's recent Living Planet report.

It tallies with other studies that show a million animal and plant species face extinction unless humanity reduces its pressure on nature.

"It is a really steep decline," said Shaw. "What it's telling us is that nature is in trouble, and it's an early warning indicator that the fabric of life that we depend on for freshwater and food and for clean air is unraveling."

Some 200 countries are gathering in Yerevan, Armenia over the next two weeks for COP17, the latest UN biodiversity summit, to advance commitments to reverse nature's unraveling by 2030. Another example is the decline in pollinators like bees and bats. More than 75% of global food crop types rely in part on animal pollination.

At the same time, soil and ecosystem degradation coupled with storms and heat waves linked to climate change are already damaging harvests. A recent UK government report stated that every critical ecosystem is on a "pathway to collapse," warning that nature's decline posed a grave threat to global food supply and geopolitical stability.

"The UK, like many other countries, relies on imports for much of the food and so is reliant on ecosystems around the world," said Louise McRae, a research fellow at the Zoological Society of London, who also contributed to WWF's Living Planet report. Nature also provides the basis for many modern human medicines, with some 50% coming from natural compounds.

Penicillin, the first antibiotic, is produced by a mold and is estimated to have saved over 500 million lives. Before its discovery, a person could die from a simple cut. Around 70% of cancer drugs come from natural products or synthetic compounds "inspired by nature," according to the World Health Organization.

As biodiversity disappears, humans risk losing access to undiscovered treatments.

To return to the "nature as a team" metaphor, if a player disappears, sometimes a species with similar functions in the ecosystem can jump in as a substitute.

But not always, particularly if it's a "keystone species." They play a disproportionately large role in their environments, and their disappearance has significant knock-on effects.

The oyster is one such species. Wild oyster populations are facing massive declines, while 85% of their reefs have been lost worldwide. An adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons of water a day.

"No one would ever think they did anything but sit in a shell and be slimy," said Shaw. "But you lose oysters, you can lose water filtration and clean water, you can lose the habitat for many other species that we interact with or sometimes consume."

And, said Shaw, the humble oyster protects coastal communities from storm surges. If you lose it "you lose the coastal protection."

The loss of a single species can also lead to more disease. One study suggests a decline in red foxes in North America is linked to the spread of Lyme disease, a tick-borne illness that also affects humans.

Extinction is a natural process. But the planet has experienced five mass extinctions, when a large proportion of species globally disappeared over a short geological period. The rate at which species are disappearing today suggests Earth is experiencing a sixth event driven largely by human activity, according to scientists.

Both McRae and Shaw called the declines extremely worrying, but said they shouldn't provoke despair.

Though she acknowledges that the underlying global trend is moving in the wrong direction and cannot be ignored, McRae said "the statistics are there to just motivate us to action rather than to cause alarm."

And the Living Planet report also highlights conservation successes, showing that nature can recover.

The number of green sea turtles, for example, had fallen significantly, but has now bounced back. This is thanks to a massive worldwide effort to protect nesting beaches and changes to fishing practices that reduce accidental capture.

Sea otters are another example. Prized for their fur, in the 1800s they were hunted to near extinction on the US Pacific Coast. When they disappeared, it meant sea urchins had no predators, leaving them free to decimate kelp forests.

That matters because as well as providing habitat for other species, these giant underwater groves protect coastlines from storm surges and absorb carbon, making them important for climate protection.

Following a ban on commercial hunting, sea otters have also rebounded, helping kelp forests recover.

"You can see that ripple effect that really had an obvious and a measurable impact. But critically, it can be reversed," said McRae, adding that biodiversity loss and climate change are intertwined and exacerbate each other but have joint solutions.

"We're learning how to turn things around at very small scales," said Shaw. "Now the real challenge is to turn things around at very big scale so we can see it in the global indicators."

Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

After five years, brothers Franz and Moritz Wagner are no longer playing together for the Orlando Magic. In the new NBA season, their separation will likely mean the new "German" team is the Charlotte Hornets.

In 2021, when Franz Wagner joined the Orlando Magic to start his NBA career, his older brother Moritz was already on the team. Franz, who is four years younger, was selected eighth overall in the draft by the team who had signed Moritz a few months earlier.

It's not just their joint appearances in the NBA that have since turned the "Wagners" into something of a brand in basketball. Just last year, the NBA had a regular-season game in Berlin, partly because of the Wagner brothers.

Moritz is a successful podcast host and often has his brother on the show. Both have their own Instagram accounts, each with around 300,000 followers. In 2024, the German public broadcaster ZDF aired a four-part TV documentary about the brothers, chronicling their basketball history and close relationship.

In recent years, they've also celebrated success with the German national team. In 2023, they became world champions together and were then at the Olympic Games in Paris a year later. This summer, their time together came to an end. After Moritz wasn't offered a new contract in Orlando, he accepted an offer from the Brooklyn Nets. He now lives in New York with his girlfriend, Maggie McQuade, who works in marketing for the NBA.

"I've already spent a lot of time in New York. It's kind of like my home in America," Wagner said on his podcast, adding that it reminds him of where he's from. "As far as the US goes, Brooklyn is actually very close to Berlin."

The call from the Nets proved a stroke of luck. "When we hung up the phone and both agreed, we both cried. Because you kind of realize that this move feels right somehow," Wagner said. "We're just staying home."

"We want to play tough, play together, play it fast and enjoy playing together," Wagner said during a preseason interview. "We got a little bit more space for our guys, to get in the paint and make plays, for themselves and for others."

The party rather wants to downsize the EU's powers and budget.

Instead of direct European Parliament elections, the AfD would like to see delegates appointed by national parliaments. The AfD would also limit the scope of the European Commission to purely administrative tasks and supplant the European Court of Justice with an arbitration tribunal.

The AfD would threaten to slash Germany's contributions to the EU budget.

The AfD hopes that it can implement its EU policies in 2029, when the next German and European elections will take place.
