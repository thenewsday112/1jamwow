Elon Musk says neoclouds are cooked, and SpaceX won't be buying them

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The SpaceX CEO took a hard stance on the future of neoclouds — cloud companies like CoreWeave and Nebius, that specialize in providing computing power for AI — in a Sunday X post.

He wrote that debt markets are reluctant to finance any company that relies almost entirely on AI data centers for revenue. He said SpaceX has secured $40 billion in debt to buy GPUs, but unlike neoclouds, his company earns revenue from its Starlink and rocket-launch activities.

"That means the 200+ neoclouds are going to see guppy-level death and consolidation," he wrote. "Some will make it through, but not many."

Musk's post was in reply to one by venture capitalist Chamath Palihapitiya, who wrote on X that neoclouds are "disorganized and poorly capitalized."

Replying to another X thread speculating if he was trying to drive down the prices of neocloud stocks and scoop them up for cheap, Musk replied, "SpaceX is not buying any neoclouds. Pointless."

This comes as the stocks of neocloud companies have taken hits recently amid fears of high debt levels and a lack of other revenue streams. CoreWeave's stock has dropped about 37% in the past month, while Nebius' has dropped 30% in the same time.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, however, has maintained that demand for AI cloud infrastructure is "off the charts." Nvidia is a major backer of neocloud companies like CoreWeave, Nebius, Iren, Lambda, and others.

Another point that Musk added, in a separate X reply on Friday, was that the industry would see a lot of "stranded compute in partially complete data centers."

By "stranded," Musk was referring to compute that is ready to use but can't be deployed because data centers aren't yet equipped to turn it on.

As Business Insider's Eugene Kim wrote last week, Amazon Web Services is expected to receive $5 billion worth of Nvidia GB300 racks, but the company does not have enough power in its data centers to power them. AWS will be storing the units in warehouses until its centers are ready.

Data centers, too, are running into some challenges. A recent Bernstein Research report projected that only about 35% of planned data center capacity in the US will be built.

Hyperscalers like Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft, which have experience building and operating data centers, will likely successfully complete construction of their planned centers, the report predicted.

But Bernstein Research said newcomers, like celebrity investor Kevin O'Leary whose company has pledged to build 15 gigawatts of data center capacity, were called "bragawatts," and their projects are unlikely to be realized.

It's not just the neoclouds facing a troubled stretch amid an AI reckoning; neolabs are, too. Neolabs are small AI model development startups, like ex-OpenAI CTO Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab and OpenAI cofounder Ilya Sutskever's Safe Superintelligence. These labs are heavily funded and face strong competition from frontier AI behemoths like Anthropic and OpenAI.

Several VCs told Business Insider's Ben Bergman recently that neolabs were overhyped and investing in them was a contentious topic.

Lula wins 4th term as Brazil's president, but Bolsonaro rejects results

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Updated on: October 26, 2026 / 1:36 AM EDT / AP

Brazil's President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a vocal critic of President Trump, was reelected Sunday in a result that provides relief to critics of Mr. Trump and keeps some distance between Latin America's biggest economy and Washington.

With 100% of the vote counted, Brazil's electoral court announced that the soon to be 81-year-old Lula had a lead of nearly 4.4 million votes over opponent Flávio Bolsonaro, giving him 51.8% of the total vote. Bolsonaro quickly refused to concede defeat and asked for the election results to be annulled after an evening of chaos at some polling places that followed a last-minute decision to extend voting by two hours. That decision by a Supreme Court justice may trigger legal action by Bolsonaro.

Bolsonaro is the son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, whom Lula defeated to take the presidency in 2022.

Lula's triumph bucks a trend in Latin America, where increasing numbers of right-of-center presidential candidates allied with Mr. Trump have been winning, further extending the United States' influence in the region.

Lula's victory leaves Mexico and Brazil, Latin America's two most populous nations, with left-of-center leaders.  It also means the nation with Latin America's largest economy, with a GDP of $2.6 trillion per year, will likely continue defying Mr. Trump's demands, despite the pressure of increased tariffs he imposed.

The announcement kicked off a celebration of supporters in and out of the Sao Paulo hotel where Lula watched the count with first lady Rosângela da Silva, allies, and friends.

Lula's victory, if confirmed by electoral authorities, will give him his non-consecutive fourth term as president. The next term begins on Jan. 5, 2027.

It is the first time since Brazil's military dictatorship ended in 1985 that the second-highest vote-getter in the first-round elections won the presidency. Bolsonaro had 47% of the vote in the first round to Lula's 45%, a difference of 2.2 million votes. However, more than 33 million voters out of nearly 160 million did not turn out in the first round.

A video published by Lula's campaign shows the veteran leader celebrating with first lady Rosângela da Silva and allies after his runoff victory was confirmed by Brazil's electoral court. Wearing star-shaped red glasses, he kissed his wife. Hundreds of his supporters began gathering on São Paulo's main artery to celebrate the victory.

In addressing supporters, Lula expressed his gratitude to the Brazilian people, and pledged to respect all Brazilians, regardless of gender or religion. He also attacked Bolsonaro."

"This was a defeat for denialism and lies. I must confess that every time I attended a debate, I felt embarrassed," he said.

He also doubled down on his commitment to Brazil's sovereignty.

"Either the homeland remains free, or we die for Brazil," he said. "This country will not accept being anyone's backyard."

In Rio de Janiero, cars beeped their horns and a woman dressed in red — the color of Lula supporters — shouted "You lost! Cry harder!" to four men in yellow and green sipping beers, Bolsonaro's signature colors. A huge roar echoed in the street as the result was confirmed.

Rodrigo Valentim, a 51-year-old businessman and supporter of Bolsonaro, said that another Lula government would harm the economy. He pointed to financial markets' positive reaction to the result of the first round, when Bolsonaro bested Lula.

"I don't understand how people can vote for Lula," he said. "The country is collapsing."

Sunday's victory will likely be the last of the veteran leader's political career. Lula began his political career in the 1970s as a union leader. He then helped found the Workers' Party, which he represented as a presidential candidate three times until he finally won it in 2002. He was reelected four years later and was succeeded by one of his cabinet ministers, Dilma Rousseff, in 2010. She was reelected in 2014.

Rousseff was impeached for accounting maneuvering in 2016 amid a sprawling corruption probe, which also ensnared Lula. He tried to run for president again in 2018 but was jailed in March after being convicted.

Lula was released from prison after 580 days and saw Brazil's Supreme Court annul his sentence after police found messages that showed the judge who convicted him was biased. He quickly became one of former President Jair Bolsonaro's biggest critics and narrowly beat him in 2022.

Lula admitted in 2024, two years into the job, that his administration had done little to earn the support of his voters.

Since he lost the first round, Lula reorganized his campaign, appealed to young voters, put millions of Brazilians on the streets to defend democratic values and his legacy, raised fears of another Bolsonaro administration and boosted several government initiatives.

Lula also defended Brazil's sovereignty and insisted that a Bolsonaro presidency would allow the U.S. to exert control over Brazil.

He joins Mr. Trump and Nicaragua's president, Daniel Ortega — all age 80 — as the oldest presidents in the Americas.

The electoral court's announcement ended an evening of confusion at polling places after Brazilian Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin ruled three minutes before the scheduled end of voting to keep polls open for two more hours, following requests by both candidates. Lula's request came after issues with buses that provide transportation to polls in Sao Paulo and Salvador, two cities that tend to favor the incumbent. Bolsonaro's request was due to heavy rains in Brazil's south, where he was favored.

In Brasilia, volunteers who oversaw the vote count at a school debated whether they needed to stay after the announcement to extend voting, but most left 15 to 20 minutes after polls closed and they had given count information to electoral authorities. At a polling station in Rio de Janeiro, the voting machines had already been switched off minutes after polls were set to close and couldn't be turned back on.

The decision delayed tallying the votes by two hours. It was unclear how many polling stations stayed open after the scheduled closing time.

Bolsonaro ally Nikolas Ferreira noted that Zanin, who made the decision, is Lula's former lawyer.

Lula, who first governed Brazil between 2003 and 2010, faced a tough reelection battle because of concerns about the cost of living and security. Bolsonaro made a tough-on-crime approach the cornerstone of his campaign and pledged to bring down food costs.

Both had been hit by corruption suspicions involving family members and allies, with Bolsonaro implicated in a sprawling scandal rocking Brazil. Lula previously spent more than a year in prison on corruption convictions that were eventually annulled.

Bolsonaro's admission that he accepted about $12 million from disgraced former banker Daniel Vorcaro to produce a film on his father's life has cast a shadow over his campaign. He is under investigation for corruption, money laundering and tax evasion in connection with the case.

As Bolsonaro voted at a school in Rio de Janeiro, his supporters shouted "Lula, thief, your place is in prison" while Lula's supporters chanted in his support.

"The time of real change has arrived," Bolsonaro said. "Brazil can't go on for another four years on its current trajectory."

In the lead-up to the vote, Lula frequently portrayed Bolsonaro, whose father is under house arrest for attempting a coup, as a threat to democracy. Bolsonaro has promised to seek an amnesty for his father if elected.

Lula, trying to lure undecided voters, has invested in costly incentives. His government endorsed a debt relief program for small businesses and promised free public transportation on weekends. A major win then came when the Senate backed a popular proposal to end the six-day workweek.

Lula thanked his voters for an outpouring of support while casting his ballot in the city of Sao Bernardo do Campo, where he began his political career as a union leader in the 1960s. "It is a lot of generosity, a lot of care, a lot of love. I will be forever grateful," Lula said.

Lula voters turned out Sunday driven by his history of supporting marginalized people and working to lift Brazilians out of poverty.

"Lula is a man of the people and he knows what it's like to be poor," Luzia da Souza, who runs a bar in Rio de Janeiro, said. "I find it impossible not to vote for him."

Anderson Falcão, a 43-year-old journalist in Brasilia who sported a red shirt in support of Lula, said he worried about Bolsonaro's long-term political vision, which he called "exclusionary."

"It's a project that harms the less fortunate, harms the worker, women, the forest, the Amazon," he said.

Bolsonaro voters, many of whom wore yellow and green Brazil jerseys in support of the senator, said they wanted change.

"Things are really expensive and taxes are too high," Fabianne Borges Santoro, a 45-year-old physiotherapist in Rio de Janeiro, said. She added that she once voted for Lula's party but now associates it with corruption.

In Bahia state, a stronghold for Lula, whose voters tend to live in areas that depend more heavily on buses, at least 75 public buses were prevented from leaving municipal garages during the presidential runoff vote, according to a state electoral judge. He ordered the arrest of the mobility secretary of the capital, Salvador, Pablo Souza, in relation to the case.

Mayor Bruno Reis, a Bolsonaro ally, blamed trade unionists for illegally sticking Workers' Party materials onto transit vehicles for the measure.

President of Brazil's Superior Electoral Court, Kassio Nunes Marques, confirmed that electoral authorities received a report on problems with buses in Bahia that is being analyzed. He did not confirm if voting would be extended, which Lula requested in Salvador and Sao Paulo, where issues with public transportation were also reported in the city with a mayor allied with Bolsonaro.

"We will not be robbed by mayors, governors or businessmen," Lula told the press. "These people need to respect democracy."

Bolsonaro did not immediately comment on the issue.

International observers earlier in the day said that voting was occurring peacefully and with transparency and did not immediately comment on the incident.

Lula criticized the U.S. president before Brazil's election, speaking about the risks of U.S. intervention in Brazilian elections after the Trump administration imposed high tariffs against Brazilian exports and classified drug-trafficking groups as foreign terrorist organizations. Experts say the moves may have been intended to make Lula look soft on crime.

Bolsonaro has touted his proximity to the White House and insisted that Brazil needs to strike better deals with the Trump administration, including in the rare minerals economy. Brazil estimates that its deposits hold up to 21 million tons, second only to China. He denies he's being helped by the U.S. government.

Mr. Trump said Sunday that he was watching the election but declined to endorse a candidate.

Un sondage de la télévision publique Kan attribue 50 sièges au bloc de Netanyahou et 52 à celui de ses adversaires, loin des 61 nécessaires pour contrôler les 120 sièges du Parlement. Netanyahou, au pouvoir depuis 2022 mais déjà Premier ministre de 1996 à 1999 et de 2009 à 2021, espère bien conserver le pouvoir grâce à son alliance avec les partis ultraorthodoxes et l’extrême droite nationaliste et religieuse d’Itamar Ben Gvir et de Bezalel Smotrich. Ancien chef d’état-major des armées de 2015 à 2019, Gadi Eisenkot, à la tête de Yashar, fédère une opposition hétérogène. Le gouvernement s’y est jusqu’ici opposé ; les principales figures de l’opposition ont promis de l’instaurer en cas de victoire. Gadi Eisenkot, accueilli chaleureusement par les manifestants, accuse le Premier ministre d’avoir failli dans la gestion de l’attaque et de la guerre à Gaza.

Le quotidien israélien Haaretz, a publié le 8 septembre une enquête affirmant que Benyamin Netanyahou avait été personnellement averti d’une opération majeure du Hamas avant les attaques du 7 octobre 2023 sans en tenir compte ensuite. Pour Netanyahou, perdre sa majorité ne signifierait donc pas nécessairement perdre le pouvoir. et pour Eisenkot, arriver en tête ne garantirait pas l’alternance. Au lendemain du vote, la bataille des coalitions pourrait s’avérer aussi décisive que celle des urnes.

l'essentiel Face à la militarisation du Grand Nord, à l'ouverture de nouvelles routes maritimes et à l'accélération du réchauffement climatique, Bruxelles veut renforcer sa présence dans l'Arctique. Une stratégie qui conjugue sécurité, ambitions économiques et protection des populations locales.

L’Union européenne vient de présenter à Strasbourg sa stratégie pour l’Arctique au moment même où les États-Unis, la Russie et la Chine en font un nouveau terrain de confrontation économique et de stratégie militaire. La Russie, qui possède la plus longue frontière arctique, y déploie toujours une forte présence militaire, avec des bases et des sous-marins comme à Mourmansk. Elle revendique également une large part du plateau continental arctique, qui s’étend jusqu’au pôle Nord et a aussi renforcé sa flotte de brise-glaces nucléaires…

L’été dernier, la compagnie chinoise Sea Legend a lancé une liaison commerciale maritime hebdomadaire entre la Chine et l’Europe qui doit emprunter la Route maritime du nord. Cette « Route de la soie arctique », au trajet beaucoup plus court, inquiète les défenseurs de l’environnement quant à l’impact d’un trafic commercial accru sur la fonte des glaces.

Enfin, que dire des velléités de Donald Trump, qu’il s’agisse de sa tentative d’annexion du Groenland – stoppée pour l’heure grâce à un accord de sécurité – ou des pressions exercées sur l’Islande. Face à cette nouvelle donne, l’UE entend marquer sa présence.

Pour Bruxelles, l’urgence est stratégique. Le Danemark, la Finlande et la Suède sont des États membres arctiques et plus d’un demi-million de citoyens européens vivent dans la région. « Il était temps », a reconnu la présidente de la Commission européenne Ursula von der Leyen en dévoilant cette nouvelle feuille de route, qui actualise la politique de 2021. Elle repose sur quatre priorités : sécurité, climat, prospérité et populations locales.

La sécurité marque une inflexion. L’Union veut renforcer sa capacité d’action dans le Grand Nord, en complémentarité avec l’OTAN. Elle prévoit d’accroître les capacités européennes de brise-glaces et de développer des équipements à usage civil et militaire. La protection des câbles sous-marins devient centrale. Un pôle dédié doit faciliter leur surveillance et leur réparation. Une plateforme de sûreté maritime permettra de partager les informations, puis de suivre la flotte fantôme, la pêche illégale et les pollutions.

Bruxelles veut aussi prendre sa place dans les nouveaux circuits commerciaux. Ports, chemins de fer, terminaux énergétiques, satellites et réseaux numériques doivent être reliés aux routes arctiques.

Le projet ferroviaire Rail Nordica figure parmi les chantiers cités. L’UE soutient un câble panarctique reliant le Groenland à l’Europe et des communications sécurisées grâce à IRIS2 Arctic Connectivity. Les matières premières critiques et les énergies renouvelables constituent d’autres perspectives de développement.

Mais cette ambition se heurte à l’urgence climatique. L’Arctique se réchauffe près de quatre fois plus vite que la moyenne mondiale. La fonte des glaces transforme les écosystèmes et la vie des habitants, tout en ouvrant de nouvelles voies maritimes. L’Union annonce un renforcement de l’observation scientifique, notamment grâce à OceanEye, pour améliorer les prévisions et l’adaptation. Elle maintient sa position : pétrole, charbon et gaz doivent rester dans le sous-sol arctique.

Vient enfin la place des habitants. La Commission promet d’associer les peuples autochtones, de développer Erasmus + et de créer un conseil de réflexion des jeunes au Groenland. Elle rappelle que plus d’un milliard d’euros de fonds de cohésion soutiennent le développement régional arctique pour 2021-2027. L’enjeu est aussi humain. Reste à transformer ces annonces en réalisations concrètes, sur le terrain.

Élections législatives en Israël : une proportionnelle intégrale lourde de conséquences

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Article rédigé par Philippe Rioux

l'essentiel Le 27 octobre 2026, les Israéliens sont appelés aux urnes pour renouveler les 120 sièges de la Knesset. Dans ce pays où l'ensemble du territoire constitue une seule circonscription électorale, les électeurs votent pour des listes bloquées. Un système proportionnel qui favorise la diversité politique, mais rend les coalitions presque incontournables.

En Israël, les électeurs ne choisissent pas directement leur député dans une circonscription comme en France. Mardi 27 octobre, ils voteront pour une liste de candidats, sans circonscriptions locales ni scrutin majoritaire. Le pays forme en fait une circonscription unique pour désigner les 120 membres de la Knesset, le Parlement israélien. De Tel-Aviv à Jérusalem, les mêmes listes sont ainsi soumises aux électeurs dans le pays entier.

Chaque formation présente ses candidats dans un ordre établi et l’électeur ne peut ni modifier ce classement ni exprimer de préférence pour une personnalité. Si un parti décroche dix sièges, les dix premiers noms de sa liste sont élus.

Le vote est ouvert aux citoyens israéliens âgés d’au moins 18 ans. Mais un citoyen israélien résidant à l’étranger ne peut pas voter aux législatives depuis l’ambassade ou le consulat, ni par courrier, procuration ou en ligne : il doit être physiquement présent en Israël le jour du scrutin dont le dépouillement est manuel.

Tous les suffrages ne donnent pas accès au Parlement. Pour participer à la répartition des sièges, une liste doit obtenir au moins 3,25 % des votes valides. Les voix recueillies par les formations restées sous ce seuil sont exclues du calcul. Un obstacle pour les petits partis.

Les sièges sont ensuite attribués proportionnellement aux voix des listes qualifiées. Les mandats restant à distribuer sont répartis selon la méthode Bader-Ofer, fondée sur les plus fortes moyennes. Deux listes peuvent conclure avant le vote un accord pour mettre en commun leurs voix excédentaires et accroître leurs chances de remporter un siège supplémentaire.

La conséquence est éminemment politique : la proportionnelle permet à de nombreux courants d’accéder à la Knesset, sans garantir à un parti une majorité. Après le scrutin, le président charge une personnalité de former un gouvernement. Pour gouverner durablement, il lui faut réunir des partenaires autour d’une coalition. En Israël, le résultat des urnes ouvre ainsi souvent une deuxième bataille : celle des négociations entre partis.
