Albania takes anti-riot crackdown cue from France

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Albania has imposed steep fines on people who take part in violent protests, citing a recent law imposed in France, in an attempt to quell weeks of clashes between police and protesters demanding the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama.

Under measures adopted Wednesday, remaining at a demonstration that turns violent will be a criminal offense, even for those not directly involved in clashes. Anyone who “chooses to remain in a crowd” engaged in violence or looting, or is found “concealing their identity,” faces fines of up to 500,000 leks ($6,130), over six-times the Balkan country’s average monthly salary.

Initially sparked by student anger over education costs and deteriorating facilities, the demonstrations have evolved into nationwide protests against inequality, soaring living costs, and cuts to public services.

Citing “urban anarchy” when announcing the crackdown, Rama acknowledged France’s controversial ‘casseurs-payeurs’ (rioters pay) bill, which would hold demonstrators liable for up to €10,000 ($11,160) in damages. The bill passed the French Senate on Wednesday and is now headed to the National Assembly, following weeks of protests and riots in which over 1,300 protesters were arrested in a single day.

Italy has also followed suit. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni’s government approved measures last week that criminalize “violent participation in a demonstration,” punishable by up to five years in prison and fines of up to €20,000.

In Albania, a “special electronic register” will track suspected offenders, while police gain powers to restrict gatherings near schools, hospitals, and government buildings over potential violence. The measures – which also provide compensation for businesses damaged in the unrest – are already in place and require parliamentary approval within 45 days to remain in force.

Anti-government protests in the country began peacefully in May over plans for a luxury resort in the Zvernec nature reserve, a project linked to US President Donald Trump’s son-in-law, Jared Kushner.

By mid-October, the protests escalated into daily clashes with police amid growing anger over corruption, economic mismanagement, unemployment, and failing public services. Demands for Rama’s resignation took center stage, but the prime minister, who has been in power since 2013 and was elected to a fourth term last year, has refused to budge.

The unrest has caused over €2 million in damage, according to official estimates, as rioters torched ministries and attacked police stations, public buildings, and businesses. Clashes flared up again over the weekend, with protesters chanting “Revolution” and “Rama in prison” and police using tear gas, rubber bullets, and water cannons in Tirana.

Despite the protests being driven by grievances over domestic matters, Rama blamed the violence on a “hybrid war” by foreign actors, specifically Russia and Iran.

Russia has dismissed the accusations. Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova suggested last week that Rama should look closer to home. “These are citizens of your own country who took to the streets to protest. Don’t shift your responsibility onto others. Deal with your own domestic problems.”

Estonia Plans 150 Bunkers Along Border With Russia

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Estonia plans to construct 150 bunkers along its northeastern border with Russia’s Leningrad region as part of a broader Baltic defense initiative, the public broadcaster ERR reported Wednesday.

“One hundred of them will be on state land, and around 50 on municipal or private land,” Estonian Defense Minister Martin Herem reportedly told lawmakers.

Herem said the government is negotiating agreements with private landowners in the northeastern county of Ida-Viru, with options ranging from compensation to long-term leases and direct property buyouts.

The installations will form the so-called Baltic Defense Line, an agreement that Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania announced in 2024 to build a series of bunkers along their borders with Russia and Belarus to defend against potential attacks.

Earlier this month, the Financial Times reported that Estonia, a nation of 1.3 million people, had pivoted toward an “active defense” strategy after studying Russia’s military tactics in Ukraine.

Under the updated doctrine, Estonia has been relocating primary combat units to towns along the border with the Leningrad region. In Narva, construction is underway for facilities that can house up to 1,000 soldiers during a crisis situation.

Kremlin officials have downplayed the Estonian troop movements near the border while asserting that Russian intelligence maintains full surveillance over NATO deployments along the frontier.

Quebec plans to ban religious symbols in schools, Education Minister says

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Parti Québécois Leader Paul St-Pierre Plamondon and current Education Minister Joel Arseneau, right, at the legislature in Quebec City in April, 2023.Jacques Boissinot/The Canadian Press

The president of the National Council of Canadian Muslims is accusing the Quebec government of attempting to take away the rights of children by expanding secularism laws.

“We’re talking about going after the rights of children to have a spirituality and go to school,” said Stephen Brown in an interview.

Quebec’s new education minister, Joël Arseneau, told journalists on Wednesday that he plans to ban schoolchildren from wearing religious symbols. He added that doing so is “non-negotiable.”

The Parti Québécois had promised the ban during the election campaign that resulted in a PQ minority government. In its platform, the party wrote that secularism “embodies the desire of the vast majority of Quebeckers to live in a society where religion is practised in the private sphere.”

But Brown said the government is already breaking its promise to be the “the government of all Quebeckers.”

“What we’re seeing now is anything but that,” said Brown. “It’s ridiculous and a missed opportunity.”

Brown said the government had pledged to reach out to communities and find balanced policy solutions to difficult questions like secularism. He believes that believes preventing children from wearing religious symbols is “blatant discrimination against religious minorities.”

He said the rules would mainly apply to those who wish to wear the kippa, hijab, crucifix and turban.

“We should not live in a country where politicians are able to literally trade away the rights of Canadian citizens in an election or political game,” said Brown. “The Charter rights of Canadian citizens have to be worth more than the paper they’re written on.”

Québec solidaire’s education and secularism critic, Roxane Milot, said the leftist party will vote against the proposed ban.

“Leave the kids alone! The priorities for the education system are clear: address the staff shortage, repair our schools, put an end to the three-tiered school system and reduce the dropout rate,” she said in a news release.

In the same release, Québec solidaire said it is concerning to see the new administration get bogged down by “identity-driven one-upmanship.”

It is unclear when the PQ government intends to table a bill to this effect, though the legislature is scheduled to resume sitting on Nov. 17.

The Quebec government has been strongly criticized for its law banning employees of the public sector, including teachers, from wearing religious symbols.

A legal challenge of that law, known as Bill 21, has made it to the Supreme Court of Canada. Lawyers argued it raised serious concerns involving several Charter rights, including freedom of expression, conscience and religion.

Brown said he is prepared to mount a legal challenge if the PQ’s proposal becomes law.

Jean-Christophe Jasmin, the Quebec director of Christian think tank Cardus, also opposes expanding secularism laws.

In an interview, he said the government’s rationale to ban religious symbols worn by government employees was “presented in the name of freedom of conscience.” The previous Coalition Avenir Québec administration had argued that children are subject to an authority figure, and that that figure must be religiously neutral as to not impose their beliefs on their students.

“I don’t see how that logic applies to children today. Children do not represent the state, they are part of civil society,” said Jasmin. “There is no duty of neutrality on the part of children. The argument doesn’t hold up.”

Brown agreed, saying the scope of secularism laws banning religious symbols has continued to expand since the CAQ took power in 2018.

At first, the government targeted those in positions of authority. It was extended to teachers, daycare workers, all school staff and parents who volunteer in schools.

The CAQ then expanded the scope of the law to the public sphere by banning praying outside, in universities and at workplaces.

“And now of course, we’re going after the rights of children. So where does this end?” said Brown.

Frédéric Bérard, a constitutional lawyer who took part in the legal challenge against Bill 21, did not want to comment on Arseneau’s comments but said it was to be expected.

He pointed out that former premier François Legault had openly said in a 2019 radio interview with Radio-Canada that Bill 21 was a concession to “people who are a little racist and don’t want to see religious symbols anywhere in public.”

“It’s never enough,” said Bérard. “It’s a slippery slope.”
