This is what the mysterious Denisovans looked like

Rachel Fieldhouse is a reporter for Nature in Sydney, Australia.

Artist depiction of the heavy set facial features of a Denisovan.Credit: Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology/Chinese Academy of Sciences

A low, sloping forehead, a massive brow ridge that curves backwards, rectangle-like eye sockets, flat cheeks and a moderately elevated bridge of the nose. Taken together, these features are the hallmarks of the ancient humans called the Denisovans, say researchers who studied a trove of fossilized skull fragments, teeth and jaws found in northern China.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia.

Denisovans were first described in 2010, after researchers sequenced genetic material from a mysterious finger bone discovered in a cave in Siberia and found that it did not resemble that of Homo sapiens or Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis). Ancient proteins have since been used to identify remains from other parts of Asia as Denisovan, including the first near-complete Denisovan skull found near Harbin, which helped to put a face on the ancient humans.

Researchers report in Nature1 today that they have an even better idea of what Denisovans looked like after analysing the physical features of remains that belong to a collection of 20 fossils found in Xujiayao.

The Xujiayao remains are at least 130,000 years old and were found in the 1970s, alongside animal remains and tools. At the time, it was unclear what type of human these fossils belonged to, and they were classified as a species called Homo juluensis. By analysing ancient proteins from some of the fossils, Qiaomei Fu, a geneticist at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, and her team identified the remains as Denisovan.

Wasef says that ancient-protein analysis is a valuable tool to complement DNA studies, particularly because proteins can survive longer than can DNA without fully degrading. Although DNA provides more-detailed information about an individual, protein analysis can still yield useful information about who an individual is related to.

Fabrice Demeter, a palaeoanthropologist at the University of Copenhagen, says that the findings add to growing evidence that Denisovans were a widespread and potentially diverse group of humans. “The study represents a major step forward” in our understanding of what these humans could have looked like and where they lived.

Fu’s team analysed small fragments of proteins, called peptides, from two Xujiayao bones and compared them with peptides found in humans and Neanderthals. They found a single amino-acid change that was present in multiple peptides from the Xujiayao bones and other East Asian fossils and is a known genetic marker for identifying Denisovans. The amino-acid change was not seen in human or Neanderthal samples.

The Bursar shook Cerise’s hand with firm confidence. “We don’t expect you to be familiar with procedures,” he said. “Orientation for our new parents is standard. Don’t be embarrassed to ask questions.”

“I suppose when Lorelei is my age the parents will take it for granted.” Cerise smiled in self-deprecation.

The Bursar nodded. “Of course, we expect our system to progress a great deal more by then. Now, your daughter Lorelei — she’s five?”

“Five in June,” Cerise agreed. “A normal, happy little girl, we’d like to think.”

“Our programme will help keep her that way.” The Bursar gestured for Cerise to follow him. The halls were bright and spacious, decorated with printouts of student artwork.

“Lorelei will have her own cubicle, kindergarten through graduation,” said the Bursar, gesturing at one of the dim nooks off the bright hallway. “One advantage of our programme is decreased contagion — although that’s not its main appeal.”

Cerise peered in at the tiny, still form. She felt like a voyeur into this strange industrial transformation of sleep. She forced a laugh. “A far cry from showing me the gym and the art room, isn’t it?”

The Bursar pursed his lips. “Our students do experience those things. In some ways the virtual environment is superior — you and I didn’t see a new gymnasium remodel more than once, if ever. Lorelei will never experience anything but. No matter how the state of the art changes, our experience changes with it.”

They finished the tour in silence — not much to see except for the nurse’s office, which didn’t look much like the nurses’ offices of Cerise’s childhood except for its antiseptic gleam. Satisfied, the Bursar escorted her back to his office.

“We used to think that children just had to take whatever life threw at them,” he beamed, relaxing into his leather chair and leaving Cerise to perch on a wooden one. “What a terrible idea, raising children on random trauma! Then we reversed course and over-restricted them until their prefrontal cortexes developed.” He shook his head. “Lunacy. Learning requires error.”

“Of course,” said Cerise stiffly, one of that protected cohort herself.

But the Bursar was giving his favourite speech and could not be distracted. “Our calibrated experience will allow Lorelei to only experience the proper amount of error, conflict and pain. We can’t do what some of the upper-end models do, but at your budget … I think you’ll be pleased with her educational experience.”

“What about external … problems? Obviously we try not to have any,” Cerise hurried to explain. “But what if Lorelei has a grandparent with cancer, or something traumatic like that?”

“It’s crucial for you to report to us regularly. Even small trauma — she twists her ankle playing at the park — is used to customize her programme. We’d substitute a nurturing experience in for a challenge there. We’ll assess her reactions constantly, fitting her challenges to her means of addressing them, but our weekly report from parents is crucial. Just think what would happen if we were giving her scenarios like fighting with a friend at recess on top of her parents getting a divorce! Disaster.”

“We’re not getting a divorce,” Cerise blurted defensively, and then made herself add, “I know, you just meant … for example. But to take your example, can she really have fights with friends at recess? Do you connect the children’s simulations to each other?”

“Absolutely not!” snapped the Bursar. “Children are chaotic! You might as well send her to traditional school if that was all you cared for her brain development.”

“But … doesn’t that mean she won’t share childhood memories with anybody?” Cerise thought about the time Lucy Meisner threw up on a field trip to the zoo, the time she and Tony Azar got in trouble for hiding at the end of recess and how it made them laugh decades later.

“Many adults don’t remain close with their classmates from traditional schooling, either,” said the Bursar stiffly. “We feel the trade-off is well worth it.”

“I understand.” Cerise sighed. “So I expect you’ll have the fee structure to go over.”

His face closed disapprovingly. “I will direct you to that department on your way out.”

The region called the insula is buried deep beneath the brain’s wrinkled surface (artist’s illustration).Credit: Alexander Leemans/UMC Utrecht/Science Photo Library

The region called the insula is buried deep beneath the brain’s wrinkled surface (artist’s illustration).Credit: Alexander Leemans/UMC Utrecht/Science Photo Library

Scientists have mapped one of the most mysterious parts of the human brain in unprecedented detail1. Their results show how this region, the insular cortex or insula, is organized to support the brain’s processing of bodily sensations such as pain. The mapping also details a pathway that allows the cortex — the seat of higher-order cognition — to work with the insula to affect bodily perceptions.

One region of the insula is responsible for the feeling of pain in certain parts of the body. Another region generates sensations, such as nausea, felt by the internal organs. A third region, at the front of the insula, ‘talks’ to other brain areas involved in cognition and emotion, suggesting that it influences not only sensations, but also how the brain interprets them.

Contrary to previous thinking, the insula is “not just reading the body. It is partly influencing the story about how the body should be felt,” says study co-author Josef Parvizi, a physician-scientist at Stanford University in California.

The study is “quite a feat”, says Lucina Uddin, a cognitive neuroscientist at the University of California, Los Angeles. It’s also one of the most comprehensive studies on the insula and how it produces bodily sensations, she adds.

The findings were published today in Nature.

The insula has been notoriously difficult to study in detail because it’s located deep in the brain. Previous studies have suggested that this hard-to-reach region is involved in receiving signals — such as pain or pressure — from various parts of the body, then processing them into sensations that we experience. But most research on the human insula has relied on brain-imaging techniques to capture what happens in the insula when various sensations arise, Parvizi says.

To build a more detailed picture, Parvizi and his colleagues needed to find a way to send electrical signals to various parts of the insula in people and assess the bodily sensations that occur as a result. But implanting electrodes deep in the brains of healthy people would be unethical. So, the researchers turned to 87 people who’d had electrodes implanted in their brains for medical reasons.

In experiments that spanned more than a decade, Parvizi and his colleagues sent electrical currents through the electrodes into a variety of sites in the participants’ insulas.

Stimulating the rear of the insula produced pain, heat and other sensations — including tingling and numbness — in specific areas of the participants’ bodies, such as the arm or leg. Meanwhile, participants experienced more ‘visceral’ sensations, including nausea, pressure and feelings of fullness in their chest, when the researchers sent electrical currents to the middle of the insula.

Despite a possible plateau in global greenhouse-gas emissions last year, average temperatures are set to hurtle past 2 °C of warming around the middle of the century, barring a major acceleration in emissions cuts, finds the United Nations’ latest report on climate change.

“The world can no longer avoid overshooting the 1.5 °C limit, and the consequences are already devastating,” said outgoing UN secretary-general António Guterres in a press briefing announcing the report — an annual accounting of progress towards tackling climate change published by the UN Environment Programme (UNEP) on 21 October. Guterres pointed to the extended heatwaves, massive wildfires and deadly floods that have disrupted much of the world in 2026. He called for faster action from governments to cut emissions by transitioning away from fossil fuels, expanding clean electricity and reversing deforestation.

Under current policies, scientists estimate warming to peak at 2.6 °C to 2.8 °C above pre-industrial levels this century — substantially exceeding the 2 °C upper limit of the 2015 Paris Agreement targets to avoid the most dangerous consequences of climate change. There is also a smaller, 10% chance of warming exceeding 3.4 °C on the current trajectory, which researchers say would be catastrophic. Even under a scenario in which countries achieve all existing emissions pledges, the report projects 2.1 °C of warming.

Although the 1.5 °C goal to limit the worst effects climate change is out of reach — 2026 is projected to be the second year in which temperatures have surpassed that level of warming — the report maintains that it might be possible to ‘overshoot’ that target, then bring temperatures back down through rapid decarbonization and a massive expansion of carbon dioxide removal.

UNEP’s assessment comes ahead of November’s COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, at which governments will take stock of efforts to mitigate and adapt to climate change. “The emissions gap is now a leadership gap. And the world is running out of time and excuses to close it,” Guterres said.

There is some good news in this year’s report. Initial estimates show that global greenhouse-gas emissions in 2025 were about the same or slightly lower than in 2024. That was mainly due to a decrease in emissions from land-use activities, particularly as a result of a quiet year for wildfires after unprecedented blazes in 2024.

That would be the first decline seen since the COVID-19 pandemic, and it could represent the start of a plateau or even a peak in emissions (see ‘Have emissions peaked?’). But researchers caution that a single year is not enough to determine if this is a lasting shift.

Source: United Nations Environment Programme

“Global emissions will have to fall several per cent before there can be any certainty that they have peaked,” says Lauri Myllyvirta, a researcher at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, a research organization in Helsinki. “Until then, one particularly hot summer or other cyclical development can cause a jump above the preceding peak, even if the structural trend is downward.”

Another encouraging trend is the rapid expansion of renewable sources of energy. Clean-energy capacity has increased around three times faster than was anticipated since 2015, and solar energy has expanded seven times faster than expected as costs of solar panelshave fallen.

Myllyvirta points out that even if this year doesn’t represent an emissions peak, the report projects a 5–8% drop by 2030 relative to 2025 levels. “The door to a much larger reduction than this over the next five years is open,” he says, as clean energy accelerates, fossil fuels become costlier, and because of the potential for China and India to overachieve their promised emissions cuts.

Sarah Wild is a freelance journalist in Canterbury, UK.

Tollulah Oni, right, running with teammates during an event at the International Conference on Sustainable Urban Mobility in Lagos, Nigeria, in 2025.Credit: LAMATA/The 2025 International Conference on Sustainable Urban Mobility Run

In 2016, public-health physician Tolullah Oni was jogging through the streets of Dakar when she saw someone doing push-ups in the middle of a busy roundabout. Cars were puffing fumes around him as the Sun baked down. As a physician, she knew that this had health implications.

More than half of the global population now lives in urban areas, but many cities are not conducive to human health and well-being. Oni, who is a researcher at University of Cambridge, UK, describes herself as an urban epidemiologist whose patients are cities. She works with colleagues around the world to diagnose and treat ‘sick’ cities with the goal of improving the health and quality of life of their inhabitants. This involves monitoring several variables, such as air quality and temperature, and their impact on the city’s residents. Those data can inform advocacy and interventions.

How I harness research to inform humanitarian relief efforts

How I harness research to inform humanitarian relief efforts

Frustrated by a lack of infrastructure on the ground in such cities, and by the struggle to connect researchers and residents, she set up the non-profit organization UrbanBetter in 2019 to support her research and advocacy. By providing training on data collection and analysis, the organization helps citizens and local researchers to diagnose their own cities and to use that evidence to lobby for change. Its research arm, the UrbanBetter Academy, has a centre in Cambridge, and its civic arm, called Cityzens Hubs, operates in 11 cities in Africa, as well as in Bogotá and Bangkok.

Oni, who was born in Nigeria, identifies as African diaspora, which she classifies as a “sixth distinct region of the African continent”. Now based in the United Kingdom, she recognizes the friction that comes with trying to treat cities that she does not live in. Oni tells Nature how she wants to create lasting virtual infrastructure through UrbanBetter that channels the diaspora’s intellectual capital back into the continent and heals sick cities.

Cities can’t tell you that they’re sick, so you must bring the symptoms to light first. What are the lungs or air quality doing? What is happening in the green spaces? How is the city metabolizing energy? What is happening to its waste? If you don’t have the data, you don’t know whether the city is sick and in a lot of our cities, we’re not even measuring those things.

It is important that the people who are identifying the symptoms are on the ground in that city, rather than me, a professor, sitting thousands of kilometres away in Cambridge. People there are breathing the air and moving through the city. We have to equip local people with the skill set to diagnose a city. The diagnostic tests can be done by sensors and their data can be mapped.

In cities, we need treatments to be grounded in lived realities. Outsiders can assess a city and propose a solution, backed by evidence, but it might not work for many reasons. We need to include residents. They can identify priority areas and advocate for change. For example, in Nairobi, a group of Cityzens collected air-pollution data and convened their own community assembly in 2025 to identify potential solutions, such as cleaner public transport, adding trees and green spaces and reducing open burning of rubbish. These points fed directly into the government’s Nairobi City County Air Quality Action Plan.

Developing treatments for a city is a collective effort and accountability is spoon-feeding that medicine in the form of small, incremental changes so that the city can get better.

I set up UrbanBetter in 2019 as an experimental platform that would situate research differently by combining it with action and advocacy. We started with a US$300,000 unrestricted grant from the funding organization Unorthodox Philanthropy, which is sponsored by the Lampert Byrd Foundation based in San Francisco, California.

Each of UrbanBetter’s hubs has different strengths — Lagos works on physical activity, air pollution and heat, whereas Bogotá has done work on transport, cycling and car-free days. This means that when another city wants to work in a new area, they don’t have to reinvent the wheel.

Ahead of the 2025 Lagos City Marathon, the local Cityzens Hub partnered with World Athletics’ project Running for Clean Air. The marathon takes place in February, which is among the hottest times of year in Lagos, and the city has high levels of air pollution.

Throughout 2025, a group of residents ran the marathon route each month, starting in the January before the marathon. They wore air-quality sensors and collected geotagged data with the UrbanBetter app. Some runners found that there was a 60% reduction in air pollution on the actual marathon day because cars were barred from the roads. These findings have informed a shift in transportation-policy implementation in the city, which is now targeting net-zero emissions by 2050. Government initiatives include increased cycling and walking, an overhaul of public transport and increased air-quality monitoring.

The Cityzens Hub then identified heat as another major issue for city dwellers, so they reached out to the UrbanBetter Academy for help analysing complex data. In 2025, researchers from UrbanBetter, local partner institutions and the Lagos Transport Authority received a £2-million [roughly US$2.6-million] grant from the biomedical funding charity Wellcome to investigate heat exposure and health risks in Lagos in a project called Hotsteppers. The ability to create protein scaffolds that impart enantioselectivity to simple, readily available metal catalysts could redefine the landscape of asymmetric catalysis. Artificial metalloenzymes (ArMs), hybrid catalysts that combine a synthetic metal cofactor with a protein scaffold, offer a potential means to achieve this goal, but scaffold discovery has remained constrained by trial-and-error approaches and the functional limits of extant proteins.1–5 This challenge is acute for metal polypyridine photocatalysts, which are widely used in visible-light chemistry but have not enabled highly enantioselective photocatalysis within protein scaffolds. In this study, we show that generative protein design6–8 can create de novo scaffolds that selectively bind these photocatalysts and that directed evolution converts the resulting artificial metalloenzymes into efficient, highly enantioselective photocatalysts9. The evolved proteins support diverse light-driven reactions, high catalyst turnover and catalyst recycling, and can function with commercially available racemic metal complexes. Spectroscopic studies show that the protein environment extends the photocatalyst excited-state lifetime and organizes substrate near the cofactor, altering energy transfer and controlling stereoselectivity. These findings establish de novo protein design as a route to functional scaffolds for synthetic metal complexes that lack natural protein partners and provide a strategy for combining protein design and evolution to control non-natural catalytic chemistry.

For more than two millennia, Archimedean screws have been used for water pumping and, more recently, as turbines in hydroelectric power stations[1]. Their helical-blade structure, essential to their function, is also found in chiral molecules and nanostructures, where injected electric current has been proposed to generate torque-mediated rotation[2,3]. Here, we present a torque-driven molecular machine with a rotor actuated by electric current and conductance enhanced by its rotation. Suspending a helicene molecule between two electrode tips, we show that an applied voltage induces cyclic molecular motion with 100% directional preference that can be inverted by the voltage’s polarity. Above a voltage threshold, the current through the rotating molecule is significantly enhanced, and the enhancement onset is tuned by stretching or compressing the helical molecule. Such effects were absent in previous helical molecular junctions, likely due to structural and operational differences[4–6]. Calculations show that molecular rotation by current-induced forces explains both the observed cyclic motion and current enhancement. This mechanism predicts a transition from stepwise to continuous rotation at a threshold voltage, yielding a current increase as rotation allows adopting highly conducting configurations not accessible at rest. Our work introduces a prototype for a new class of torque-driven molecular machines with perfect rotor unidirectionality and significant current modulation.

Terence Tao is a mathematician at the University of California, Los Angeles.

Advanced artificial-intelligence models are transforming mathematics at an astonishing speed. In the past few months, AI-aided proofs — logical arguments that demonstrate why a mathematical result is correct — have been released for hundreds of long-standing maths problems.

Mathematicians might need several months to fully understand the underlying arguments and implications of these automatically derived solutions. As a mathematician who uses AI tools for my own research, I can see that the field has fundamentally transformed. We now urgently need to establish a ‘Maths 2.0’ era, in which ‘proof scarcity’ is no longer a defining feature of the field.

How AI is reshaping discovery in maths and physics

How AI is reshaping discovery in maths and physics

The challenges associated with this new way of doing maths are already evident. Many AI-generated results are not described clearly enough to meet quality standards for publication. Some researchers using AI tools simply move on to the next problem once they have a minimally presentable proof, leaving results that can be difficult for other mathematicians to assess. And it still remains unclear how extensively AI models are drawing on the previous work of mathematicians without providing proper attribution.

The centuries-old practice of openly sharing maths problems is under serious threat because mathematicians are increasingly fearful that disclosing their research directions will lead to their work being ‘scooped’ by a rival who is equipped with a frontier AI model. Graduate students and junior mathematicians used to develop their skills and gain early recognition from their peers by solving such problems. On 6 October, technology firm OpenAI dumped hundreds of solutions to such problems online in one go. It disrupted countless research programmes overnight.

Perhaps the most counter-intuitive consequence is that human understanding of mathematics is falling behind, because AI systems generate results faster than mathematicians can digest and absorb them. Confined to pure mathematics, this ‘proof indigestion’ might be a minor concern. But AI-generated mathematical models deployed in the real world without a matching understanding of their mechanisms could have serious consequences. For example, complex AI-generated models are already used to some extent to determine who qualifies for a bank loan and to measure financial risk in markets. If the complexity of these models outpaces the capacity for humans to understand when they might malfunction, the risks of unforeseen mistakes will grow, affecting the general public.

These negative impacts are not inevitable. Used creatively, and with the right incentives, AI tools could enhance human understanding. It is still possible to change course, through a radical reassessment of the conventional incentive structure of mathematics and by introducing more-responsible practices around AI use.

The speed at which regions of the brain mature during early adolescence can forecast the emergence of mental-health conditions up to four years before symptoms develop, according to a study of nearly 12,000 young people in the United States.

By tracking the brains of adolescents over six years in nearly 30,000 scans, researchers mapped how the brain’s outer layer, the cerebral cortex, thins as children grow into teenagers. The team observed that sensory areas of the cortex thinned, or matured, first, followed by the regions responsible for complex thought.

The findings, posted on 11 October on the preprint server bioRxiv1 ahead of peer review, challenge the theory that psychiatric disorders are caused by defects in specific brain circuits. Instead, they indicate that vulnerability to mental-health symptoms could involve disruption of the brain’s developmental timeline and could be set in motion by genes that are active before birth.

The work “provides one of the clearest pictures yet of how the cortex develops from childhood into adolescence”, says Avram Holmes, a systems neuroscientist at Rutgers University in New Brunswick, New Jersey, who was not involved in the study. “It makes you think about the timing of brain development, not just the location [in the brain], when we look at psychiatric symptoms,” he says.

But he cautions that the population-level patterns found in the study — trends that become visible only when averaging data across large numbers of people — are subtle and cannot yet be used in the clinic to forecast the mental-health trajectory of an individual child.

During childhood and adolescence, the cerebral cortex thins. Neuroscientists attribute this process to the pruning of unused connections between neurons, which helps the brain to operate efficiently.

Researchers have long suspected that disruptions to cortical thinning contribute to mental illness, because psychiatric conditions such as depression are often diagnosed during late adolescence, when the thinning process is winding down. But proving that link has been difficult because brain-imaging studies have historically relied on small sample sizes or single snapshots in time.

To overcome these hurdles, study co-author Jacqueline Clauss, a child psychiatrist at the University of Iowa in Iowa City, and her colleagues turned to data from a massive project that is tracking nearly 12,000 children from around the age of nine. Participants’ brains are scanned every two years, and their parents complete surveys annually about their children’s mental-health symptoms.

When analysing the data, Clauss and her colleagues found that rather than thinning at a steady rate, brain regions change following a distinct trajectory that fits an S-shaped curve. Thinning of a region of the cortex initially accelerates, then proceeds steadily before decelerating as the region nears maturity.

A powerful gene-editing technique has been used to edit DNA directly in a person’s body — the first known instance of such a treatment.

The milestone treatment is part of a clinical trial of prime editing, a technique that can alter DNA with more precision than standard CRISPR–Cas9 methods can. In this case, prime editing was used to treat a condition called Wilson’s disease by rewriting DNA in liver cells, to erase a mutation that prevents them from eliminating excess copper.

Prime Medicine, a biotechnology company in Cambridge, Massachusetts, announced that it had treated the first person in its trial earlier this month. It expects to announce the first results from the study next year.

Bigger than CRISPR? A guide to the latest genome editors

“I never dreamed that in my lifetime I would see this applied to my rare disease,” says Rhonda Rowland, president of the Wilson Disease Association in Atlanta, Georgia. “It’s going to be a really exciting year.”

In launching the trial, Prime Medicine joins a host of companies aiming to engineer cells directly in the body, rather than using the standard method of extracting a person’s cells, editing their DNA in a laboratory and then reinjecting the altered cells. Rewriting DNA in the body, or in vivo, is appealing because manufacturing and administering the treatment might be much cheaper and faster than making a bespoke therapy for each person from their own cells.

But in vivo approaches have their own challenges, says Krishanu Saha, a biomedical engineer at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. “It depends on what types of risks you’re willing to accept,” he says.

Wilson’s disease is caused by mutations in a gene called ATP7B, which encodes a protein that transports excess copper out of the liver. If left untreated, the resulting build-up of copper can cause liver and nerve damage, as well as neurological disorders.

People with Wilson’s disease are eager for a one-and-done treatment for their condition, says Rowland, rather than the current, complicated regimen of copper-clearing molecules and other medicines. And gene editing could, in theory, provide a lasting solution by correcting the faulty ATP7B gene.

So far, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved only one gene-editing therapy, called Casgevy, which is used to treat two inherited blood disorders. Casgevy relies on CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing, which is often used to disable genes rather than to rewrite their sequences.

Another form of gene editing, called base editing, can rewrite individual DNA letters, but cannot make the particular corrections needed to fix the most common ATP7B mutations. That left prime editing, a more complex and versatile system that can insert, delete and rewrite segments of DNA. In another clinical trial, Prime Medicine used the technique to treat two people with a rare immune disorder, by rewriting DNA in each participant’s blood stem cells outside of the body and then injecting them back in.

So far, no gene-editing therapies have been approved for use in vivo. But the FDA is expected to issue a decision early next year about an in vivo CRISPR–Cas9 therapy for a condition called hereditary angioedema, which causes severe swelling.
