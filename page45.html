Massenproteste gegen Rundfunkreform in Tschechien

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Die tschechische Regierung will den öffentlich-rechtlichen Rundfunk künftig aus dem Staatshaushalt finanzieren - und Mittel für die Sender deutlich kürzen. Landesweit protestierten Zehntausende. Sie befürchten weniger Unabhängigkeit.

Dicht gedrängt stehen die Demonstrierenden am Sonntagnachmittag trotz wechselhaftem Wetter auf dem Altstädter Ring im Prager Stadtzentrum. Zehntausende sind dem Aufruf der Initiative "Eine Million Augenblicke für Demokratie" gefolgt.

Sie demonstrieren gegen die Pläne der Regierung, die Gebühr für den öffentlich-rechtlichen Rundfunk abzuschaffen und Fernsehen und Radio stattdessen direkt aus dem Staatshaushalt zu finanzieren. Das Budget der öffentlich-rechtlichen Medien würde damit um mehr als 15 Prozent gekürzt werden.

"Hände weg von den Medien" ist auf Plakaten zu lesen oder auch "Heute Geld, morgen Inhalt". Kritiker fürchten, dass durch eine Finanzierung aus dem Staatshaushalt die Unabhängigkeit der Berichterstattung gefährdet ist.

Die 18-jährige Abiturientin Sara ist mit einer Freundin zur Demo gekommen. "Wir sind hier, weil wir finden, dass die Medien unabhängig sein sollten und nicht durch das bedroht werden dürfen, was ein Politiker denkt. Und wir finden, dass die Art, wie die Regierung mit diesem wichtigen Gesetz umgeht, unangemessen ist."

Den Plan, den Rundfunk ab Januar 2027 aus dem Staatshaushalt zu finanzieren, hatte die rechtspopulistische Regierung schon im Sommer verkündet.

Am Freitagabend hatte die Koalition dann aber kurzfristig und für viele Beobachter überraschend den Gesetzesentwurf ins Parlament eingebracht und darüber in einer ungewöhnlichen Nachtsitzung abstimmen lassen, um kurz nach halb fünf am Samstagmorgen.

Schon am Freitagabend fand deshalb spontan eine Demonstration vor dem Parlament statt. In einigen anderen Städten Tschechiens wurde am Wochenende ebenfalls protestiert.

Daran, wie die Parlamentssitzung abgelaufen ist, stört sich auch Demonstrant Zdenek Skala auf dem Altstädter Ring. "Wir finden, dass über eine solche Änderung ausführlich und öffentlich debattiert werden müsste. Das wird aber nicht getan - und offensichtlich soll diese Debatte absichtlich verhindert werden."

Neben Plakaten, tschechischen Flaggen und auch EU-Flaggen sind auf dem Altstädter Ring am Sonntag auch viele Menschen mit Hüten aus Zeitungspapier zu sehen. Eine Anspielung auf die Fernsehfigur Večerníček, das tschechische Äquivalent zum Sandmännchen.

Auch Plakate mit der tschechischen Zeichentrickfigur des kleinen Maulwurfs, der weint, ragen aus der Menschenmenge. Lenka Bartakova ist mit ihrem Kind zur Demonstration gekommen. Den Kinderwagen hat sie mit bekannten Fernsehfiguren aus Plüsch dekoriert.

"Ich habe das unter dem Kommunismus erlebt, deshalb möchte ich auf keinen Fall, dass so etwas zurückkommt", sagt Lenka Bartakova. "Sie sagen zwar, es gehe nur um die Gebühren und sie würden keinen inhaltlichen Einfluss nehmen - aber das werden sie tun. Sobald die Politiker das in der Hand haben, weiß niemand, was danach passiert."

Premierminister Andrej Babiš widerspricht: Niemand habe die Absicht, in die Berichterstattung einzugreifen. Die Finanzierung des Rundfunks würde durch die Gesetzesänderung stabiler und vorhersehbarer, teilte Babiš auf X mit.

Besorgt äußerte sich hingegen der liberale Präsident Petr Pavel. Er kritisierte, ebenfalls auf X, die Art und Weise, wie das Gesetz zur Abstimmung gebracht worden ist. Diese untergrabe den "Glauben an die guten Absichten der Initiatoren", so Pavel.

Die Politikwissenschaftlerin Vladimira Dvorakova vermutet in der kurzfristig anberaumten Parlamentsabstimmung auch politisches Kalkül. Bei den Senatswahlen Anfang Oktober hatte die Opposition deutlich besser abgeschnitten als die Regierungsparteien.

"Es ist der Versuch, die Aufmerksamkeit auf etwas anderes zu lenken und einen Kulturkampf statt einer Debatte zu führen", sagt Dvorakova. "Einen anderen Grund dafür, warum es so schnell ging, gibt es eigentlich nicht."

Bislang hat die Abschaffung der Rundfunkgebühren nur die erste Hürde genommen, sie könnte aber trotzdem bereits Anfang des nächsten Jahres in Kraft treten.

Für den 3. November haben die Beschäftigten des öffentlich-rechtlichen Rundfunks zum Streik aufgerufen. Und für Mitte November ist bereits die nächste große Demonstration angekündigt.

Brazil election results 2026: Key takeaways from Lula’s historic win

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Lula wins a fourth term after a dramatic run-off marred by voting confusion and allegations of interference.

Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has pulled off a political comeback that no Brazilian presidential candidate has managed in nearly four decades.

On Sunday, the 80-year-old leftist president won a record fourth non-consecutive term, overcoming a first-round defeat to right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of jailed former President Jair Bolsonaro.

Lula won more than 51 percent of the vote to Bolsonaro’s 48 percent. Some 98.76 percent of the ballots were counted when electoral authorities declared him the winner.

Sunday’s results were far from certain, keeping Brazilians on edge until the very end. Bolsonaro and Lula had been virtually tied in the polls in the lead-up to the election.

A last-minute decision to extend voting hours sparked accusations of election interference and questions about whether the result could face legal challenges.

Already, Senator Bolsonaro has pledged to annul the vote, crediting the extended hours with sowing voter confusion.

Just three weeks ago, Lula looked like he was in trouble.

Bolsonaro had surprised many observers by beating the incumbent in the first round of voting on October 4, winning 47.03 percent of the vote to Lula’s 45.16 percent. The difference was 2.2 million votes.

But Lula spent the next three weeks trying to bring back voters who stayed home in the first round, while making a renewed pitch to those concerned about the cost of living and the future of his signature social programmes.

In the final days of the campaign, opinion polls showed the two candidates virtually tied.

Bolsonaro briefly took the lead as ballots were counted on Sunday evening. But Lula ultimately pulled ahead as results began arriving from the northeast, according to the Reuters news agency.

The northeast has long been a strong source of support for Lula and his Workers’ Party.

Bolsonaro, meanwhile, has large bases of support in the south and southeast of the country, as well as the centre-west.

For Lula, the victory means an unprecedented fourth term as president. For Bolsonaro, Sunday marks a defeat in his bid to return his family to the presidency.

No other candidate in modern Brazilian history has placed second in the first round of a presidential vote only to win in a run-off.

At age 80, Lula is also on track to beat his own record as the oldest Brazilian president to be sworn in since the country’s return to democracy.

As the presidential run-off entered its final hours on Sunday, both campaigns were calling for voting to be extended.

Lula’s campaign accused local officials of deliberately reducing public transport services in several cities, making it difficult for lower-income voters to reach polling stations.

Bolsonaro’s campaign, meanwhile, requested more time for voters in areas affected by heavy rain and flooding.

In Minas Gerais, the regional electoral court reported that 349 polling locations had lost electricity, although voting machines continued operating on backup batteries, according to local media.

Originally, voting was scheduled to end at 5pm Brasilia time (20:00 GMT).

But faced with the two campaigns’ petitions for an extension, Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin ordered polling stations to remain open for another two hours, until 7pm (22:00 GMT).

The decision, however, came after some stations had already closed and shut down their electronic voting machines.

Zanin later clarified that polling stations that had already closed would remain shut. Only those that were still open could continue accepting voters until 7pm.

The result was confusion over who could still vote, with some Brazilians turned away despite the extension.

In a brief post-election address, Bolsonaro pointed to those issues as a reason to annul the vote. He also accused Zanin of judicial malfeasance.

“He changes the rules of the game mid-game,” Bolsonaro said.

Brazil’s Federal Police are investigating alleged election-related transport disruptions in five states.

In Salvador, a court ordered the arrest of the city’s transport secretary over allegations he had directed cuts to bus services. Lula’s camp pointed to such incidents as evidence of election interference.

Bolsonaro levelled similar allegations against Zanin, pointing to his history as Lula’s personal lawyer. Lula named Zanin to the Supreme Court in 2023.

“It is impossible to recognise the results of the election,” Bolsonaro told his supporters, calling the circumstances “unacceptable”.

He is likely to pursue a court challenge in an effort to overturn his defeat. Already, some figures on the global right have signalled their support.

Donald Trump Jr, the son of United States President Donald Trump, voiced outrage over the decision to extend the voting hours.

“If a conservative government in Brazil did this, everyone in the mainstream press in America would lose their minds, but I guess they’re just choosing to be silent because it’s all for the cause,” Trump Jr posted on social media.

The Trump administration has backed the Bolsonaro family, echoing its claims that Jair Bolsonaro, the former president, was subject to a political “witch-hunt” in the courts.

Flavio Bolsonaro has called for reforming Brazil’s courts in the aftermath of his father’s legal troubles. Earlier this month, Bolsonaro said that if he were elected, he would seek changes to Brazil’s Constitution to “re-democratise” the country.

Al Jazeera correspondent Monica Yanakiew signalled that the tensions between Brazil’s right wing and its court system are likely to re-emerge after Sunday’s election results.

“The judicial system itself is very divided,” Yanakiew said, pointing to rifts within the Supreme Court. “There will need to be a lot of political negotiation.”

Higher-than-expected turnout may have helped Lula win, according to Andrei Roman, the head of polling firm AtlasIntel, who cited a drop in voter abstention as one factor behind the president’s victory.

An estimated 33.4 million Brazilians stayed home during the October 4 first round, despite voting being compulsory for most adults. That amounted to about 21 percent of the electorate, the highest abstention rate in a general election since 1998.

The question now is how much of a difference those voters made. With more than 2.2 million votes separating the candidates in the first round, even a relatively small change in turnout could have mattered in the run-off.

The final turnout figures, particularly in each candidate’s strongholds, could help explain how Lula managed to turn the race around.

Lula also made last-ditch pitches in the final weeks of his campaign, designed to energise his working-class base. Among them was a promise to pursue free public transport on weekends.

Lula’s victory comes amid a wave of right-wing electoral wins across Latin America, where Trump has sought to expand Washington’s influence.

The Bolsonaro family has long enjoyed Trump’s support. Reuters reported on Saturday that officials in Trump’s State Department had pushed for sanctions against Brazil as part of an effort to weaken Lula and return the Bolsonaro family to power.

Asked on Sunday whom he wanted to win, the US president would not name a candidate.

“I don’t want to get into that, but we are watching,” he told reporters.

Though Trump has been vocal in his support for other right-wing candidates in Latin America, he was relatively circumspect with regard to the Brazilian election, something critics have speculated was intentional.

While Bolsonaro has promised closer ties with the US, Lula made defending Brazil’s sovereignty and resisting foreign interference a central theme of his campaign.

He has often pointed to Trump’s aggressive tariff campaign and his public statements about the Jair Bolsonaro trial as evidence of foreign interference. Lula’s campaign has also clashed with US-based social media companies, prompting Trump to accuse his government of censorship.

In his victory speech, Lula described having to “overcome interference coming from the outside” in his bid for a fourth term.

“The election was a defeat for denialism,” Lula said on Sunday night. “It was a defeat for lies.”

He finished his speech by praising his “army” of supporters.

“I highly doubt that Russia, China, or the United States possess an army as victorious as the army of democracy in this country,” Lula said, thanking the voters.

The North Korean permanent representative to the UN wrote to the UNSC on Sunday regarding the “provocation the ROK [Republic of Korea] committed by groundlessly shifting the blame for the dubious ‘mine explosion incident’”, warning that violations of the country’s sovereignty could trigger self-defence.

Tensions have intensified after South Korea blamed a September 21 explosion in the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) separating the two countries on North Korean landmines. North Korea has rejected the accusations.

Pyongyang’s UN representative alleged the landmine incident was “a sinister obstructive move to brand as a ‘military invasion’”, adding that South Korea’s claims were a “farce to evade the responsibility for the ever-escalating tensions on the Korean peninsula”.

North Korea’s ongoing border fortification efforts were “a legitimate and just exercise of its sovereignty going in strict conformity with the UN Charter”, the letter states, while also criticising South Korea for violating the charter by firing warning shots at soldiers in the DMZ.

South Korea has said it fires shots to make North Korean soldiers return to the north when they cross the Military Demarcation Line (MDL) that runs through the DMZ. In recent years, North Korean personnel have been building tactical roads and fences near the MDL.

The South Korean Unification Ministry said on Monday that Pyongyang’s letter aims to “justify its border fortification work” and serve as a “countermeasure” to Seoul’s message to the UN, the Yonhap news agency reported.

Last week, South Korea’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs sent a letter to the president of the UNSC, casting blame on North Korea for the landmine blast and calling it a “clear violation of the armistice agreement”.

The United States-led UN Command also said mine placement by North Korea violated the Armistice Agreement, signed in 1953 to end hostilities in the Korean War.

Peranakan food stall Kokoyo Nyonya Delights shutting down in November after S$50,000 in losses

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“It’s quite a pity, it’s like I failed. But I gave it my best shot,” said the hawker, who opened Kokoyo to honour his Peranakan grandmother’s recipes.

Kokoyo Nyonya Delights will shut down in November 2026. (Photos: 8Days, Kokoyo Nyonya Delights)

Kokoyo Nyonya Delights, a stall selling Peranakan food including blue pea nasi lemak, ayam buah keluak and beef rendang, is closing after two years in business. Its last day at Hougang Mall’s fourth-floor food court is on Nov 2.

Co-owner Ernest Ang, 25, told 8days.sg that the business has accumulated an estimated S$50,000 (US$39,100) in losses since February this year. Sales have also fallen by about half, with business becoming particularly difficult since September.

While the Kokoyo brand is closing for good, Ang and his brother, co-owner Irwin Ang, 29, are staying put. They plan to launch a seafood porridge concept in the same space called The Porridge Stall. The siblings hope this comforting dish will help turn things around.

The brothers announced Kokoyo’s closure in a video posted on the brand’s Instagram page on Oct 21.

They explained that footfall at the food court was initially healthy when there were fewer dining options elsewhere in Hougang Mall. Following renovations and the arrival of more eateries on other floors, they found it increasingly difficult to draw customers up to the fourth floor.

“It’s been rough these past few months, foot traffic to the food court has dropped drastically compared to when we first entered,” said Ernest Ang.

With monthly rent of S$11,000, the drop in sales has been difficult to sustain.

“The rent is high, so the breakeven point is high,” Ang told 8days.sg. “There has to be a benchmark we need to hit every day, but then the footfall is not good, so it’s directly related.”

Kokoyo first opened as a standalone eatery in Serangoon Central in 2024, before relocating to Jelita Mall as a food court stall on Holland Road, then to Hougang Mall in February this year. Ernest Ang said the business had already begun incurring losses at Jelita Mall, and earlier earnings had gone towards covering those losses.

So why take another risk at Hougang Mall? Ang, who lives in Hougang, said he was initially confident about customer traffic there. He estimated that food establishments made up only about 30 per cent of the mall when Kokoyo moved in.

What he hadn’t anticipated was the extent of the mall’s refurbishment under an asset enhancement initiative. As more eateries gradually opened, competition for diners increased.

When 8days.sg first featured Ernest Ang in 2024, he was a fresh electronics engineering diploma holder who had stopped job hunting after six weeks to open his own eatery.

His grandmother contributed S$40,000 – half the initial investment – while he funded the rest with savings from part-time jobs. She also taught him her closely guarded Peranakan recipes, tasting and approving dishes before they went on the menu.

Her involvement extended beyond sharing recipes: when Kokoyo first opened, she made around 200 pieces of nonya kueh daily from home for the eatery. Ang shared that she has since stopped supplying them due to mobility issues.

Even the brand’s name was a tribute to her: Kokoyo came from the way she pronounced “coconut”, while the cartoon logo was also modelled after her.

His older brother Irwin, who has a degree in marketing, has also been involved in the business since the start. Hougang Mall is their only remaining outlet, and the brothers work at the stall daily.

For Ernest Ang, giving up the brand means letting go of a business he started to honour his grandmother’s cooking.

“It’s quite a pity. It’s like I failed, but I gave it my best shot, so that’s all that matters,” he shared.

His grandmother has taken the decision in her stride. According to Ang, she feels that if the business isn’t working, they should “stop the bleeding”.

For those hoping to visit before the closure, Kokoyo’s current menu centres on blue pea nasi lemak. Its Nasi Lemak Kosong (S$4.90) comes with a fried egg, ikan bilis, peanuts, cucumber, achar and sambal.

Other options include the Inchi Kabin Berempah Set (S$7.50), featuring spiced fried chicken, and Ayam Buah Keluak Set (S$7.50). There’s also a Fried Pork Belly Set (S$8.50) and Beef Rendang Nasi Lemak (S$11.50), featuring beef slow-cooked in a spiced coconut gravy.

À la carte dishes include Homemade Ngoh Hiang (S$5.80) and Nyonya Chap Chye (from S$4.80), while sharing sets for two start at S$20.50.

The Porridge Stall will take over the space after Kokoyo closes. Staying at the same spot is a practical decision – their existing lease runs until February 2027.

He settled on seafood porridge after visiting eateries in Malaysia in search of a new concept. He describes his version as Guangdong-style porridge, with a consistency somewhere between thick Cantonese congee and the looser Teochew style.

The base will be a broth made with chicken and pork bones. The opening date has yet to be confirmed.

Ang hopes the switch will help the brothers reach a wider customer base.

“I just want to go for a very mass market dish. I don’t want to go for something very niche like Nyonya food anymore,” he said. “I believe for many people in Singapore, porridge has a childhood connection.”

He also saw a gap in the mall’s food offerings. According to him, there are four nasi lemak sellers, including Kokoyo, but no dedicated porridge stall.

“I feel the success rate will be higher, and I think it’s worth a gamble rather than not doing anything about it,” he said.

If the new concept does well, the brothers are open to continuing the business beyond their current stall’s lease.

Would Ang consider getting a corporate job if things don’t work out?

“I will study the numbers and make a decision,” he mused. For now, he prefers to stay in F&B and may try his luck at another location.

Why stick with a notoriously punishing industry that hasn’t been kind to him?

“I feel like this shouldn’t be the end of my journey. I’m not the type of person that gives up easily,” he reasoned.

He added that building a brand is something both he and his brother enjoy.

And what do his dad, a technician, and mum, who works in HR, think of their sons’ rocky F&B ventures?

“My parents are pretty chill about it [despite our losses],” said Ang simply.
