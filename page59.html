Trump leans into Islamophobic rhetoric at Michigan rally

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

President Donald Trump embraced Islamophobic rhetoric Sunday as he rallied in Port Huron, Michigan, in support of the state’s Republican candidates, including the GOP Senate nominee, former Rep. Mike Rogers.

Trump alleged that Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic Senate nominee who is locked in a dead heat with Rogers, is a “jihadist,” calling him a “Muslim Brotherhood member” while saying Rogers is “apple pie.”

“Anyone with that record of support for jihadists would be screened and unable to get a seat on an airplane. They certainly should not give him a seat in the United States Senate,” Trump said about El-Sayed.

Throughout his comments Sunday, Trump offered no evidence for any of the claims about El-Sayed.

“He would be the only senator that will have to go through a metal detector before he’s allowed to go into the Senate. I want him to have a metal detector,” Trump said.

Trump sought to draw a comparison between the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks and El-Sayed as he introduced speaker David Beamer, whose son, Todd Beamer, was a passenger who died on the hijacked United Flight 93.

“Twenty-five years later, Michigan needs to stand up and fight just like Todd did to stop a jihadist from walking into the Capitol,” Trump said.

Beamer went on to extend Trump’s comparison between the Senate race and 9/11.

“What has changed? Well, one thing is 9/11 was a surprise attack. What’s going on right now in our country and here in Michigan? It’s not a surprise. We can see the attack coming that culminates in this battleground state on Tuesday, November the 3rd,” Beamer said as Trump stood behind him onstage.

“What’s different this time is the enemy has not come from abroad. The enemy is right here among us, local terrorists, local citizens that want to demolish our country,” he added.

Beamer, who has been featured in Rogers’ campaign ads, compared the two men and claimed Rogers is “an American” while saying El-Sayed is not, going on to call him “anti-American.”

“Abdul has a birth certificate. The birth certificate says he’s a citizen of America. You know what? A birth certificate doesn’t make you an American,” Beamer said.

“He has blood that’s red. However, he has chosen to remove any strain of the white and blue part in his life. We cannot, cannot allow this man to invade and occupy our country,” he added.

Both Rogers and El-Sayed were born in Michigan.

El-Sayed’s campaign did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Trump’s and Beamer’s remarks.

El-Sayed went viral for a heated interaction last week with a town hall attendee who asked him to condemn the organization.

“You assume ... that because I am Muslim that somehow I would have some allegiance to something that is not our Constitution,” El-Sayed told the attendee last week.

Trump has accused El-Sayed of having ties to the Muslim Brotherhood, an international Sunni Islamist organization founded in Egypt in 1928. The Trump administration has designated certain chapters of the group in Lebanon, Jordan and Egypt as terrorist organizations. El-Sayed has repeatedly denied any ties to the group, which he has denounced.

Rogers was asked Sunday about Trump’s calling El-Sayed a “Muslim Brotherhood jihadist” and a “violent terrorist” in an interview on NBC’s “Meet the Press.” Pressed about whether he agreed with Trump’s characterizations, Rogers said the election is about “me versus Abdul,” adding, “I’ve never called him a terrorist.”

Rogers has previously questioned El-Sayed about his mother, Fatten Elkomy, over her work decades ago at a charity the U.S. government later labeled a supporter of terrorist groups, leading to its closure. Elkomy was not accused of wrongdoing at the time.

Trump helped popularize a false conspiracy theory about former President Barack Obama, claiming he was not a U.S. citizen and demanding to see his birth certificate. He has since repurposed “birther” attacks against other political rivals, including former U.N. Ambassador Nikki Haley, who was born in South Carolina.

Trump on Sunday referred to Obama, who rallied in Detroit on Saturday on behalf of El-Sayed and other Michigan Democratic candidates.

“My competitor was here yesterday,” Trump, who is not running for office, said about Obama, who is also not running for office. “He didn’t have a crowd like this, I can tell you.”

Raquel Coronell Uribe is a politics reporter for NBC News.

Thousands protest in Prague against public media funding reforms

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Demonstrators allege Prime Minister Andrej Babis and his coalition’s proposed reforms pose a threat to press freedom.

Several thousand people have taken to the streets in Prague to protest against plans to change the funding system for the Czech Republic’s public media, accusing the government of stifling press freedom.

On Sunday, protesters demanded the withdrawal of legislation seeking to abolish household licence fees and replace them with state budget allocations for public radio and television.

The Czech lower house of parliament approved fast-tracking the bill in a rare overnight vote on Saturday after governing coalition lawmakers rushed to open the issue, limiting the opposition’s ability to engage in debate before the vote was held.

The bill, proposed by the governing coalition led by billionaire Prime Minister Andrej Babis, which includes two far-right parties, must still be passed by the upper house before becoming law.

The opposition has pledged to challenge the legislation in the constitutional court. The plan would cut funding for public media by about 15 percent, possibly as early as next year.

At the protest in Prague’s Old Town Square, demonstrators chanted, “We’ve had enough”, “This must not be allowed to pass”, and “4:36”, referencing the early morning hour when the draft was approved by parliament.

Babis has said the plan is not an attack on media independence but a matter of funding transparency.

Critics warn the proposed change would allow the governing coalition to take control of the media. They compare it with the actions of populist governments, such as Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orban and Slovakia’s Prime Minister Robert Fico.

Babis and his coalition, whose agenda includes reducing support for Ukraine as it fights off a Russian invasion, have a record of attacks against the media.

Public media staffers announced they would go on strike on November 3. They are backed by labour unions, university rectors, churches, international media organisations and many others.

A rally is also planned at Prague’s Letna park on November 15, two days before the anniversary of the 1989 anti-communist Velvet Revolution that ended more than 40 years of totalitarian government.

A Bizarre 170-Million-Year-Old Predator Is Rewriting What We Know About Ancient Crocodiles

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

A Moroccan crocodile relative’s teeth resemble those of prehistoric filter feeders, suggesting that different reptile groups independently evolved similar tools for catching tiny aquatic prey.

A crocodile relative that lived around 170 million years ago had a long, narrow snout lined with exceptionally thin, needle-like teeth. No known close relative has teeth quite like them. Their delicacy suggests a diet of very small fish, crustaceans such as fairy shrimp, and other small aquatic creatures rather than large prey.

Scientists at the University of Portsmouth and Hassan II University of Casablanca, Morocco, identified the new species, named Ctenosuchus tenuidens. It belonged to the thalattosuchians, a group of marine and freshwater reptiles that lived during the age of the dinosaurs. Its fossils came from rocks formed in ancient rivers and lakes in what is now the Atlas Mountains of southeastern Morocco.

The researchers examined the fossil teeth under powerful microscopes and studied their internal structure, finding specialized ridges and a closely spaced arrangement. For lead author Hamzah Imran, from the University of Portsmouth’s School of the Environment and Life Sciences, the teeth’s fragility offered a clue to what the animal could safely catch.

He said: “The teeth are so fine that we don’t think it was catching normal prey. If it caught a big fish, and the fish wriggled, those delicate teeth would get smashed. We think the animal was probably eating soft prey like shrimp, worms and tiny fish, so nothing that would break its teeth.

“These feeding habits make this particular species very different to other long-snouted crocodiles, so it really is quite an exciting discovery!”

Recognizing the teeth in the first place required a microscope and careful work with a scalpel. Imran recalled: “We noticed these little black circles on either side of the long bone in the middle of one of the fossils, so we put it under a microscope.

“We carefully scratched around one of the black circles with a scalpel, and as we uncovered more of it, it became longer and longer. That’s when we realized these little black circles were actually incredibly thin teeth.

“These comb-like teeth inspired the name Ctenosuchus tenuidens as ‘cteno’ means ‘comb,’ ‘suchus’ means ‘crocodile,’ and ‘tenuidens’ means ‘thin teeth.’”

Although unusual among its close relatives, Ctenosuchus had teeth resembling those of other prehistoric animals. Similar teeth occur in ancient aquatic reptiles called mesosaurs and in certain long-snouted pterosaurs that lived millions of years later. Its jaws and teeth share features with those of the Jurassic pterosaurs Gnathosaurus and Ctenochasma, which scientists believe were filter feeders, using their teeth to separate small food items from water. These similarities indicate that different groups independently evolved comparable feeding adaptations.

The Moroccan fossils broaden scientists’ understanding of the habitats and feeding roles occupied by Jurassic crocodile relatives in Gondwana, the southern supercontinent that included modern-day Africa. They also add to the picture of the varied prehistoric ecosystems that once existed in Morocco.

Imran added: “It just shows there is much more out there that needs to be explored. Morocco has an incredibly rich fossil history, but there is still so much waiting to be discovered.”

Reference: “A new teleosauroid (Crocodylomorpha, Thalattosuchia) with a unique dentition from the Middle Jurassic of eastern Morocco” by Hamzah Imran, David M. Martill, Roy E. Smith, Samir Zouhri, James Gunn and Nizar Ibrahim, 8 October 2026, Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology. DOI: 10.1080/02724634.2026.2728670

Manchin is now out of power, mostly owing to his refusal to bow to the far left zeitgeist of the current Democratic Party. This is thus far a fate Susan Collins has avoided over the past decade when she distances herself from Trump and MAGA populism.

If Troy Jackson does manage to topple Collins, the message to Republicans in Maine and New England will be that centrism doesn’t work, that they need bare knuckle brawlers more like Rep. Brandon Gill of Texas, than bipartisan peacemakers like Collins.

Nationally, there are some hardcore Republicans who would welcome a Collins loss in this respect. They see moderate Republicans as more of an obstacle than an asset. But most in the GOP concede that holding the Senate for Trump’s final two years in office outweighs the broader ideological intraparty fights.

Win, lose, or draw for Collins, in the future it will be younger Republican politicians like Libby, who will have to define what a Republican is in Maine, New England, and to some degree in America as a whole.

Democrats exiled Joe Manchin for his lack of political purity; they are on the way to doing the same to Sen. John Fetterman, D-Pa., even though, somewhat hilariously, he really could wind up as the 51st vote in a one-seat Democratic majority next year.

If Maine Republicans can save Susan Collins once again, it will show that the GOP really can, at least in part, and unlike Democrats, embrace moderate and centrist positions — a point that could play heavily in their favor as both parties try to define themselves for the post-Trump age.

David Marcus is a columnist living in West Virginia and the author of "Charade: The COVID Lies That Crushed A Nation."

‘Ticking time bomb’: The world's pension funds face an ageing crisis

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The world's pension systems are confronting a demographic crisis as longer lifespans and dwindling birth rates threaten to stretch retirement finances, raising questions about their ability to sustain payouts for future generations.

Pension offerings generally fall into two categories: defined-benefit plans, which promise retirees a predetermined income, and defined-contribution plans, where payouts depend on accumulated savings and investment returns.

Ageing poses different challenges for the two systems: defined-benefit schemes face mounting liabilities as retirees live longer, while defined-contribution members risk outliving their savings, placing greater pressure on funds' investment performance.

The problem is "huge, and it's gotten more pronounced over time,” said Sebastien Betermier, executive director at the International Centre for Pension Management, noting that some pension funds have members who have been retired for nearly half a century, aged aged 107 or 108.

"The higher the longevity, the more years the pension funds have to cover, and so that immediately comes in as an additional liability," he said. "Think about the cost that it imposes actually on the next generation … it is a ticking time bomb," Betermier told CNBC.

The scale of the challenge is laid bare in the 2026 Marsh CFA Institute Global Pension Index, or MCGPI, report published Tuesday last week. Of the 55 pension systems covered by the index, 43 have fertility rates below the replacement level of 2.1 children per woman, while 23 have rates below 1.5.

Meanwhile, life expectancy at age 65 in more developed regions has increased by 50.9% since 1950 to about 20 additional years, meaning retirement benefits must now last considerably longer. The number of potential workers supporting older people is also set to decline sharply.

“The funding of future pensions and aged care both become more critical as the ratio of workers to retirees continues to decline,” the MCGPI report stated. “Very few systems, as they currently operate, are secure.”

The problem is particularly acute for pay-as-you-go pension systems in which contributions from today's workers largely fund benefits for current retirees, leaving them especially vulnerable to a shrinking workforce.

The pressures are already evident in the U.S., where the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund, which finances retirement and survivor benefits for tens of millions of Americans, is projected to exhaust its reserves in the fourth quarter of 2032, according to its latest trustees' report.

Fully funded pension schemes, which invest contributions from workers and employers to finance future retirement benefits are not immune either.

As their members retire and begin drawing down benefits, some funds could find themselves paying out more than they receive in fresh contributions, forcing them to rethink their investment strategies and liquidity needs, MCGPI cautioned.

“Future benefit outflows will exceed the level of contributions or premiums ... this financial strain will inevitably grow in the future,” the report highlighted.

The demographic crunch is reshaping how pension funds invest, with some taking on greater risks to generate the returns needed to meet growing retirement obligations.

Many mature funds are already paying out more in benefits than they receive in contributions, forcing them to rely increasingly on investment returns and accumulated assets.

In Australia, about a third of superannuation funds recorded negative natural cash flows from assets over a three-year period, meaning benefit payments exceeded contributions, according to a 2025 report by the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority.

Industry watchers say the pressure to generate higher returns has pushed some funds toward riskier and more complex investments, including private equity, infrastructure and private credit.

"You can try to earn higher returns on your investments, but that's going to require taking on greater levels of risk," said Betermier.

Jean-Pierre Aubry, associate director of state and local research at the Center for Retirement Research at Boston College, said pension funds were increasingly shifting toward riskier investments, although the extent varied considerably across markets.

"I think everybody is shifting a little more risk where they can. The costs are going up with this demographic shift," Aubry said.

He pointed to U.S. state and local government pension funds, which hold only about 20% of their assets in bonds, with much of the remainder invested in equities and private assets.

Such allocations make U.S. public pension funds considerably more exposed to investment risk than their counterparts in Europe and Canada, he said.

Not every pension manager sees ageing as a reason to take bigger bets.

David Elias, CEO of Australian pension fund Hostplus said demographic changes were only one of many factors shaping investment decisions.

He stressed the importance of maintaining a diversified portfolio across asset classes, geographies and industries, while ensuring that potential returns adequately compensate for the risks involved.

Elias also said that governments could find ways to mitigate the pressures of ageing populations, including through immigration policies and technological advances, while demographic shifts themselves could create new investment opportunities.

"I don't underestimate governments being incredibly creative in terms of dealing with these types of issues," he said. "You know, but they're going to have to address it, no doubt about that."

A gradually weakening Tropical Storm Vance churned Sunday off Mexico's southwest Pacific coast but still threatened heavy rains and flash flooding in several western states of the country.

Vance, a former Category 2 hurricane as of Friday, was losing strength but still was expected to dump heavy rain on the Mexican states of Colima, Nayarit and Sinaloa, as well as parts of Michoacan, Jalisco and Durango, the U.S. National Hurricane Center in Miami said.

The cyclone was located about 315 miles (510 kilometers) west-southwest of Acapulco, or about 285 miles (455 kilometers) south of Manzanillo, with maximum sustained winds of 70 mph (110 kph), the center said. Vance was moving to the northwest and expected to turn northward in coming days.

Rainfall totals of 2 to 4 inches (5 to 10 centimeters) could produce flooding and mudslides, especially over steep terrain, according to the hurricane center. It said isolated totals of 7 inches (17 centimeters) of rain were possible in some spots.

Meanwhile, Tropical Storm Olana formed far out in the central Pacific, where it posed no threat to land. Olana had top sustained winds of 45 mph (65 kph) and was located about 580 miles (935 kilometers) west-southwest of Johnston Island, a central Pacific atoll, the hurricane center said.

It’s been an intense hurricane season in the Pacific Ocean, with storms spawning in assembly-line fashion near Hawaii and Mexico as El Niño conditions worsened by global warming create unusual weather, like this week's severe flooding and tornadoes hitting the southwestern United States and the prolonged drought in Central America's Dry Corridor.
