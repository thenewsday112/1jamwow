I'm an American who signed a yearlong lease in the Netherlands, sight unseen

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After living as digital nomads for nearly two years, my husband and I decided to move from the US to the Netherlands.

We'd visited several times and fell in love with the walkability and bike culture. Plus, I have a connection to the country, since my great-grandparents were Dutch.

With the help of a real-estate agent, we found an apartment remotely from the US, signing a yearlong lease in the city of Utrecht before ever setting foot in the home.

Since the process of finding an apartment abroad can often take months, we chose to search remotely from the US. To make things easier, we hired a Dutch real-estate agent with local connections who could view apartments for us in person.

The prep work was much more intense than I was used to. In the US, I've leased apartments in three different states, and the extent of the application typically included filling out a basic form and sometimes completing a background check.

That wasn't going to cut it in the Netherlands.

Our agent gave us a long list of materials to prepare, from 12 months of bank statements and links to our LinkedIn profiles, to a "get to know us" letter, which included our hobbies and photos of us. Then she went looking.

Rentals often get snatched up within a few hours or days, so my husband and I were regularly waking up at 4 a.m. in Colorado to read our agent's emails about the new listings each day.

When we finally found one that checked all our boxes, we asked her to schedule a walkthrough. Luckily, she recorded a video for us to review, so we didn't need to wake up early for this part of the process.

It was during the walkthrough, however, that we learned Dutch apartment listings aren't as detailed as the ones we were used to in the US. When our agent arrived, we discovered the listing had omitted several important pieces of information.

For example, there was a fully fenced-in back garden, complete with a shed, picnic table, and blackberry and rose bushes. The kitchen had a dishwasher. And there was a washing machine hidden away behind a cabinet door — and a dryer (which is not as common in Europe).

Score, right? So we jumped at the opportunity, put in our bid, and jumped for joy when our offer was accepted.

We arrived in the Netherlands a few weeks later, traveling directly from the airport to our rental. When we unlocked the door for the first time, hoping we'd fall in love with our new home, it was … freezing.

Turns out, the heat had stopped working, and the rental company couldn't send someone to fix it until the next day. So, we spent our first night in the Netherlands at a hotel.

Thankfully, the heat was fixed the following day, and then we could start unpacking. This is when we began to notice some of the more nuanced differences of a Dutch house compared to an American one.

The first thing to get used to? The alarmingly steep stairs (a hallmark of skinny, tall Dutch houses) which made moving furniture up to our second-floor office feel like a Herculean feat.

We also learned to accept moths and gnats as houseguests, since Dutch windows don't typically have screens. However, with no air conditioning and a 100-year-old house that was built to retain heat, we definitely want to keep those windows open.

Overall, aside from a few stowaway insects and some hot days, we've been very fortunate with our rental. I particularly love the neighborhood and how we can walk to a grocery store, bakery, or park within five minutes.

Although finding our rental was a whirlwind process, I've found the most interesting parts of living here have come from the cultural differences between American and Dutch neighborhoods.

For example, shortly after arriving, our neighbor invited us to the neighborhood WhatsApp group. If you need to borrow a drill or lose your cat, you send a message to the group.

It's very active, and joining the group helped me feel like I was part of a community before knowing anyone here. Although I've heard of similar groups in the US, I've never been invited to join one.

Ordering packages is also very different. Back in the States, packages would be left by my door. In the Netherlands, however, they aren't left unattended.

Instead, the delivery driver will start ringing the neighbors' doorbells until someone is home. Then, they'll leave a digital note about which house has your package.

One place I didn't expect to feel such a contrast is the sidewalk. On sunny days, my Dutch neighbors bring out their dining room chairs, park them on the bricks outside their front door, and enjoy a cold drink. Sometimes, it'll feel like an impromptu block party — and I couldn't be happier to join in the festivities.

After leasing an apartment across the world, sight unseen, would I do it again? I sure would — especially if I could hire a real-estate agent to check out the home in person.

The quirks of our apartment and neighborhood are all part of the experience of living abroad, and although I may not have expected them, I've now grown attached to these cultural differences.

Anlässlich der Verleihung des Deutschen Umweltpreises hat Bundespräsident Frank-Walter Steinmeier dazu aufgerufen, dem Klimaschutz trotz aller "tagespolitischen Konjunkturen" hohe Priorität einzuräumen.

Mit Blick auf die Sorgen um Stabilität und Sicherheit seien derzeit bei vielen Menschen die Gedanken an Umwelt- und Klimaschutz vermutlich eher in den Hintergrund getreten, sagte er bei der Zeremonie in Aachen. "Zugleich aber, auch wenn das paradox klingt, spüren wir alle den Ernst der Lage ganz wortwörtlich, auch wir Europäer. Die Fieberkurve unseres Planeten zeigt deutlich nach oben." Dabei treffe der Klimawandel Europa besonders stark - "unser Kontinent erwärmt sich mehr als doppelt so schnell wie alle anderen".

What are Brazilian voters' main concerns?

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

SAO PAULO, Oct 25 - Brazilians headed to the ballot box on Sunday, in a runoff vote that pits right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the eldest son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, against leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva.

Here are the main themes that have driven the campaign:

Cost-of-living concerns are widespread. More than two-thirds of respondents told a Quaest poll in August their purchasing power had declined versus the previous year.

• Annual inflation stood at 4.58% in the 12 months through September, above the central bank's 3% target, pushing prices — including for food — higher

• Lula has pledged to implement measures to allow for steady cuts in borrowing costs and keep inflation under control

• Bolsonaro wants to reduce and stabilize public debt to bring interest rates closer to international levels and steer inflation toward the central bank's target

Crime is another major concern for Brazilians. While Brazil's homicide rate fell by 40% over the last decade, improvements in fighting crime have been uneven, according to the Brazilian Public Security Forum, a think tank.

• Bolsonaro has pledged a heavy-handed approach to criminals, promising maximum-security prisons and chemical castration for serious sexual offenses

• Lula wants to increase the federal prison system's capacity to hold gang leaders, while still investing in poor areas to reduce the influence of organized crime

Corruption is also at the top of voters' minds, with both Lula's circle and Bolsonaro facing scrutiny in high-profile cases.

• A major scandal that led to the collapse of mid-size lender Banco Master has sparked multiple probes, including into Bolsonaro over alleged corruption and money laundering, accusations he denies

• Bolsonaro has pledged to fight corruption by shielding state-run companies and pension funds from political appointments and expanding transparency in public spending

• Lula has vowed to deepen anti-corruption and transparency policies even as his son - who has denied the allegations - is being investigated in probes involving alleged influence peddling, corruption and fraud

Brazil's public finances are under pressure, especially due to pensions and social benefits. The difficult fiscal outlook has sparked fears of welfare cuts, while many Brazilians are struggling with debt arrears due to years of double-digit interest rates.

• Public debt has risen by more than 10 percentage points during Lula's current term, reaching 82.9% of gross domestic product

• Brazil's benchmark interest rate of 13.75% remains among the highest in the world

• Long-term interest rates fell sharply after Bolsonaro's stronger-than-expected first-round performance

• He has promised to ramp up fiscal adjustment, targeting 1.5% of GDP improvement in the primary balance within 18 months to lower borrowing costs

• Lula has reiterated commitments to improve Brazil's primary balance targeting an effective surplus near 1.3% of GDP by 2030, versus a projected 0.4% deficit this year

After US President Donald Trump imposed tariffs on a swathe of Brazilian goods in 2025, foreign policy became a more prominent issue for many Brazilians.

• Bolsonaro has courted Trump and other right-wing allies globally

• He wants to rebuild ties with Argentina, the US and Israel, arguing relations with each are close to breaking point

• Lula has campaigned for a foreign policy based on multilateralism and stronger ties with the Mercosur bloc of South American states and the BRICS nations

'It's hire the robot, fire the human and don't look back': Bill Gates warns AI will replace nearly all work skills and cause widespread job losses

Published: 13:10 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 15:52 GMT, 25 October 2026

Bill Gates has warned the continued development of artificial intelligence and robots will cause widespread job losses – including in manual labour.

The billionaire founder of Microsoft said that both 'white-collar work' and the 'skills of physical human labour' were at risk.

He blamed the 'incentive structure' of businesses looking to increase their profits. 'It's hire the robot, fire the human and don't look back,' said Mr Gates.

AI has already been costing jobs over the past few years, particularly in office work, as it sweeps up data-driven analytical roles such as software development, data entry and customer service.

Last week Barclays bank announced it would replace 5,000 jobs – 5 per cent of its global workforce – with AI by the end of the year to cut costs.

It came after the head of the International Monetary Fund last week described AI as ‘a slow-moving tsunami’ that would wipe out jobs around the world and destroy opportunities for the young in particular, who used to do the entry-level menial work that the technology already excels at at very little cost.

Bill Gates has warned that the continued development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and robots will cause widespread job losses

But Mr Gates warned physical labour, such as construction or factory work, could also be at risk as humanoid robots become more common.

The billionaire also argued AI could soon overtake humans as the most intelligent things on the planet.

He told The Observer that the 'skills of invention, skills of white-collar work and then with humanoids, the skills of physical human labour' could all be replaced by AI.

Mr Gates said governments would need to adapt to be able to deal with mass unemployment through education and re-skilling.

Despite the technology likely leading to more 'prosperity', governments would have less money to deal with the fallout of huge job losses, Mr Gates warned.

He said that these challenges would be presented at a time of competing priorities for states as geopolitical tensions continue to rise.

He said: 'You could say this isn’t the best time to say, "Hey, rich countries, let’s do the hardest thing you’ve ever done".'

He also predicted that by the end of the 2020s AI could be operating beyond human control, which could mean that AI could operate against human interests.

In September, a British researcher at a major AI company quit, warning that the 'out of control' tech could 'kill us all'.

Jacob Coxon, who helped train the latest versions of AI at San Francisco-based Anthropic, urged people to ‘not underestimate’ its power – particularly if it becomes ‘super-intelligent’.

The 27-year-old claims neither Anthropic, the firm behind the popular Claude system, nor ChatGPT creators OpenAI, where he previously worked, were 'acting responsibly' over AI safety.

Superintelligence is the point at which an artificial system becomes more powerful than any individual, company or even nation.

It is expected Chancellor John Healey will commit millions of pounds in next week's Budget to guard against the risks of AI while aiming to harness its power for economic advantage across the UK.

Ahead of his maiden statement on Wednesday, he said AI would 'transform daily life' and 'every firm, community, hospital, school and every aspect of our economy' could benefit from AI.

He said he understood growing concerns that AI would take away people's jobs which is why he would be 'setting out a distinctively British approach to AI – one that doesn't leave workers behind and defends our national interest, while ensuring the benefits of AI-enabled growth are felt right across the country'.

A new AI Centre of Excellence in Manchester is planned alongside AI investments in areas including Edinburgh.

The Government will also spend £300million backing an AI Biosecurity Lab which will look at protections from emerging biological threats.

The lab will be a public-private partnership supported by leading AI developers including OpenAI and Anthropic.

Genetic test may spare younger women chemotherapy for breast cancer

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Published: 13:09 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 19:52 GMT, 25 October 2026

The NHS could save £4m a year by offering women with low-risk breast cancer a genetic test that could allow them to avoid toxic and unnecessary chemotherapy.

The test - called Prosigna - is currently only approved for use in post-menopausal women whose cancer has spread to no more than three nearby lymph nodes.

But experts at University College London say offering the test more widely, to women as young as 40, could save the NHS £654 per patient.

It could also spare more than 4,000 women a year the side effects of chemotherapy, which can include nausea, vomiting, hair loss and an increased risk of infection.

Presenting the findings at the annual meeting of the European Society of Medical Oncology in Madrid, Dr Rob Stein said: 'Prosigna testing not only allows about two-thirds of patients with this type of breast cancer to safely avoid chemotherapy, but doing so also saves the NHS money.'

The current study focused on the first two years after diagnosis, but experts predict the savings would be even greater in the longer term.

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide, with around 60,000 women diagnosed with invasive breast cancer each year in the UK alone.

Around 80 per cent of these are oestrogen-receptor positive cancers, meaning they are fuelled by the female sex hormone oestrogen and can be treated with hormone-blocking drugs alone.

Making genetic testing more widely available could save the NHS £4m a year - and spare thousands of women the debilitating side effects of chemotherapy

Many of these patients are also offered chemotherapy, as it can help lower the risk of the disease returning.

However, there is growing concern that many women gain little or no benefit from the treatment, while still suffering its debilitating and sometimes dangerous side effects.

Now experts at UCL say Prosigna could help address this dilemma.

The trial - known as OPTIMA - saw 4,429 women aged 40 and over whose cancer had spread to up to nine lymph nodes or who had a tumour measuring 30mm or more given the test.

The test analyses the activity of 50 genes involved in breast cancer growth, taken from the tumour site during standard surgery or biopsy.

A score is then generated based on the risk of the cancer spreading - with low scores indicating that a patient can be safely treated with hormone therapy alone.

Results showed that 67 per cent of patients tested did not need chemotherapy.

According to UCL analysis, this equates to around 4,200 patients a year.

Squash is my go-to winter vegetable... my six favorites that ease digestion, keep you full and ward off the flu: JOHANNAH KATZ

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

When it comes to the healthiest winter squash, you might expect butternut or pumpkin to take the top spot.

But many of their often-overlooked cousins have a surprisingly impressive nutritional profile.

From bright orange butternut to pasta-like spaghetti squash, winter squashes offer a wide variety of vitamins, minerals, fiber and healthy plant compounds.

And as flu season gets underway, they may even have protective antioxidants that could ward off pesky winter bugs.

As a registered dietitian, I'm constantly recommending my patients add more squash to their sheet-pan dinners, curries, bowls and pastas.

While there is no single healthiest squash for everyone, certain varieties stand out for my clients and on my grocery list.

Below, I have ranked six of my favorites based on their nutritional strengths, versatility and benefits for different health goals.

Squash is packed with vital nutrients that immune, heart and digestive health

Delicata squash earns its place on this list because sometimes the healthiest vegetable is the one you'll actually make and eat.

Unlike butternut squash, which usually needs peeling, delicata has thin, edible skin that softens when cooked. That means less prep time and less food waste.

Delicata also contains fiber and potassium, which support digestion, fullness and healthy muscle function. Its slightly sweet, nutty flavor also makes it particularly appealing to people who aren't usually excited about eating their vegetables.

My favorite way to eat it is simple: slice it into half moons, toss with olive oil and roast until golden.

Add to salads or serve alongside salmon or chicken - no peeling required.

Spaghetti squash can serve as a replacement for traditional pasta. Consider pairing it with lean protein for added balance

Spaghetti squash has a unique advantage: When cooked, its flesh separates into thin strands resembling noodles.

That makes it a practical option for people looking to cut calories or carbohydrates - or simply add more vegetables to comforting fall meals.

One cup of plain cooked spaghetti squash has about 42 calories, 10g of carbohydrates and 2g of fiber. For comparison, a cup of cooked traditional pasta generally contains around 200 calories and more than 40g of carbs.

Despite the name, spaghetti squash isn't nutritionally equivalent to pasta, so what you serve with it matters when rounding out a meal. Adding chicken, turkey, shrimp, tofu or beans can make it more balanced.

And you don’t have to give up pasta altogether. Mixing spaghetti squash with regular spaghetti is another easy way to increase your vegetable intake and get used to the differences in texture and taste.

I like to roast the squash halves until tender and then use a fork to separate the strands. Toss with marinara, sautéed vegetables and your favorite protein for a lighter take on spaghetti.

Sometimes called Japanese pumpkin, kabocha has a dense, creamy texture and a naturally sweet flavor similar to a sweet potato.

Its standout nutritional feature is its carotenoid content. These plant pigments, also found in carrots and sweet potatoes, include beta-carotene, which the body converts into vitamin A, lutein and zeaxanthin.

These nutrients are concentrated in the retina and help support low-light vision and protect against degeneration.

A recent study published in HortScience found that certain kabocha varieties contained substantial amounts of carotenoids, although exact amounts varied depending on the variety, maturity and storage conditions.

Kabocha also provides fiber and carbohydrates, making it a satisfying side dish, particularly when paired with a source of protein.

Consider cutting it into wedges and roasting with olive oil and a little salt. Like delicata, the skin also softens when cooked and can be eaten.

When shopping for canned pumpkin, choose plain varieties to avoid excess amounts of added sugar

Pumpkin might be the most recognizable winter squash, and it isn’t just good for making pies.

Like butternut squash, pumpkin gets its orange color from beta-carotene, which the body converts into vitamin A to support vision, as well as immune and skin health.

Vitamin A helps maintain protective mucosal barriers in the respiratory and digestive tracks, the body's first line of defense against the flu and common cold.

It also comes in a can, which can be far cheaper and more shelf stable than fresh produce.

One cup of plain canned pumpkin contains about 83 calories, 7g of fiber and 500mg of potassium. It also provides immune-boosting iron and vitamin C.

Unlike many winter squashes that require peeling, chopping and roasting, canned pumpkin is ready to use straight from the pantry and is incredibly versatile, making it great for both sweet and savory recipes.

Stir plain pumpkin puree into oatmeal, blend it into smoothies or add it to soups, chilis and sauces for extra fiber and nutrients. Just make sure to choose 100 percent pumpkin puree and not pumpkin pie filling to cut down on added sugar.

Butternut squash can easily be blended into a soup or cubed with olive oil, cinnamon and rosemary

Butternut squash is particularly rich in beta-carotene, the pigment responsible for its bright orange color.

One cup of baked butternut squash contains about 82 calories, nearly 7g of fiber and about 580mg of potassium.

Butternut squash also provides about 34 percent of the daily recommended value of vitamin C, which helps produce disease-fighting white blood cells and reduces inflammation during infections.

That's an impressive nutritional package for relatively few calories. The fiber supports digestion and fullness, and potassium plays an important role in maintaining healthy blood pressure.

Butternut squash has a naturally sweet, slightly nutty flavor, making it an appealing way to add more vegetables to meals, even for picky eaters.

Consider roasting cubed butternut squash with olive oil, cinnamon and rosemary, then add it to grain bowls or salads. It also blends nicely into creamy soups without needing much cream.

One cup of baked acorn squash contains more than twice the potassium of a medium banana, which supports heart health

Acorn squash takes my top spot thanks to its impressive fiber and potassium content, making it a standout choice for digestive and heart health.

One cup of baked acorn squash contains around 9g of fiber and nearly 900mg of potassium. For perspective, that's about one-third of the recommended 28g daily fiber intake and more than twice the amount of potassium in a medium banana.

Fiber supports digestion and regular bowel movements, helps lower cholesterol and promotes fullness, while potassium helps counteract sodium's effects on blood pressure.

Acorn squash also provides about 21 percent of the daily value for magnesium and 24 percent for vitamin B6 in just one cup. Both support important functions such as muscle health and energy metabolism.

Johannah Katz (pictured) is a registered dietitian who frequently recommends winter squash to her patients

Acorn squash contains more carbs than some other winter varieties, but those carbs come packaged with fiber and beneficial nutrients.

I like to slice acorn squash in half, scoop out the seeds and roast it until tender. Then, stuff the center with quinoa, lentils, vegetables or ground turkey for a balanced and delicious meal.

The answer depends on your nutritional goals and preferences.

Acorn squash takes the top spot for its high fiber and potassium content, while butternut stands out for vitamin A and other essential nutrients.

Kabocha offers an impressive range of carotenoids, and canned pumpkin is an affordable, convenient source of beta-carotene. For lighter, lower-carbohydrate meals, spaghetti squash is a great option, and delicata makes prep quick and easy.

Some winter squash varieties are particularly rich in potassium, so people with kidney disease who need to limit potassium should pay attention to portions.

Ultimately, I wouldn't choose just one favorite. Diversifying the winter squashes you eat is a great way to enjoy a wider range of nutrients, flavors and textures while making healthy eating more interesting and enjoyable.

Johannah Katz, MA, RD, is a registered dietitian based in Florida.

Vor zwanzig Jahren verstarb das legendäre Polopferd. Heute jagen Dutzende seiner Klone dem Ball nach.

Kurzerhand liess Adolfo Cambiaso Hautzellen dessen einfrieren. Der reiche Texaner Alan Meekere half dem Polostar schliesslich dabei, Cuartetera zu neuem Leben zu erwecken. Die ersten Klone entstanden über die gemeinsame Firma Crestview in den USA.

2016 trat Cambiaso beim traditionsreichen Palermo Open mit einem Team an, das ausschliesslich aus Cuartetera-Klonen bestand. «Das Temperament, ihre Reaktionen, einfach alles. Es ist beeindruckend, wie ähnlich sie dem Original sind», erklärte er gegenüber der «NZZ».

Schliesslich wurde die Klontechnik direkt nach Argentinien gebracht, wo heute im grossen Stil Klonpferde in Laboren herangezüchtet werden.

Rein sportliche Interessen verfolgt Cambiaso jedoch nicht. Mit seinen Pferdeklonen betreibt er inzwischen ein Millionengeschäft. Schon 2010 wurde eine Cuartetara für die Rekordsumme von 800'000 US-Dollar versteigert, im zarten Alter von drei Monaten.

Für das Klonen benötigt man zunächst eine Eizelle von einer Spenderstute. Diese wird dann «entkernt», also quasi die Erbmasse entnommen. Auf der anderen Seite werden dem zu klonenden Tier Fibroblasten entnommen, also Zellen des Binde- und Stützgewebes. Schliesslich wird das Erbgut in die leere Eizelle eingepflanzt. Dort reift es heran und wird dann in eine Empfängerstute transferiert, die das Fohlen austrägt.

Seit 2012 erlaubt der Weltverband für Pferdesport das Klonen von Polopferden. Heute boomt die «Serienproduktion» der Champions vor allem in Argentinien. Ein extrem lukratives Geschäft, in dem Hunderte Klone pro Jahr produziert werden.

Das erste Klonpferd überhaupt kam 2003 auf die Welt, wie der Veterinärmediziner Dominik Burger schildert: «Damals ging es vor allem darum, dass man Zuchttiere reproduzieren wollte.»

Wertvolle Hengste wurden vervielfacht, damit sie noch mehr Fohlen zeugen konnten. Oder auch kastrierte Tiere – also Wallache – sollten so für die Zucht zurückgebracht werden.

«Schliesslich entstand die Überlegung, dass man auch erfolgreiche Sportpferde vervielfachen konnte», sagt Burger. In Argentinien, dem Mekka des Polos, jagten sie bald einmal dem Ball hinterher. Doch auch für den Dressur- oder Springsport werden Pferde geklont.

Besonders erfolgversprechend wäre Klonen im Pferderennsport. Dort ist es allerdings verboten. «Bei Rennpferden wird der genetische Einfluss auf 60 Prozent beziffert», sagt der Veterinärmediziner. «Bei Springpferden beträgt er aber nur etwa 20 bis 25 Prozent.»

Burger selbst sieht das Klonen von Pferden kritisch. Zum einen wegen des Tierwohls. «Häufig kommt es zu Aborten, Missbildungen und Fehlgeburten.» Viele der Tiere sterben, auch bereits als Fötus. Dazu kommt für Burger die ethische Dimension: «Die Würde eines Tieres wird schwer angetastet, indem man es einfach vervielfacht.»

Generell seien die Züchter in Europa weit restriktiver eingestellt als in Übersee. Und eine Klonindustrie, wie es sie heute in Argentinien gibt, ist für Burger gerade auch in der Schweiz undenkbar. «Hier wird nicht geklont und es gibt auch niemanden, der das machen möchte.»

La cerámica fabricada en sus obradores tradicionales desde hace siglos se exhibe con orgullo en colecciones de ciudades como Nueva York, París o Londres. Este prodigio nació tras la conquista cristiana de 1238, cuando los alfareros moriscos locales convirtieron su saber en un motor económico sin igual. Lo que comenzó como alfarería doméstica evolucionó hacia una industria admirada por gran parte de Europa. Hoy, contemplar una pieza hecha en Manises en un gran museo extranjero supone presenciar el legado de un pueblo que convirtió el barro en arte.

La consolidación internacional de Manises aconteció en el siglo XIV, bajo el señorío de Pere Boïl. Tras sus embajadas ante el Reino de Granada, se introdujeron en los talleres locales los conocimientos de la loza dorada o reflejo metálico. Los primeros documentos que atestiguan esta producción datan de 1325, destacando sagas de maestros artesanos de origen mudéjar como los Almurcí. Esta técnica exigía aplicar un pigmento de óxidos de cobre, plata y cinabrio sobre un esmalte de plomo y estaño. Tras la cocción en atmósfera reductora, el fuego creaba una película iridiscente que mutaba según la luz. Su belleza dorada sorprendió a las cortes de la época, convirtiendo a la villa en epicentro cerámico.

A finales del mencionado siglo, la loza dorada y azul de Manises dominaba las rutas comerciales del Mediterráneo. En su escrito de 1383 titulado Regiment de la Cosa Pública, el fraile Francesc Eiximenis dejó constancia del deslumbramiento que provocaba esta vajilla. Afirmaba el cronista que la belleza de la obra de Manises tenía enamorado a todo el mundo civilizado de la época. Papas, cardenales y príncipes la solicitaban con devoción, maravillándose de que de la simple tierra surgiera una obra tan noble. Los barcos que partían de Valencia transportaban estas piezas hacia los estados italianos y las cortes de Europa, consolidando una tradición insuperable.

Durante los siglos XIV, XV y XVI, las figuras más poderosas de Europa encargaron piezas personalizadas a los talleres maniseros. La reina María de Castilla, tras enamorarse de un plato decorado con su escudo en 1415, encargó una vajilla completa. Reyes de Aragón como Alfonso V solicitaron miles de azulejos y lozas doradas para sus castillos de Nápoles y Gaeta. En Florencia, Lorenzo de Médici el Magnífico quedó deslumbrado en 1475 por una hermosa ánfora de reflejos metálicos. Asimismo, los papas Borgia, Calixto III y Alejandro VI, ordenaron cargamentos de azulejería para el Vaticano, conservadas hoy como tesoros en museos.

El secreto técnico del reflejo metálico guardado por los artesanos de Manises constituía un saber valiosísimo en toda Europa. La combinación de pigmentos disueltos en vinagre y el control del humo en el horno permitían obtener tonos que imitaban el oro y la plata. Esta maestría se aplicó a vajillas suntuarias y a la arquitectura mediante una floreciente industria de azulejería policromada. Edificios emblemáticos del Reino de Valencia como la Lonja de la Seda, el Palau de la Generalitat y la Casa de la Ciudad lucieron pavimentos maniseros. Incluso la cúpula de la iglesia de San Juan Bautista se revistió con tejas doradas que deslumbraron a visitantes de todo el continente durante centurias.

A pesar de su éxito, la cerámica de Manises superó momentos de profunda adversidad a lo largo de su historia. La expulsión de los moriscos en 1609 supuso un golpe para la industria local, al privarla de sus maestros alfareros más experimentados. No obstante, la creación del Gremio de Mestres d'Obra de Terra en 1614 logró mantener viva la compleja fórmula del lustre. Durante el siglo XVIII, el auge de la Real Fábrica de Alcora introdujo novedades estéticas que filtraron hacia Manises. Esta renovación impulsó la loza popular decorada por mujeres pintoras, devolviendo la competitividad a la producción manisera y preservando un legado único.

El siglo XX transformó el tejido artesanal de Manises en una industria moderna capaz de unir tradición y vanguardia. En 1917 se fundó la Escuela Práctica de Cerámica, impulsada por profesores como Manuel González Martí para formar a nuevas generaciones. Las fábricas locales obtuvieron medallas de oro en las exposiciones internacionales de Barcelona, Filadelfia y París durante los años veinte. Surgieron invenciones como el famoso filtro de agua Sinaí y sofisticados recubrimientos arquitectónicos modernistas. Artesanos ilustres conservaron los métodos antiguos del reflejo metálico, evitando que las fórmulas sucumbieran ante la masificación del mercado.

Este museo está dedicado a la tradición cerámica de este pequeño pueblo y alberga piezas, herramientas y hasta un horno de leña

En la actualidad, Manises mantiene viva su vocación cerámica, adaptando su saber a la artesanía contemporánea. Este esfuerzo colectivo de más de siete siglos culminó en 2021 con su ingreso en la Red de Ciudades Creativas de la UNESCO. Hoy en día, talleres artesanales continúan elaborando el reflejo metálico tradicional, diseñando además vajillas para restaurantes de prestigio. Visitar su Museo de Cerámica o sus calles permite constatar cómo el barro sigue siendo la esencia identitaria de esta villa valenciana. La presencia de sus obras en museos de grandes ciudades recuerda al mundo que Manises sigue moldeando belleza eterna con la misma maestría que antaño.

Cutting ‘red tape’ is nothing but a red herring – and arguably counterproductive | Heather Stewart

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Blaming a moribund economy on too many rules is a hackneyed neoliberal trope – UK plc needs smarter regulation, not less regulation

Before becoming prime minister, Andy Burnham pledged to “end 40 years of neoliberalism”. But as he prepares for his first budget on Wednesday, his government is yet to let go of one of its most tedious cliches: red tape.

In the run-up to the chancellor John Healey’s high-stakes Commons address, the government issued a press release last week promising his plans would include measures to “slash the red tape choking firms”.

Named after the tape once used to tie up government bills, and popularised by the historian Thomas Carlyle, the phrase is straight from the neoliberal playbook and has long outlived its usefulness.

Getting the interfering state out of the way of the wealth creators was a trope of Thatcher and Reaganite economics, and on this side of the pond it has been repeated ad nauseam by every government since.

Thatcher unleashed the City with her “big bang” deregulation drive, and her successor John Major was enthusiastic about “hacking back the jungle of red tape”. It was the man he appointed for the job – Michael Heseltine – who coined what has become the most hackneyed description of this particular approach: the “bonfire of red tape”.

With Labour back in power, Gordon Brown celebrated a light-tough regulatory regime for the UK’s hard-driving financial sector – until the Treasury had to bail out the banks after the 2008 financial crisis.

Boris Johnson wanted to unfetter Britain’s businesses from “Brussels bureaucracy” and waffled on during the Brexit campaign about bendy bananas. Yet, after Brexit, business groups said their members would rather comply with longstanding EU standards in many areas than adopt an entirely new UK regime. And bananas are no more or less bendy than before.

Rachel Reeves had a more unpleasantly graphic way of making a similar point, referring to regulation as a “boot on the neck” of British business.

There is nothing wrong with ensuring the interaction between business and the state is smooth. But even during the early years of neoliberalism, the free market good, regulation bad dichotomy was always unhelpful, for several reasons.

First, good regulation – a robust competition regime, for example – is essential to make markets work well. And even most enthusiasts for a laissez-faire approach would acknowledge the need for some basic quality standards – for food and medicines, for example.

When it comes to the labour market, Labour has unashamedly adopted a pro-regulation stance. Its Employment Rights Act is the most significant increase in workers’ rights in a generation – but it inevitably, and deliberately, increases the rules firms must follow.

That is to the benefit, ministers rightly argue, of low-paid employees trapped in insecure work. But a bonfire of red tape it is not. Labour posing as a champion of deregulation will seem unconvincing, given that context.

Second, and ironically, the march of neoliberalism itself is a reason regulation has mushroomed in recent decades. As the Labour MP Yuan Yang argued in a recent lecture for the journal Renewal, as the state retreated from providing important services, watchdog institutions to oversee the private sector became necessary.

As she put it: “The neoliberal illusion, that privatisation liberated markets from state interference, obscures the reality that the state already significantly controls our privatised utilities, but through a maze of arm’s length regulatory agencies which operate outside democratic influence.”

For the water sector, privatised in 1989, these include Ofwat, which sets bills (with the Competition and Markets Authority acting as an appeals body in the event of a dispute); the Drinking Water Inspectorate; the Environment Agency; and the Consumer Council for Water.

Yet, as Yang points out, none are responsible for drawing up a coherent plan for the sector as a whole. “Everyone knows how this story ends: with our rivers polluted and our water infrastructure dangerously lacking.”

Hacking away at the regulatory thicket fails to deal with the structural features of the economy that make the rules necessary in the first place.

Mat Lawrence, the director of the thinktank Common Wealth and author of The Productive State, a policy paper influential among those close to Burnham, calls this “the regulatory state”. He cites affordable housing targets for private developers as another example of regulation taking the place of stuff the state once did itself.

The third reason the endless red tape discourse is unhelpful is one raised by Mike Clancy, the general secretary of the Prospect union, which represents workers at many of the regulators.

He has urged the government to lay off these often not lavishly paid staff, who are trying to do their jobs with stretched resources. “Regulators continue to be labelled as ‘blockers’ despite the vital work they do keeping us safe and protecting our natural environment,” he said.

Prospect has been urging the government to ditch a promise to “cut the administrative burden” of regulation by 25% by the end of the parliament, arguing it is arbitrary and potentially counterproductive.

Finally, we would all benefit from some over-mighty businesses being reined in by regulation, not least the tech firms, which insouciantly concede that their AI agents have been busting through safety controls and may be about to start spawning spontaneously.

Embracing the tired idea that red tape is “choking” business is hardly likely to help the government make the case for the more stringent approach that is clearly necessary.

As it happens, Bonfire Night is almost upon us. If Burnham really wants to embrace a different approach to the economy, he could start by consigning the phrase “red tape” to the flames.
