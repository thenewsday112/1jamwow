Así llega el otoño a la mesa

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Restaurantes urbanos y rurales para disfrutar de la estación con platos de setas y cazas.

La bajada de las temperaturas, la llegada de las lluvias y los días más cortos son los mejores aliados para que la despensa se transforme en función de la temporada para dar la bienvenida a productos como setas, caza y verduras de otoño-invierno y, por lo tanto, para cambiar la forma de comer y de cocinar. Es así la forma en la que llega el otoño a la mesa, que puede disfrutarse a través de esta decena de pistas, sea en restaurantes rurales o urbanos.

Medio siglo avala la historia de un negocio familiar situado en la localidad palentina de Villoldo y capitaneado por las hermanas Pilar, Mercedes y Paula Pedrosa. En este hotel-restaurante, lleva años implicada la siguiente generación. Setas del bosque, legumbres, verduras de la huerta propias de la llegada del otoño, lechazo churro y pichones de Tierra de Campos conforman los productos en los que se basa la propuesta con opciones como el Revuelto de hongos o las Alubias con hongos. En Madrid, cuentan con una sede: Villoldo, con Alfonso y Anselmo Fierro, hijo de Pilar Pedrosa, al frente. Precio medio de 45 a 65 euros.

En Madrid, muchos recordarán ese nombre y a sus dueños. El negocio fundado en 1968 por los abuelos de Víctor Membibre funcionó hasta el cierre en 2022 en su sede histórica del barrio de Argüelles bajo un modelo de “casa de cocina castellana familiar”. El joven cocinero, que es tercera generación, decidió regresar a la localidad zamorana de unos 300 habitantes, de la que procede su familia para emprender proyecto, que acabó inaugurando en la primavera de 2025 manteniendo el nombre original y apostando por un formato de taberna. Su mano para bordar platos con caza y emplear productos de temperada aún es más llamativa en los platos de otoño de concepto informal de barra y mesas en El Puente de Sanabria, localidad zamorana de donde sus abuelos procedían.

Es uno de los grandes destinos españoles para comer platos de caza. Situada en la localidad zamorana de Castroverde de Campos, es la casa de Luis Alberto Lera y Natalia Fernández, donde defienden una propuesta con estrella Michelin apegada a los productos de Tierra de Campos. Se puede elegir entre un menú de recetas tradicionales y cocina de temporada con precio de 80 euros o el degustación de caza de platos clásicos o creativos, que se despacha en dos extensiones por 110 y 168 euros. Como “complemento de la mesa”, cuentan con un hotel con el desayuno como una prolongación de una experiencia que puede ser una buena escapada de otoño.

En donde una fonda arrancó allá por 1700 en Rionegro del Puente, localidad en la provincia de Zamora, se ubica la casa de Gloria Lucía y Elías Martín. Esta peculiar pareja regenta un negocio familiar que tiene su especialidad en la gastronomía micológica y la caza. La colección de setas funciona durante todo el año dependiendo de la temporalidad de cada ejemplar, pero es en el otoño cuando alcanza su máximo esplendor. El Empalme se hace con variedad de setas de zonas como Rabanales, San Vitero y Fradellos. Se cocinan se muchas formas, igual que sus platos de caza. Para muchos, es un restaurante-destino que merece el viaje en sí mismo. Precio medio de 60 a 90 euros.

Pista en el barrio de Lavapiés, en Madrid, que materializa el proyecto de Eduardo Antón y Álvaro de La Torre. Estos “expertos recolectores de setas” optan por una casi monografía en torno a estos productos que encuentran su máximo esplendor en el otoño. Así, construyen una oferta de platos ligados a “setas & productos silvestres” y a un modelo de “recolección y compra del producto de la mejor calidad, cocina propositiva y siempre cambiante”. Se pueden probar recetas con variedades como amanita caesarea, angula de monte y oreja de madera, entre otras muchas y según lo que el bosque ofrece a diario, a través de platos a la carta o un menú cinco platos con setas de temporada y postre, con un precio de 57 euros.

Es la casa de los hermanos Pedro y Luis García de la Navarra en la madrileña calle Montalbán, donde se reparten roles: el primero capitanea un formato de “cocina tradicional y de temporada” y el segundo ejerce como sumiller a cargo de una bodega con más de setecientas referencias. En otoño, llega el momento de probar sus platos con setas y darse el lujo de disfrutar de las verduras que empiezan a llegar con temperaturas más frías como el cardo o las acelgas. Se puede comer en su barra o sus mesas.

Un negocio imprescindible para comer setas en Madrid, con una historia que parte de la apertura en el año 1975 por Julio Pulido, cuya familia siguió posteriormente gestionándolo. Cuenta con dos sedes, ambas a un paso de la Plaza de Chueca: un bar-taberna y un restaurante. Durante todo el año, hay variedad de setas, oferta que en otoño se multiplica con todo tipo de ejemplares, desde níscalos y setas de cardo, a lengua de vaca o boletus. Se preparan al momento a la plancha, con yema de huevo o con foie, entre otras opciones. Tícket medio de 50-75 euros.

“Cocina de mercado, elaborada con productos de temporada”, definen su casa de César Martín y Marina Launay. En este negocio del barrio de Chamberí, se puede probar la cocina de uno de los grandes expertos en caza en la ciudad. Sus recetas cinegéticas, como el Pichón bravío con estofado-escabechado entero con verduras, se pueden probar a la carta (precio medio 70-100 euros) bajo una oferta que varia cada día y que añade setas y platos de cuchara en esta época del año.

Es la sede central de Nandu Jubany, chef catalán que ejemplifica el rol de empresario hostelero capaz de abarcar una colección de negocios en España y Andorra. Can Jubany es su casa madre en la localidad barcelonesa de Calldetenes, en la Comarca d’Osona. Llega el otoño y eso significa que en platos entra el producto de temporada con las setas y la caza como opciones que pueden colarse en sus dos menús degustación (precios de 122 y 172 euros), con platos como Carpaccio de amanita caesaria edulis con virutas de foie y piñones y la alternativa de comer a la carta en lugar de otra por menú.

Portuetxe. Negocio abierto en 1982 por la familia Bereciartua en San Sebastián, bajo un concepto de asador con propuesta de cocina tradicional vasca. Javier Bereciartua (padre) y Borja Bereciartua (hijo) defienden un negocio donde carnes y pescados pasan por la parrilla, mientras en otoño llega la colección de setas, que dan pie a opciones como el Revuelto de hongos, basado en reunir en un plato lo mejor llegado cada día del bosque y receta a la que se pueda agregar una yema de huevo. Precio medio de 50 a 75 euros.

In early August, Sagi Tzadik asked an AI agent to take a security flaw it had found in WordPress and see whether the same weakness could be made to work against Drupal, another popular content management system. Eight hours later, the agent reported that it had failed. Then it added that, in the course of trying, it found a flaw in PHP, the programming language on which both systems—and as much as 70% of the web—depend.

Tzadik, a security researcher at cybersecurity firm Wiz, assumed that the AI had made a mistake. “Models tend to hallucinate,” he tells TIME. But the exploit, which took advantage of a weakness that had gone undetected for more than a decade, worked.

Unbeknownst to Wiz’s team, researchers at another cybersecurity firm, Calif—working with OpenAI—had used AI to find a related flaw. It suggests that once AI reaches a certain capability level, it is a matter of time before some longstanding weaknesses are unearthed. AI companies have been trying to ensure that defenders including Wiz and Calif get there first by giving them early access to new AI models before the equivalent capabilities trickle down to malicious users. This time, it worked.

The episode offers a glimpse into a new phase in cybersecurity in which AI helps defenders find vulnerabilities while giving them less time to fix them. While the bugs’ announcement has prompted alarm of a much-speculated “cyber-apolocalyse”—in which AI discovers world-melting bugs, plunging society into chaos—those who helped discover it are optimistic. “We are fixing vulnerabilities at an unprecedented rate, and this is doing security overall a very good thing,” Tzadik says.

But the details reveal just how close things came to disaster. Word of the vulnerabilities began circulating online two days before the patch’s scheduled release, prompting PHP’s maintainers to bring it forward by a day. Hours later, with the patch still unavailable, Calif mistakenly posted a video showing its exploit at work, and copycat attempts multiplied. Cloudflare counted thousands before the public announcement, tens of thousands within 45 minutes of it, hundreds of thousands within two hours, and eventually millions. Even after the patch was released, many webpages, including those of government agencies and parts of Wikipedia, were taken down as a precaution as security teams scrambled to install the update.

“If it had leaked a month earlier,” before a patch was ready, “this would have been a very different situation,” says Tomer Schwartz, CTO of exposure management and appsec at Wiz.

That a patch was prepared was the result of a loosely organized sprint. After Calif and Wiz sent reports to PHP, the groups joined forces. Researchers from competing companies shared their findings in common channels, with no contracts governing the arrangement. Over the following weeks, the group of roughly 15 people spoke almost daily to trace the vulnerabilities to their common source and devise a fix. The process, Schwartz says, was “just based on trust.”

That process will have to sustain increasing pressure. OpenAI has said that the PHP bugs were among “several significant vulnerabilities we’ve already found and are working to fix,” and predicted that similar discoveries would “increase sharply in the coming months.” As those findings multiply, so will the number of dangerous secrets waiting for fixes. “We have to rethink all of our processes, all of our methods, all of our tech stack … to be able to keep up the pace against attackers,” Schwartz says.

Though defenders are being given a head start, they face a disadvantage of another sort. Attackers only have to succeed once. “But, as defenders, we need to make sure that every single vulnerability is covered,” Schwartz says.

AI is helping defenders find those vulnerabilities first. Previously, testing suspected bugs could take weeks, forcing researchers to prioritize which were most promising. But now AI agents allow researchers to test many ideas at once, Tzadik says. “It’s months of traditional research condensed to, like, days—couple hours to days, really.”

But even with AI, the process is labor intensive. After the agent helped Wiz uncover the PHP vulnerability, it still couldn’t describe how the bug worked, Tzadik says. Its explanations were “just slop.” He and colleagues spent days untangling the cause of a bug and eventually reproducing it.

Cost is another constraint. Joshua Saxe, Meta’s former AI security tech lead, who left in February to co-found Abundance Security says he’s working with an organization that has spent roughly $1 million using Anthropic’s Mythos to identify bugs, adding that more bugs remain.

“As an industry, I think we’re deer in the headlights right now,” Saxe says.

"In the climate that we’re in today, you have to do cybersecurity well. It’s not an option to kind of half-a-- it,” says Adam Meyers, senior vice president of counter adversary operations at CrowdStrike. But even a successful exploit offers further opportunities for defenders to intervene. “Exploits are going to happen, and that’s not the end of the story,” he says. “It’s the beginning of the attack.”

Scientists Discover a Hidden Cholesterol Switch That Could Change How We Treat Heart Disease

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Researchers uncovered how a high-cholesterol diet weakens the liver’s defenses and lowered LDL in mice by blocking the pathway, pointing to a potential new use for a shelved heart failure drug.

A diet high in cholesterol can gradually weaken the liver’s ability to remove harmful cholesterol from the blood. Cholesterol-related heart disease remains the leading cause of death worldwide, even as doctors have gained more medications to treat it.

Researchers at the University of California San Diego School of Medicine have identified a previously unknown biological pathway that helps explain why the liver’s cholesterol-clearing capacity declines. Their study, published in Nature, combined experiments in mice and human cells and identified a potential treatment target. An experimental drug that acts on that target has already undergone initial safety testing in humans, potentially shortening the route to a clinical trial for high cholesterol.

The liver normally removes cholesterol from the blood so it can be broken down and used elsewhere. Proteins called LDL receptors sit on the surface of liver cells, where they capture harmful low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and pull it inside for processing. More receptors mean more cholesterol can be cleared. Most cholesterol-lowering drugs, including statins and PCSK9 inhibitors, work by increasing or preserving the number of these receptors.

Some patients, however, cannot tolerate the side effects of available medications.

“There’s still a real need for new cholesterol-lowering options, since some people can’t get to safe levels even with the drugs we have now,” said senior author Alan Saltiel, PhD, professor of medicine at UC San Diego School of Medicine and director of the UC San Diego/UCLA Diabetes Research Center. “This new pathway we discovered is completely separate from anything that existing drugs target, so it gives us a new opportunity to fill that gap.”

The newly identified pathway works against cholesterol removal by gradually depleting LDL receptors. It begins when high dietary cholesterol activates Ral, a protein Saltiel previously studied in fat cells. As Ral activity increases, fewer LDL receptors remain available to pull cholesterol from the bloodstream, contributing to higher blood cholesterol.

That receptor loss ultimately depends on an enzyme called cathepsin A, or CTSA. When the researchers blocked CTSA with a small molecule inhibitor, they stabilized LDL receptors and sharply lowered circulating LDL cholesterol in mice.

“We’ve known for a long time that a high-cholesterol diet reduces the liver’s ability to clear cholesterol from the blood, but we didn’t fully understand why,” said Saltiel. “This new discovery explains a critical piece of that puzzle.”

Turning a biological discovery into a treatment usually requires substantial additional research to find a suitable drug. In this case, a CTSA inhibitor had already been developed as a potential treatment for heart failure. It reached a Phase 1 clinical trial, where it was successfully tested for safety, before being shelved for strategic reasons.

That earlier work could allow the investigational drug to move into a Phase 2 trial for high cholesterol. The reduction in LDL cholesterol demonstrated in mice has yet to be established in people.

“Luckily, there’s an experimental drug sitting on the shelf that’s already been shown to be safe in humans,” said Saltiel. “We hope to test whether this might be effective by conducting a clinical trial — which could potentially bring a new treatment option to patients much sooner than would have been expected.”

President Donald Trump on Friday continued to defend a highly controversial ad from Ohio GOP gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy featuring his Democratic opponent Amy Acton's estranged mother -- who Acton says stood by the man who was criminally charged with sexually abusing her as a child.

As he left the White House Friday morning, Trump repeatedly dismissed questions from reporters about the specific allegations of Acton's sexual abuse, asserting at multiple points that the ad has "nothing to do" with Acton's stepfather, who was criminally charged and pleaded not guilty.

Her stepfather, who died in 2004, fled the state with her mother before trial and never appeared before a judge, according to court records and an interview given by Acton's mother.

"It's a mother speaking badly against Amy Acton. And, to me, it's very easy," Trump said when asked about the ad, which features Acton's mother, Donna Arthur, saying that her daughter has lied about her past and her struggles growing up.

Acton has been public about her experience with abuse and openly spoken about her tumultuous relationship with her mother, whom she says she has not spoken to since she was 12 years old. Acton alleged her mother's second husband molested her between the ages of 9 and 12.

Pressed on the fact that Acton has been estranged from her mother for decades, Trump said he did not care and insisted that the ad was fair game.

“I don't care,” Trump said. “If it were my mother speaking badly about me, you'd be making it the number one story on the news."

Trump also referred to Acton’s mother as a "smart woman" and said that he did not "know anything about" the stepfather.

“This has nothing to do with a stepfather or anything else," Trump said. "This has to do with the fact that a woman got out, the mother of a person running for governor of one of the greatest states, where I set records and said that she's a liar."

Trump defended the ad on Thursday afternoon as well, writing on social media that the ad was "powerful" and that he himself would run such an ad.

Several key Republicans have blasted the ad with Ohio's Republican Gov. Mike DeWine saying on Thursday that the ad is "deeply, deeply troubling" and that Ramaswamy's campaign should cease airing it.

Iowa Republican Senate candidate Rep. Ashley Hinson also condemned the ad and said to ABC News Chief Washington Correspondent Jonathan Karl that "they should take the ad down" -- just hours before she shared the stage with Trump at a campaign rally in Sioux City, Iowa, Thursday night.

Trump spoke about the ad during the rally, saying that he advised Ramaswamy to "ride with it."

“When I first saw it, I said, ‘Wow, that means Vivek easily will win the race,’” Trump recalled of the controversial ad, later adding "I said, 'you got to ride with it.'"

Acton has criticized the ad, calling it "horrible and hideous" during a campaign event on Wednesday, adding that the ad is "despicable" and "an exploitative thing to do."

Ramaswamy addressed the ad during a rally on Friday, saying it "had nothing to do with childhood abuse," according to audio provided by Cleveland ABC affiliate WEWS.

Product-market fit — when a startup’s product sells well — has become even harder to find in this AI era. Despite startups achieving record-fast growth, not all customers are sticky and products can be replicated more easily than ever.

Anu Bharadwaj, a partner at Iconiq; Rajeev Dham, a partner at Sapphire Ventures; and Rahul Vohra, the founder of Superhuman Mail, discussed the new rules for product-market fit last week at TechCrunch Disrupt.

“It’s never been easier to gain a bunch of pilots because lots of companies across the board are open to trying new things,” Bharadwaj said to the audience. “But going from pilot to production is really the hard thing.”

Right now, she said, a lot of founders pitch revenue metrics to investors without specifying which revenue they are talking about. “Is it true ARR or is it run rate?” she said. “There’s many ways to obfuscate the whole thing.”

Lately, much chatter on X has focused on AI founders inflating ARR numbers (which could mean “annualized run rate” or the more solid “annual recurring revenue”) to make it seem like their companies are doing better than they are. A common gimmick is counting pilot customers, who might only use the product for a couple of months, in the annualized run rate revenue number, implying that income is expected to continue for the entire year.

It should be obvious, but Bharadwaj pointed out, “It’s definitely in your long-term interest that you be very honest and upfront about what quality of revenue this is.”

She said companies need to separate hype from reality when looking at the quality of their revenue. It’s a red flag, for example, if only a few customers make up much of a company’s revenue or if the customers can easily go to another vendor for a better price. “Churn rate and retention [are] really dependent on that,” she continued.

That’s why Vohra advises startups to track customer engagement and dollar retention, especially for consumer products. “Figure out what benchmark [is] good, great, and excellent for your space,” he said.

For example, at Superhuman, he said that the company tracked ARR but also had their own internal metric called “delighted ARR,” which, he said, “was the ARR only from customers who are super engaged with the product.” He said that metric helped them see how sticky the customers were and how long they were likely to stay around.

“Those users are now still users, those companies are now still customers,” he continued. “We obsessed over serving that particular category of our user base and our customer base.”

The panelists also discussed green flags that indicate a startup has indeed found product-market fit. For Vohra, he said talk to customers to understand what value they find in the product and how they would feel if they could no longer use it. Then be honest with yourself when measuring those responses.

“If you’re 15%, 14%, 13%, or below, consider a complete pivot. Take the codebase, take the team, build something entirely new. “But if you’re 20%, you can totally optimize your way from 20% to 40%,” Vohra said.

Actually, Dham said, it’s important for a founder not to confuse having a vision with having a good business.

At the seed level especially, Bharadwaj said that it’s fine to pivot. She doesn’t even look for product-market fit at that stage, but “does the founder have a thesis that seems plausible and do they have the agility to duck and wave as the situation changes?” she said.

Dham added that he values founders even more these days who have made numerous pivots because it shows they’ve had to make very hard decisions in this ever-shifting moment.

Product-market fit, therefore, is something founders will always have to redefine and re-earn. “Even at a billion in revenue, these companies are re-earning their product-market fit,” he said. “You constantly have to be innovating on products.”

La IA impulsa inversiones extremas

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

El capital riesgo tecnológico vive un cambio radical impulsado por el auge de la IA y el retroceso del software tradicional. Los inversores han abandonado su clásica aversión al riesgo para respaldar ideas antes consideradas imposibles, como la fusión nuclear o la tecnología espacial.

El boom de la inteligencia artificial está impulsando un resurgimiento de las apuestas ambiciosas, ya que los enormes rendimientos obtenidos por los primeros inversores en SpaceX, sumados a la caída en las valoraciones de las empresas de software tradicionales, obligan a los inversores tecnológicos a adoptar operaciones más arriesgadas y que requieren un mayor capital.

Ideas que en su momento parecían descabelladas -desde la fusión nuclear hasta la integración de seres humanos con máquinas- están captando la atención de firmas de capital riesgo que, hace tan sólo unos años, jamás habrían apostado por start up en estos sectores

“El mundo ha cambiado, sin duda”, afirma Matt Robinson, socio de la firma de capital riesgo Accel. “Si pensamos en cualquier firma de capital riesgo, veremos que el tipo de empresas de las que han estado hablando en los últimos años ha cambiado por completo”, añade.

Sin contar las enormes sumas destinadas a start up de IA, la inversión global en empresas cuyos productos se basan en importantes avances científicos o de ingeniería ha superado los 150.000 millones de dólares desde principios de 2024; una cifra superior a los 133.000 millones invertidos durante toda la década anterior a finales de 2019, según datos de Dealroom.

Los fundadores más ambiciosos de Silicon Valley celebran que los inversores vuelvan a asumir mayores riesgos, tras un largo periodo, posterior al estallido de la burbuja tecnológica, en el que las firmas de capital riesgo se centraron casi exclusivamente en empresas de software para el sector empresarial, cuyos resultados eran más previsibles.

“Durante una época, cuando Internet era algo nuevo y estaba en pleno crecimiento, las start up más rentables eran, sin duda, las de software”, explica Max Hodak, cofundador de Science, una empresa emergente dedicada a las interfaces cerebro-computadora. “Eso le restó mucho oxígeno al sector del hardware, que es más complejo. No se sabe si fue beneficioso. Probablemente no, pero hemos vuelto”, asegura. Hodak, que también cofundó Neuralink junto a Elon Musk, sostiene que el interés de las firmas de capital riesgo por el software fue, en realidad, un desvío respecto de las iniciativas de hardware que hace décadas permitieron el surgimiento de Silicon Valley. “El capital riesgo original -el de toda la vida- fue el que financió la construcción de los ferrocarriles. Esa es, en esencia, la magia del capitalismo”, afirmó.

Las inversiones en deep tech de este año aún no han superado el pico alcanzado en 2021, impulsado entonces por operaciones relacionadas con baterías y vehículos eléctricos -como las de Rivian y Northvolt-, muchas de las cuales terminaron mal, lo que pone de relieve los riesgos que implica apostar por proyectos muy ambiciosos.

Destinos viajeros que amplían el concepto de lujo

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Viajes ultratop para alejarse del ruido, pero sin renunciar al confort y el servicio.

El parque Kruger está de estreno. El pasado 1 de agosto abrió sus puertas Sandringham Private Game Reserve, un paraíso para los amantes de los safaris, pero también para aquellos que busquen un entorno natural, tranquilo y aislado sin renunciar a la comodidad de un servicio muy cuidado. ¿Es esto el nuevo lujo? “Estamos pasando de un lujo pensado para impresionar a uno diseñado para disfrutar. Para una parte del viajero de alta gama, lo importante no es que los demás sepan dónde está, sino poder elegir con quién comparte su espacio y cómo utiliza su tiempo. El nuevo lujo es poder desaparecer sin renunciar a nada, alejarse del ruido y de las obligaciones, pero no del confort, del buen servicio ni de una experiencia extraordinaria”, define Enrique Bellver CEO de Maestroviajes.

Esta nueva reserva forma parte de Sabi Sabi Collection, grupo referente de la hospitalidad de lujo en Sudáfrica, y está ubicada en la confluencia de cuatro ríos y bajo la imponente silueta de las montañas Drakensberg. Se extiende sobre aproximadamente 4.500 hectáreas de naturaleza protegida, que, tras cuatro años de rehabilitación, ha pasado de ser un terreno olvidado a convertirse en un ecosistema protegido, seguro y sin vallas donde la fauna ha vuelto a confiar en el ser humano y puede disfrutar en su hábitat natural, regalando al huésped una experiencia realmente auténtica. ¿Está el cliente dispuesto a pagar más por este tipo de experiencias? “La disposición a pagar más se entiende mejor cuando dejamos de hablar únicamente de habitaciones y hablamos de lo que ese viaje permite vivir. Cuando una familia reserva una embarcación completa, no está comprando camarotes, sino intimidad, tiempo juntos y la tranquilidad de compartir la experiencia con quienes ha elegido. No se paga solo por un lugar excepcional, sino por la libertad de disfrutarlo de una manera propia y sin interrupciones”, explica Bellver.

En este contexto, los detalles son cada vez más importantes. En el caso de Sandringham Private Game Reserve, hay lujos contemporáneos, fáciles de encontrar en otros establecimientos, como piscina, gimnasio, spa, biblioteca o bodega, pero el huésped tiene la oportunidad de disfrutar también de servicios realmente únicos. Por ejemplo, la suite más exclusiva es Sandringham House, una residencia de 1.100 metros cuadrados con cuatro dormitorios en suite y a la que se asigna un equipo privado de trabajadores que incluye chef, mayordomo y ranger con vehículo propio para recorrer la reserva. Un hogar lejos de casa que redefine el concepto de exclusividad y que da la oportunidad de ver ejemplares de los cinco grandes de África –león, leopardo, rinoceronte, búfalo y elefante–. Un deseo que muchos viajes organizados prometen en el Continente negro y que no siempre se cumple. “Hay algo que el dinero no puede multiplicar: los días que podemos dedicar a nuestra pareja, a nuestros hijos o a nosotros mismos. Evitar fricciones, sentirse bien atendido y no tener que organizar cada detalle puede tener tanto valor como el alojamiento. El verdadero lujo no debería darte más cosas de las que ocuparte, sino menos”, asegura el fundador de Maestroviajes.

La atmósfera elegante y residencial de la villa Sandringham House nace de una composición de materiales naturales, como la madera, la piedra y el uso de tonos tierra, haciendo de cada rincón un espacio cómodo y acogedor. Desde la terraza con piscina hasta las vistas sobre el río y la reserva. “No confundiría precio con exclusividad. Una habitación puede ser muy cara y ofrecer una experiencia bastante convencional. Lo verdaderamente especial aparece cuando hay algo difícil de sustituir: una ubicación, una historia, una forma de conocer el destino o una atención que entiende qué necesita cada persona”, define Bellver.

Según Virtuoso Luxe Report 2027, que recoge la opinión de más de 3.100 asesores de viajes de más de cincuenta países, el gasto en viajes seguirá en aumento el próximo año, pero algunas tendencias tendrán más impacto que otras en las cuentas de los viajeros. Por ejemplo, el presupuesto sigue siendo un factor relevante, pero en alta gama deja de serlo cuando el itinerario se construye desde el contraste, combinando la intensidad de un safari con la comodidad de la playa, la energía de una ciudad con la tranquilidad del campo o una profunda inmersión cultural con tiempo para relajarse. También serán relevantes los viajes intergeneracionales, que unen a familias en un destino ultratop y que se diseñan como un capricho para todos. Esta forma de viajar está definiendo el ultralujo, que según los encuestados aumentará un 47% en 2027.

El lujo hoy no consiste en ofrecer lo mismo a un precio más alto, sino en entender que no todo el mundo busca lo mismo. Este concepto de ultrapersonalización está cambiando la forma de viajar, sobre todo en el segmento de alta gama. Un ejemplo es Sandringham House, una residencia de 1.100 metros cuadrados con cuatro dormitorios en suite, que es la joya de la corona de Sandringham Private Game Reserve. Esta reserva, que se inauguró el pasado mes de agosto, se extiende sobre aproximadamente 4.500 hectáreas de naturaleza protegida en el parque Kruger y ofrece una experiencia que suma aventura y confort.

Palau, un remoto archipiélago de Micronesia situado en el Pacífico occidental, a unos 800 kilómetros al este de Filipinas, tiene más de 340 islas esmeralda, lagunas protegidas por la Unesco, arrecifes intactos y una cultura con más de 4.000 años de historia. En este destino remoto no hay complejos turísticos ni lugares masificados. El privilegio no es tener el Nilo a tus pies, sino recorrerlo desde un barco que es solo para ti”, explica Bellver, que vende cada vez más este tipo de experiencias exclusivas.

Pluralistic: Critique and writing (23 Oct 2026)

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

Through my own writing life, nothing has done more to improve my writing than diligent participation in peer-based workshops; that's where a group of writers, all at about the same stage in their artistic development, gather to read one another's work and offer feedback specifically aimed at improving each other's stories.

This is a deceptively simple exercise, and the reasons it works so well are quite counter-intuitive. The most obvious benefit is the way that a workshop encourages you to write at all. When you're at the start of your writing career, you are almost totally reliant upon your own internal self-discipline to keep producing.

Acquiring that self-discipline is infinitely valuable, but it's also incredibly hard. Being part of a group of writers who expect you to regularly turn in new work adds a very useful bit of external motivation that augments your still nascent self-discipline. Knowing you have to turn in a story for the group by Friday can get you in gear when you feel discouraged or blocked.

Before you're getting published regularly (or at all), your critiquing circle might be the only source of validation you get for your work, so the knowledge that you're in for that validation if you get that story in on time is a great motivator. But this is where the counter-intuitive part comes in: as great as it feels to be the center of your peers' attention and to get close feedback on the work you sweated over, that's not the real value you get from a writing workshop, at least not at the start of your career.

Your peers' critiques can help you fix weaknesses in your story, and even teach you a little about how to avoid those weaknesses in future work. You might get feedback that turns an unpublishable story into a published one. But the point of a writing workshop isn't merely to make your stories better – it's to make you better at writing stories.

That's where critiquing other writers' stories comes in. The process of reading and re-reading someone else's imperfect work and devoting real mental energy to figuring out what's not working in it and how to fix it is how you develop the creative instincts that allow you to automatically and unconsciously avoid pitfalls and make breakthroughs in your own work.

Even if none of the feedback you offer is ever incorporated into the story you're critiquing, the act of preparing and delivering that critique is the most reliable fast-track to becoming a better writer yourself. There's something very valuable in the combination of thinking critically, being exposed to other workshop members' ideas about the story under discussion, and working with source material that you can look at with more objectivity because it didn't come out of your own head.

I bring all this up because I've now heard from a few beginning writers who tell me that they're finding LLMs to be good editorial feedback generators. They value the editorial suggestions they get from a chatbot after they finish their work, and use it to help them with a new draft. Without commenting on whether I think LLM feedback is editorially useful (I certainly wouldn't ever ask a chatbot for that kind of feedback), I want to point out that when it comes to improving your writing, getting editorial notes on your work is distinctly inferior to offering criticism of other writers' work.

The reality is that when you're starting out as a writer, the vast majority of work you produce will be artistically unsalvageable. The much-vaunted "million words of crap" you write on the way to becoming a publishable writer are about learning to marry technique to artistic vision and to sharpen both. Getting editorial feedback that helps you shore up the weakest spots in a story that is ultimately never published and constitutes a learning exercise has value, but it has far less value than applying the things you've learned from making and fixing those mistakes to your next story.

With vanishingly few exceptions, a commercially and artistically successful writer isn't someone who writes one great story or novel: a writing career is defined by a body of work, which means that you have to do more than rewrite. You have to write the next thing, and the next thing, and the next.

Having an editor-on-demand of highly variable (and often dubious) quality might help you improve your writing, but it's no substitute for participating in a peer workshop. The idea that an LLM might be able to stand in for a workshop is grounded in the fallacy that the point of a workshop is to have people tell you how to fix your story, rather than analyzing other people's writing in order to develop your own critical skills.

Look: it's a cliche to say "every writer is different," but I really believe that every writer is different, and that's down to the legibility of the writing process to other writers. If you're a painter or sculptor or other visual artist, you've likely spent many hours in a studio with many other practitioners while all of you work from the same life model or still life. If you're a musician, you've almost certainly played music with other musicians. If you're a dancer, you've definitely danced with other dancers.

But writers get precious little exposure to other writers' process. At best we might look at each other's drafts. A musician can listen to every note another musician produces while riffing or composing a new work or interpreting a work. Writers don't get to watch words emerge from each other's fingertips. Being observed that closely while you write would be ghastly and paralyzingly invasive.

I've taught a lot of writers over the years and I've had many discussions with my peers about their writing process, and I'm here to tell you that pretty much every writer finds every other writer's process baffling. We each of us invent an idiosyncratic process for ourselves that will never fully work for anyone else.

So if you value editorial feedback from a chatbot (ideally, a local one running on your computer and not one of the "foundation models" that are torching the planet and elevating the worst people on earth to positions of political, technical and commercial prominence), that's fine, you do you.

But even if that's the case, I still want you to know that whatever you're getting out of the chatbot is fundamentally different, and (I believe) much less useful than participating in a peer workshop.

That's what I wanted to say today, but I predict that an appreciable number of readers are about to email me to ask how these "peer workshops" work. There's a ton of variability in how these are conducted, but the basic shape of them is that in advance of each session, one or more writers submits a finished work to the other writers, who then read through the manuscript at least twice, making notes that include both minor copyedits and broader, thematic notes. They may write a brief editorial response as well, ranging from a paragraph to a couple of pages.

Then the workshop convenes. Each writer briefly expresses their views about the story under consideration, focusing on those thematic and structural notes on what worked and didn't work in the story, with suggestions for improving the latter. You don't need to point out every typo – they're noted on the manuscript. Critiquers don't need to repeat one another. If another workshop member says the thing you were planning to say, you can just say "Ditto what so-and-so said about whatever." If you have nothing to add when your turn comes about, it's fine to pass.

Those are pretty much universal characteristics of a peer workshop. Here are some variations that I am dogmatically committed to, but which other workshoppers might disagree with:

The subject of each session must not speak during the critiques, except to answer direct questions that are not related to the writing itself (e.g., "I couldn't figure out what word this was supposed to be – can you clarify?"). The subject can speak after all the other writers have spoken.

Each person in the critiquing circle speaks once, in turn, without being interrupted. No cross-talk allowed. It is useful to set a time-limit for critiques.

No partial manuscripts. It's not really possible to critique a story unless you have the complete tale. Novels can be workshopped as three chapters and an outline.

Stories should be distributed in advance and read in advance. Don't have writers read their work aloud for critique, as this can lead to critiquing the delivery rather than the material.

Critique the story, not the writer. It's fine to be offended by something in the story, but not OK to accuse your workshopmates of being bad people for having offended you.

Discourage resubmission of revised stories. Workshopping produces sharply diminishing returns. If you really want feedback on a rewrite, approach people individually and ask if they'll read the new draft.

Any group of three or more writers can start a workshop. You'll need to locate writers who are at about the same stage in their artistic and professional lives as you, and find a physical place (or a videoconference service) where you can meet. Library meeting rooms are a favorite, and so is rotating between workshop members' homes. Set a regular schedule – weekly, fortnightly, monthly, possibly quarterly – and stick to it.

The “enslopification” of B2B marketing is here

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There’s a phenomenon happening in advertising and marketing. If you’ve been on LinkedIn lately, you’ve experienced it in the endless scroll of bland thought leadership posts. Your inbox is filled with decks and documents that your coworkers and vendors are churning out, expecting you to decipher.

The verbose, garishly-designed, and ultimately meaningless content you now have to wade through daily has a name: “enslopification.”

It’s a variation on Cory Doctorow’s coined term “enshittification.” He used it to describe the inevitable decay of a digital platform (in his example, TikTok) as it abandons user experience in a relentless pursuit to squeeze out as much profit as possible. “Enslopification” is what happens when content trends toward the same AI-flattened, meaningless mush, because generating content has suddenly become frictionless, homogenized, and free (for now).

THE AI EFFECTS

We are now seeing the effects. Instead of sharing unique insights, marketers are pushing cheap, identical noise into the market. Audiences can feel the difference between real opinions and AI slop, even when they can’t put a finger on it.

To hone the Quantious team’s “slop detectors,” we recently hosted a workshop pitting producer-written copy and original visuals against AI drafts. Everyone was able to spot the AI giveaways, agreeing that while the synthetic text and images were functional, they completely lacked soul, character, and depth. There was just something “icky” about them.

This is the biggest threat to B2B marketing, and dare I say small agencies. We leverage AI to work faster with fewer resources, pushing to hold onto our margins, and to land that next dream client. But if we let the slop slip by, we risk sawing off the branch we’re sitting on.

5 WAYS TO CHANGE COURSE

As an industry, we need to change course, fast. These five principles are our only defense.

1. Share a clear point of view that can only come from you

At their core, LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude are probability engines built to anticipate the most likely next word. Without providing deeper context, that results in bland content that tries to appeal to everyone. And if you know anything about marketing, you know most products shouldn’t.

B2B buyers are exhausted from sorting through this slop. They are drowning in information and thirsty for interpretation. The brands cutting through the noise are the ones willing to say something original and relevant. They are telling real, first-hand stories that their audience can relate to.

2. Develop content that actually earns attention

Modern buyers don’t wade through 4,000-word blog posts. They engage with content that is brief, visual, and conversational.

As a small marketing team, we’ve leveraged AI to help with social media content. But in addition to our growing list of ways to ensure more strategic and on-brand output, we’ve developed a simple test. Before publishing, we ask: “Would this content stop me mid-scroll?” If the answer is no, we pull back and look for ways to have a stronger point of view, be instantly relatable, and provide more value.

3. Build trust from the start

Before you ever get a chance to pitch a buyer, they may have seen your website, visited your LinkedIn profile, received a cold email, or read your case studies. If any of that is AI slop content, their trust is eroded instantly.

That means every touchpoint has to do more than fill space. Show your work. Share the details. Let your experts have opinions. Use real examples instead of vague claims about “driving transformation.” Give buyers enough substance to decide you know what you’re talking about and you truly understand their challenges—before you ask for 30 minutes on their calendar.

4. Ensure human judgement behind the machine

Your team might use AI, but do they think with it? AI can give you 50 headline options, summarize your research, and help you get from concept to implementation in a jiffy. But it can also turn out something that looks impressive and thorough, but is really just 10 pages of word salad.

For example, we recently had a B2B client come to us with an urgent campaign request. They presented us with a detailed project brief, so we dove in. At first glance, it seemed organized. At second glance, we noticed a few AI “tells.” And then we realized that the deliverables list didn’t make sense, the due dates were unreasonable, and creative reference assets didn’t actually exist. We were almost two days in before we could craft and approve a real project brief and get everyone aligned.

It showed us (and our client) just how vital human experience and judgment are, especially when the deadlines are real.

5. Build community, not just a lead list

Connection is the antidote to slop exhaustion: More and more, we want proof there’s a person on the other end.

This can be as formal as a customer advisory board, or as informal as a monthly office hours call with your key customers. We recently put this into practice at a conference, staffing our booth with the marketers who do the work every day (not just salespeople).

We want the first conversation with us to feel like chatting with a future colleague, not a sales pitch. When you build real connection, sales become natural.

And you know what? Our clients LOVE that we are centered on our humanity. They want to work with people who care enough to question a brief and have an honest conversation when something isn’t working.

We all have access to tools that can crank out a month’s worth of marketing in an afternoon. Before we unleash it on everyone, we owe them a little more thought. For the future of our industry, let’s stop the slop.
