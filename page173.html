Als im Sommer Tausende Migrantinnen und Migranten aus Afrika in der spanischen Exklave Ceuta ankamen, wurde in der EU der Ruf nach einem Notfall-Mechanismus laut. Bei solchen Massenankünften sollte das EU-Asylverfahren ausgesetzt werden können, fordern manche Regierungen. Dagegen regt sich jetzt Kritik, wie SRF-Korrespondent Matthias Strasser in Brüssel weiss.

Matthias Strasser ist Korrespondent für die EU und die Benelux-Staaten. Der Historiker ist seit 2019 für Radio SRF tätig. Davor war er Bundeshauskorrespondent für private Radiostationen in Bern.

Weil die Grundrechte gefährdet sein könnten. Die zuständige Agentur der EU mit Sitz in Wien sieht die Gefahr, dass die Massenankunft in Ceuta die Balance zwischen dem legitimen Schutz der EU-Aussengrenzen und dem Schutz der Grundrechte der Flüchtenden durcheinanderbringt. Es geht vor allem ums Prinzip, dass niemand in ein Land zurückgewiesen werden darf, in dem ihm oder ihr Tod oder unmenschliche Behandlung drohen. Dieses «Non-Refoulement-Prinzip» ist in der Genfer Flüchtlingskonvention festgeschrieben. Die EU-Grundrechteagentur bezeichnet es als «Eckstein des Flüchtlingsschutzes». Wenn die EU einen Notfallmechanismus für Situationen wie Ceuta schaffen wolle, dann müsse dieser in jedem Fall mit dem internationalen Recht und mit den EU-Regeln konform sein.

Fast drei Monate nach der Massenankunft in der spanischen Nordafrika-Exklave Ceuta haben die Regierungen Spaniens und Italiens ihren Streit über Einreisekontrollen beigelegt. Sie vereinbarten, die Aussetzung des Schengen-Abkommens nicht mehr zu verlängern und bei Grenzübertritten keine Passkontrollen mehr durchzuführen. Die italienische Premierministerin Giorgia Meloni wollte damit sicherstellen, dass keine Migranten aus Ceuta nach Italien weiterreisen können, woraufhin die Regierung in Madrid mit ähnlichen Schritten reagierte. (reuters)

Vor Ort, an der Grenze, geht das fast nur mit einem weiteren Ausbau der Grenzbefestigungen und Überwachung. Wie ein Notfallmechanismus rechtlich ausgestaltet werden soll, ist noch offen. Kommissionspräsidentin Ursula von der Leyen sprach davon, dass «unter gewissen Bedingungen und für begrenzte Zeit» von den Standardverfahren im Asylbereich abgewichen werden solle. Migrationskommissar Magnus Brunner sagte gegenüber SRF, er wolle das nicht «Pushback» nennen, aber es gehe darum, flexibler und schneller zu reagieren.

Einige Staats- und Regierungschefs können sich durchaus vorstellen, das Recht auf Zugang zum Asylverfahren nicht mehr in jedem Fall zu gewähren. Griechenland, Deutschland und Österreich etwa haben sich im Grundsatz dafür ausgesprochen. Die Begründung leuchtet zunächst ein: Migration hat sich seit Unterzeichnung der Flüchtlingskonvention nach dem Zweiten Weltkrieg verändert. Sie findet heute viel öfter statt und ist teilweise von anderen Motiven angetrieben. Und sie kann über die sozialen Medien gesteuert und manipuliert werden – und so zum Druckmittel gegen die EU werden.

Ihre Stellungnahme ist politisch nicht verbindlich. Sie ist eher ein EU-interner Zeigefinger in dieser laufenden Debatte. Klar ist: Die EU muss eine Antwort finden auf Herausforderungen wie die in Ceuta. Mit einer Einschränkung des Zugangs zum Asylverfahren würde aber das absolute Prinzip, dass niemand zurückgeschickt werden darf, dem unmenschliche Behandlung droht, nicht mehr garantiert. Denn die EU könnte nicht wissen, wem sie die Türe verschliesst, wenn diese erst einmal zu ist.

Und wenn dieser Grundsatz nicht mehr gilt, stellen sich viele Fragen: Wann ist es ein Notfall? Wann ist die Belastung gross genug für eine Aussetzung des Asylrechts? Die Beantwortung wäre eine politische Diskussion, die wohl nicht immer von allen Mitgliedsstaaten gleich beurteilt würde. Die Stellungnahme der Grundrechte-Agentur ist daher vor allem als Mahnung zu lesen, im Sinne von: Seid ihr euch bewusst, welchen Weg ihr da gerade geht?

India slashes cost of cancer drugs after court rules mark-ups are ‘extortion’

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Case filed in 2022 by patient unable to afford medicine leads to cap on drug prices for millions who face ruin if diagnosed

The Indian government has moved to reduce the price of cancer drugs after a retired bank clerk took her case to the supreme court.

From next month, 137 cancer drugs in India will be cheaper as the government tries to tackle the tragedy of poorer patients either forgoing treatment, selling land or falling into debt to meet the high cost of some medicines, which are making large profits for hospitals and pharmacists.

Sarojam Radhakrishnan, a former bank employee in Kerala, was diagnosed with breast cancer in 2021. Doctors prescribed life-saving targeted treatments but the monthly cost, of more than 63,000 rupees (£500), was far beyond her family’s means.

Realising that thousands of families were being ruined by the cost of such drugs, Radhakrishnan filed a petition to the Kerala high court in 2022 demanding government intervention to lower the costs.

When the case reached the supreme court, the judges looked at hospitals’ and retailers’ mark-ups on cancer drugs and called the situation “carnage” and “extortion”.

During a hearing on 29 September, justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta noted how a life-saving cancer drug supplied to hospitals at 2,700 rupees carried a vastly marked-up price of 27,000 rupees.

The judges found that hospitals, distributors and chemists enjoyed large margins on medicines. Analysis by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority revealed a trade mark-up on some cancer drugs averaging 170%, with some extreme examples stretching to more than 700%.

A 30% trade margin cap on the price of 42 drugs has been in place for some years but many others remained off this list. Now, prodded by the supreme court’s findings, the government has imposed the cap on 137 other drugs.

“The measure is expected to reduce medicine prices by up to 70% and help cancer patients save 2,500 crore rupees [£199m] annually, significantly reducing the amount they pay from their own pockets,” a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said.

People on low incomes in India dread illness, as the cost of treatment can mean financial ruin, particularly for cancer patients.

Whenever the Indian Cancer Society holds a free diagnosis camp in Gurgaon where he lives, Krishan Kalra urges local drivers, maids, cooks and tailors to turn up because tests they cannot afford are offered free.

As a former member of the council of the Rajiv Gandhi Cancer Institute and Research Centre, Kalra knows the importance of early diagnosis, but he said very few people attended. “When I ask why, they say they’re too scared – if something serious like cancer is detected, they haven’t got the money to pay for the treatment so they prefer to remain in the dark,” he said.

With the cost of cancer drugs ranging in price from 38,000 to 380,000 rupees, many families quietly walk out of the doctor’s consulting room upon hearing the estimated cost of treatment.

Studies by the Public Health Foundation of India show that spending on healthcare pushes about 55 million Indians a year below the poverty line.

Few Indians can afford the premiums for comprehensive health insurance. The government’s nationwide insurance scheme, Ayushman Bharat, covers treatment for some cancers but it provides only 500,000 rupees’ worth of annual cover for a family – the cost of just one dose of Kadcyla (ado-trastuzumab emtansine), a drug used to treat breast cancer. Patients may need up to 14 doses.

Dr Ravi Kannan is a surgical oncologist and director of Cachar cancer hospital and research centre in Silchar, Assam. He has worked in hospitals elsewhere in India and is familiar with doctors’ trauma of seeing patients walk out to go home and die.

“Some families start the treatment, hoping against hope to manage the cost. They start but they don’t come back for chemotherapy or radiation. They can’t sustain the cost,” he said. “The decision is a welcome step if the outcome is that more patients can access the drugs they need.”

India records about 1.56m new cancer cases annually – up from 1.39m cases in 2020 – making it a significant public health challenge.

As the supreme court delves deeper into the subject, what was clear, said Jyotsna Singh, co-convener of the Working Group on Access to Medicines and Treatments, a civil society network, was that a “total overhaul” of the healthcare system was needed.

“Right from the cost of manufacturing a drug to the point of sale, we need to make the system transparent and regulate the entire supply chain. Only then will these drugs become available to those who need them,” she said.

Singh said companies and hospitals were likely to try to find legal loopholes to wriggle out of the 30% cap, suggesting that hospitals may offset the lower price charged for a drug by raising charges on other items.

The supreme court judges have also asked the government’s lawyers why the cap should apply only to cancer drugs and not the entire range of non-patented drugs. The court has given the government until 27 October to respond.

Unfortunately, the court’s ruling came too late for Radhakrishnan, who died in September 2022 while her case was still pending in Kerala.

A 250-Million-Year-Old Habit May Explain How Crocodiles Outlasted the Dinosaurs

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

It was their preference for warmer climates.

Some 66 million years ago, an asteroid slammed into Earth, bringing the age of giants to a catastrophic end. T. rex, Triceratops, and all other non-avian dinosaurs vanished. Only one branch of the dinosaur family tree survived—birds.

But what about another great prehistoric lineage? How on Earth did crocodiles survive?

After all, the broader crocodile lineage had already existed for roughly 180 million years when the asteroid struck. Its evolutionary family tree included colossal predators that could attack dinosaurs, armored plant eaters, and strange creatures that looked nothing like the crocodiles we know today.

A new study offers a surprising explanation. Despite their extraordinary diversity, crocodiles and their ancient relatives spent roughly 250 million years following a remarkably consistent rule: follow the warmth.

That seemingly simple preference may have put some of them in exactly the right places when disaster struck.

Today’s crocodilians are variations on an exceptionally effective ambush-predator design. Their armored bodies lurk unseen beneath the waterline, while eyes and nostrils perched high on their skulls remain exposed. Powerful tails propel them forward, exploding into action with muscular jaws seizing unsuspecting prey.

It’s a successful design, but for most of their evolutionary history, crocodile relatives were anything but predictable.

More than 200 million years ago, phytosaurs patrolled Triassic rivers looking uncannily like modern crocodiles, complete with long snouts and armored bodies. Their nostrils, however, sat much farther back, near their eyes. Whether phytosaurs belonged to the crocodile evolutionary branch or were more distant relatives remains debated. Either way, they were an extraordinary example of evolution producing similar solutions to similar problems.

But the crocodile family tree was never limited to this aquatic ambush-predator blueprint.

Some crocodile relatives were powerful land hunters, evolving body plans that looked more like dinosaurs than modern crocodiles. Poposaurus stalked prey on two legs, with feet remarkably similar to those of bipedal dinosaurs. Meanwhile, Fasolasuchus was a colossal four-legged predator that may have reached 9 meters (30 feet) in length, potentially ranking among the largest land predators outside the dinosaurs.

Other branches took an entirely different evolutionary path and became herbivores. Simosuchus, a small crocodile relative from Madagascar, had a short, pug-like snout and unusual leaf-shearing teeth adapted for eating vegetation.

And in the Triassic, aetosaurs like Desmatosuchus roamed the landscape covered in bony armor, with enormous spikes protruding from their shoulders. They looked more like miniature tanks than crocodiles.

Metriorhynchus evolved into an ocean-going predator, complete with flippers and a powerful tail adapted for swimming. Meanwhile, enormous semiaquatic creatures such as Deinosuchus stalked Cretaceous waterways, with some estimates putting the largest individuals at around 10 to 12 meters long. These giants were capable of preying on dinosaurs that ventured too close to the water.

“They have occupied almost every conceivable ecological role,” Alessandro Chiarenza, a paleontologist at University College London and the study’s lead author, told Reuters.

Today, only a fraction of that diversity remains. Almost everything about crocodile relatives changed over the centuries, from what they ate to how they moved.

But something else barely changed at all.

To investigate, Chiarenza and his colleagues assembled an evolutionary tree containing more than 400 living and extinct species, drawing on over 7,000 fossil records and extensive modern observations.

They compared these records with 52 reconstructions of Earth’s ancient climates, stretching back 245 million years. Their habitat models focused on nonmarine crocodile-line animals, rather than every ocean-going offshoot.

The results revealed that despite evolving radically different bodies, diets, and lifestyles, crocodile-line animals largely retained their ancestral preference for warmth.

This wasn’t simply a matter of crocodiles living in the tropics. For much of their history, the tropics weren’t their only option.

Around 50 million years ago, when Earth was approximately 10° Celsius warmer than today, crocodile relatives inhabited high-latitude regions of North America, Europe, and even Antarctica.

As the planet cooled, suitable habitats shifted toward the equator, and crocodile populations largely followed. In other words, rather than evolving to withstand increasingly cold conditions, they generally moved to places that still offered the warmth they needed.

Their climatic preferences weren’t completely fixed. The researchers identified changes in temperature preferences around 94 million years ago and a shift toward drier conditions among certain Australian crocodile relatives around 34 million years ago.

But the broader pattern remained remarkably consistent. Rather than continually evolving new tolerances, crocodiles generally followed the climates they already preferred.

Scientists call this climatic niche conservatism: the tendency of a lineage to retain its inherited environmental preferences over evolutionary time.

It’s a strategy that sounds almost stubborn. But 66 million years ago, it became a lifeline.

The Chicxulub impact didn’t just kill animals in the immediate vicinity of the collision. It sent enormous quantities of dust and other particles into the atmosphere, reducing the sunlight reaching Earth’s surface.

Temperatures plummeted, photosynthesis was disrupted, and food webs began to collapse. For animals dependent on stable temperatures and abundant food, the consequences could be devastating.

With sunlight reduced by 5% or 10%, tropical and subtropical regions retained climates suitable for crocodile relatives. Only the most extreme scenario, involving a 15% reduction, eliminated suitable habitats globally. The researchers noted that this particular scenario is considered unlikely.

The results contrast with an earlier 2020 study involving several of the same researchers, which found that an asteroid-induced impact winter drastically reduced suitable habitats for non-avian dinosaurs. The new findings suggest that the same global catastrophe could have affected two ancient lineages very differently, partly because of where suitable habitats remained.

Living crocodilians are cold-blooded, requiring far less energy than comparably sized warm-blooded animals. They can endure long periods without food, and some enter dormant states when conditions deteriorate. These characteristics would have been especially useful after the impact, when the collapse of plant productivity disrupted food supplies.

Smaller body sizes, semiaquatic lifestyles, and flexible diets probably helped some surviving crocodile relatives, too.

The simulations don’t prove that tropical refuges saved crocodiles, nor do they explain why particular species survived while others disappeared. But they reveal how geography and inherited climatic preferences could have tipped the odds.

There’s also a sobering lesson for modern crocodilians. Their ancestors often survived changing climates by moving to places that remained suitable. But today’s crocodilians face a world increasingly fragmented by roads, farmland, cities, and other human barriers. The habitats they need aren’t always connected, and moving to a better location may no longer be so simple.

Crocodile relatives spent 250 million years experimenting with extraordinary ways to live, becoming giants, herbivores, land hunters, and marine predators. Yet one of the traits that may have helped their lineage endure was something they changed surprisingly little: their preference for a warm place to live.

The study was published in the journal Science Advances.

Scientists May Have Identified the World’s First Corporate Psychopath

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

One shipwreck, two leaders, and a horrifying lesson about the abuse of power.

Imagine a master of office politics. He charms his way into power, surrounds himself with loyalists, and pushes aside anyone who might challenge his authority. He manipulates his followers, rules through fear, and takes no responsibility when things go wrong. Cross him, and you have much to lose.

It sounds like a particularly nightmarish boss. Except this one didn’t just ruin careers. He killed people.

The year is 1629. The man is Jeronimus Cornelisz, a Dutch merchant who seized control of a group of shipwreck survivors stranded on a tiny island off Western Australia. Under his leadership, more than 100 men, women, and children were murdered.

Nearly 400 years later, a researcher argues that Cornelisz may be the earliest identifiable example of a “corporate psychopath” working for a multinational company.

In a new study, management researcher Clive Boddy of Anglia Ruskin University examined historical accounts of Cornelisz’s behavior and compared them with 10 traits associated with workplace psychopathy. According to Boddy, the merchant displayed all 10, including manipulation, superficial charm, deceitfulness, and a lack of remorse.

But there’s another fascinating side to this grisly story.

On a nearby island, another shipwreck survivor faced the same catastrophe. Rather than terrorizing his followers, he organized them to survive and eventually helped bring Cornelisz’s murderous regime crashing down.

The contrast offers a remarkable glimpse into the psychology of leadership, and just how differently people can respond when given extraordinary power over others.

The Batavia had left the Netherlands in October 1628 on its maiden voyage to the Dutch East Indies, carrying hundreds of passengers and crew, merchandise, and chests of silver coins. It belonged to the Dutch East India Company, one of the world’s first multinational shareholder-owned corporations. Long before the modern office existed, this enormous trading enterprise already had shareholders, executives, and fierce internal power struggles.

Its destination was the company’s trading headquarters on Java. But trouble was brewing long before the ship reached Asia.

The expedition’s commander, Francisco Pelsaert, and its skipper, Ariaen Jacobsz, bitterly disliked each other. Cornelisz joined forces with Jacobsz, and together they plotted to seize the ship and its riches.

The ship followed a fast route across the Indian Ocean, propelled by powerful westerly winds. However, in the early hours of June 4, 1629, the Batavia struck a coral reef in the Houtman Abrolhos archipelago, off Western Australia. The crew couldn’t free it. Dozens drowned, while the survivors scrambled onto small, exposed islands with almost no food or fresh water.

With supplies running dangerously low, Pelsaert, Jacobsz, and 46 others departed in the ship’s small, overloaded longboat to search for fresh water. After failing to find it on the nearby islands and Australian coast, they undertook a desperate voyage to Java to summon help. Hundreds of survivors were left behind.

As the most senior company official remaining, he assumed command and quickly turned the makeshift settlement into his personal kingdom. He replaced members of his ruling council with conspirators, forced followers to swear loyalty, and dispatched potential opponents to other islands, ostensibly to search for water. He even awarded himself the grand title of “Captain-General.”

Cornelisz had been plotting a mutiny before the shipwreck, and now he had an entire community at his mercy. He intended to maintain that power until a rescue ship arrived — and then seize that vessel, too.

Cornelisz’s followers collected valuables and supplies, but that was just the start of it.

Claiming that food and water could not sustain everyone, he ordered a killing campaign. Estimates of the death toll vary, but historical accounts document the murder of more than 100 people. Some women also endured sexual violence. He generally left the killing to subordinates, but demanded their complicity and ruled the remaining survivors through fear.

The murders served several purposes. Fewer survivors meant fewer mouths to feed, but also fewer potential opponents. And by forcing his followers to participate in the violence, Cornelisz made them accomplices who had much to fear if the authorities ever returned.

Yet the soldiers he had marooned elsewhere hadn’t died as intended. In fact, they were about to become his worst nightmare.

The group, led by a soldier named Wiebbe Hayes, had been sent away under the pretense of searching for water. Cornelisz apparently expected the men to perish on the barren-looking islands.

Instead, Hayes and his companions discovered two freshwater wells, along with plenty of fish, seabird eggs, seals, and wallabies. They had stumbled upon something the murderous merchant had failed to find: enough resources to survive.

One survivor reportedly believed there was enough food to sustain everyone for a thousand years. It was almost certainly an exaggeration, but it captured just how different life had become for the two groups.

As survivors escaped Cornelisz’s massacres and joined them, Hayes’s group grew to 46 men and a boy. By then, word of the atrocities had reached them. Hayes organized the men into a fighting force, fashioning makeshift pikes from shipwreck debris, collecting chunks of coral to hurl at attackers, and building a crude stone fort on a hill. He also posted sentries to watch for approaching enemies.

Then, 20 of Cornelisz’s men attempted to invade. Hayes’s defenders stood their ground, forcing the attackers to retreat without bloodshed. A second assault failed, too. During a third confrontation, Cornelisz attempted to use negotiations as cover for a surprise attack. But the defenders turned the tables, killing several mutineers and capturing Cornelisz himself.

The ordeal still wasn’t over. The remaining mutineers launched another attack, this time armed with muskets. Some of Hayes’s defenders were killed. Just as the resistance appeared to be in serious trouble, a rescue ship appeared on the horizon.

Both sides raced to reach it first. The mutineers hoped to hijack the vessel, while Hayes’s men desperately needed to warn its captain. Hayes’s group won the race, exposing the plot and bringing the mutiny to an end. Cornelisz was tried and hanged, while Hayes was rewarded with a promotion.

The episode offered a startling contrast between leaders who treated people as expendable and those who organized them to survive.

“The same playbook we see in toxic workplaces today appears in this case from 1629,” Clive Boddy, a management researcher at Anglia Ruskin University, said in a statement.

The same shipwreck had produced two very different kinds of leaders. One used his authority to exploit and terrorize the people around him. The other organized his followers, found resources, and protected those who depended on him.

In a new paper, Boddy compared Cornelisz’s reported behavior with 10 traits in a workplace psychopathy measure. Drawing heavily on historian Mike Dash’s Batavia’s Graveyard, along with eyewitness accounts and trial records, he concluded that Cornelisz displayed all 10, from superficial charm and habitual deceit to lack of remorse and refusal to accept blame.

For Boddy, the remarkable thing isn’t simply that Cornelisz was extraordinarily cruel. It’s how recognizable his methods of obtaining and retaining power appear today.

He cultivated loyal insiders, removed potential challengers, divided people who might otherwise have opposed him, and created a climate in which disobedience carried terrifying consequences. These are extreme versions of behaviors that researchers studying abusive corporate leadership still recognize.

Boddy has examined similar patterns in modern organizations. In an earlier case study of a British charity, managers described a chief executive whose bullying leadership coincided with departing staff and falling organizational performance.

More broadly, a 2026 review of workplace studies found that psychopathic traits were associated with bullying, although results varied substantially between studies.

The massacre itself is well documented. Archaeologists have recovered victims’ remains from Beacon Island, and forensic analysis has documented injuries inflicted with sharp weapons.

But the psychological assessment is another matter. Boddy assessed historical descriptions, not a living person, and the analysis cannot establish Cornelisz’s mental state with clinical certainty. Nor were Hayes’s and Cornelisz’s groups randomly assigned to identical conditions: their access to water and food differed.

What the shipwreck does offer is a chilling illustration of how leaders can shape a group’s fate. Nearly 400 years later, the methods Boddy identifies remain disturbingly familiar — even if modern workplaces are worlds away from the horrors of a shipwrecked island.

The study was published in the Asian Journal of Business Ethics.

The Ultra 4’s body, screen, colours and design are identical to the previous versions. It weighs about 1g more, but the bigger changes are on the back and inside.

Upgraded sensors on the back of the watch take heart rate (HR) readings every five seconds during the day, up from every five minutes on previous generation Apple Watches. They also enable the measurement of your heart rate variability (HRV) during the day, not only at night, which feeds into some new recovery metrics.

The Ultra 4 is the first Apple Watch in several generations to get a meaningfully faster chip with the new S11. The battery life for general use in my testing now stretches to 79 hours, too, meaning the watch lasted well into its third day between charges. Most will be able to charge it every third morning, or less if you use the low power modes, although that is far behind the week-plus stamina of top adventure watch rivals.

El historiador Enrique Soria Mesa afirma que los linajes reales españoles compartían el mismo “pecado capital”: tenían origen judío

La paradoja es notable. Despreciados por el pueblo, vigilados por la Inquisición y sometidos a las exigencias de una sociedad obsesionada por la limpieza de sangre, los judeoconversos lograron pese a todo alcanzar enorme poder en la España de los siglos XVI al XVIII, emparentando además con la nobleza. Durante generaciones se vieron obligados a afrontar condenas, sospechas y oprobios, pero las necesidades del imperio, tanto financieras como de servidores competentes, acabaron por encumbrarlos. La monarquía hispánica alentó esa integración convirtiendo a la sociedad española, contra lo que se cree, en una de las más dinámicas de Europa. Este es en parte El gran secreto al que alude desde su título el libro del historiador Enrique Soria Mesa, que destapa también otra realidad escondida: la del origen judío de lo más granado de la nobleza española.

Para empezar, este catedrático de Historia Moderna de la Universidad de Córdoba y del Laboratorio de Estudios Judeoconversos afirma (pese a la imposibilidad de una demostración exhaustiva) que los linajes reales fundamentales compartían, por decirlo así, el mismo “pecado capital”, es decir, tenían sangre judía. Es el caso nada menos que de Fernando el Católico, un Enríquez por parte de madre, linaje creado en la Edad Media al adoptar ese apellido un sobrino del rey Enrique II de Castilla (hermanastro del depuesto Pedro I el Cruel) en honor a su tío. Este sobrino en cuestión, Alonso Enríquez, nació de la relación de su padre, Don Fadrique, con una judía llamada Paloma. Los Enríquez tuvieron una amplia descendencia y acabaron emparentando con lo más granado de la nobleza española. También hay mezcla de sangre judía en los Castilla. El vínculo parte del nieto bastardo de Pedro I el Cruel, llamado Pedro de Castilla. Aunque destinado al clero, fue padre de varios hijos ilegítimos, dos de ellos habidos de una amante judía llamada Isabel Drochelin. Lo mismo encontramos en la Casa de Aragón. En este caso, es un hijo ilegítimo del rey Juan II (padre de Fernando el Católico) llamado Alonso, que sería nombrado maestre de la Orden de Calatrava, quien introduce la mezcla al tener varios hijos con una joven judía llamada Estenga Conejo. En cuanto a la nobleza inferior, el libro dedica un apéndice de 15 páginas a enumerar uno a uno los centenares de títulos otorgados a cristianos nuevos.

El gran secreto tiene notable interés también como fresco social de la España de ese “largo Siglo de Oro”, como lo denomina el autor, que recoge coplillas, dichos y costumbres que nos dan una idea de las dificultades a que se enfrentaban los judeoconversos por importantes que fueran. Desde danzas burlescas como las llamadas judiadas hasta insultos en público, que podían obligar al que los recibía a interponer pleitos por injurias. En estos casos, una sentencia favorable, cosa nada infrecuente, servía para establecer por vía judicial la limpieza de sangre del ofendido. Algo fundamental, porque estos señores de nobleza adquirida gracias a matrimonios de conveniencia, y apuntalada a veces con cambios de apellidos y genealogías falsas, ansiaban vestir los hábitos de las órdenes militares, o formar parte del corpus rector de las catedrales y hasta del Tribunal del Santo Oficio. El año negro de Meta: las claves de la mayor multa a una tecnológica por cometer 43 millones de infracciones

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

La compañía de Mark Zuckerberg deberá pagar 22.000 millones al Estado de Nuevo México, que se suman a los 18.000 acordados para frenar otra demanda por los efectos de las redes sociales. Y el curso todavía no ha acabado

2026 no está siendo el mejor año para Meta. Un juez de Nuevo México, EE UU, ordenó este miércoles a la empresa matriz de Facebook, Instagram o WhatsApp el pago de una multa de unos 22.000 millones de euros al considerarla culpable de engañar a los usuarios de Facebook sobre su política de privacidad de datos.

El Estado de Nuevo México se querelló contra Meta en 2021 a raíz del escándalo de Cambridge Analytica, revelado en 2018 gracias a una investigación periodística de The Guardian y The New York Times. Gracias a estos periódicos se supo que la consultora política Cambridge Analytica tuvo acceso a los perfiles de 50 millones de usuarios de Facebook sin su consentimiento, y que esos datos se usaron a favor de Donald Trump durante la campaña electoral que le llevó a la Casa Blanca en enero de 2017.

La noticia llega dos meses después de que el grupo que dirige Mark Zuckerberg alcanzara un acuerdo con los fiscales generales de 52 estados por el que abonará unos 18.000 millones en diez años a cambio de que retiren su pleito contra la compañía, a la que acusan de diseñar y operar productos (sus plataformas) a sabiendas de que tienen efectos perjudiciales para la salud mental de los jóvenes.

La nueva multa impuesta a Meta resucita el escándalo de Cambridge Analytica cuando el foco estaba puesto en los procesos judiciales abiertos por los efectos de las redes sociales en los menores. ¿Por qué ahora? ¿Es la mayor multa de la historia? ¿Y por qué otra vez en Nuevo México? Estas son algunas de las claves de la sentencia.

Un jurado de la Corte del Distrito de Santa Fe, en Nuevo México, resolvió que Meta había cometido 43 millones de violaciones de las leyes estatales de protección al consumidor, y que cada una de esas violaciones acarreaba una multa de hasta 5.000 dólares. En el caso de que el juez hubiera optado por alcanzar ese límite, el monto total habría alcanzado los 215.000 millones de dólares.

No ha sido así. La cantidad final, 25.000 millones, es abultada, totalmente inasumible para muchas compañías. En el caso de Meta, supone menos de la mitad de sus beneficios acumulados entre enero y septiembre de este año, que ascienden a 52.481 millones de dólares, según un comunicado de ayer de la propia compañía.

El escándalo de Cambridge Analytica se descubrió en 2018, con Donald Trump ya sentado en el Despacho Oval. Tres años después, en 2021, una vez tuvo toda la documentación preparada, el Estado de Nuevo México presentó su demanda contra Facebook. La sentencia se ha demorado cinco años. ¿Por qué?

En primer lugar, Nuevo México, como otros Estados, estuvo negociando con Meta. “Pero el acuerdo no les ha satisfecho, así que decidieron seguir adelante con el juicio. Hay que tener en cuenta que estas negociaciones pueden llevar mucho tiempo”, advierte Rodrigo Cetina, profesor de Derecho y Políticas Públicas en la Barcelona School of Management de la Universitat Pompeu Fabra y especialista en los retos jurídicos de las tecnologías digitales.

En segundo lugar, la acusación reclama 43 millones de violaciones de las leyes estatales de privacidad. Preparar la documentación que justifique ese volumen de infracciones se antoja una tarea poco ágil.

Los 25.000 millones de dólares constituyen la mayor sanción individual impuesta hasta ahora a una de las grandes tecnológicas, pero las ha habido todavía más severas en otros sectores. Las cuatro mayores tabaqueras estadounidenses acordaron en 1998 el pago de más de 200.000 millones de dólares en 25 años por haber ocultado los efectos nocivos de fumar.

La multa de Meta supera a las impuestas a Bank of America y JPMorgan, que entre 2013 y 2014 acordaron pagar más de 30.000 millones de dólares entre las dos por la venta fraudulenta de hipotecas subprime que desencadenó la Gran Recesión. Meta pagará más también que la británica BP, que en 2015 fue condenada a pagar 20.800 millones de dólares por el vertido de crudo de la plataforma petrolera Deepwater Horizon, en el Golfo de México, que todavía es la mayor catástrofe ambiental marina de la historia.

Nuevo México es, junto a California, el estado que lidera muchos de los procesos abiertos contra las grandes tecnológicas. Buena parte de la culpa la tiene el fiscal general del Estado, el demócrata Raúl Torrez, muy implicado en la defensa de los derechos digitales de sus conciudadanos.

En agosto, un juez de Nuevo México obligó a la compañía a pagar 567 millones de dólares por considerarla culpable de perjudicar el bienestar y la salud mental de menores con sus redes sociales. Unos meses antes, en marzo, otro tribunal de Nuevo México impuso a la compañía una multa de 375 millones de dólares (unos 323 millones de euros) por su falta de seguridad para los menores.

En ambos casos, se acusó a Meta de violar las leyes estatales de protección al consumidor. Algunos analistas consideran que esta estrategia está funcionando para litigar contra las grandes tecnológicas cuando estas apelan a la libertad de expresión para defender que no son responsables de los contenidos que se difunden en sus plataformas.

Meta considera desproporcionada la sanción, y se agarra específicamente a la libertad de expresión. “Esta sentencia, junto con el enfoque del fiscal general en materia de aplicación de la ley, hace que sea prácticamente imposible desarrollar cualquier actividad empresarial en el estado de Nuevo México”, ha dicho en un comunicado C. J. Mahoney, el responsable legal del grupo. “Esta demanda pretende gravar la libertad de expresión para imponer nuevas normas sobre lo que la gente puede decir en internet. Estamos seguros de que ganaremos en apelación”, ha asegurado.

Cuando Meta cerró el acuerdo con los 52 fiscales generales para que retiraran su demanda por afectar a la salud mental de los menores, también accedió a pagar 459 millones para disipar posibles reclamaciones por privacidad relacionadas con Cambridge Analytica. Nuevo México no entró en ese acuerdo y siguió adelante con el juicio.

Pero el frente de las consecuencias de las redes sociales en los menores sigue abierto. Está previsto que en las próximas semanas se inicien los juicios que afectan a familias de afectados y a distritos educativos que demandaron a la compañía por perjudicar la salud y el rendimiento académico de los estudiantes.
