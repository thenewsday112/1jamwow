French TV channels scramble to apologize after airing Albanian protests as Paris and Lyon

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Two French news channels backpedaled and apologized this weekend after they misidentified images of protests in Albania as demonstrations taking place in France.

CNEWS made the mix-up first on Saturday, as a mass march was getting underway peacefully in southern Paris — the largest of nearly 300 demonstrations nationwide over school funding and soaring living costs that together drew hundreds of thousands of people.

But images that CNEWS showed of people gathered in protest on a street, some of them clapping and one waving a red flag, were shot not at Paris' Porte d’Italie, as labelled by the broadcaster, but in Tirana, the Albanian capital that was among multiple locations in Europe where demonstrations took place on Saturday.

Some of the people shown had covered their faces with ski goggles and masks. A commentator speaking over the CNEWS broadcast alleged they were “behaving like future vandals, like future rioters," suggesting that the Paris demonstration could turn violent, even though the images were from Albania.

There were clashes at the end of the Paris demonstration, with masked protesters hurling objects and fireworks at police, who answered with clouds of tear gas, deafening sound grenades and jets of water cannons. There was also scattered violence in other cities and towns. Police made 1,314 arrests, the Interior Ministry said Sunday.

The Associated Press also reported Saturday from Tirana and protests elsewhere. AP verified that images shown on CNEWS, labelled “Porte d’Italie (Paris)," were shot at Rruga e Durrësit, a central Tirana street.

A CNEWS presenter subsequently apologized on air for what the channel described on its website as “a confusion of images."

The presenter, Olivier Benkemoun, explained that the channel received video feeds from multiple locations and had misidentified some of the images.

“Earlier, you saw the guys who were covering their faces. Well, that wasn’t in Paris. We made a mistake in the control room,” he said.

“So, really, I want to apologize, also on behalf of the newsroom, because the images of guys covering their faces came from Albania,” he added.

Critics of the conservative news channel savaged the mistake.

“Footage from Albania to make it look like a live broadcast from Porte d’Italie! Pathetic," said far-left presidential candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon, in a post on X.

Public broadcaster franceinfo also confused Albania with France on Saturday.

It broadcast images of officers in riot gear advancing down a street, taking cover behind two trucks firing water cannons.

AP verified the location as Bulevardi Dëshmorët e Kombit in central Tirana. But franceinfo's television service said the images were coming live from Lyon, with a presenter saying that the situation in the city in southeastern France looked “a bit tense.”

Three minutes later, the presenter corrected the mistake.

“It followed an error on our part. These weren't live images from Lyon but were live images from Tirana,” he said.

Associated Press journalists Zana Cimili and Florent Bajrami in Tirana, Albania, contributed.

Open Senate seat up for grabs in Iowa as candidates tout populism, bipartisanship

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

GOP Rep. Ashley Hinson and Democratic state Rep. Josh Turek face off Nov. 3.

With days to go until the midterms, President Donald Trump is traveling the country on a red-state blitz to stave off a potential blue wave, including in Iowa, where an open U.S. Senate seat is up for grabs.

Democrats are pinning their hopes on state Rep. Josh Turek, a two-time Paralympic gold medalist, to flip the seat for the first time since 2008. He's running as a centrist on what he calls a "common-sense prairie populist" platform against Republican Rep. Ashley Hinson.

Iowa Republicans hold the governorship, majorities in the statehouse and represent the state's entire congressional delegation. But in a midterm environment broadly seen as unfavorable to Republicans, Democrats see a competitive opportunity to flip control of Congress amid growing economic discontent over rising gas and fertilizer costs in the agriculture-heavy state.

Trump, who is backing Hinson, handily won the state in the last three presidential elections, but Hinson is walking a political tightrope. Though she praised Trump at his Sioux City rally last week for "preserving our country for generations to come," she has criticized the president's recent deal with Russia he said would lower diesel fuel costs. And in September, she reversed her position on Iran, calling for an end to the war.

When asked by ABC News about the Pentagon's timeline for a new offensive after the midterms, Hinson deferred to the commander-in-chief.

"I trust the president and the U.S. military to get this done on the time frame that works right to make sure we can actually achieve the result of making sure Iran does not get a nuclear weapon," Hinson told ABC News' Jon Karl.

On Ukraine, Hinson disagreed with the president's waiving of sanctions against Russia President Vladimir Putin.

"I don't think we should be making any deals with Russia," Hinson said, later adding, "I want to bring all of these conflicts abroad to an end as quickly as possible. I don't think anybody likes forever wars, but certainly we cannot be empowering dictators to win wars."

The congresswoman also took issue with the U.S. Special Envoy Steve Witkoff calling Putin a "humanitarian" last week when Russia released formerly detained American Stephen Hubbard.

"He's a murderer. I don't think we should be making deals with murderers and dictators, and that's why I stood up for Ukraine," Hinson said.

Former President Barack Obama, who twice carried the Hawkeye State, endorsed Turek in July and reminded voters, "I know a little something about winning Iowa."

"Probably not," Turek told ABC News. "One thing I do believe is that we're not a red state. We're a common-sense state that's masqueraded more red than what we actually are."

Turek has leaned into his personal story. The state lawmaker was born with spina bifida due to his father's exposure to Agent Orange during the Vietnam War. "I am an example of the generational consequences of these forever wars," he has said.

On Thursday, Turek visited a local manufacturer in Shell Rock, home to some 1,260 people in Butler County, Iowa, which Trump carried by 45 percentage points in the 2024 presidential election. He said too many Democrats have overlooked small communities like this one – areas where some Iowans say they're reeling from the economic fallout of Trump's seesaw tariff policies and the Iran war.

A recent CNN poll found that majorities of likely Iowa voters say Trump's policies have increased their cost of living, that gas prices have caused them hardship and that tariffs are bad for the state.

Hinson told ABC News she is unafraid "to tell [Trump] when I think he's wrong on something."

"I think that's how Iowans expect you to do your job. They don't like empty rhetoric," she added. "They like results here in Iowa, and that's exactly what I've done."

Turek has slammed Hinson for her votes in Congress to continue the war with Iran. But the Democrat has also touted his bipartisan bona fides in ads, promising voters he would work across the aisle, including with Trump, on issues like immigration.

"He's done a better job on the border than Biden," Turek said. "I agree with him that we need to drive private equity out of housing. I would also like to see us drive private equity out of housing, healthcare, and nursing homes. And I agree with him that we should have no tax on tips, overtime, and Social Security."

Turek, who hails from the deep-red city of Council Bluffs, also recently launched a coalition of Republicans backing him, including members of his own family. He told ABC News his father is a two-time Obama voter and three-time Trump voter. His sister, he added, is a registered Republican and he's unsure whether his mother "has ever voted for a Democrat besides me."

Democrats like Turek have also slammed the GOP for voting to pass Trump's signature tax legislation, the "One Big Beautiful Bill," arguing it has threatened rural hospitals and reduced healthcare access, even as the state faces rising cancer rates.

The bill slashed Medicaid funding by $1 trillion over the next 10 years, in part through new work and eligibility requirements. Republicans said their goal is reducing "waste, fraud and abuse" in these programs.

Hinson, pitching herself as a "minivan-driving mom," told ABC News that she knows Iowans are economically struggling and defended the "One Big Beautiful Bill" for delivering tax relief to families. The congresswoman pledged to take more action to lower healthcare premiums and gas prices.

At 43, Hinson could become the youngest elected Republican woman in the U.S. Senate. And like Turek, she's pitching herself as a bipartisan lawmaker, despite her initial campaign promise to be Trump's "strongest ally" if elected.

"I found common ground with President Biden. He and I didn't agree on much, but we were able to get some things done for Iowa, even when I was in the minority," Hinson said. "I'm going to operate that same way, no matter who's in the White House.

In Shell Rock, Turek said that Iowans are telling him they're tired of the "political theater" and "tribalism."

"'I'm struggling to keep food on the table, a roof above my head, pay my electric bill, and keep gas in my tank,'" Turek said voters are telling him. "Can you please go up there and solve problems through policy in a bipartisan way?'"

Czechs rallied in big numbers in Prague on Sunday to renew protests against a plan by the government led by billionaire Andrej Babiš to overhaul funding for public broadcasters that many consider dangerous for their independence.

The crowd of thousands was bigger than the picturesque Old Town Square could take with many filling the nearby streets. The ruling coalition angered the protesters by unexpectedly moving ahead with the plan in the lower house of Parliament two days ago.

Coalition lawmakers hastily opened the issue and limited time for the opposition to take part in the debate before taking a vote to give the draft a green light in the first of three parliamentary readings at 4:36 a.m.

“We've had enough,” “This must not be allowed to pass” and “4:36,” the demonstrators chanted.

“You’ve gone too far, we won’t let this country be destroyed,” Mikuláš Minář, a main organizer from the Million Moments for Democracy group said. “It is mafia syndicates or the Soviet Politburo who make decisions at 4:36."

The protesters demanded a withdrawal of the draft and called on Babiš to enable a thorough public debate on the issue.

According to the plan, public radio and television would be financed from the state budget, and not from regular fees from individuals, households or businesses. The media would receive about 15% less money than they do now.

Critics say that change would give the coalition a means to take control of the media following the examples of populist governments in Slovakia under Prime Minister Robert Fico and in Hungary before former Prime Minister Viktor Orbán was ousted from power in April.

Babiš and his allies have a record of attacks against the public and other mainstream media.

The public media's staffers announced they would go on strike on Nov 3. They are backed by labor unions, rectors of universities, churches, international media organizations and many others.

The protesters vowed to organize a rally at Prague's Letná park on Nov. 15, two days before the anniversary of the 1989 anti-Communist Velvet Revolution that ended more than 40 years of the totalitarian rule.

Babiš formed a governing coalition last year with two small political groups whose agenda includes reducing support for Ukraine and rejecting some key European Union policies.

Stagnant housing market leaves American sellers facing deep price cuts

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Sellers now far outnumber buyers, leaving owners faced with difficult decisions

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Until recently, American property owners could reasonably expect swift sales that yielded prices above their asking rates.

Today, that is no longer a winning prospect.

Across the country, a stagnant housing market has left many vendors stranded in uncertainty, unable to move for employment, purchase larger properties for expanding families, or execute major life decisions dependent on selling their homes.

Real estate agents report that sellers who can afford to pull their listings are generally doing so, while those remaining face growing pressure to reduce asking prices and contribute thousands of dollars toward buyer costs.

This trend marks a pronounced shift from earlier in the decade, when low mortgage rates energized buyers, creating an intense market that routinely drove property values significantly above asking prices.

Today, elevated mortgage rates, cumulative price growth from recent years, and high inflation have caused market activity to stall. These factors are placing homeownership out of reach for many while others delay purchases until conditions improve.

Sellers now far outnumber buyers, leaving owners faced with difficult decisions: implement steeper price cuts and financial concessions to attract buyers, or remain listed longer and delay significant personal plans.

"The dynamic has shifted back toward more of what we call buyers’ markets rather than sellers’ markets," said Mark Fleming, chief economist at First American Financial. "(That) means you might have to reduce your price, you might give a concession to get your home sold, it might have to stay on market longer."

Following the departure of her three children, Liz Suder sought to sell her four-bedroom, three-and-a-half-bath home in Murphy, Texas, roughly 20 miles outside Dallas, and purchase a smaller property.

"I was waiting to sell the house before I started looking," said Suder, 56. "I didn’t want to have two mortgages."

Suder ended up putting her life on hold for over a year. The finance director, who owned the residence for 21 years, originally listed it in April 2025 for $750,000.

After six months and multiple price reductions, the house remained unsold, prompting her to remove it from the market.

She relisted the property in February for $725,000 ahead of the spring buying season.

A buyer eventually agreed to purchase it for $650,000, with Suder providing $9,000 toward repair costs.

"I never expected it to take a full year, and for me to have to reduce the price by that much," she said.

Rick Ganga needs to secure a buyer for his property in Blaine, Washington, before moving forward with his personal plans.

A pending divorce requires the sale of his three-bedroom, two-and-a-half-bath home, which went on the market on June 1 for $825,000. Lowering the price to just below $800,000 has failed to attract interest.

"There’s (been) no offers, virtually zero traffic," said Ganga, 58.

Although some sellers in his neighborhood have reduced asking prices by $100,000 or more, Ganga notes that having owned the property only since 2022 leaves him with insufficient equity to make further cuts.

"That’s not anything I’m going to entertain," he said. "It doesn’t make sense."

Until a sale occurs, the operations manager must set aside plans to transfer out of state with his company.

"I honestly believe that it will probably sit until spring or summer of next year," he said.

After dropping below 6% in February, average 30-year mortgage rates climbed to 7.4% this month—their highest point in nearly three years. This upward trajectory in interest rates has stifled housing sales nationwide.

As price reductions became widespread, the national median listing price fell 1.2% year-on-year in September to $419,250, marking the 11th straight monthly decline, according to Realtor.com. Approximately 21% of active listings saw initial price cuts, a four-year peak.

Buyers frequently request and receive financial contributions from sellers to cover closing costs. Rising mortgage rates have also allowed buyers to negotiate for seller contributions toward rate buy-downs, reducing monthly borrowing expenses.

"It’s sort of like an indicator of where the market is right now, with buyers gaining leverage at the expense of sellers, and sellers doing more now to meet buyers halfway," said Jake Krimmel, a senior economist at Realtor.com.

While advantageous for purchasers, these shifts may not suffice to offset the broad affordability challenges keeping many buyers out of the market.

Last month, sellers outnumbered buyers by approximately 63% across the 50 largest metropolitan regions in the U.S., according to Redfin. The active buyer count of roughly 929,000 marked the lowest figure recorded since 2013, while seller numbers reached their highest level since 2020.

This significant disparity between buyers and sellers means Ethan Butner faces a longer wait to sell his home in Troutdale, Oregon, located 16 miles east of Portland.

Butner, a service manager, has had an offer accepted on a larger home near Portland for his family. However, completing that purchase depends on selling his current three-bedroom, two-bath residence.

Progress has stalled. The house has remained listed for over two months at $480,000. A $30,000 price drop yielded only one offer, which subsequently fell through.

To improve the home's appeal, Butner recently replaced the flooring and installed a new water heater and dishwasher.

"It’s been a roller coaster of emotion for myself, my family, especially my fiancée,′ said Butner, 36. "We’ve had this vision of being in our own home and all the kids having their own rooms and all the good stuff that comes along with that. We definitely feel stuck at the moment."

Around 40 stranded Iranian sailors aboard U.S.-sanctioned oil tankers anchored just outside Sri Lanka’s territorial waters received urgent medical treatment in the island nation on Saturday (October 24, 2026), Sri Lanka’s Foreign Ministry said.

On Saturday (October 24, 2026), Sri Lankan authorities facilitated the transfer of the Iranian sailors from their vessels to the shore through the port of Galle in the country’s Southern Province, Foreign Affairs Ministry spokesman Thushara Rodrigo told The Hindu. “We coordinated the transfer alone as per our humanitarian obligations. The Iranian Embassy bore all the expenses,” he said.

A senior official at the Iranian Embassy in Colombo confirmed the development and said the sailors were taken from Galle to Colombo for medical attention before leaving Sri Lanka on Saturday night (October 24, 2026), indicating they spent only a few hours on the island.

The development comes a month after The Wall Street Journal reported that U.S. “pressure” through sanctions had left the Iranian vessels stranded in Sri Lankan waters. At the time, the Sri Lankan Foreign Ministry said Colombo would not be extending assistance to the sanctioned vessels, while emphasising that the secondary sanctions were part of a broader directive from Washington, D.C. and not specific to Sri Lanka. Denying media reports of U.S. pressure on Sri Lanka, Foreign Minister Vijitha Herath said the Sri Lankan government made decisions “strictly protecting the country’s sovereignty”, even as it was prepared to intervene appropriately on humanitarian grounds.

Earlier this year, Sri Lanka made headlines when a U.S. submarine attacked and sank Iranian frigate IRIS Dena in the Indian Ocean, off the island’s southern coast. At least 84 people were killed in the torpedo attack, which sent shockwaves across the region. Sri Lanka’s ruling Anura Kumara Dissanayake administration, while reiterating its commitment to a “neutral foreign policy”, directed its Navy to evacuate the injured sailors. They were subsequently treated at a public hospital in the Galle district.

Après un cross particulièrement sélectif samedi, le concours complet international des 5 Étoiles de Pau s’est achevé ce dimanche 25 octobre par l’épreuve de saut d’obstacles. Au domaine de Sers, passionnés, familles et curieux ont vibré au rythme des derniers parcours de l’élite mondiale de l’équitation

Dans les tribunes du domaine de Sers, tous les regards convergent vers la piste copieusement arrosée. Ce dimanche, pas question de perdre une seconde du spectacle. Chaque obstacle franchi rapproche un cavalier du sans-faute. Chaque barre renversée peut bouleverser le classement. Après trois jours de compétition, les 5 Étoiles de Pau se jouent sur les ultimes parcours de saut d’obstacles. Et dans les gradins, la tension est palpable.

Les connaisseurs scrutent les trajectoires, anticipent...

Les connaisseurs scrutent les trajectoires, anticipent les difficultés, retiennent leur souffle. Les néophytes découvrent cette complicité entre le cavalier et sa monture, où tout se joue parfois à quelques centimètres. « À Pau, le saut d’obstacles du dimanche livre un spectacle d’une intensité rare. Après le juge de paix du cross, la moindre barre renversée fait basculer le podium », témoigne Richard, turfiste patenté.

La veille, le cross avait déjà offert son lot de sensations fortes. Sur les 31 efforts du redoutable parcours imaginé par Pierre Michelet, aucun concurrent n’était parvenu à terminer dans le temps imparti. L’obstacle numéro 9 avait notamment provoqué neuf refus ou éliminations.

Le Néo-Zélandais Tim Price avait pris la tête avec Jarillo, devant la Britannique Bubby Upton et son épouse Jonelle Price. Moins d’une barre de pénalité séparait les trois concurrents avant l’ultime épreuve. Tim Price pouvait déjà espérer un doublé historique à Pau. Côté français, seuls trois couples avaient terminé le cross, le meilleur étant Bastien Commecy, 20e avec Crack du Noyer, pour sa première participation à ce niveau.

Ce dimanche, changement de décor. Après les grands espaces du cross, le spectacle se concentre dans la carrière. Précision, maîtrise et sang-froid prennent le relais de la vitesse.

Dans les tribunes, les passionnés côtoient les familles venues profiter de cette grande fête du cheval. « C’est devenu notre rendez-vous annuel. Les enfants adorent voir les chevaux de si près, surtout sur le cross, confie Pierre, accompagné de ses deux enfants. Il y a le village d’exposants pour flâner et l’accès reste super convivial malgré la dimension internationale. C’est un dimanche très festif. »

Car les 5 étoiles de Pau ne sont pas un concours ordinaire. Ils appartiennent au cercle très fermé des sept compétitions de concours complet cinq étoiles organisées dans le monde. Cette 36e édition a réuni 54 couples au départ, dont plusieurs des meilleurs cavaliers de la planète.

De quoi impressionner Marie, 20 ans, venue pour la première fois avec des amis. « Franchement, je n’y connaissais rien en équitation en arrivant, mais la claque ! La puissance des chevaux sur le cross samedi et la précision du saut d’obstacles aujourd’hui, c’est bluffant. » La jeune spectatrice découvre aussi la ferveur des tribunes. « La tension dans le public quand le cavalier entre en piste, le silence total puis les encouragements… L’ambiance est palpitante, même pour une néophyte. »

Entre exploits sportifs, émotions partagées et convivialité, Pau aura vécu quatre jours au rythme de l’élite mondiale de l’équitation. Et c’est finalement Tim Price, associé à Jarillo, qui s’est imposé, réalisant un doublé inédit dans l’histoire des 5 Étoiles de Pau après sa victoire de 2025.

Auteur d’un sans-faute lors de l’ultime épreuve de saut d’obstacles, il devance la Britannique Bubby Upton, sur Its Cooley Time, et son épouse Jonelle Price, sur Senor Crocodillo. Le couple Néo-Z place même quatre chevaux parmi les neuf premiers. Côté français, Bastien Commecy, sur Crack du Noyer, termine à la 19e place, meilleur tricolore de cette 36e édition.

Blanche defends DOJ lawsuit blocking Alaska Senate results if Republican loses

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

President Donald Trump walks past Attorney General Todd Blanche after speaking at a roundtable event in the East Room of the White House, Wednesday, Oct. 21, 2026, in Washington. Alex Brandon/AP hide caption

U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche Sunday defended his department's lawsuit seeking to stop Alaska from automatically certifying the results of its Senate election if the Republican incumbent there loses.

Earlier this week he had said the Justice Department could get involved in the vote certification process in a "doomsday" scenario.

Pressed on CNN about whether the suit was setting the stage for President Trump to challenge the outcome of next week's midterm elections, Blanche denied it.

The seat is seen as one vital for Republicans if they are to maintain control of the Senate for the second half of President Trump's term in office.

The Justice Department claims that potential confusion over candidates with similar names is the reason for the suit, though some Democrats have argued that it is actually an attempt to interfere in the democratic process.

A candidate on the Nov. 3 ballot is a retired schoolteacher from Petersburg, named Daniel J. Sullivan, with a remarkably similar name to the current Republican senator, Dan Sullivan.

Republicans have accused Daniel J. Sullivan of running as a spoiler to try and divert votes away from the incumbent. But the other Sullivan has said the decision to run was his own and denied his candidacy was intended to confuse voters or siphon votes in Republican Sullivan's tight race against Democrat Mary Peltola, a former congresswoman.

Inside the controversial call that ended Game 2 of the World Series

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Milwaukee Brewers are two wins away from the first World Series title in franchise history, and they got a little help from an incredibly close call Saturday night.

With the Brewers leading 5-4 in the top of the ninth, the Rays had a runner on third with two outs and designated hitter Yandy Díaz at the plate. On a 3-2 pitch that would have been ball four, Díaz tried to check his swing on a curveball from Aaron Ashby.

Home plate umpire Jim Wolf initially ruled that Díaz hadn’t swung, which would have sent him to first base. But Milwaukee catcher William Contreras appealed to the first base umpire, who ruled that Díaz had gone around. The strikeout ended the game.

Here’s how the swing looked in real time:

“In the moment, yeah, I disagreed. But the umpire said it was a swing and it is what it is,” Díaz said after the game.

“I didn’t go look at it, but from my vantage point, it did look like that he swung,” Tampa Bay manager Kevin Cash said.

The postgame crew on the Fox broadcast seemed split over the swing as well.

It’s a great question, and one that MLB’s rulebook doesn’t answer.

According to MLB.com, “there is no definition for ‘check swing’ in the official baseball rules. The closest thing comes in the definition of terms where the first example of a strike is a pitch that ‘is struck at by the batter.’”

That leaves it to the umpires’ discretion when a pitch is considered to be “struck at.”

Colloquially, a batter has to break an imaginary plane for a checked swing to be considered a full one. What exactly is that plane? Again, there’s no official rule. As with obscenity in Jacobellis v. Ohio, umpires typically know it when they see it.

Much to the delight of fans, MLB introduced the automated ball-strike (ABS) system this season, allowing players to challenge, as the name suggests, balls and strikes. ABS came on top of the expanded replay review rules instituted in 2014, which granted managers at least one challenge per game on calls such as force plays, tag plays and even fan interference.

Unfortunately for Tampa Bay, checked swings aren’t on the list of reviewable plays, so the Rays couldn’t ask for a review of the play in Game 2.

“We got ABS for the first time this year, getting the balls and strikes right. We need to get a system that can get [checked swings] right,” Fox play-by-play announcer Joe Davis said during a replay of Díaz’s swing.

Given all the technology MLB has at its disposal, it’s hard to believe the league and players union will let a situation like Game 2’s happen again, particularly when the stakes are as high as the World Series.

In 2024, MLB tested checked-swing challenges in the Arizona Fall League, using the same Hawk-Eye technology used for the ABS system. In those challenges, teams were able to review if a checked swing “crossed a 45-degree threshold from home plate,” according to MLB.com.

The checked-swing challenge has since made it to the minors, introduced to the single-A Florida State League in 2025 and the Triple-A Pacific Coast League this year.

In June 2025, MLB Commissioner Rob Manfred said he wanted teams to be accustomed to the ABS system before introducing the checked-swing challenge to the majors.

French far-right leader Marine Le Pen delivers a speech during a political rally to launch her presidential campaign, after the party's congress on Oct 25 in Orleans, France.

ORLEANS, France - Far-right leader Marine Le Pen, the frontrunner in the 2027 French presidential election, formally launched her campaign on Oct 25 with a promise to restore order and national confidence.

As France faces one of its most turbulent periods in years, Le Pen cast herself as the best placed to navigate what she described as mounting economic, social and security crises.

“We are not living through the end of the world but the end of a world, their world,” she said of her political opponents.

Le Pen chose to make her first campaign address in Orleans, home to Joan of Arc, the 15th-century warrior heroine revered by nationalists as a symbol of patriotism and sovereignty.

Addressing a crowd of cheering supporters, she promised “zero tolerance” policing and said restoring security was the state’s first duty.

Mass protests over living costs and inequality last weekend descended into looting and vandalism.

The street turmoil has given Le Pen a boost and an opinion poll published on Oct 25 put her on 32% to 33.5%, well ahead of her nearest rival, hard-left leader Jean-Luc Melenchon on 17%, as voters desert mainstream parties for the political extremes.

Surveys suggest she would easily defeat Melenchon in a run-off ballot, expected to be held in early May.

Seeking to project optimism, Le Pen built her speech around the theme of a national “Renaissance”. She said France was living through a period of upheaval but would emerge stronger if it embraced her vision of security, prosperity and renewal.

“We say: ‘Things were good before, they will be better tomorrow!’,” she said.

Le Pen promised a hard line on immigration, pledging to restore border controls, automatically expel foreign criminals and crack down on gangs and drug traffickers.

Le Pen herself could have to campaign wearing an ankle tag after she was convicted of embezzling European Parliament funds. She is appealing the court verdict.

Marine Le Pen (centre), an MP from France’s far-right National Rally party, reacts on stage alongside party president Jordan Bardella and other delegates at the rally.

On the economy, Le Pen pledged spending restraint, lower business taxes, reduced bureaucracy and support for industry, innovation and artificial intelligence. She rejected further increases in the retirement age.

The congress was designed to showcase unity at the top of the National Rally party, with Jordan Bardella re-elected party president and presented as Le Pen’s choice for prime minister.

Bardella’s speech was interrupted by activists from a Jewish student union, drawing attention to recent accusations he made antisemitic comments in his youth, which he denies.

Le Pen has spent more than a decade trying to transform the party she inherited from her father, Jean-Marie Le Pen, in 2011, softening its image and broadening its appeal beyond its traditional anti-immigration base.

On Europe, Le Pen stopped short of advocating a French exit from the European Union, but said she would roll back Brussels’ influence, restore national control over borders and laws, and put French interests first, including avoiding foreign wars.

“The future belongs to us,” she said, before leading the flag-waving crowd in singing the national anthem. REUTERS

People attend a protest against government plans to change the funding system for public television and radio at the Old Town Square in Prague, Czech Republic, on Oct 25.

PRAGUE - Thousands protested in Prague on Oct 25 after government lawmakers gave initial backing to public media funding changes in a rare overnight vote despite criticism the overhaul will damage media independence.

The proposal’s first reading was unexpectedly brought to the lower house floor outside the planned order of proceedings on Oct 23.

Opponents and media advocacy groups have objected to fast-tracking the legislation, which will abolish household fees that currently fund Czech public television and radio stations.

The populist government of billionaire Prime Minister Andrej Babis, backed by the far-right SPD party, has frequently clashed with public and private media.

It wants to move funding for Czech Television and Czech Radio to the state budget, which critics say will leave broadcasters vulnerable to political pressure.

Babis says it is a matter of funding transparency and not an attack on independence.

Smaller protests first broke out on Oct 23 at the Bill’s unexpected introduction. Speaker Tomio Okamura of the SPD limited debate and scheduled a vote for 4.36am local time on Oct 24.

The Bill is now at the committee stage before a later vote.

A woman holds a placard reading 'We won't give up the media' during the protest at Old Town Square in Prague.

“Mr Babis ... Mr Okamura, nobody in this country believes that your intention was pure,” Mikulas Minar, a protest leader, said on Oct 25, criticising the overnight vote.

“(This protest) is a clear message to this government: you have really gone too far this time,” he told protesters who had filled Prague’s historic Old Town Square, amid chants of “Resignation” and “We’ve had enough”.

Czech Television is the country’s most-watched broadcaster across major age groups. Public media are among the most trusted news sources in the country, according to a 2025 Reuters Institute survey.

The proposal would cut funding for Czech Television and Czech Radio by about 15%, possibly as early as 2027.

Opposition parties have compared the funding changes with reforms in Hungary under former Prime Minister Viktor Orban, who oversaw a major expansion of pro-government media.

On Oct 23, the European Broadcasting Union, along with 15 other groups including other public broadcasters as well as Reporters Without Borders, called for more debate and the removal of any measures threatening the broadcasters’ financial sustainability.

President Petr Pavel also called for more expert scrutiny.

“The Czech Republic is among the few countries in central and eastern Europe where the system of public service and private media works very well,” he said on X on Oct 24. REUTERS
