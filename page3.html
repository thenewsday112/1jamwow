<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/pharma/kennedys-hhs-relaxes-enforcement-6-popular-wellness-peptides" hreflang="en">Kennedy's HHS relaxes enforcement on 6 popular wellness peptides</a>

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Kennedy's HHS relaxes enforcement on 6 popular wellness peptides" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Stopping short of a formal declaration allowing their use, Department of Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has cleared the way for the compounding of six popular wellness peptides.

The move, which comes after the same peptides won favor at an FDA advisory committee meeting this summer, is being hailed by some in the industry as offering a more accountable alternative to the gray market many peptide-seekers currently use, while others have charged that it undermines institutional FDA pushback on the drugs, which have become increasingly popular among fitness influencer circles and the MAHA crowd.

Under Kennedy’s “enforcement discretion policy” issued Wednesday, HHS will not take enforcement action against pharmacies, physicians and federal facilities compounding six specific peptides, assuming those parties meet certain specific qualifications.

The specific compounds included (PDF) in the list are BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500, all of which were discussed in a meeting of the FDA’s Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) in July, with the panel ultimately voting to recommend including the treatments onto the 503A Bulk Drug Substances list.

Advisory committee votes are recommendations, not binding decisions, and the peptides still haven’t been added to the drug substances list in an official capacity.

Kennedy cited that meeting in his enforcement decision, alongside data on utilization of peptides, noting that HHS believes there is “significant interest in the use of these peptides in compounding given the lack of availability of approved products.”

The letter clarifies the agency may revisit the interim policy if it leads to new safety signals or product quality issues.

The new policy comes with several other caveats that compounders must satisfy too, including sourcing their bulk drug substance from an FDA-registered facility and ensuring that the ingredients are properly characterized and identified. The original manufacturer of the bulk drug substance must also be registered with the FDA, according to HHS’ document.

In another change from typical compounding practice, compounders will be required to report quality issues and serious adverse events associated with the newly unrestricted peptides that are associated with their use, whether the drug itself is considered related or not, HHS clarified.

Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. Traditionally, the compounding practice has focused on the mixing or altering of ingredients of a drug to create a custom medication for a patient.

The practice can also come into play in order to fill drug shortages, which led to the pop-up of a GLP-1 compounding cottage industry for obesity that branded drugmakers Novo and Eli Lilly have continued to grapple with, even after supply shortfalls of their licensed medicines were declared formally ended in the United States.

In a statement Wednesday, Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding (APC) CEO Scott Brunner said he welcomed the decision, noting that it isn’t a question of whether patients are already using these six peptides, but of “where they’re getting them.”

“Right now, far too many patients are obtaining peptides from illicit suppliers with little assurance about what's actually in the vial,” he said. “That's an existing public health threat, and one that isn't going away simply because compounding of the substances is prohibited.”

While noting that the policy now offers a “more accountable alternative” to that murky peptide supply chain, Brunner emphasized the importance of the limits the enforcement action puts in place as well, while also highlighting questions that remain, including when the policy actually takes effect, how API testing and analysis will be validated and how product utilization reporting will work.

“That last point is especially important,” Brunner said. “A credible federal policy with meaningful patient safety safeguards gives states a sound basis for their own oversight and helps discourage a patchwork of state rules and restrictions that could undermine the access HHS is trying to provide.”

Others, including consumer advocacy nonprofit Public Citizen, adopted a decidedly more antagonist tone toward the new enforcement policy. In a statement issued this week, the group noted the peptides included in the new list are “popular but unproven and unapproved,” flagging Kennedy’s own personal support of the compounds.

Questions around conflicts of interest among panelists on the PCAC panel were also raised this summer, with the FDA quickly slotting in eight temporary voting members ahead of the confab in a bid to appease some of the criticism.

With the move, Kennedy “has acted recklessly and unilaterally to put Americans in danger,” Robert Steinbrook, M.D., Public Citizen’s health research group director, said in a statement, contending that the HHS secretary ought to “support, not sabotage, needed FDA enforcement actions to protect consumers.”

Oura, UC San Diego Health collaborate to study GLP-1 outcomes using continuous smart ring data" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Oura is pushing further into clinical healthcare research with its first clinical trial to study the link between smart ring data and obesity treatment outcomes.

The company is collaborating with UC San Diego Health on the clinical trial, called SMART-BMI. The multi-year study will investigate whether continuous data from the Oura Ring, including sleep, heart rate and activity, are associated with outcomes for patients receiving GLP-1 medications or bariatric surgery, the organizations said.

The Oura Ring data will capture changes between care visits, and researchers plan to examine associations with outcomes including weight loss, HbA1c and treatment persistence. The study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07736729.

“Longitudinal and surgical management of obesity can unlock the power of continuous monitoring and precision medicine offered by wearables,” said Jeff Reeves, M.D., UC San Diego Health.

“UC San Diego Health is precisely where innovative technology and subject matter experts come together to advance human health,” said Alexander Khalessi, neurosurgeon and chief innovation officer at UC San Diego Health, in a statement. “We are proud of the thought leadership of Dr. Reeves and Oura’s engagement to take on these important clinical questions for our patients.”

The smart ring maker and health tracking company has ambitions to build an artificial intelligence-powered clinical health platform. Founded in Finland in 2013, Oura's smart rings continuously track more than 50 health metrics, including heart rate, sleep, daily movement and activity, stress, fertility windows and metabolic health. The company was gearing up to go public, filing an initial public offering in late September with plans to raise $2.2 billion in a U.S. IPO by offering 50 million shares priced at $40 to $44 per share.

In October, the company announced it was postponing its IPO due to "uncertainty in the IPO market." The company said it was delaying its public listing "despite strong demand."

The multi-year obesity outcomes study is part of a broader clinical push being coordinated through Oura's Office of Regulatory and Clinical Affairs (ORCA), which was built over the past year under Chief Medical Officer Ricky Bloomfield, M.D. ORCA combines scientific and medical evidence, regulatory and quality work, outcomes research and clinical informatics.

"Since I started at Oura, my focus has been on formalizing the clinical rigor behind the trust people already place in their ring,” Bloomfield said. "As we take on a bigger role in healthcare with new use cases, partnerships and levels of clinical evidence, that trust has to be earned at every step. We do that by ensuring the right measurement, the right validation, the right outcomes data at every layer. ORCA is how we hold ourselves to that standard, alongside the researchers and institutions we work with."

Oura Ring has been included in over 200 peer-reviewed publications spanning sleep, cardiovascular health, women’s health, metabolic health, mental health, pregnancy and other areas.

The company is pursuing other outcomes-focused studies to examine how continuous Oura data relates to health outcomes, healthcare utilization and cost of care. Oura is working with Maven Clinic on a study of fertility and pregnancy outcomes. The study will look at both Oura-only and Oura-plus-Maven experiences and is designed to enroll at least 5,000 participants. It's also working with the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine to investigate whether wearable data can identify changes associated with rehospitalization and recurrent acute kidney injury following hospitalization.

The company recently completed a large-scale blood pressure study through Oura Labs to explore how Oura data could inform future regulated features related to blood pressure.

Separately, researchers at hospital systems and academic medical centers have independently used Oura Ring in more than 150 studies. Researchers at institutions including NYU Langone, Mount Sinai, Cornell Tech, Weill Cornell and the University of Central Florida are using Oura data to study areas such as depression, heart failure and inflammatory arthritis.

Health systems are also beginning to bring Oura data into care delivery. Mount Sinai Doctors–Concierge Care’s Healthspan Membership is integrating Oura Ring 5 data to give physicians a continuous view of biometric trends between visits.

“The real opportunity is to understand what’s happening between visits, how a patient is sleeping, recovering, moving and responding to changes in their daily life,” said Fiorina Kyritsi, M.D., of Mount Sinai Doctors–Concierge Care. “By bringing those insights into the care relationship, we can have a much more informed conversation with our members.”

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/eli-lillys-340b-data-policy-draws-new-opposition-missouri-ag-massachusetts-hospitals" hreflang="en">Eli Lilly's 340B data policy draws new opposition from Missouri AG, Massachusetts hospitals</a>

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Eli Lilly's 340B data policy draws new opposition from Missouri AG, Massachusetts hospitals" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Eli Lilly’s policy requiring 340B hospitals to submit claims-level data in order to receive statutory drug discounts garnered a fresh wave of pushback this week from Missouri’s attorney general and a cadre of Massachusetts hospitals, joining others who have described the requirements as unlawful.

Lilly announced its controversial policy for 340B dispensations in January and first put it into effect for a subset of hospitals in June. It applies to all pharmacy and medical dispensations, including in-house pharmacy and contract pharmacy dispenses, in most states.

The company said doing so would allow it to spot instances when a drug purchase is improperly granted multiple discounts under overlapping programs. Hospitals have contended that the extra scrutiny is unnecessary and introduces substantial administrative burden.

Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway has come in on the side of the opposition. On Monday, she issued a cease-and-desist letter to Lilly, writing that the policy violates the Missouri Merchandising Practices Act, a consumer protection law. If the drugmaker doesn’t end its requirement for Missouri providers, the policy “may result in subsequent legal action,” including an injunction and monetary civil penalties, the letter reads.

“Big Pharma does not get to bully Missouri hospitals or hold prescription drug prices hostage,” Hanaway said in an accompanying release. “Eli Lilly is abusing its market power to impose higher prescription prices in Missouri. These tactics fall hardest on rural communities that depend on the 340B program to keep clinics open and ensure patients receive the care they need.”

Hanaway’s letter and release highlight Lily’s decision to end discounts for the University of Missouri Health Care in July. The system later agreed to provide the data as requested, but “now bears the heavy costs and burdens of complying with Lilly’s requirements,” the attorney general’s office wrote.

A day later, six Massachusetts hospitals hit Lilly with a new lawsuit to block the policy. Massachusetts General Hospital, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Salem Hospital, UMass Memorial Medical Center and UMass Memorial Health — Harrington Hospital filed the lawsuit.

Their complaint filed in federal court describes “corporate extortion” from “the largest pharmaceutical company in the world” that “deprives them of resources essential to serving their most vulnerable patients.”

The hospitals “rightfully refused to comply” with the policy and had their 340B discounts pulled, echoing the claimed injuries from others who have sued over the policy, such as Tampa General Hospital and Mary Hitchcock Memorial Hospital. Failing to “capitulate” would cost the plaintiff Massachusetts hospitals “tens of millions of dollars in annual losses” collectively.

However, the new complaint criticizes Lilly’s 340B ESP platform, “a unilaterally selected, private-equity-owned third-party platform,” whose use is required by Lilly as “a data dragnet that, so far, has been free from public scrutiny.”

The hospitals said the platform’s claim to de-identify submitted claims data is superficial, and through non-negotiable contractual terms allows the company to use data beyond integrity review while limiting the company’s liability to “the unconscionable sum of $100.” Hospitals forced to use the platform to receive the drug discounts, meanwhile, take on “the full burden of privacy, cybersecurity and regulatory compliance risk” related to the claims data, which they said is protected health information under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

“Lilly has thus intentionally abused its substantial market power to create an untenable ‘Sophie’s choice’: Plaintiffs must capitulate to Lilly’s demands, incur higher operational costs, and surrender valuable privacy-protected Claims Data on non-negotiable Terms, or forgo their 340B savings, absorb substantially higher upfront drug costs, and jeopardize services for vulnerable patients,” they wrote in the complaint.

The hospitals wrote that the practices violate Massachusetts’ consumer protection law and are unjustly enriching the company. They asked the court to enjoin Lilly from enforcing its policy and terms, and to require compensatory damages “including but not limited to the amounts plaintiffs have paid to defendant above the applicable 340B Ceiling Price, lost 340B savings and other consequential damages.”

Fierce Healthcare has reached out to Lilly for comment on the attorney general’s letter and the new lawsuit. Lilly has historically disputed hospitals’ portrayal of its policy’s burdens in prior litigation and asserted that the required data is already routinely collected by the organizations.

This week’s developments add to the substantial legal showdowns surrounding the controversial drug discount program, which has ballooned in scale over the last several years. Drugmakers critical of the program have sought to impose restrictions on contract pharmacies, prompting litigation in states where lawmakers have prohibited such restrictions.

Policymakers’ own appetite to curb the subsidy program, such as through a pilot rebate program, has also spurred its fair share of litigation.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/look-optum-rxs-new-program-managing-menopause" hreflang="en">A look at Optum Rx's new program for managing menopause</a>

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
A look at Optum Rx's new program for managing menopause" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Optum Rx is launching a new program that aims to integrate specialized care for women navigating menopause into the pharmacy benefit.

The program is a virtual-first offering that connects eligible women with clinicians that have been certified with the Menopause Society. Through the program, they can access both hormonal and nonhormonal therapies, symptom tracking, education and one-on-one patient coaching.

Tracy Spencer, senior vice president for product and strategy at Optum Rx, told Fierce Healthcare that 50 million women are currently experiencing symptoms related to either perimenopause, menopause and post-menopause. And 40% of those women say those symptoms impact their work performance.

"This is an area that remains one of the most underserved in healthcare, which is why we're here today, because closing that gap for women's health is really important," Spencer said.

Optum initially piloted the program beginning at the end of 2025, and Spencer said that pilot ended in September. She said that participants in the pilot reported that, on average, they eliminated five symptoms related to menopause. Most (80%) of the women in the pilot saw an improvement in their symptoms, she said.

And beyond the symptoms directly linked to menopause, having a program in place that supports women during this phase of life can also mitigate the risk of developing future conditions like osteoporosis, diabetes, heart disease and Alzheimer's disease, said Spencer.

When a member enrolls in the program, they'll be given a personalized health assessment by the clinical team to determine what their treatment plan should look like. The Optum Rx clinicians work with the member's existing clinical team, including primary care doctors or gynecologists.

Members can also access a digital interface that allows them to track symptoms, access educational materials and connect with the support coaches, Spencer said.

"It's really meant as a complement to the woman's current care team," she said.

Menopause can also be a lengthy stage of life for many women, with some experiencing symptoms for a decade or more. Spencer said the median length for hot flashes and night sweats, for example, is seven years.

So the program is built to support women in the long term over their menopause health journey, Spencer said.

"The ability to have those ongoing resources that are accessible for women when they want to receive them, how they want to receive them, is incredibly important," she said. "We always feel it's important to meet the patient where they're at."

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/new-york-leaders-present-opioid-treatment-progress-2027-medicaid-plans" hreflang="en">New York officials share OUD treatment progress, plans for new pilot to treat stimulant use disorder</a>

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
New York officials share OUD treatment progress, plans for new pilot to treat stimulant use disorder" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Two years after federal regulations permanently adopted greater flexibility for medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), New York experts convened to discuss the benefits and challenges of take-home medications.

The annual COMPA Symposium, which was also livestreamed, was hosted by the Coalition of Medication-Assisted Treatment Providers and Advocates, a nonprofit membership organization in New York. State officials also discussed how to prevent interruptions to OUD services amid coming Medicaid eligibility and work requirement changes as part of H.R. 1.

Chinazo Cunningham, M.D., a commissioner of New York’s Office of Addiction Services and Supports (OASAS), presented on the successes the state has had thus far in expanding OUD treatment access.

“People of color in the state of New York are dying [at] the highest levels,” Cunningham began, adding that the good news is that Black New Yorkers had the largest reduction in overdose deaths from 2023 to 2025. Part of that is thanks to flexible dosing, first introduced during COVID-19 and made permanent by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) in 2024.

This flexibility has both pros and cons, highlighted by a separate clinical panel. Allowing patients to take vital MOUD home acknowledges various social determinants that present barriers to arriving in-person on a regular basis for treatment, speakers agreed. At the same time, some cases call for extra caution, like if a patient is unstable and may be using while in treatment.

“The safety of methadone—it is a medication that we have to be very careful with,” Brett Palmer, P.A.-C, an outpatient physician assistant, said during the clinical panel. There is real risk of diversion, which patients sometimes even own up to, he said. Thus, take-home allowance “is a frequent discussion” and requires strong judgment on the part of counselors.

Myra Mathis, M.D., medical director at Strong Recovery, a certified community behavioral health clinic and opioid treatment program (OTP) in Rochester, praised the new rule while acknowledging its drawbacks. “I think it’s a positive change that medication access is no longer tied to therapy requirements,” Mathis said. But taking home MOUD also means some patients will fall behind on therapy.

To help address this, Strong Recovery added a new role of engagement specialist, whose job is to track and identify disengaged patients who have not attended individual or group therapy in three months. They can then opt into a “dispensing-only” track. That program takes into account special considerations, like access to Narcan for unstable patients and other harm reduction supplies. Patients in the track also need safety planning and screening for suicidality. The specialist meets with them at least once quarterly, which helps the program meet its billing requirements.

The state has been making progress on MOUD access and uptake across a variety of settings.

There are currently 140 outpatient methadone programs in New York. “I’m so proud of that,” Cunningham said. As the drug market continues to rapidly evolve, with fentanyl and other powerful opioids, “the role of methadone has never been more important.

Also across the state, 41 prisons and 58 jails offer all forms of MOUD. These institutions are notoriously difficult to get treatment into. Fewer than half of jails offer MOUD nationwide.

“When you’ve seen one jail, you’ve seen one jail,” Cunningham said, explaining that every county and sheriff has a different mindset. But thanks to state and community partners, MOUD access is now widespread. “There is no other state that even comes close to this,” Cunningham said.

From 2022 to 2025, the number of people receiving MOUD in New York prisons increased 7x, and those starting MOUD for the first time increased 360x. The increase was largely driven by buprenorphine access, though even the number of people on methadone doubled. In jails, from 2022 to 2024, there has also been progress. The number of people enrolled in MOUD tripled, with buprenorphine making up the bulk of MOUD use in 2024 (83%).

“Individuals who are incarcerated have among the highest rates of overdose deaths,” Cunningham said, at over 100x the risk compared to the general population. Black and Hispanic New Yorkers, who have among the highest overdose deaths in the state, are also incarcerated at much higher rates. MOUD provision in jail settings has been shown to decrease overdose risk upon release.

A big focus of Cunningham’s presentation was stimulant use disorder, which slightly more Americans have than opioid use disorder. The condition has no FDA-approved medications. “It can be frustrating for providers and programs to provide treatment,” Cunningham said. New York’s approach is to pilot a treatment modality known as contingency management, which provides incentives to reinforce positive behavior change.

“Contingency management has been studied for decades around drug use, and it’s definitely known to be effective,” Cunningham said. The reason it’s not widely used is “implementing it in the real world is very difficult.” Cunningham led a seven-year-long NIH-funded study on the modality, and even with a large annual budget, she struggled to pull it off.

The pilot in the state will begin with eight outpatient non-OTPs, set to launch next month and run for 18 months. For 12 weeks, patients with primary stimulant use disorder will come to the clinic twice a week, several days apart, and get toxicology urine testing on the spot. If it is stimulant-negative, they will get a gift card of about $15. After 12 weeks, they will come in once weekly for another 12 weeks. The financial incentive will build over time, up to $750, as allowed by SAMHSA. If they miss an appointment or relapse, the incentive restarts at $0.

“Decades of data show this is the most effective treatment for stimulant use disorder,” Cunningham reiterated. If the pilots work well, “then the goal is to really expand this statewide.”

Some regulatory changes have helped make this treatment more feasible. SAMSHSA used to cap the financial incentives at $75, not nearly enough to drive behavior change, Cunningham said. The state can also use an 1115 waiver to get reimbursed for contingency management care, though its current waiver expires in March 2027. Its renewal request is under review.

Up to a million New Yorkers on Medicaid will be subject to new work requirements under H.R. 1 changes.

Trisha Schell-Guy, director of the Division of Program Development and Management in the Office of Health Insurance Programs, presented on the ways the state is preparing to minimize coverage loss.

There are three ways to meet the new requirements in New York. A household could have an income of at least $580 a month. A member could complete 80 hours of an approved activity in a month. Or it could be a combination of the two. Starting Jan. 1, 2027, new applicants must show compliance with the new requirements for the month before they apply. Existing members face renewal dates in March.

Certain groups are exempt from the work requirements, Schell-Guy noted, due to age or a certain condition. Groups like American Indian/Alaska Native people and foster care youth are also exempt. Additionally, there are short-term hardship exemptions, which last for six months and require an application. At the county level, these include high-unemployment areas and declared emergency or disaster areas. At the individual level, these include institutional or high-acuity care and extended travel for healthcare. The state is working to secure a short-term exemption for the Bronx as a high unemployment area, Schell-Guy noted.

For work requirements, New York is using an attestation approach for the first year—meaning members answer a short questionnaire—in recognition that it takes time to gather the necessary documentation. For 2028 and beyond, attestation will not be permitted.

“We have to use data,” Schell-Guy said, which will require collaboration between countless agencies. “There’s tons of data sources that we are trying to access here.”

Also under direction from CMS, New York has a plan for provider enrollment and re-validation. This is part of a new CMS push requiring every state to form a plan to re-validate Medicaid providers, especially those in “high-risk” areas. The state is taking a risk-tiered approach in phases. Providers considered high-risk include durable medical equipment providers, those that haven’t re-validated since the start of COVID-19, applied behavioral analysis therapy providers and home- or community-based services providers. The state’s re-validation process was on paper until 2025, proving unreliable, Schell-Guy said. Now, it is all digital.

To get the word out about coming changes, the state is staffing up its customer service center with a direct line for H.R. 1 questions; is sending member notices via a number of communication channels; has social media campaigns and TV ads; and is doing in-person events. It has also upped its number of assistors who can answer questions and help fill out applications.

“Now we expect there to be a lot of utilization of these assistors,” Schell-Guy said.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/judge-rejects-nc-state-health-plans-request-block-atrium-affordability-program" hreflang="en">Atrium Health, NC State Health Plan reach deal in preferred provider network dispute</a>

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Atrium Health, NC State Health Plan reach deal in preferred provider network dispute" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Atrium Health and North Carolina's public health insurance plan have come to terms on a dispute over inclusion in a tiered network and an alternative pricing program the health system had planned to implement in response.

The pair announced in a joint statement the signing of a new multi-year contract on Tuesday, a week before the close of open enrollment for the North Carolina State Health Plan. The deal followed months of negotiation, exchanged barbs and a trip to the courtroom (see that coverage below the break).

“Thank you to our hundreds of thousands of members who have trusted us and trusted the process to bring you the high-quality care you want at costs we can all afford,” North Carolina Treasurer Brad Briner, who chairs the State Health Plan Board of Trustees. “We know there has been a lot of confusion for our members right now as we are in the middle of Open Enrollment. We hope this agreement provides members with more clarity about provider tiers and puts concerns to rest while avoiding drastic premium and benefit changes statewide.”

The 750,000-member plan had recently implemented a Preferred Provider Program, authorized under state statute, that reduced members’ copays if they received care from the lower-cost providers assigned to preferred tiers. Most of Atrium's facilities and providers did not receive a preferred tier, leading the large nonprofit to unveil an Affordability Protection Program that it said would charge plan members smaller copays. The state tried, and failed, to have Atrium's program blocked, noting that the system would still charge its full rates to the plan at taxpayers' long-term expense.

Under the new agreement, Atrium's hospitals that did not initially receive a preferred designation will be added to the Preferred Provider Program's in-network Access tier (the middle of three tiers). The parties said the decision will improve access and reduce costs for members while still reducing the plan's overall spending.

“For every teacher, state employee and family member we serve at Atrium Health, this new agreement is about providing the peace of mind that comes from knowing the caregivers they trust will remain within reach,” said Steve Smoot, president of Advocate Health's North Carolina and Georgia Division, which includes Atrium Health. “We are grateful to move forward together with a shared commitment to making high-quality care more accessible and affordable for every State Health Plan member who depends on us.”

The agreement sees Atrium sunsetting its Affordability Protection Program and the state withdrawing its outstanding legal complaint. The pair wrote in their statement that they recognize the past few months have been unsettling for members and their families, and each committed to put any differences aside to move forward and keep working together for the benefit of every teacher and state employee who counts on them both.

Atrium Health scored an early courtroom win on Tuesday when a judge rejected the state of North Carolina’s request for a temporary restraining order on an affordability program the health system plans to implement for State Health Plan members in the 2027 calendar year.

The Atrium Health Affordability Protection Program, unveiled last week, would lower members’ out-of-pocket costs but, according to the State Treasurer of North Carolina’s office, effectively shift those costs back to the plan.

Notably, the cost sharing would make Atrium’s facilities more appealing to plan members than other health systems in the state with lower rates. The office had recently implemented a Preferred Provider Program, authorized under state statute, that reduced members’ copays if they received care from the lower-cost providers.

Atrium, which only had some of its services and locations designated as a preferred provider, announced its Affordability Protection Program bringing copays even lower than those in the state’s Preferred Provider Program “to deceive Plan participants into believing” that its services were lower cost, the state wrote in a complaint filed last Friday. In reality, the state said, Atrium’s program would “maintain higher reimbursement rates … without reducing the overall cost of services, particularly for high-cost services and very sick patients who use a significant number of services.”

The treasurer in recent weeks has described Atrium’s program as unlawful and a “shell game” that would bankrupt the plan. The state also alleged in its complaint that Atrium acted in bad faith by waiting until just days before the 750,000-member plan’s open enrollment period to sow confusion among plan members and “undermine” the state’s program.

Atrium has described its program as addressing “any remaining affordability gap” for plan members and a response to cost concern feedback from clinicians and other community members—and noted in its announcement that the Affordability Protection Program “is not affiliated with, authorized by or endorsed by the NC State Treasurer, the NC State Health Plan or the State of North Carolina.

After hearing arguments surrounding the temporary restraining order request, Judge Matthew Houston denied the state’s request from the bench, allowing Atrium’s program to go into effect during the upcoming Open Enrollment period and 2027 plan year.

"We're pleased by the court’s ruling to deny the temporary injunction," Steve Smoot, president of Atrium parent company Advocate Health's North Carolina and Georgia Division, where Atrium is located, said in a Tuesday statement. "From the very start, this has been about the people we serve: the more than 80,000 teachers, first responders, state employees and their families, from across North Carolina, who trust Atrium Health with their care. Thousands of State Health Plan members have told us they didn't want to choose between paying more and leaving the physicians and care teams who have walked with them through some of life's most serious health challenges."

The treasurer’s office, in its own statement, said that “while this is not the outcome we were hoping for, … the judge did acknowledge our argument that the Program could cause irreparable harm to the Plan.”

The State Health Plan said it’s still exploring “other legal pathways,” but is also weighing how best to maintain solvency through 2027 and beyond. That could include raising premiums for all of its members by $50 per month in 2028, it said.

“The State Health Plan is funded by member and taxpayer dollars, and those resources are finite,” Treasurer Brad Briner said in a statement. “When a single provider can manipulate the Plan’s cost structure, dictate how benefits are designed or undermine our tiered network, it won’t just be disruptive—it could be catastrophic for the Plan, its members and taxpayers.”

Atrium, in its statement, said that the office’s suggestions of a premium increase haven’t been detailed, and would be “the result of the Plan’s own flawed design.” It also again contested the state’s characterization of its rates as high cost, and pointed to a September statement from the Plan that called Atrium “no more expensive than other leading systems in North Carolina” prior to the launch of its Preferred Provider tiering.

The legal conflict had been simmering for months. The lowest overall vascular risk was seen among tea drinkers who were also physically active and didn’t smoke.

The study followed 485,934 people ages 30–79 from the China Kadoorie Biobank for an average of 9 years.

The researchers found that regular tea drinkers had an 11% lower overall risk of vascular disease compared with those who did not regularly drink tea. They also observed a 37% lower risk of ischemic stroke and a 17% lower risk of heart attack.

There was no clear link for hemorrhagic stroke or pulmonary heart disease.

Among people who drank tea at least weekly, the lowest estimated risk was seen in those consuming approximately 6–8 cups per day. However, the findings do not establish this as a recommended daily intake.

Amit Blumfield, MD, a cardiologist with Manhattan Cardiology in NYC who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that it is important to remember this is observational data.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining the effects of drinking black and green tea on lipids and blood pressure showed only modest, non-significant benefits.

This Is How Fast You Should Walk to Help Lower Your Risk of Death

New research shows that how fast you walk, whether measured in a clinical test or through daily activity, can help predict your risk of dying from any cause.

The study compared two ways of measuring walking: a short walking speed test conducted in a clinic and the step rate people take in their everyday lives, measured with a wrist-worn device.

Both measures were linked to mortality risk and showed similar ability to predict it.

The findings, published on October 23 in JAMA Network Open, could help doctors and researchers choose the best method for assessing health risks in different settings.

The study was part of the Whitehall II accelerometer substudy in the United Kingdom.

It included 4,067 participants, mostly males, with an average age of about 69 years. About one-third of the subjects were female.

The researchers collected data from January 2012 to December 2013 and followed up through August 2024.

To measure clinical walking speed, participants completed a standard walking test. They walked a marked 2.44-meter course at their usual pace. The test was repeated three times, and the average speed in meters per second was used for analysis. Participants who normally used a cane were allowed to use it during the test.

For free-living walking measurements, participants wore a wrist-worn GENEActiv accelerometer for 9 consecutive days. This device recorded movement at a high frequency and used special software to detect walking episodes and count steps.

From this data, two key measures of walking cadence were calculated: the median walking cadence (the middle value of steps per minute during walking periods) and the 95th-percentile walking cadence (a higher step rate indicating brisk walking). The study also calculated the average number of daily steps.

The researchers additionally gathered information on factors known to affect mortality, such as:

National death records were used to identify deaths during the follow-up period.

Statistical methods were then used to estimate the risk of death associated with walking speed and cadence while accounting for other risk factors.

The models were adjusted step-by-step: starting with no adjustments, then adding sociodemographic factors, behavioral factors, and finally health-related factors.

The ability of walking measures to predict mortality was assessed using a statistical measure.

The study also examined differences between males and females.

The researchers additionally conducted analyses to test how well the results held up when they excluded early deaths, looked at subgroups by age and health status, and used different definitions of walking cadence.

During a median follow-up of nearly 12 years, 739 participants died.

The average clinical walking speed of participants was 1.2 meters per second. The median walking cadence during daily life was about 75 steps per minute, and the 95th percentile walking cadence was about 112 steps per minute.

The study found only a weak correlation between clinical walking speed and free-living walking cadence, meaning the two measures do not strongly reflect the same walking behavior. However, both measures were independently associated with a lower risk of death.

After adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle, and health factors, for every standard increase in clinical walking speed, the risk of death was 23% lower.

Similarly, higher median and 95th-percentile walking cadences were associated with 17% and 18% lower risks of death, respectively.

When examining how well these walking measures predicted mortality, each on its own showed modest predictive ability.

Adding clinical walking speed to a model with other known risk factors slightly improved mortality prediction, while adding free-living walking cadence also improved prediction, albeit to a similar degree to that of clinical walking speed.

Combining clinical walking speed with free-living walking parameters provided a small boost in predictive ability, but the effect was not significant after accounting for detailed health factors.

The study also found that walking speed and cadence had similar predictive power for both men and women, although the overall prediction models performed slightly better for women.

Several sensitivity analyses supported the main findings. Results were consistent when excluding early deaths, focusing on healthier or older participants, and using different ways to define walking cadence.

Overall, this study suggests that clinical walking speed tests and free-living walking cadence measurements from wrist-worn devices are both useful in predicting all-cause mortality risk. They provide similar information but reflect different aspects of walking behavior.

The choice between methods may depend on the purpose of the assessment, available resources, and the context in which the measurement is taken.

Neha Sabharwal, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and director of rehabilitation specializing in geriatric mobility and functional decline at Vintage Faire Nursing and Rehabilitation Center in Modesto, CA, said that walking speed helps predict longevity because it’s an indicator of someone’s overall health.

“Walking is something most of us do without thinking about it, but it requires several body systems to work together,” she told Healthline. “Your heart and lungs have to keep up, your muscles need to produce enough force, and your brain has to coordinate each step while keeping you balanced.”

Sabharwal, who wasn’t involved in the study, said that in her work, she pays attention to when someone starts walking more slowly than before.

“It is easy to dismiss that change as part of getting older, but there may be something else going on,” she said. This could include muscle weakness, pain, reduced endurance, balance problems, or underlying medical conditions.

According to Sabharwal, the current study is valuable because it suggests that useful information about people’s movement can be obtained outside a clinical setting.

However, she cautioned against misinterpreting the findings to mean that simply walking faster will make you live longer.

“People who naturally walk faster may already have better underlying health,” said Sabharwal.

“I would be less concerned about whether someone walks faster than their neighbor and more interested in whether they are walking slower than they did six months ago,” she said.

Theodore Strange, MD, chairman of medicine at Northwell Staten Island University Hospital and senior vice president of the Metro Market Region at Northwell Health, shared some insights for those who may notice their walking speed has declined. Strange wasn’t involved in the study.

“I mean, if it’s slowing down because a week or two ago, you were walking up a flight of steps with no problem, and now, you’re having some shortness of breath, I think it should be evaluated,” he told Healthline.

Strange added that if you’re experiencing aches or pains that weren’t there before, you should consult a healthcare professional, as they can make exercise more difficult.

It’s also essential to manage any chronic diseases that you have so that you can exercise as well as possible within the constraints of your condition, he said.

“And I think slow and steady wins the race here,” said Strange. He suggests monitoring your walking with a device like a pedometer and progressively increasing your steps.

“Everybody talks about 10,000 steps a day. I think the most recent studies show probably 7,000 to 8,000 is adequate,” he said. You can start where you are and work your way up gradually.

Strange further noted that physical therapy can sometimes be helpful if you have issues with gait mechanics, balance, or strength. Additionally, you might benefit from exercise that is tailored to your condition, for example, if you’ve had a stroke.

Finally, Strange advised stretching before and after exercise to keep your muscles moving.

“I go back to adequate nutrition and hydration being important,” he said. You want to make sure you are adequately supporting your body’s ability to exercise, said Strange.

Females using menopause hormone therapy (MHT) alongside GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic or Wegovy may experience greater weight loss, according to a study presented at the 2026 Annual Meeting of the Menopause Society.

Weight gain is a common concern among middle-aged women. While much of this is associated with aging, hormonal changes during menopause can also contribute to changes in body fat and its distribution.

GLP-1 medications have been shown to be effective for weight loss. However, the results may vary by sex and reproductive stage.

For example, studies have found that women and adults under age 55 may experience greater weight loss with these medications.

A previous small observational study in postmenopausal women found that using MHT alongside semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, was associated with greater weight loss.

However, whether hormone therapy influences weight loss outcomes with different GLP-1 medications remains unclear.

To investigate this question, researchers compared weight loss in middle-aged women receiving either semaglutide or tirzepatide, with or without MHT. The findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

The study included 3,855 women with an average age of 57 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 36 kg/m2.

The semaglutide cohort included 2,379 individuals, while the tirzepatide cohort included 1,476 individuals.

The average follow-up in the semaglutide cohort, both with and without MHT, was 26 months. The average follow-up in the tirzepatide group was 17 months for those using MHT and 16 months for those not using it.

The researchers found that in the semaglutide cohort, those using MHT alongside the GLP-1 medication had significantly greater weight loss at 9 months than those not using MHT.

These differences were sustained at 12 and 15 months and at the final follow-up. Those using MHT were also more likely to reach up to a 30% total body weight loss than those not using it.

In the tirzepatide cohort, those using MHT had a modestly greater weight loss at 3 months and were more likely to achieve more than 5% total body weight loss by the final follow-up. However, no significant difference in weight loss was observed at later time points.

These findings suggest that MHT use may be associated with greater weight loss among middle-aged women taking semaglutide. However, the researchers did not observe a sustained, statistically significant difference in weight loss among those taking tirzepatide.

Betul Hatipoglu, MD, Director of the Diabetes and Obesity Center at the University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center, who was not involved in the study, told Healthline that the findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that people should be careful not to interpret them as evidence that hormone therapy should be added to GLP-1 treatment specifically to enhance weight loss.

“This is an association, and the study provides an opportunity to discuss how menopause, aging, changes in body composition, and metabolic health can be present,” she said.

It is important to keep in mind that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved certain hormone therapy medications to treat menopause symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats, and vaginal dryness. However, these medications are not FDA-approved for weight loss or obesity management.

The study authors noted that larger studies are needed to confirm their observations and to inform individualized strategies for middle-aged females.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB-GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who was not involved in the study, noted that weight gain is one of the most common concerns she hears from women during menopause. Beyond affecting self-esteem and quality of life, excess abdominal fat can increase the risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease.

“Finding effective, sustainable ways to manage weight is important for women’s long-term health and healthy aging,” she said.

“I would like to see prospective, randomized studies examining GLP-1 medications with and without hormone therapy to determine whether hormone therapy directly improves weight loss and helps women maintain it over time. In the meantime, treatment decisions should be individualized and guided by a healthcare professional.”

Ross advised that the decision should be based on your individual symptoms, health history, and treatment goals.

“Hormone therapy can significantly improve disruptive menopausal symptoms, including hot flashes, sleep disturbances, and mood changes. By helping women feel better and more energetic, it may also make it easier to exercise and manage their weight,” she said.

Hatipoglu noted that GLP-1-based medications, on the other hand, are effective treatments for obesity and can improve important aspects of metabolic health. Their use should be based on an individual’s weight-related health risks, medical history, and treatment goals.

“For a woman who has both bothersome menopausal symptoms and obesity, it may be entirely appropriate to consider both treatments. But I would not recommend starting hormone therapy solely to improve the weight-loss response to a GLP-1 medication based on the evidence currently available,” she said.

“It is also important to remember that successful weight management during and after menopause is about more than the number on the scale. Preserving muscle mass, maintaining bone health, improving metabolic health, and supporting long-term physical function are equally important.”

But despite bumps in her journey, Kitty remains convinced that the benefits of GLP-1s for her life and health outweigh any drawbacks she’s experienced. “I am conscious that I will probably be on this drug for a long time if not for life. It is truly a miracle drug.”

I also spoke to Ann Marie Maguire, AGNP-C, RN, TCTCN, a nurse practitioner specializing in transplantation and cellular therapy, the sister of Healthline’s Chief Business Officer Christine Macguire, and someone who has lost over 50lbs since starting GLP-1s in 2022.

“I struggled with my weight since my teens and always believed I should be able to manage it on my own. But in my 30s, my knees were hurting just getting out of bed or walking to dinner. I realized how much my weight was affecting my quality of life. Starting a GLP-1 in 2022, alongside healthier eating and exercise, made a tremendous difference. My only regret is not starting sooner,” said Ann Marie.

As a healthcare professional, Ann Marie also identified cost as a major factor that can’t be ignored regarding GLP-1s and their impact.

“There’s understandable debate about the long-term use and cost of these medications,” she says. “But we also need to consider the health and financial burden of obesity, including cardiovascular complications, hospitalizations, surgeries and ongoing medications. The cost conversation should account for the potential benefits of preventing or reducing those complications.”

Ann Marie points to a frustration many patients will recognize: reaching a healthier weight can become the reason coverage gets cut off. “We don’t automatically stop blood pressure medication because a patient’s readings improve,” she says. The treatment may be what’s keeping the condition controlled. Obesity, she argues, deserves the same approach. “Reaching a goal weight can mean treatment is working, and decisions about continuing it should reflect the patient’s ongoing needs, benefits and side effects.”

It’s important to note that this article looks at the experiences of only a handful of people out of the millions who now take GLP-1s. Like all drugs, these medications are not right for everyone. Side effects are common; some of them can be serious, and both the long-term impact and what happens when people stop are still being worked out. Ultimately, your care team, with knowledge of your medical history, is a far better guide than a comment thread.

Yet the evidence now reaches well beyond the scales: large trials have linked semaglutide to fewer heart attacks and strokes in people with heart disease and slower kidney decline in people with diabetes and kidney disease, and tirzepatide became the first FDA-approved drug for moderate to severe sleep apnea in adults with obesity.

Stories like Kitty’s, Ann Marie’s, Mayim Bialik’s, and those of the 3.5k commenters on the Facebook post aren’t proof of what will happen to you one way or another. But for a lot of people, my mom included, these drugs have changed what an ordinary day looks like, and in many cases changed what their future can look like.

One of the most significant drug developments of the century so far? Hard to argue with.

ADHD Medications May Help Ease Menopausal Brain Fog

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Medications commonly used to treat attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) may help reduce certain symptoms of menopausal brain fog, according to a review presented at the 2026 Annual Meeting of the Menopause Society. The findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

The research suggests that both lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) and atomoxetine (Strattera) may improve self-reported executive function in perimenopausal and postmenopausal people. However, they showed little to no effect on objective cognitive tests.

While this scoping review showed promising results, experts caution that it may be too early to begin recommending these medications to females experiencing menopause.

Sara Vaughn, MD, Chief Medical Officer at Clair Health, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are interesting and hypothesis-generating, but they do not yet establish ADHD medications as a treatment for menopausal brain fog.

Many middle-aged females report experiencing forgetfulness, slow thinking, reduced concentration, and difficulty finding words. This is often referred to as “brain fog,” and is a common occurrence during various stages of menopause.

These symptoms of brain fog also mirror the symptoms of ADHD.

Brain fog may be due to declining or fluctuating levels of estrogen during perimenopause, which affects dopamine and norepinephrine signaling in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus. This can impact memory, focus, and executive functioning.

Despite how disruptive these symptoms can be to quality of life, there is currently no medication approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to manage them. This often leaves clinicians in a position of trying to differentiate between brain fog from menopause and undiagnosed ADHD.

The review analyzed five studies involving females ages 45–60 with their last menstrual period within the past 10 years. The studies all focused on how ADHD medications affect outcome measures of cognition and ADHD severity.

The researchers found that with lisdexamfetamine, scores on the Brown Attention Deficit Disorder Scale (BADDS), a self-report rating scale that measures ADHD symptoms, dropped from 35.7 to 21.2, compared with 29.8 with a placebo. With atomoxetine, BADDS scores decreased from 38.6 to 25.5, compared with 30.1 with a placebo.

There was no significant benefit observed on objective cognitive tests (ex., memory and processing speed).

These findings suggest that certain ADHD medications may improve perceived cognitive symptoms during menopause, although benefits were not demonstrated on objective cognitive tests.

Jennifer Tat, a medical student at the University of Tennessee Health Sciences Center and lead author of the study, noted, “In the population data, symptom severity, not a formal ADHD diagnosis, predicted menopausal burden. Many women were never diagnosed, so relying on a chart diagnosis misses the women most at risk.”

“We have evidence that the menopause transition is associated with measurable brain changes,” said Vaughn. “But no specific brain signature of menopausal brain fog has been established. To study it rigorously, we first need to define what the term means and measure it consistently.”

Limitations of this review include the small number of studies and the small sample sizes. Most of the measures were also self-reported, which leaves room for error.

Also, the FDA has not approved these types of medications for menopausal brain fog, meaning using them for these purposes would be off-label.

“ADHD medications are not without risks, and I think it is too early to use them routinely for menopausal ‘brain fog,’” said Vaughn.

ADHD medications can carry risks, including cardiovascular side effects. Stimulants such as lisdexamfetamine also have the potential for misuse and dependence. These medications should only be used with medical supervision.

“I am a strong proponent of treating the underlying disorder rather than treating isolated symptoms. ADHD and menopausal cognitive symptoms may look alike, but that does not mean they share the same underlying mechanism—or require the same treatment,” noted Vaughn.

Further research, including additional long-term prospective studies, is needed before clinical guidance can be changed

“We hope this opens up the conversations with patients and providers. We recommend clinicians to screen patients for ADHD and, for those who screen positive, refer them to our psychiatric colleagues who have the knowledge and resources to perform a diagnostic test so that those women who have gone undiagnosed with ADHD can get the treatment they need and deserve,” said Tat.
