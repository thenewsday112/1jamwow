Permitting deal draws lobbying blitz as hurdles mount

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The fragile Senate permitting deal is a major priority across the energy industry, per newly filed lobbying disclosures.

Why it matters: The filings reveal behind-the-scenes efforts by powerful interests and companies representing oil and gas, mining, renewables, environmentalists and plenty in between to shape the legislation.

The big picture: The bill title (Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act), its acronym (BAAJA) and its bill number appear throughout third-quarter lobbying disclosures.

  • The bill was introduced Sept. 30, the last day of the third quarter. The disclosures, due Oct. 20, offer a window into efforts to influence the legislation during its drafting and negotiations.
  • The lobbying extends beyond groups that specifically named the bill in their filings. Many others with a stake in the bill — like the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the National Association of Manufacturers — list permitting legislation in other ways.

Driving the news: Here's just a small subset of the parties in the arena.

  • Industry groups like the American Petroleum Institute, the Edison Electric Institute, the American Forest & Paper Association, the National Mining Association, and the Data Center Coalition.
  • Green groups including Earthjustice, the Nature Conservancy, and Defenders of Wildlife.
  • Low-carbon power trade groups such as the Solar Energy Industries Association.

State of play: The lobbying goes far beyond trade associations, with many individual companies also involved.

  • They range from Chevron and BP to power giants like Duke Energy and Southern Company to geothermal startup Fervo.
  • Industrial and equipment heavyweights like Caterpillar, Schneider Electric and Cummins are there, too.

Zoom in: The disclosures also offer clues about which provisions attracted lobbying attention. For example:

  • The National Mining Association identified BAAJA provisions that would change Clean Water Act permitting, including state water quality reviews and limits on lawsuits challenging permits.
  • The Interstate Natural Gas Association of America listed BAAJA provisions affecting interstate natural gas pipelines among its lobbying topics, alongside other pipeline permitting proposals.

Threat level: The latest filings land amid new friction over the bill.

  • This week the Interior Department asked a federal judge to allow it to reconsider the Biden-era prior approval of Avangrid's planned New England Wind project off Massachusetts.
  • "This is how you blow up a permitting deal," Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.) told Axios and other outlets about the latest in a string of Trump team moves against previously approved projects.

Yes, but: An administration official cast the federal filing as a procedural move in the case brought by other opponents of the project.

  • "Our filings defend the current government's litigating position," the official said.
  • "We have been very transparent about the need for permitting reform and have also been transparent with Senator Whitehouse's and Senator [Martin] Heinrich's teams on all these legal filings," the official said, citing the two Democratic co-authors.

🆕 The latest: Democratic attorneys general from nine states express "substantial concerns" with the bill in a new letter to lawmakers, Punchbowl News reported this morning.

What we're watching: The public advocacy and closed-door maneuvering should reach a fever pitch ahead of the first procedural vote on Nov. 9, and in the days and weeks that follow.

  • The next wave of disclosures, covering October-December, should provide an even clearer look at the interests at play.

Millions take fish oil for brain health. A two-year study found no benefit

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Millions of Americans take fish oil supplements in hopes of protecting their memory and keeping their brains healthy as they age. These products have become a major business, with Americans spending more than $1 billion each year on supplements containing omega-3 fatty acids. These essential nutrients help build and maintain the connections between brain cells that support memory, learning, and other cognitive functions.

But a new study suggests that getting more omega-3s into the brain through supplements may not provide the protection many people expect.

Researchers at Keck Medicine of USC found that even when high doses of omega-3s successfully reached the brain, they did not produce measurable improvements in memory, thinking abilities, or protection against brain shrinkage.

The findings, published in eBioMedicine, come from a two-year clinical trial involving older adults considered to be at increased risk of developing Alzheimer's disease. Despite taking omega-3 supplements daily, participants showed no significant cognitive advantage over those who received a placebo.

"We all wish there was a silver bullet for preventing Alzheimer's, but our findings showed that fish oil supplements do not appear to protect brain health," said Hussein Naji Yassine, MD, director of the USC Center for Personalized Brain Health and lead investigator of the study. "While omega-3s play an important role in forming brain cell connections needed for cognition, our results do not support fish oil supplements as a preventive measure against Alzheimer's."

A Two-Year Study Tests Fish Oil's Brain Benefits

To investigate whether omega-3 supplements could protect the aging brain, researchers enrolled 365 adults between the ages of 55 and 80. All participants rarely ate fish, a major dietary source of omega-3 fatty acids, and were considered at elevated risk for Alzheimer's disease.

Nearly half (47%) carried APOE4, a gene variant associated with a substantially higher risk of developing Alzheimer's later in life. Although having this variant does not mean someone will necessarily develop the disease, it is the strongest known genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's.

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a daily fish oil supplement or a placebo, an inactive treatment used for comparison. The supplements provided 2,000 mg of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an omega-3 fatty acid that is especially important for the brain.

DHA is a major component of brain cell membranes, helping maintain the structure and function of neurons. It also supports communication between nerve cells, making it an important nutrient for normal brain activity.

The study was double-blind, meaning neither participants nor researchers knew who was receiving the active supplement during the trial. This approach helps reduce bias and makes it easier to determine whether any observed benefits are actually caused by the treatment.

Omega-3 Levels Rose, but Memory Did Not Improve

Before evaluating whether the supplements improved brain health, researchers needed to answer a fundamental question: Were the omega-3 fatty acids actually reaching the brain?

To find out, they measured DHA concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid that surrounds and cushions the brain and spinal cord. Examining this fluid can provide information about substances entering and circulating within the central nervous system.

After six months, DHA levels in the cerebrospinal fluid had increased by an average of 17% among participants taking the supplements. This provided evidence that the omega-3 fatty acids were reaching their intended destination.

However, getting DHA into the central nervous system was only part of the goal. The more important question was whether those higher levels would translate into better brain health.

Researchers assessed participants' memory and other cognitive abilities at the beginning of the trial and again after two years. Despite the increase in DHA, those taking fish oil performed no better on the cognitive tests than participants receiving the placebo.

Brain imaging revealed similarly disappointing results.

The supplements did not prevent shrinkage of the hippocampus, a region deep within the brain that plays a central role in forming and organizing memories. The hippocampus is particularly vulnerable to changes associated with aging and Alzheimer's disease, making its size an important indicator researchers use when studying brain health.

Taken together, the results suggest that simply increasing DHA availability in the brain is not enough to produce measurable cognitive benefits in this group of older adults.

Why Fish Oil Supplements May Work Differently Than Food

The unexpected findings have raised a new question for researchers: If omega-3s can reach the brain, why don't higher levels necessarily improve its function?

Yassine and his colleagues are now investigating how the brain absorbs, processes, and uses these nutrients. Their earlier research suggests that omega-3s may be more beneficial when consumed as part of a broader dietary pattern rather than taken in isolation as a supplement.

One possibility involves the Mediterranean-style diet, which emphasizes foods such as fish, vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil. This eating pattern naturally supplies omega-3 fatty acids alongside other nutrients and has been associated with a lower risk of Alzheimer's disease.

The researchers suspect that the combination of nutrients in a balanced diet, along with an individual's overall health, may influence how effectively omega-3s support the brain.

"We're focused on better understanding how the brain processes omega-3s and whether factors, such as poor health, dietary pattern, genetic risk and age, may change the brain's ability to effectively absorb and use omega-3s," said Yassine. "We are working to develop medications that may help the brain better utilize these nutrients to preserve cognitive function."

Understanding these differences could eventually help scientists identify why certain people might respond better to omega-3s than others and whether new treatments could improve the brain's ability to benefit from these essential fats.

Importantly, the findings do not mean omega-3 fatty acids are unnecessary or that eating fish offers no health benefits. Instead, they indicate that taking high-dose DHA supplements alone did not provide measurable protection against cognitive decline or hippocampal shrinkage in the population studied.

Healthy Habits Remain Key to Alzheimer's Prevention

Although the trial focused specifically on fish oil supplements, the researchers emphasize that protecting brain health requires a much broader approach.

Rather than relying on a single supplement, they recommend maintaining healthy habits throughout life, including regular physical activity, sufficient high-quality sleep, and a nutritious, balanced diet.

These behaviors support overall health and may help reduce factors associated with cognitive decline. Physical exercise, for example, promotes cardiovascular health and blood flow, while sleep plays an important role in memory formation and normal brain maintenance.

"Staying healthy throughout life remains the most powerful tool we have for reducing Alzheimer's risk, including regular exercise, quality sleep and a balanced diet," said Yassine. "Living a healthy lifestyle is the brain's equivalent of getting regular car maintenance and high-quality oil changes. The brain is more likely to lose greater function if health issues in other parts of the body go unaddressed, in the same way that car engines stop working if regular maintenance is skipped."

While these lifestyle factors were not directly tested in the trial, the researchers believe they offer a more promising foundation for long-term brain health than depending on fish oil supplements alone.

The results also highlight an important distinction: A nutrient can be essential for healthy brain function without providing additional protection when consumed in large doses as a supplement. Understanding why that happens may open new avenues for research into Alzheimer's prevention and the nutritional needs of the aging brain.

‘The tech bro fight club is real!’ The titans of AI get the Succession treatment in a wild new TV show

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Audacity is a fearless satire taking on Silicon Valley’s ‘egomaniac weirdos’. Its creator and stars talk ayahuasca ceremonies, mining kids for data – and punching CEOs

Showrunner Jonathan Glatzer wanted the tagline of his latest TV creation to be: “We’re so fucked.” He laughs. “Sadly it got rejected by the network. Hey, maybe I can use it for season two!” The show in question is The Audacity, his darkly comic drama about AI tech titans. Instead, it went with the more PG tagline “Move fast and break people” – a twist on “Move fast and break things”, the Facebook motto coined by Mark Zuckerberg. The Audacity promises to do for tech bros what Succession did for legacy media. Considering Glatzer is a former writer on Succession, that is no empty threat.

This acerbic and all-too-timely Silicon Valley satire follows CEO Duncan Park (played by Billy Magnussen), the obscenely wealthy founder of a data analytics company. He’s a self-proclaimed “inventor of the future” who also happens to be a morally bankrupt man-baby. “Duncan is worth about half a billion,” says Glatzer. “Naturally, that’s not enough. He desperately wants to be a billionaire and ascend to what we call ‘God Mountain’. But he’s no God.”

Like Succession, The Audacity’s scripts are liberally spiced with baroque swearing. Park boasts about keeping investors “moist” and “Viagra-ing the share price to keep it up”. Magnussen delivers such juicy dialogue with relish. “Jonathan has a way of painting a picture with words while also getting nasty with it,” he grins. “My favourite line in season two is: ‘I pulled it out of my ass and it came out clean.’ You’ll have to wait and see what it refers to.” It’s a measure of The Audacity’s whip-smart relevance that the second season was commissioned before the first had even aired.

“Unquestionably, there’s a tech bro mentality that is sexualised,” says Glatzer. “Silicon Valley is such a male-dominated culture. It’s also very white or south Asian. When I first came here, I didn’t see a person of colour for two weeks. That struck me as very odd in a major US city. Hiring practices are dictated by algorithms. The algorithms say that if you went to these universities, you’re our man. If you came from someplace else, and are maybe not a man, the algorithm says you might be problematic. And you could end up writing a book about us.”

The Audacity pulls no punches on how the tech sector is wrecking the planet. “It sure is,” says Glatzer. “Generative AI is environmentally ruinous. Datacentres are tremendous suckers of energy. Worryingly, it doesn’t take too many rampantly ambitious entrepreneurs to fuck up an entire planet for the rest of us.” When Park is warned about the huge carbon footprint of his “algo”, he shrugs: “I’ll sponsor a penguin.”

Zach Galifianakis plays billionaire genius Carl Bardolph, who “basically created spam”. He’s rich enough to retire but becomes a disruptor out of boredom. “I’ve always been very concerned about the people running tech,” says Galifianakis. “They have math minds, but that sometimes comes with a lack of empathy. About 12 nerds are controlling our lives and designing our futures. And they all tend to have the same profile. Most come from Stanford University. I don’t know what they’re teaching there.” He laughs. “It’s weird and kind of evil.”

Did Galifianakis base his portrayal on anyone specific? “He’s pulled from one dude I’ve watched over the years. I won’t give his name but he’s a real piece of shit. A major jerk. The guys running Silicon Valley are damaged. Carl has a giant hole in his heart and a ton of anger because of his dad. I’ve seen a lot of men like that in Hollywood, so I drew on them too. Most of the puppet masters are egomaniac weirdos and I wouldn’t mind saying that to their faces. Zuckerberg has something missing. Elon Musk is bizarre. They’re little boys. You almost feel sorry for them.”

“Duncan also deals with daddy issues and a lack of love,” says Magnussen. “A lot of tech bros are trying to fill voids. The great irony is that the most antisocial people work in social media. Those who don’t know how to be personable are controlling how we communicate. It’s nuts.”

Both actors view the show as a howl of protest. “I’ve done some cool shit in my career,” says Magnussen. “I’ve been in Bond [he played a CIA agent in No Time to Die]. I’ve been in fun, escapist movies. But this feels different. It’s a call to arms. People need to wake up to the audacious behaviour of this handful of people. Their decisions affect 8.3 billion people. That’s scary, man. I just hope our show opens some conversations. We’ve got to start making a difference.”

Galifianakis agrees. “It’s a warning. I’ve never felt this way before with my work – come on, I was in a movie with a baby wearing sunglasses [The Hangover] – but this show’s important. This industry has no guard rails. You can’t sue them, you can’t do anything, so of course they’re going to take advantage of the population. That’s exactly what they’ve done. The destructive environmental impact alone is enough reason to take to the streets and protest. Sure, there’s all this darkness online but also, we’re going to take your water and electricity from you, too.”

Park blackmails his therapist – sorry, “performance psychologist” (played by Barry’s Sarah Goldberg) – into giving him insider-trading tips from her other powerful clients. The pair are soon engulfed in a scandal over fraudulent stock pumping. It’s a familiar-sounding storyline. “Elon got into trouble for market manipulation,” says Glatzer. “It’s like Duncan says in the show: ‘It’s not a felony in Silicon Valley, it’s good hustle.’ That’s the mentality here. Laws are for morons, not us.”

Real world echoes are everywhere in The Audacity. The search engine of choice is called Spookle (slogan: “You Agreed to This”). A corporation called Cupertino is a thinly veiled reference to Apple, while its mythical boss “Big Tim” nods to Tim Cook. Are any of Glatzer’s characters directly based on real-life figures? “The truth is, I don’t find any of them worthy of a three-dimensional character that could go the distance of multiple seasons,” says Glatzer. “They’re interesting enough for a film like The Social Reckoning but not much more.”

Indeed, Magnussen can currently be seen in The Social Reckoning as a Meta spin doctor. Is this his tech bro era? “AI is the most pressing issue of our time,” he says. “It affects our planet, our politics, our mental health, everything. Technology can do wonderful things but once it starts dividing us or swaying elections, it’s deeply damaging.”

“I don’t understand why people get on board with social media,” sighs Galifianakis. “We fed these machines for years. Now these machines are going to find us. I don’t believe the promises of AI because I don’t trust the people doing it.”

The Audacity’s protagonist has been described as “highly punchable”. Magnussen concurs: “You’ve seen these guys in front of Congress. They’re pretty fucking punchable.” An early episode includes a sequence at a Palo Alto fight club, where nerds whack each other with keyboards and strangle each other with cables. “That’s real!” laughs Magnussen. “The tech bro fight club is an actual thing.”

Despite the plentiful real-world parallels, Glatzer has never met a tech mogul. “That’s fine by me,” he says. “I don’t imagine the conversation would be anything other than stage-managed and dishonest. They have people workshopping their handshake and smile, so what am I going to get from that? But I have talked to a number of people who work in the tech world. The first thing they say is: ‘I’m not like that. That’s those other guys. I’m one of the good ones.’”

“Me too,” says Magnussen. “The response I get is: ‘I know that guy!’ It’s always someone else. You want to go: ‘No, it’s you. You’re that guy.’”

In America’s richest enclave, hi-tech goes hand-in-hand with hippy-dippy. The Audacity lampoons heath fads like ayahuasca ceremonies, microdosing and bio-hacking. “Tech titans feel as if they’ve got the golden ticket,” says Glatzer. “They want their prime to last as long as possible, perhaps for ever. Maybe the singularity comes when they upload themselves into an AI consciousness and become gods. I don’t have proof of that being the reason why so many datacentres are being built by these guys – but I don’t think it’s uninvolved.”

“There’s no limit to these people’s overreach,” agrees Magnussen. “They truly believe their life is more valuable than anyone else’s.”

The show also skewers the hypocrisy of execs who ban their own offspring from smartphones, reasoning that “arms dealers don’t give their kids landmines”. “It’s like how a lot of Hollywood don’t have TVs in their houses,” says Galifianakis. “Because they know the poison they’re selling.”

“They know the dangers,” nods Glatzer. “It’s a classic case of capitalism gone haywire. I don’t think they’d live next door to a datacentre either. None of them do what they’re telling us to do. It used to be that people in public life had to practise what they preached. That has quietly gone away. US congresspeople invest in the same tech companies they’re supposedly regulating. Look at what Trump’s doing. It’s a kleptocracy.”

He’s equally concerned by “screenification” in schools. “The amount of information gathered on our kids is frightening,” says Glatzer. “Shopping habits, medical history, exam grades. When schools get given tablets in the States, the law says it’s OK for the provider to track that student. We’re handing our kids these screens with the patina of being an educational tool but they are data-collecting devices. Just like everything is.”

“I can’t believe how much information people give up,” says Galifianakis. “If you go to LA, there’s all these cars that say Google on the side, taking pictures of everything. If those cars said US Government, people would lose their minds. But because it’s a corporation, we all sit back and go: ‘I guess this is the future.’ I think people are starting to wake up but maybe it’s too late.” He laughs. “What do I know? I’m just a dumb actor trying to alarm people. But our children are the new oil. They’re being mined for data.”

“I’m most scared for the kids,” agrees Magnussen. “They’ve been digital since they were born. They’re being manipulated without even knowing.”

In The Audacity, even seemingly benign tech is sinister. Eccentric inventor Martin Phister (The Big Bang Theory’s Simon Helberg) is developing an empathetic bot to be a digital “friend” for alienated teens. US viewers have likened him to OpenAI’s Sam Altman, who initially presented as a sympathetic mogul.

“Martin’s trying to do something good,” says Glatzer. “You later learn that he’s compensating for something very bad. Carl has a speech about his generation coming to a crossroads: ‘Leave a legacy of good or go full Dr Evil.’ Most choose the latter. They are drawn to doing something positive but they are Hungry Hungry Hippos. They can’t resist the allure of a good deal.”

“Carl knew the internet could have been a good thing for society if we’d done it right,” adds Galifianakis. “But greed and old-fashioned American competition, the need to win, became more important than human beings.”

Geeky idealism is long gone. “It began as a desire to change the world for the better,” says Glatzer. “A bunch of nerdy engineers wore hoodies rather than suits and put ‘Do No Evil’ slogans on their office walls. That’s all in the rear-view mirror.”

Glatzer freely admits that his work on Succession informed The Audacity. “There’s no way that it didn’t. But that was about institutional, dynastic wealth, whereas our characters are self-made. They are Logan Roy as a young man, if he had decided to be a tech guy rather than a media baron. What I certainly gleaned from Jesse Armstrong was finding a tone that enables you to give the establishment some real gut punches. Sarcasm might be the protest of the weak, but that’s all we’ve got. Speaking as a weakling.”

The invasive cyber-surveillance and data-mining in The Audacity are deeply chilling. “We consulted experts but all the information is out there,” says Glatzer. “The Guardian and New York Times do some terrific reporting on AI. It always feels like the journalists secretly want to headline their articles: ‘Wake the fuck up! This is happening!’”

Dead Star Devours a Newborn Planet That Formed From Its Own Remains

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Exotic chemical elements in the atmosphere of a dead star hint at the presence of a planet that formed after the star’s death—the first such second-generation planet ever seen.

For star systems, the answer seems to be yes. Astronomers have identified the first strong hints of a newborn planet orbiting a white dwarf, which is a remnant of a star like our Sun after it has exhausted its nuclear fuel. In a cruel irony for the planet, the hints of its existence come from the white dwarf actively eating its progeny’s atmosphere. But for astronomers, it’s an exciting possibility.

On the basis of the strange chemical composition of the white dwarf atmosphere, the new exoplanet candidate seems to be a second-generation planet, formed out of the debris shed by the dying star rather than the debris shed by its birth.

“This white dwarf is unique because it has a lot of traces of copper, zinc, and niobium,” said Jamie Williams, an astronomer at the University of Warwick in the United Kingdom who was the lead author of the study, published in Nature Astronomy. “Niobium is especially interesting [because] that’s the first time it’s ever been discovered in a white dwarf.”

When astronomers see chemical elements heavier than hydrogen and helium in a white dwarf’s atmosphere, it’s likely “pollution” from a planet. The planet might have been destroyed when the star died—Earth’s possible fate when our own Sun goes kerplooie in 5 billion years or so—or might be a survivor from the original star system.

“I don’t know of any other source that comes close to being this strange,” said Juliette Becker, a planetary astronomer at the University of Wisconsin–Madison who was not involved with the study. “This [white dwarf] is very different from pollution profiles that have existed so far, which is very exciting.”

Like all stellar corpses, white dwarfs are weird objects. This particular example, which carries the memorable name HS 0209+0832, is probably about half the mass of the Sun but the size of Earth, measurements that are fairly typical for a white dwarf. However, HS 0209 is relatively newly formed and very hot: about 35,000 kelvin (~63,000°F), compared to the Sun’s 5,800 kelvin surface temperature (10,000°F). A more fitting name might be “ultraviolet dwarf” since its spectrum peaks in the UV.

When astronomers observed HS 0209 in 1999 using the Hubble Space Telescope, they identified a relative excess of helium in its atmosphere. The amount came as a surprise because a white dwarf this hot should have little atmospheric helium. The new study drew together additional observations from a wealth of other telescopes, including later Hubble data, the Far Ultraviolet Spectroscopic Explorer (FUSE), and the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS).

These additional data turned up further unexpected elements, including 3 times the niobium abundance seen in the Sun’s spectrum. When the researchers compared this spectrum to other polluted white dwarfs and solar system objects like comets and meteorites, they found no matches: HS 0209 is unique. However, because astronomers think atmospheric pollution comes from material stripped from orbiting planets, it seemed possible that was the case here. That’s where the TESS data, which measure fluctuations in the visible light output from stars, came in handy.

“We also see a variability in the brightness of the system, which we think is from a day-night phase curve of the planet around it,” Williams said. In other words, as the hypothetical planet orbits, the total light from the system varies on the basis of which side of the planet is facing Earth on a 4.4-day orbital period, a type of planet known as a “hot Jupiter.”

These data together indicate a planet whose chemistry corresponds to the very end stages of life for the progenitor star before it died and formed the white dwarf. In the scenario the researchers think is most probable, the gas the original star shed—containing zinc, copper, and niobium—provided the raw material for a second-generation planet.

“We have a white dwarf orbited by a disk of this kind of material [from the dead star],” Williams said. “This disk will fragment and then you’ll form a planet.”

Both Williams and Becker pointed out that HS 0209, pollution aside, is a very typical white dwarf, meaning it’s possible many other second-generation planets could be out there. Becker, who studies unusual scenarios for planetary formation, noted that second-generation planets were actually the first exoplanets discovered. However, those were orbiting pulsars: the remnants of stars many times more massive than our Sun.

“I also have a strong bias: Because there are [pulsar] second-generation planets, I think there must be also second-generation planets around white dwarfs,” she said.

Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Birds living in cities appear to react differently to men and women, and scientists are struggling to explain why. An international research team discovered that urban birds, including great tits, house sparrows, and blackbirds, tend to fly away sooner when approached by women than by men. The surprising behavior raises new questions about how birds perceive the people around them.

The study took place in five European countries, where male and female participants (matched for height and clothing) walked directly toward birds in city parks and other green spaces.

On average, men were able to approach about one meter closer than women before the birds flew away. The researchers measured this response by recording how close a person could get before a bird took flight (termed flight initiation distance).

Published in the British Ecological Society journal People and Nature, the findings showed the same pattern in Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain. The difference also appeared across all 37 bird species examined, including easily startled magpies and pigeons that typically allow people to approach much more closely.

The researchers believe their findings indicate that city birds can distinguish between male and female humans. However, they have yet to determine which characteristics the birds recognize or why they appear more cautious around women.

Professor Daniel Blumstein of the University of California, Los Angeles, who helped conduct the research, said the results were convincing despite the lack of an explanation.

"I fully believe our results, that urban birds react differently based on the sex of the person approaching them, but I can't explain them right now. We used bleeding-edge comparative analysis techniques that showed our findings were consistent across cities and species, but we simply don't have a conclusive explanation yet."

Dr. Yanina Benedetti of the Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, another member of the research team, said the discovery challenged her expectations about how birds respond to human observers.

"As a woman in the field, I was surprised that birds reacted to us differently. This study highlights how animals in cities 'see' humans, which has implications for urban ecology and equality in science. Many behavioral studies assume that a human observer is neutral, but this wasn't the case for urban birds in our study."

Scientists have proposed several possible explanations for the difference. Birds might be responding to chemical signals such as pheromones, variations in body shape, or differences in how men and women walk. For now, however, these ideas remain speculative, and additional experiments will be needed to identify the actual cause.

Dr. Federico Morelli of the University of Turin, a co-author of the study, said the unanswered question is one of the discovery's most fascinating aspects.

"This is maybe the most interesting part of our study," said Dr. Federico Morelli from the University of Turin, a co-author of the study. "We have identified a phenomenon, but we really don't know why. However, what our results do highlight is the birds' sophisticated ability to evaluate their environment."

The findings suggest that urban birds may be sensitive to human characteristics that people themselves rarely notice. Identifying those signals could help researchers better understand how wildlife assesses potential threats in environments shared with humans.

Dr. Benedetti said: "Urban birds clearly react to subtle cues that humans do not easily notice. Follow-up studies could focus on individual factors such as movement patterns, scent cues, or physical traits, testing them separately rather than grouping them under observer sex. This approach would help identify the specific cues birds detect."

More Research Is Needed to Explain the Bird Behavior

Although the investigation included 2,701 observations, the researchers emphasize that their findings are still preliminary. Further studies will be necessary to establish whether the difference between birds' responses to men and women consistently appears in other settings and populations.

For now, the discovery points to an intriguing possibility: Birds sharing our cities may be paying attention to subtle differences between people that scientists have yet to fully understand.

Including Musk’s contribution to various Republican-aligned Pacs, the tech industry and its leaders have spent more than half a billion dollars to influence elections and ballot proposals in 2026.

The cumulative effect of such vast spending, lobbying and advertising is that tech issues and the tech industry have become far more pervasive in the midterms than previously seen in US elections, marking the first major test of how American voters will react to the AI boom.

Concern over AI’s potential effect on society has grown over the last year, and the backlash has been most intense where there is a physical target for voters’ ire. None of those targets have loomed larger than the sprawling datacenter facilities that are part of the tech industry’s multitrillion-dollar infrastructure build-out. He spent years talking with the man convicted of Loofe’s murder and hearing firsthand from the women he drew into his orbit. “I wouldn't say this experience made me a believer in the supernatural, but it did make me more open-minded about what might exist beyond the physical world.”

A Facebook post from the period when Loofe was missing Courtesy of Hulu

Loofe, a 24-year-old hardware store clerk at Menard's in Lincoln, Neb., first connected on Tinder with a woman she believed to be named Audrey. Loofe went on two dates with “Audrey.” But she never came home from the second one, on Nov. 15, 2017. When she didn’t show up for work the next day, Loofe’s coworkers called the police to do a wellness check at her home; the officers found her car in the driveway and her purse still in her apartment. Loofe was reported missing.

It was then that one of Loofe’s friends took matters into their own hands: They created a fake Tinder profile to find Audrey, who seemed to be the last person Loofe had made contact with. Loofe’s friend matched with Audrey and gave her details to investigators, who soon discovered that the phone number belonged to a 23-year-old woman named Bailey Boswell. The search for Loofe continued.

Her family held out hope that she would be found unharmed. But their prayers went unanswered. Loofe’s remains were found in garbage bags on Dec. 4 along the state highway in rural Clay County, Neb. She had been dismembered into 14 pieces, in what seemed to be a ritualistic manner. Her left upper arm was never recovered, and her internal organs were still missing. Authorities then set out to find out what really happened to the woman her family described as “the crystal-blue-eyed girl with a soft heart”—and why.

Aubrey Trail and Bailey Boswell Courtesy of Hulu

Boswell’s social media apps were just the beginning of the disturbing investigation. Boswell turned out to be part of a couple with Aubrey Trail, 51, a self-declared vampire. The duo shared an apartment and both had extensive criminal histories, including theft and financial scams, across various states. Loofe was just one of the many targets that both Boswell and Trail had found on Tinder to try to lure into their makeshift “coven” under a cult-like manifesto.

A trio of women in their early 20s,  all of whom have chosen to remain anonymous, testified in court and later in Swipe Right on the Devil that they had each met Boswell on Tinder, and were soon also introduced to Trail. They were showered with expensive clothes, spa treatments, and drugs, and given weekly allowances ranging from $150 to $200. The cost? Participating in group sex with Trail (“Daddy”) and Boswell (“Mommy”), while also helping the couple sell stolen goods. The women each testified that Trail would call himself a “vampire,” and claim that he had supernatural powers such as being able to fly and read minds. Trail also told the women that they were all now “witches” belonging to his cult, with Boswell as their leader.

Both Trail and Boswell claimed they were practicing witchcraft and black magic with required ritualistic violent acts to gain entry to their coven. Trail told the members of the “coven” that they would have to torture someone and complete a killing in order for him to gain more power as their messiah; he also wanted to film the murder and share the profits of the snuff film with each of them. The women claimed they left Trail’s cult shortly thereafter in November 2017, right before Boswell lured in Loofe.

After authorities discovered that Trail was connected to Boswell, they searched the couple’s home and found a BDSM dog leash, duct tape, “a sauna suit with the crotch cut out, zip ties, a hatchet, a plastic drop cloth smeared with blood, and a book on anatomy.”

In the trailer for Swipe Right on the Devil, one of the women claims that Trail told her after meeting on Tinder that he and Boswell “knew witchcraft and had 12 women [and] needed a thirteenth to complete the coven.” Another survivor describes how Trail had “rules” for the coven which included being naked while around him and allowing him to watch lesbian encounters. The documentary features a segment in which one of the women tells an officer that to be in the “the inner circle,” he said she would “have had to kill somebody and take their soul.”

“While this is fundamentally Sydney's story, it's also about the other young women manipulated by Aubrey Trail and Bailey Boswell at a vulnerable time in their lives, when they were searching for something universal: love and connection,” says Biegel. “They spoke candidly about the challenges of finding queer relationships in their conservative Midwestern communities, and how Bailey exploited that loneliness through Tinder. Once drawn into their world, they found themselves trapped in a disturbing web of manipulation, witchcraft, and demands to kill as a path to supernatural power.”

Biegel’s fellow executive producer and Imagine Documentaries executive Christopher St. John adds that the team behind docuseries was “struck by the bravery of the three women who tell their stories at the center of this series.” St. John continues, “They lived through such an incredibly traumatic experience, but they still believe it’s valuable to share the story, whether in court or on camera.” He says the team elected to tell the story in three episodes because they had too much material to do the story justice in a feature-length film, and even still, there were elements that didn’t make it into the final cut. “Hopefully, viewers will really get an accurate sense of their thinking and where they were during this intense period of their lives.”

Aubrey Trail during questioning Courtesy of Hulu

Trail confessed to Loofe’s killing right away, but maintained that her death was an accident. He told the police that he had engaged in consensual BDSM acts with Loofe and two other women, with the participants paying him $15,000 to film. Trail said that he had strangled Loofe with an extension cord during an erotic act and did not intend to kill her. Following her death, Trail dismembered Loofe’s body to avoid getting caught. He emphasized that Boswell was not part of the crime at all. During messages uncovered throughout the investigations, Trail is shown telling Boswell that he should be the one taking all the blame, and that she should change her narrative to come across as another “victim.”

During the three-week trial, Trail yelled out in court that Boswell was “innocent” and then slit his throat with a shank; the trial was put on hold as he was rushed to the hospital and recovered. Trail also suffered from two heart attacks and a stroke over the course of the case being presented to a jury. He changed his story during the trial, telling the jury that he killed Loofe out of fear that she would “expose my lifestyle.”

Trail was found guilty on all counts of first-degree murder, conspiracy, and improper disposal of human remains. He was sentenced to death on June 9, 2021. Trail currently is on death row and petitioned the state of Nebraska in August 2023 to set his execution date; Trail is 10th in line on death row for the state. The first and only execution via lethal injection in Nebraska took place in 2018, with the execution of convicted murderer Carey Dean Moore. Trail’s appeal in 2025 was denied.

It was during Trail’s time on death row that he started to open up to Swipe Right on the Devil filmmaker Biegel. Over the course of a year, Biegel sought to gain Trail’s trust and participation. “Ultimately, that persistence gave us something rare in true crime: 360-degree access to the story, allowing us to explore its many layers and conflicting perspectives,” explains Biegel.

Boswell stood trial in September 2020. There was ample physical evidence for the case against her, including DNA found on a latex glove that was in the same trash bag as Loofe’s remains. Boswell was found guilty on all counts of first-degree murder, conspiracy, and improper disposal of human remains in October 2020, and was sentenced to life imprisonment in November 2021.

“I don't see Swipe Right on the Devil as another true-crime retelling. It's a discovery of a mystery, unfolding for the first time through the firsthand accounts of those who lived it,” says Biegel. “This isn't a whodunnit: It's a whydunnit. What really happened to Sydney Loofe, and why? The series explores the unsettling collision of ancient witchcraft beliefs and modern digital dating culture. [These are] two killers who, by their own accounts, would have continued killing had they not been caught so soon.”

Sydney Loofe’s family launched the Sydney Loofe Foundation in 2019. The foundation has raised more than $30,000 to be awarded in scholarships and $26,000 to be distributed to community causes. “Sydney, with a ‘y,’ never stopped to ask ‘why’ when it came to helping others,” the Loofe family wrote on the foundation website. “Although she often struggled physically, financially, and emotionally, she opened her home and heart to others. And those who knew Sydney knew of her love of family, her affinity for animals and pets (especially cats), her interest in fishing as a pastime, her fascination with beautiful crystals, and her soft heart…Sydney Loofe was a unique child of God, one who proudly had this tattooed on her arm, ‘Everything will be wonderful someday.’ Her family holds tight to the faith that indeed in heaven, that is true.”

Nuclear Weapons Waste Made Workers Sick. Despite a Law Meant to Help, the DOE Keeps Fighting Their Claims.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Investigative Journalism in the Public Interest

ProPublica is a nonprofit, investigative newsroom that exposes corruption. We report in all 50 states and partner with local newsrooms. Our work spurs real-world impact and has received numerous awards, including nine Pulitzer Prizes.

This article was produced for ProPublica’s Sustainability Desk, a project of the Local Reporting Network, in partnership with Cascade PBS and co-published by KUOW. Sign up for Dispatches to get our stories in your inbox every week.

These highlights were written by the reporters and editors who worked on this story.

Lonnie Rouse shuffled into a wood-paneled conference room at the Washington state Capitol in March 2018. It had been nearly a decade since medical experts diagnosed him with a neurological disease they attributed to his exposure to chemicals at work. In the time since, he had acquired a stutter, begun limping and lost several teeth biting into a banana.

For nearly 20 years, Rouse had packaged plutonium and monitored radioactive waste at the Hanford Site, a now-decommissioned Department of Energy nuclear weapons production plant in south-central Washington and one of the most contaminated nuclear sites in the country. Tanks full of radioactive sludge exposed him to at least 48 potentially hazardous chemicals. The worksite was so toxic, he said, that the floor once melted the rubber off his boots.

Rouse was among the thousands of workers responsible for the ongoing environmental cleanup at Hanford, which has been underway since the DOE shut down the last reactor in the late 1980s. Congress has acknowledged, based on voluminous studies, that radiation at DOE sites poses health risks to workers.

Over time, some people at Hanford became so sick they could no longer work. Like Rouse, they filed for workers’ compensation. The DOE consistently challenged their claims and tried to shift the blame onto the workers themselves.

By the time Rouse took his place behind then-Gov. Jay Inslee, he was desperate. His 2009 workers’ compensation claim had been denied. He had burned through his retirement savings and declared bankruptcy.

As he watched, the governor signed a bill that was supposed to change his life.

For years, Hanford employees filing workers’ compensation claims had to prove the chemicals they were exposed to on the job made them sick. The new law Inslee signed flipped that burden of proof. Under the law, agencies were instructed to presume that working at Hanford had caused certain cancers, neurological diseases and respiratory illnesses. Now the Department of Energy would have to prove to the state that something other than the job had made the workers sick if it wanted to deny their claims.

Rouse thought the presumption law would speed up his claim for benefits. State lawmakers and the former governor thought it would force the DOE to take responsibility for the harm Hanford workers were experiencing.

But despite the law, the DOE continues to fight new and existing claims, Cascade PBS and ProPublica found. We asked the state for claims records going back to the presumption law’s passage in 2018, but the state only provided records going back three and a half years, to 2023. In that period, workers filed 102 new claims for benefits, state data shows. The DOE did not recommend approving a single one.

The state, which has final say, overruled the DOE and approved 75% of those claims. The DOE appealed all but seven of those approvals.

Cascade PBS and ProPublica spoke to workers, surviving family members, lawyers representing workers and state officials. They all pointed to a pattern: The DOE systematically challenges and then drags out workers’ compensation claims for years with recommended denials and subsequent legal appeals. Along the way, it has spent millions on outside lawyers and expert testimony, including doctor evaluations that point to other causes for workers’ illnesses.

The DOE has paid out benefits to Hanford workers in some cases, lawyers and families said, but it has done so while still actively appealing the claims. It warns workers that they may have to pay the money back.

Our reporting found that not even death ends the cycle of appeals. About 152 workers’ claims remain stranded in this legal limbo — approved by the state but still under appeal by the DOE. Half of those workers are dead. The DOE continues to fight to deny their families survivors’ benefits.

“It seems pretty coldhearted, calculated, that they’re trying to save money on the back of sick workers who they made sick,” said Gerry Pollet, one of the Washington state representatives who sponsored the 2018 presumption law, who is also a co-founder of the Hanford cleanup watchdog group Heart of America Northwest. “That’s why we needed a state law.”

After Cascade PBS and ProPublica raised questions about the DOE’s pattern of litigating claims, regulators at the Washington State Department of Labor and Industries met twice with DOE staff, state agency spokesperson Carrie McCausland said. But she could not point to any other actions the agency has taken beyond the two meetings.

“Agency staff are concerned and are right now assessing the severity of patterns we’ve seen,” McCausland wrote in an email.

The DOE did not answer detailed questions or respond to the newsrooms’ findings. In a statement, a spokesperson wrote that the DOE is following “all applicable local, state, and federal laws” and “has a long and successful record of accomplishment of protecting human health and the environment.”

The DOE has the right to file multiple appeals against a claim. According to court records, its appeals have sought to overturn claims approved by the state, narrow the period of back wages, or block survivors’ benefits, among other things. In appeals filings, the DOE’s lawyers have argued that factors other than work at Hanford played a role in the workers’ illnesses, including smoking, drinking, obesity or genetics.

In Rouse’s case, the energy department blamed alcoholism and family conflicts. The state agency that handles appeals did not find those arguments persuasive. DOE awarded Rouse most of his back pay in 2024, his lawyer said. Rouse declined to say how much that was, but said he is not touching the money until the legal process concludes.

In the nearly eight years it has been fighting his claim, the DOE has filed more than 20 appeals contesting different aspects of his claim.

“We were always told that nothing is out here that’s going to harm you — you do what you are supposed to do, and you do what you are trained to do, and you’re going to be fine,” said Rouse, who now receives 24-hour care.

The smell of ammonia serves as a warning, workers say, that the underground tanks holding a mix of radioactive sludge have burped chemical vapors into the air above. From 1998 to 2008, it was Rouse’s job to monitor the toxic waste held in the “tank farms.” The containers together hold more than 50 million gallons of liquid waste. Rouse says he lost count of how many times tanks released toxic vapor that took his breath away, sometimes knocking him to the ground and making him vomit and bleed from his nose. According to a 2014 report funded by the federal government, tank farm workers exposed to chemical vapors commonly reported nausea, headaches and nosebleeds.

The liquid sloshing around in those tanks represented the radioactive byproducts of the Cold War. Hanford produced two-thirds of the plutonium for the nation’s nuclear arsenal and fueled the atomic bomb dropped on Nagasaki, Japan, in 1945.

The end of production in the late 1980s launched a cleanup effort that is expected to continue until at least 2086. As of January 2025, more than 13,000 people were working at Hanford, nearly all of them for private companies contracted by the DOE. It is one of the largest and most expensive efforts to remediate toxic waste in the nation’s history.

In 2000, Congress passed the Energy Employees Occupational Illness Compensation Program Act, which provides medical care and one-time payouts to workers at DOE sites across the country. It was an acknowledgement that the nation’s nuclear weapons workers had been exposed to radioactive substances known to cause cancer. In it, lawmakers referred to a DOE policy “to litigate occupational illness claims, which has deterred workers from filing workers’ compensation claims and has imposed major financial burdens for such employees who have sought compensation.”

But the benefits of the federal program were limited: It capped payouts at $400,000.

Washington’s workers’ compensation system, by contrast, can provide steady wage replacement or a pension benefit, in some cases, for the rest of an injured employee’s life. It’s a program that can easily surpass the maximum federal benefit.

The state went further with its 2018 presumption law. Such laws generally protect firefighters who are regularly exposed to smoke, burned plastics and extreme heat. The National Council on Compensation Insurance, the Washington state attorney general and Pollet all said they were unaware of any other state that has extended that protection for radiological workers.

The federal government challenged Washington’s state law later that year in court, arguing it was discriminatory because it only applied to Hanford and the DOE.

Anticipating a Supreme Court decision that ultimately agreed, lawmakers passed an expanded version that covered all workers at “radiological hazardous waste facilities” in the state, including the state’s only functioning nuclear power plant and a French-owned facility that manufactures nuclear fuel rods. Nonetheless, roughly 75% of the 428 claims filed since 2018 have come from the DOE’s Hanford workers, according to the state Department of Labor and Industries.

Kathleen Kersey spent 10 years at Hanford, during both the production and cleanup stages. Much of her work involved measuring radiation around the site and making sure levels were safe. She says her safety training covered radiation, but not other hazards like chemical vapors burping from the waste tanks.

Kersey struggles to walk, has short-term memory problems and at one point lost vision in her right eye for three months. A doctor diagnosed her with multiple sclerosis, which is a neurological disorder, one of the categories of illnesses the state’s presumption law recognizes as related to chemical exposure at Hanford. In 2019 she filed a claim, which the state approved.

The DOE has appealed, arguing that something other than her time at Hanford caused her multiple sclerosis.

Kersey is one of the 152 workers still awaiting a resolution on her workers’ compensation claim. The DOE has not paid her any back wages, she said. The DOE declined to comment on her case.

“I feel depleted and disgusted,” Kersey said of the appeals process. “It makes me feel like I’m worthless. I have no value.”

State records show that the DOE has filed more than 500 appeals since the presumption law passed in 2018. In 20 cases, as it did with Rouse, the DOE has appealed a single worker’s claim half a dozen times or more.

“We have seen tremendous pushback from DOE on essentially almost every single case that’s been brought,” said Washington State Attorney General Nick Brown.

Rouse’s wife, Melinda, said she knew something was wrong when he could not remember how to cut an orange. His vision started to blur, he stumbled often, and suddenly he felt irritable all the time. His primary care physician first thought he might have had a brain tumor. The physician referred him to a neurologist who noted cognitive impairment, possibly due to exposure to multiple toxins at the Hanford Site. The neurologist then referred him to a neuropsychologist, who diagnosed him with “toxic encephalopathy” in 2009, saying it was “more likely than not” that chemical exposure damaged his brain, according to court records.

The U.S. Department of Labor agreed too, approving him in 2010 for benefits under the Energy Employees Occupational Illness Compensation Program Act, saying it was “at least as likely as not” that Hanford contributed to his illness. A federal medical consultant even praised the neuropsychologist who assessed Rouse for his “unusually thorough analysis of causation.”

Rouse refiled his 2009 state workers’ compensation claim under the new law in 2018. Washington state approved it later that year.

The DOE appealed. It hired a medical toxicologist, who reviewed Rouse’s medical records and testified that he did not have the disease he claimed, and brought in an industrial hygienist, who downplayed the exposure risks at Hanford. The DOE’s toxicologist testified that Rouse’s drinking was “the most significant, what we might term, ‘toxic exposure’ that’s described in the medical record.”

A group of three physicians and an industrial hygienist have collectively earned over $1.7 million testifying against Hanford workers since the presumption law’s passage, according to DOE invoices the newsrooms obtained by a federal records request. One of the DOE’s go-to expert witness doctors earned more than $200,000 from the DOE last year alone. He did not respond to multiple emails seeking comment.

A single law firm, Wallace, Mann, Capener, Bishop & Debney, handles all of the DOE’s appeals. In addition to the fees paid to expert witnesses, the department paid the firm nearly $10 million between 2018 and 2025. The law firm did not reply to multiple requests for comment.

In cases where the DOE has appealed the state’s approval of a claim since 2018, the agency that handles appeals has sided with workers nearly 9 times out of 10, according to state data.

Pollet, the state representative who sponsored the 2018 presumption bill, acknowledged that the law has fallen short of his expectation.

“This was supposed to have ended a decade ago,” he said about sick Hanford workers still awaiting back wages and medical care. “It’s even worse than it was 10 years ago.”

“I think their actions are not only despicable, but they are criminal,” he said of the DOE.

The state Department of Labor and Industries said it expected “to engage further with the DOE,” which could include putting the department on an improvement plan and monitoring its progress.

Another option state regulators have is to revoke the DOE’s ability to accept and pay out claims directly. According to the state Department of Labor and Industries, the contractors that employ Hanford workers on behalf of the DOE would then decide whether to join the state-managed fund or seek state approval to manage claims themselves. Doing so could limit the DOE’s power to appeal, but contractors would still have that right.

Brown, the state attorney general, said he wants to wait and see how the DOE’s appeals of claims play out in higher courts. He hopes that once the DOE’s losses start stacking up, it will slow the volume of its litigation.

Attorney Charlynn Hull said she thought the DOE’s approach might soften during the Biden administration. But Hull said she observed no change in the DOE’s behavior. Her firm, Smart Law, represents the majority of Hanford workers’ compensation claimants, including Rouse and Kersey.

One of the hardest things about the DOE’s appeals, Hull said, is the testifying that the children and spouses of dead workers must do.

“I think that it’s just, it’s tormenting to them, in many ways, to relive how awful that experience was, especially with the cancer-related death,” Hull said. “When does this end?”

For Isidoro Mata Jr., it ended with his children draining fluid from his lungs through a tube doctors had surgically inserted into his chest.

Mata demolished buildings at Hanford for nearly 30 years. Court records show he was diagnosed in 2015 with thyroid cancer, one of the illnesses that Washington’s law ties to work at Hanford. Sara Chilcoat, his daughter, said he kept working as long as he could.

State claims managers approved Mata’s claim in 2019. The DOE appealed, arguing that “medical opinion supports denial of this claim as not related to the distinctive conditions of employment.” The appeal did not elaborate further. The state forced the DOE to pay his wages and the cost of his medications, Chilcoat said. But the DOE filed a separate motion to stop his benefits while the appeal was adjudicated.

In its motion, the DOE argued that it was likely to prevail in its appeal because a doctor who examined Mata in 2019 believed his cancer was unrelated to his work at Hanford, according to court records. The state found the doctor’s examination “lacking in objective findings.”

“He was fighting for his life while they were trying to do that,” Chilcoat said. “That makes me mad.”

Mata’s case file notes that he missed three meetings with DOE lawyers in 2020. The state attorney general’s office sent the appeals judge a letter in July explaining that Mata was unable to participate because of his chemotherapy treatments. The DOE, which did not respond to questions about Mata’s case, dropped its appeal in December.

The federal government challenged Washington’s state law later that year in court, arguing it was discriminatory because it only applied to Hanford and the DOE.

Anticipating a Supreme Court decision that ultimately agreed, lawmakers passed an expanded version that covered all workers at “radiological hazardous waste facilities” in the state, including the state’s only functioning nuclear power plant and a French-owned facility that manufactures nuclear fuel rods. Nonetheless, roughly 75% of the 428 claims filed since 2018 have come from the DOE’s Hanford workers, according to the state Department of Labor and Industries.

Kathleen Kersey spent 10 years at Hanford, during both the production and cleanup stages. Much of her work involved measuring radiation around the site and making sure levels were safe. She says her safety training covered radiation, but not other hazards like chemical vapors burping from the waste tanks.

Kersey struggles to walk, has short-term memory problems and at one point lost vision in her right eye for three months. A doctor diagnosed her with multiple sclerosis, which is a neurological disorder, one of the categories of illnesses the state’s presumption law recognizes as related to chemical exposure at Hanford. In 2019 she filed a claim, which the state approved.

The DOE has appealed, arguing that something other than her time at Hanford caused her multiple sclerosis.

Kersey is one of the 152 workers still awaiting a resolution on her workers’ compensation claim. The DOE has not paid her any back wages, she said. The DOE declined to comment on her case.

“I feel depleted and disgusted,” Kersey said of the appeals process. “It makes me feel like I’m worthless. I have no value.”

State records show that the DOE has filed more than 500 appeals since the presumption law passed in 2018. In 20 cases, as it did with Rouse, the DOE has appealed a single worker’s claim half a dozen times or more.

“We have seen tremendous pushback from DOE on essentially almost every single case that’s been brought,” said Washington State Attorney General Nick Brown.

Rouse’s wife, Melinda, said she knew something was wrong when he could not remember how to cut an orange. His vision started to blur, he stumbled often, and suddenly he felt irritable all the time. His primary care physician first thought he might have had a brain tumor. The physician referred him to a neurologist who noted cognitive impairment, possibly due to exposure to multiple toxins at the Hanford Site. The neurologist then referred him to a neuropsychologist, who diagnosed him with “toxic encephalopathy” in 2009, saying it was “more likely than not” that chemical exposure damaged his brain, according to court records.

The U.S. Department of Labor agreed too, approving him in 2010 for benefits under the Energy Employees Occupational Illness Compensation Program Act, saying it was “at least as likely as not” that Hanford contributed to his illness. A federal medical consultant even praised the neuropsychologist who assessed Rouse for his “unusually thorough analysis of causation.”

Rouse refiled his 2009 state workers’ compensation claim under the new law in 2018. Washington state approved it later that year.

The DOE appealed. It hired a medical toxicologist, who reviewed Rouse’s medical records and testified that he did not have the disease he claimed, and brought in an industrial hygienist, who downplayed the exposure risks at Hanford. The DOE’s toxicologist testified that Rouse’s drinking was “the most significant, what we might term, ‘toxic exposure’ that’s described in the medical record.”

A group of three physicians and an industrial hygienist have collectively earned over $1.7 million testifying against Hanford workers since the presumption law’s passage, according to DOE invoices the newsrooms obtained by a federal records request. One of the DOE’s go-to expert witness doctors earned more than $200,000 from the DOE last year alone. He did not respond to multiple emails seeking comment.

A single law firm, Wallace, Mann, Capener, Bishop & Debney, handles all of the DOE’s appeals. In addition to the fees paid to expert witnesses, the department paid the firm nearly $10 million between 2018 and 2025. The law firm did not reply to multiple requests for comment.

In cases where the DOE has appealed the state’s approval of a claim since 2018, the agency that handles appeals has sided with workers nearly 9 times out of 10, according to state data.

Pollet, the state representative who sponsored the 2018 presumption bill, acknowledged that the law has fallen short of his expectation.

“This was supposed to have ended a decade ago,” he said about sick Hanford workers still awaiting back wages and medical care. “It’s even worse than it was 10 years ago.”

“I think their actions are not only despicable, but they are criminal,” he said of the DOE.

The state Department of Labor and Industries said it expected “to engage further with the DOE,” which could include putting the department on an improvement plan and monitoring its progress.

Another option state regulators have is to revoke the DOE’s ability to accept and pay out claims directly. According to the state Department of Labor and Industries, the contractors that employ Hanford workers on behalf of the DOE would then decide whether to join the state-managed fund or seek state approval to manage claims themselves. Doing so could limit the DOE’s power to appeal, but contractors would still have that right.

Brown, the state attorney general, said he wants to wait and see how the DOE’s appeals of claims play out in higher courts. He hopes that once the DOE’s losses start stacking up, it will slow the volume of its litigation.

Attorney Charlynn Hull said she thought the DOE’s approach might soften during the Biden administration. But Hull said she observed no change in the DOE’s behavior. Her firm, Smart Law, represents the majority of Hanford workers’ compensation claimants, including Rouse and Kersey.

One of the hardest things about the DOE’s appeals, Hull said, is the testifying that the children and spouses of dead workers must do.

“I think that it’s just, it’s tormenting to them, in many ways, to relive how awful that experience was, especially with the cancer-related death,” Hull said. “When does this end?”

For Isidoro Mata Jr., it ended with his children draining fluid from his lungs through a tube doctors had surgically inserted into his chest.

Mata demolished buildings at Hanford for nearly 30 years. Court records show he was diagnosed in 2015 with thyroid cancer, one of the illnesses that Washington’s law ties to work at Hanford. Sara Chilcoat, his daughter, said he kept working as long as he could.

State claims managers approved Mata’s claim in 2019. The DOE appealed, arguing that “medical opinion supports denial of this claim as not related to the distinctive conditions of employment.” The appeal did not elaborate further. The state forced the DOE to pay his wages and the cost of his medications, Chilcoat said. But the DOE filed a separate motion to stop his benefits while the appeal was adjudicated.

In its motion, the DOE argued that it was likely to prevail in its appeal because a doctor who examined Mata in 2019 believed his cancer was unrelated to his work at Hanford, according to court records. The state found the doctor’s examination “lacking in objective findings.”

“He was fighting for his life while they were trying to do that,” Chilcoat said. “That makes me mad.”

Mata’s case file notes that he missed three meetings with DOE lawyers in 2020. The state attorney general’s office sent the appeals judge a letter in July explaining that Mata was unable to participate because of his chemotherapy treatments. The DOE, which did not respond to questions about Mata’s case, dropped its appeal in December.

Oxford scientists discover how viruses build themselves without a blueprint

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Researchers at the University of Oxford have observed virus-like particles assembling themselves one molecule at a time, uncovering the surprisingly simple rules that allow complex biological structures to form with remarkable reliability. The findings, published in Nature, offer a detailed look at a process that scientists have struggled to observe directly for decades.

Understanding how viruses construct their protective protein shells, and how that construction might be interrupted, could lead to new antiviral drugs, improved vaccines, and other medical treatments. The discovery helps explain how individual proteins consistently organize themselves into the correct structures despite having thousands of possible ways to connect.

The researchers found that viral assembly resembles navigating a complicated maze, where only certain paths lead to lasting progress. At first, protein components form weak connections that can easily break apart if the arrangement is unsuccessful. This flexibility allows the molecules to keep trying different combinations until they form particular closed structures. Once multiple connections hold these structures together, they become far more stable.

These stable arrangements serve as molecular checkpoints, narrowing the enormous number of possible assembly routes into a much smaller set of successful pathways. One especially important milestone occurs when five larger protein building blocks connect to form a closed pentagonal ring. This is the first highly stable structure in the assembly process. Once it forms, each subsequent stable stage requires fewer additional building blocks, allowing the particle to grow more quickly.

Co-lead author Dr. Roi Asor (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) explained: "A virus has to solve an extraordinary construction problem. Its components somehow have to find the right arrangement among a huge number of possibilities, without a blueprint or machinery directing the process. We can now watch what happens molecule by molecule and see the physical rules that make it possible."

Watching Virus Particles Assemble Molecule by Molecule

Viruses store and protect their genetic material within organized protein shells known as capsids. Depending on the virus, these shells may consist of tens or even thousands of individual protein components. Remarkably, in many cases, the proteins can arrange themselves into the correct structure without any external machinery directing their assembly.

Although scientists have investigated viral self-assembly for decades, capturing the process as it unfolds has been a major challenge. Many of the intermediate structures exist only briefly and appear in very small quantities. As a result, traditional methods have generally allowed researchers to examine the starting materials and finished particles without clearly showing how one transforms into the other.

To overcome this limitation, the Oxford researchers studied an engineered virus-like particle composed of 60 protein units. They used mass photometry, a technique developed at Oxford that determines the mass of individual molecules by measuring the light they scatter. By combining this technology with a new approach for keeping individual molecules confined and continuously observable, the team could repeatedly measure the mass of a growing particle as additional proteins attached.

This approach allowed the scientists to follow the construction process as it happened, effectively tracking the addition of individual molecular building blocks over time.

Co-first author PhD student Dan Loewenthal (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) said: "This method lets us study the assembly process directly, we just take a video!"

Co-lead author Professor Philipp Kukura (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, Oxford University) said: "Until now, much of our understanding of how these structures assemble has had to be reconstructed from snapshots or theoretical models. Being able to both quantify the underlying interactions and follow one particle as it grows changes that. We can see the important intermediate structures appear and deduce a detailed model of the construction process."

The Surprising Trick That Makes Viral Assembly Work

The results help explain how a process involving an overwhelming number of possible molecular arrangements can consistently produce the correct structure. Rather than exploring every possible route, the proteins progress through a relatively small number of stable intermediate stages that guide assembly toward completion.

The findings also clarify why the earliest stage of construction, called nucleation, is particularly difficult and why growth becomes substantially faster after the first stable structure appears.

A key part of this efficiency comes from the ability of protein building blocks to make mistakes without permanently disrupting the process. Weak initial connections allow incorrect combinations to separate, giving the proteins another opportunity to find a better arrangement. When enough components eventually connect in the correct closed configuration, the resulting structure becomes stable enough to support further growth.

Dr. Asor added: "The weak interactions give the system room to make mistakes. Most encounters don't have to be successful: the components can separate and try again. But once enough of them come together in the right closed arrangement, the structure becomes stable, and assembly can move forward. That combination of trial and error followed by locking in successful structures is what makes the process so reliable."

New Possibilities for Antiviral Drugs and Vaccines

By identifying the molecular interactions that control viral assembly, scientists may eventually be able to develop treatments that interfere with the construction of viral protein shells. The same insights could also support efforts to engineer virus-like particles for vaccines and other therapeutic applications.

Co-author Dr. Jack Tan (MRC Weatherall Institute of Molecular Medicine and CAMS-Oxford Institute) noted: "The ability to understand these processes at the molecular level could have important applications in vaccine development and antivirals that disrupt viral assembly."

The implications may extend far beyond viruses. Self-assembly is essential throughout biology, helping create everything from the internal skeletons of cells to specialized molecular compartments and large protein complexes.

By making it possible to observe these intricate construction processes one molecule at a time, the Oxford team's approach offers researchers a powerful new way to investigate how complex biological structures emerge from relatively simple interactions.

A revolutionary new microscope could reveal the hidden machinery of life

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Almost a century ago, a breakthrough in microscopy gave scientists an entirely new way to examine the inside of cells. Known as phase contrast, the technique made previously faint or nearly invisible cellular structures much easier to distinguish. Its importance was recognized with a Nobel Prize in 1953.

Now, physicists at the University of California, Berkeley, have brought that same principle to electron microscopy, a technology capable of magnifying objects roughly 10,000 times more than conventional light microscopes.

The researchers have developed a device called a laser phase plate that could significantly improve cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM). This powerful imaging method has transformed the study of protein structures and helped speed up drug discovery over the past decade. Even so, it has a major limitation: Most human proteins are so small that obtaining clear images of them remains extremely difficult.

The new laser technology could help overcome that obstacle, potentially allowing scientists to image proteins as small as one-third the size of those that challenge current microscopes.

An even greater impact could come from improvements to cryoelectron tomography (cryo-ET). This newer approach combines images captured from multiple angles to construct three-dimensional views of proteins and other molecules. Unlike techniques that study isolated molecules in a solution, cryo-ET can reveal how proteins are arranged and interact within their natural cellular surroundings.

“Cryo-EM has become the new, fastest-growing method for resolving the structure of biological macromolecules, and cryo-ET is expected to show how these molecules work together in their natural, cellular context,” said Holger Müller, a UC Berkeley professor of physics and faculty scientist at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory who led the development effort. “But because of signal-to-noise limitations, the majority of human and animal proteins are too small to be analyzed by these methods. The increase in signal-to-noise ratio provided by this laser phase plate is expected to overcome these important limitations.”

The breakthrough relies on what the researchers describe as the world's most intense focused continuous-wave laser. Rather than simply illuminating a specimen, the laser interacts directly with the microscope's electron beam, altering its phase to increase image contrast.

This adjustment makes it easier to distinguish small molecules, including hemoglobin, from their surroundings. It could also improve the visibility of structures inside cells, such as the nucleus and mitochondria.

“With cryo-ET, we're looking at small, very complicated cellular material that’s incredibly crowded inside the cell,” said Bridget Carragher, founding technical director of imaging at Biohub in Redwood City, California. “It’s like a forest of trees, and you’re trying to find one leaf on one tree in there. Cryo-ET needs a dramatic step forward in contrast, so we can start to see what's going on inside the cell. That's what the laser phase plate promises to give us.”

With financial support from Biohub, Müller acquired an advanced cryo-EM instrument and equipped it with the new laser phase plate. He named the resulting microscope Theia, after the ancient Greek Titaness associated with light and radiance.

Meanwhile, Carragher is directing the construction of a related microscope at Biohub's imaging facility in Redwood City. Her team's instrument uses two lasers rather than one, building on theoretical research conducted by Müller and his collaborators.

The two laser beams are arranged at right angles to one another and each operates at approximately half the power. This configuration helps prevent components from burning out while also reducing optical distortions that could interfere with image quality.

Both research teams are working with Thermo Fisher Scientific, the leading manufacturer of cryo-EM instruments.

“Theia is the Formula 1 microscope,” Müller said. “It has extra electron optics that give it better resolution than the standard cryo-EM, even without the laser. With the addition of the laser phase plate, we hope that it really becomes the world's best instrument overall.”

The Berkeley team's latest images and technical findings are presented in the journal Science. Details of Biohub's two-laser design are available in a recently posted preprint.

To understand why the new technology matters, it helps to look at a longstanding challenge in biological imaging.

Because plant and animal cells consist largely of water, they are relatively transparent when viewed under an ordinary light microscope. Although this transparency might seem beneficial, it creates a problem. Small cellular structures, including mitochondria and nuclei, scatter very little light and therefore appear only slightly darker than the surrounding material.

Scientists have traditionally addressed this problem by adding stains to create greater contrast. However, staining also kills the cells.

In 1930, Dutch physicist Frits Zernike realized that light passing through biological material changes in more ways than simply becoming brighter or dimmer. When light scatters inside a cell, it also experiences a tiny delay that changes its phase, or the timing of its wave peaks.

Although the human eye cannot directly detect this phase shift, Zernike discovered that it could be converted into a visible difference in brightness. By shifting the phase of the unscattered light by 90 degrees, he made subtle cellular features stand out more clearly against their background when the light reached the viewer's eye.

This innovation became the foundation of phase-contrast microscopy, earning Zernike the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physics.

By the early 1940s, scientists had already recognized how useful phase contrast was for studying cells. They began exploring whether the same idea might improve electron microscopes, which use electrons instead of light to examine structures as small as individual proteins.

Unfortunately, early attempts encountered serious technical difficulties. Devices designed to alter the phase of electron beams often weakened the beam too much, introduced image instability, or reduced resolution.

A possible solution emerged in 2010, when Müller and Robert Glaeser, now a UC Berkeley professor emeritus of molecular and cell biology, proposed using an intense laser to produce the necessary phase shift. Their approach offered a way to improve contrast without sacrificing electron beam intensity.

Glaeser has played an important role in developing cryo-EM, which offers a potentially simpler way to determine molecular structures than traditional X-ray crystallography.

X-ray crystallography requires researchers to grow crystals of the molecules they want to study and obtain access to an intense X-ray source. Cryo-EM avoids the need to crystallize molecules, but it presents difficulties of its own.

One of the biggest problems is that an electron beam can heat and eventually damage the specimen being examined. This limits how much information can be collected and how detailed the resulting images can become. Covering specimens with metal can reduce damage and improve contrast, but it also makes the images less sharp.

During the 1960s, researchers began investigating whether freezing biological samples could protect them from radiation damage.

Glaeser demonstrated that cooling specimens helped preserve them during imaging. He also proposed using a less intense electron beam to examine thousands of frozen molecules at once, further limiting the damage.

Because the molecules would be positioned at different angles, computers could analyze and combine their images to reconstruct a highly detailed molecular structure.

These advances helped establish the foundations of modern cryo-EM, whose developers received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. During their acceptance remarks, the Nobel laureates acknowledged Glaeser's contributions.

The Nobel Committee praised the technology, explaining that cryo-EM “both simplifies and improves the imaging of biomolecules. This method has moved biochemistry into a new era.”

Turning the laser phase plate concept into working technology required years of engineering.

Following their 2010 proposal, Müller spent 15 years pursuing the idea, with part of the research supported by the National Institutes of Health.

The first major challenge was generating an extraordinarily intense laser beam concentrated into a tiny area. The researchers needed enough light intensity to change the phase of an electron beam by 90 degrees.

It took a decade to develop the necessary system.

Their solution was to confine a continuous laser beam inside a spherical chamber lined with mirrors. As the light repeatedly reflected through the cavity more than 10,000 times, its intensity increased dramatically while the beam became focused into an extremely small spot.

“It’s 75 kilowatts focused to a few microns,” Müller said. “That’s more powerful than what you use for welding. It's more power than a military laser. It builds up the brightest continuous laser focus ever.”

The team first demonstrated that the concept worked by installing a laser phase plate in one of Glaeser's older electron microscopes.

Additional funding from Biohub then made it possible to acquire a customized, state-of-the-art Thermo Fisher Krios cryo-EM microscope and modify it to accommodate the laser technology.

In their latest research, the scientists tested the upgraded microscope using six different samples that varied in size and preparation method. The laser consistently improved image resolution, with particularly important gains for specimens that are difficult to image using standard methods.

“For the most challenging cases — small particles, bad specimens — the laser produces a very considerable advantage,” Müller said.

To demonstrate the new microscope's capabilities, the researchers reconstructed images of two different proteins.

The first was aldolase, a protein found in muscle that modern cryo-EM systems can already image relatively easily. The second was hemoglobin, the protein responsible for carrying oxygen through the bloodstream.

Hemoglobin is much smaller and lies near the lower size limit of proteins that conventional cryo-EM instruments can reliably analyze.

Using the laser phase plate, the researchers improved the resolution of both protein structures. The improvement was especially pronounced for hemoglobin, highlighting the technology's potential to overcome the difficulties associated with smaller molecules.

“The bottom line is, if you have a large protein and a really good sample — a fresh one or one frozen without bubbles, for example — you may not need the phase plate to get a single, high-quality image. But for a small protein and a bad sample, laser-on is best,” Müller said. “This could fill an enormous gap in our knowledge of protein structures that can’t be crystallized or are too small for today’s cryo-EM. And it will be revolutionary for cryo-ET.”

Protein mass is typically expressed in daltons, a unit named after English chemist John Dalton. One dalton is defined as one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Conventional cryo-EM struggles to image proteins smaller than approximately 70 kilodaltons. That limitation is particularly significant because proteins below this threshold account for about 90% of the human proteome, the complete collection of proteins produced by the human body.

With the laser phase plate, researchers can now image proteins as small as 50 kilodaltons, although doing so remains challenging. Such proteins are even smaller than hemoglobin.

Müller believes the technology could eventually allow scientists to study proteins as small as 17 kilodaltons (the size of the protein myoglobin).

Reaching that goal may require another improvement: using a tightly focused electron beam rather than the defocused beam currently needed to produce sufficient contrast without the laser phase plate.

According to Müller, the laser system should make it possible to obtain contrast directly from phase changes in a focused electron beam.

If successful, this adjustment could double the contrast and signal-to-noise ratio again, adding to the improvement already achieved with the laser phase plate.

Such progress could make it possible to study a far larger proportion of the body's proteins and provide unprecedented insight into how they interact within the complex environment of cells.

“This technology is a step function change for biology,” said Stephani Otte, Biohub’s Vice President of Imaging Science. “We are going to be able to see how molecular machines operate inside the living cell, in context, for the first time. What was once invisible will become visible — and that changes everything about how we understand disease.”

The Berkeley research team also includes Glaeser; postdoctoral fellows and co-first authors Petar Petrov and Jessie Zhang; staff scientist Jonathan Remis; postdoctoral fellow Hang Cheng; and current and former physics graduate students Jeremy Axelrod, Eric Cooper, Ian Hicklin, Shahar Sandhaus, and Cooper Schnurr.

At Biohub, Carragher and David Agard, the organization's founding scientific director of imaging, jointly lead the Dynamic Structural Cell Biology group. Both are co-corresponding authors of the Biohub preprint, alongside Biohub engineer Pavel Olshin.
