Germany: Labor Minister's resignation puts a spotlight on cracks within the government

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Following the sudden resignation of Bärbel Bas as Labor Minister and co-chair of the Social Democratic Party, accusations are flying in Chancellor Merz's coalition government.

The week kicked off with a bang: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas announced her resignation as Labor Minister and as Co-Chair of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD). "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I realized that I need time to come to terms with my grief," Bärbel Bas wrote in a personal statement to party members.

However, she also cited "the public attacks against me — unfortunately, including some from within the ranks of our coalition partner" as reasons for her resignation. In fact, the pressure on Bas increased significantly in recent days. The centrist coalition government is urgently awaiting key legislative proposals from the Ministry of Labor.

Before the summer recess in July, a commission had already presented comprehensive proposals for reforming Germany's statutory pension system. The coalition partners of SPD and the center-right Christian Democratic Union and Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU) wanted these proposals to be quickly turned into legislation. To date, however, no finalized drafts have been presented.

"The minister is no longer tenable. For months, she has been blocking the reforms our country so urgently needs," railed Christian von Stetten, head of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group's caucus representing medium-sized enterprises, said in reference to Bas in September.

In early October, such criticism was echoed by CDU Economy Minister Katherina Reiche, who demanded that the pension reform be passed by the end of the year. Bas had to submit the bill "promptly," Reiche said, although it was known at the time what a difficult personal situation her cabinet colleague was in at the time.

SPD co-leader, Finance Minister and Vice-Chancellor Lars Klingbeil reacted sharply on Monday, saying the political attacks against Bas had been "disgraceful."  Following a meeting of the SPD leadership, he told journalists in Berlin: "This poisons the political discourse, and — in the interest of preserving our political culture — I hope that everyone will take a step back and reflect on what has actually happened over the past few weeks." He called on all centrist politicians to be careful "that we don't destroy the good people in politics by attacking one another."

Such accusations carry significant weight at a time when the coalition is already mired in a deep crisis.

Adding to this is the new vacancy at the top of the SPD. Without Bas, Klingbeil will lead the SPD alone. A party convention will be scheduled for December or January to elect new leadership for the Social Democrats. It is not certain whether Klingbeil will run again. He received a very poor result during his last reelection as party leader.

Alexander Schweitzer is to succeed Bärbel Bas as Federal Minister of Labor. The former state premier of the state of Rhineland-Palatinate had previously served as that state's Minister of Labor and Social Affairs. Chancellor Friedrich Merz expressed confidence that Schweitzer's experience would ensure " good cooperation within the federal government," adding that he hoped that the pending legislative proposals "will now be quickly approved by the cabinet and can be introduced into the parliamentary process."

The chancellor refuted the accusations leveled by Bas, saying that he had never discussed personal matters with her. Friedrich Merz said he had explicitly advised Bas to take the time she needed to grieve.

Mistrust between the coalition partners has increased significantly, with SPD lawmakers speaking of an increasingly toxic political climate.

For months, the CDU and SPD have been grappling with low poll numbers, disappointing election results, and disputes over key reform projects. Plans for changes to the electricity tax, the introduction of a sugar tax, the pension and the labor market reform — hardly any initiative has been able to proceed without public controversy.

Adding to this is the crisis within the chancellor's party, the CDU, which saw a historic election defeat in the eastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania in September, when it even lost representation in a state parliament for the first time in history.

On Sunday and Monday, the party leadership met for a closed-door session to discuss the way forward.

They produced a four-page document which indicated a shift in priorities, putting more emphasis on social issues: affordable housing, lower electricity prices, better health care, and greater respect for citizens' lifetime achievements are mentioned in the paper. This is seen as a response to criticism that the CDU had been focusing too much on economic and regulatory issues.

"We have to work our way out of this slump — and get out of a crisis of confidence," Merz said. "You can't just reinvent a party overnight over a weekend, but we want to show that we're ready for change... and we have to be, given the election results."

The chancellor announced that he would lower the electricity tax for everyone — a promise he had made before the general election in 2025 but had not kept.

Both CDU/CSU and SPD know that calling early elections could primarily benefit the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), which is leading in the polls nationwide. So despite the deep rifts, Klingbeil and Merz currently see no alternative to their coalition.

125 years of Thomas Mann's 'Buddenbrooks'

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This over 1,000-page masterpiece offers a panoramic view of society in the 19th century and exposes the collapse of a merchant family: unrecognized sons, disillusioned daughters and the loss of middle-class ideals.

Who would have imagined it in the beginning? A 26-year-old man without a high school diploma, but greatly ambitious, writes a two-pound book with a melancholy undertone. "I'm not exactly delighted," his publisher initially responded. But he ended up publishing the novel anyway, more out of goodwill than conviction. And the rest is history.

For many, Thomas Mann's Buddenbrooks is considered THE German social novel. It is indeed a classic, and one that garnered the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. Global fame, an international best-seller, and required reading for many German students. Anyone who mentions Thomas Mann thinks of Buddenbrooks in the same breath. Why is that?

Does the explanation lie in the autobiographical connections between the work and the author?

Thomas Mann was born in Lübeck to a merchant family, but his artistic aspirations did not fit in well in the middle-class mainstream. This struggle inspired Mann's first work. The inability of this sensitive bohemian to carry on the family's time-honored business after the death of his father was sniffed at and derided.

Reflecting the situation of his novel's character Hanno, Mann had a privileged life in a wealthy family, but one that was at risk of collapse. The aspiring novelist also had to withstand disappointing the expectations of others. He was forced to defend his dream of an artistic life. Yet as much as he was inspired by real life, he forcefully altered these biographical details to place his unique literary mark on his writing.

But does that really matter in light of people who choose not to make the distinction between an author's life and his work? Even though the name "Lübeck" does not appear in the novel, citizens there felt markedly insulted after the publication of Buddenbrooks in 1901.

That likely lies in the fact that the image the author portrayed was not one of an idyllic northern German city, but one of a place that had difficulty blending tradition with modernity.

This contradiction is also seen in each of the figures in the novel. Nothing is like it appears. Mann lays down this foundation of this skepticism from the very beginning, in a scene taking place on a very ordinary Thursday in October 1835. The Buddenbrook family comes together for their ordinary late lunch.

"There they all sat, on heavy, high-backed chairs, consuming good heavy food from good silver plates, drinking full-bodied wines and expressing their views freely on all subjects. When they began to talk shop, they slipped unconsciously more and more into dialect, and used the clumsy but comfortable idioms that seemed to embody to them the business efficiency and the easy well-being of their community. Sometimes they even used an over-drawn pronunciation by way of making fun of themselves and each other, and relished their clipped phrases and exaggerated vowels with the same heartiness they did their food."

There sits the leader of the family company, Johann Buddenbrook the Elder, and his wife, his son and counsel Jean, along with his family. The grandchildren are also there: Tony the dreamer, the quarrelsome rebel Christian, and Thomas, the eldest grandchild.

It is Thomas who is supposed to later take on the responsibility of continuing the family tradition, regardless of the cost. Mann weaves his narrative work starting with this main family, and then across several generations.

But appearances in the family are deceptive. The reader senses it's all headed toward a downfall. The new house is taken over from a family that was once as illustrious as the Buddenbrooks, but faced financial ruin and had to leave.

Johann and his family aim to make a better go of it. They make decisions on what is demanded economically, but fail — financially, interpersonally, and mentally.

Tony and Thomas end up falling in love with people not befitting their social standing, but do not have the courage to stand up to their parents. They enter marriages of convenience, yet it is these partnerships that paradoxically set disaster in motion.

From an early age, Thomas Mann was influenced by Schopenhauer's pessimistic notion of the will being a blind force at work in the world. Thus, there is likewise no escape for the Buddenbrooks. They speak of emotions and morality, yet it is money and economics that steer the ship. Failure, decline and even death seem to be the only viable outcome.

Mann also punctuates this hopelessness in a stylistic manner, by consistently repeating entire passages word for word, or evoking motifs such as the family registry or the estate in all their various forms.

The extent of this demise appears linguistically molded and exaggerated. The Buddenbrooks, this proud north German family of refinement, actually speaks the vernacular studded with empty French expressions. "Je, den Düwel ook, c'est la question, ma très chère demoiselle!"

The lowly servants, the courtesans, and the demi-monde: all of them speak the language of the street, raw and unpolished. In a virtuoso manner, Thomas Mann orchestrated this mass of voices into a panorama of society in the 19th century. The snobbishness on the part of the Buddenbrooks thus appears as a relic of a long lost time. It's a symbol of their inability to adapt to change and place their faith in progress.

Yet the fact that this extensive work spanning several generations does not get weighed down by aesthetic excess is purely due to Mann's brilliance as an author. He is able to bring every one of his numerous characters to life in words. Life that is full of complexity, contradictions and ambiguities. Some of these characters accompany the reader page for page; others appear only briefly.

And yet one begins to contemplate how someone like Grünlich could become a dodgy, unscrupulous husband and catches oneself wanting to chase the fearful Hanno out of the music conservatory and yell to him: "Go, throw yourself into life! You are not a failure." It is precisely this empathy that spurs one on to read through the night and prompts one to chuckle at all the intricate sentences and philosophical digressions.

Thomas Mann was born into a privileged Lübeck merchant family in 1875. He died in Zurich, Switzerland in 1955. "I am a townsman, a citizen, a child and a great-grandchild of German bourgeois society," the author once wrote about himself. Mann received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929 for his debut novel, Buddenbrooks. He also garnered numerous other prizes and honors for his extensive work, including Death in Venice (1911), The Magic Mountain (1924), and the tetralogy Joseph and His Brothers (1933-1943). He emigrated to Switzerland in 1933, and to the US in 1938. From there, he made his famous radio commentaries in which he agitated against the Nazis. He returned to Switzerland in 1952.

Germany's China calculation shifts as import pressures eclipse export benefits

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Germany has long resisted tougher action against China, fearing retaliation. But rising pressure on key industries has pushed Berlin to seek stronger EU trade-defense powers to protect its industrial base.

Germany's cautious approach toward Beijing is cracking. For years, Berlin blocked a harder line on what the European Union describes as China's  unfair trade practices.

In the last few months, that caution — a fear of Chinese retaliation that could hurt German exporters — has given way to a willingness to escalate.

Last week, Chancellor Friedrich Merz and French President Emmanuel Macron wrote jointly to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen seeking a new tool to cut a trading partner off from the EU's single market if unfair practices continue.

Clearly aimed at Beijing, the letter warned the measure was necessary against countries that "deliberately seek to undermine ... fair market conditions by political or economic means."

China's Commerce Ministry warned Europe would "suffer the consequences" if it adopted such a "kill-switch" plan, which was timed to coincide with crucial EU-China trade talks in Beijing last week.

Noah Barkin, a senior adviser with research consultancy Rhodium Group's China practice, told DW that the threats to Germany's export powerhouse "have convinced Merz and his closest advisers that the benefits of action outweigh the costs of inaction."

Before the COVID pandemic, the two economies rose in tandem and German industry benefited from China's ascent.

Since 2021, however, China has fallen from Germany's second-largest export market to its ninth, according to data from Germany Trade & Invest for the first half of 2026.

Chinese competitors have quickly closed the technology gap and benefit from much lower costs and state subsidies. That pressure is being felt especially in Germany's automaking, precision engineering and chemicals industries.

German vehicle makers have suffered a large sales drop in China, which was once their second-largest market. According to data from Rhodium Group, German car exports to China plummeted by 66% between 2022 and 2025.

Big German brands now face heavy rivalry from a new wave of Chinese electric vehicle (EV) manufactures in other major markets, including Europe, which is forcing a wave of layoffs and possible factory closures.

In Germany alone, imports of new Chinese-made cars rose 121% in the first seven months of the year, according to Destatis, the federal statistics agency. Overall, Chinese-made vehicles accounted for nearly 14% of total vehicle imports.

"After continuously losing market share in China ... it [the rivalry] seems to be hitting their business within the European automotive market," Andreas Mischer, an associate fellow at the Berlin-based Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS), told DW.

Mischer said pressure from Germany's powerful automotive lobby was key to Berlin's "shifting attitude toward trade defense instruments."

Among the many German business leaders who have demanded a more hard-line approach in recent months, Oliver Richtberg, head of foreign trade at VDMA, Germany's mechanical engineering association, was perhaps the most blunt:

"We are currently being deindustrialized through unfair measures — and most companies are no longer willing to accept this," Richtberg told Welt am Sonntag newspaper in August.

According to Eurostat, the wider EU's goods deficit with China was around €360 billion ($402 billion) last year — effectively €1 billion per day — and widened again in the first half of 2026.

While Brussels has been pushing back against the dumping of cheap Chinese goods, a separate industrial protection law and other anti-coercion measures are running into trouble from Beijing and at home.

The EU's Industrial Accelerator Act, proposed in March, aims to pull manufacturing investment back to Europe by favoring EU-based firms in public contracts and subsidies.

Foreign investments in key sectors would only be approved if European partners keep majority control, which Beijing has framed as discriminatory.

The European Automobile Manufacturers' Association (ACEA) warned last month that the draft act does little to counter the threat from cheaper Chinese rivals. In a position paper, it said the incentive to produce within the bloc has to be large enough to "cancel out the extra cost of producing locally."

Berlin's chief concern was that China could place new export curbs on rare earth minerals and the magnets made from them. China has a stranglehold on these elements, which are essential for German automotive, renewables and defense manufacturing.

Bloomberg last week cited unnamed German officials as saying that a new EU kill-switch could be a "second-strike weapon" if Beijing blocked those exports. Brussels is also considering unilateral limits on how many hybrid cars China can export to the EU, according to the same Bloomberg report.

Perhaps to avoid such an open confrontation, the EU and China reached a "shared understanding" last week at the EU-China trade talks in Beijing that could cut Chinese exports of hybrid and plug-in hybrid vehicles to the European Union by more than half, according to EU Trade Commissioner Maros Sefcovic, who headed the European delegation.

Reports say the agreement would cap such Chinese imports at 350,000 vehicles annually, with the limit increasing by 6% each year over four years.

Overall, new hybrid-electric cars in the EU reached nearly 37% of all new cars registered from January to August, according to ACEA data.

MERICS, meanwhile, calculated that the EU is a more critical export market for China than the US. In the first half of the year, Chinese exports to the EU were $254 billion (€354 billion), versus $172 billion to the US.

This could give EU negotiators a firmer hand as they seek to avoid a trade war while protecting Europe from China's latest export glut.

"The EU has more leverage than it sometimes thinks," MERICS' Mischer told DW.

Formula 1: Rwanda to bring sport back to Africa

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The last time Formula 1 hosted a race in Africa was way back in 1993. But a new track in Rwanda is set to host a Grand Prix in "the early 2030s," continuing the country’s investment in international sport.

After nearly 40 years of false starts, motor racing's premier sport is returning to Africa, after Formula 1 announced on Tuesday that Rwanda would soon host a Grand Prix.

The race will be know as the African Grand Prix after a 10-year contract was signed between the parties. With no suitable track ready, Rwanda will build one in Bugesera, southeast of the capital Kigali. F1 said it will be ready "early in the next decade."

The last time F1 raced in Africa was the South African Grand Prix in 1993. While there has since been plenty of talk of the sport returning to the continent, most often mentioning South Africa itself, nothing concrete had happened, until now.

"Today is a landmark moment for Formula 1," F1 President and chief executive Stefano Domenicali said. "With Rwanda joining the calendar from the next decade our sport will race in every inhabited continent, bringing the world championship closer than ever to fans around the globe.

"Africa has always been part of Formula 1's future, and from the beginning we were determined to return in the right way, with the right partner, a long-term commitment and a clear vision for creating meaningful impact."

One of the most vocal proponents of a race in Africa has been seven-time world champion Lewis Hamilton. The 41-year-old Black British driver, currently with Ferrari, has said he has been "fighting in the background" for years to have a race in Africa. With the date not yet set in stone for the African Grand Prix, it remains to be seen whether he can prolong his career enough to be on the grid when the lights go out in Rwanda.

For Rwandan President Paul Kagame, the event is "a success and a milestone for the entire continent, especially for the African fans." He added that he hoped it could lead to more F1 events in Africa.

Kagame has been president of Rwanda for 25 years, and essentially ran the country for the six years before that, following the genocide in 1994. He routinely wins over 99% of the vote in elections, and the country is often criticized for its human rights record.

The 68-year-old has bet big on sports. Rwanda has had sponsorship deals with a number of top European football clubs, including Arsenal, Atletico Madrid, Paris Saint-Germain and Bayern Munich. However, fan and media backlash over the deal caused Bayern to drop the commercial element with the country's tourist arm, Visit Rwanda, last year. Arsenal did not renew its deal. Many critics have labeled the policy as sportswashing.

The country also has deals with top US basketball and American football teams, and hosted cycling's Road World Championships last year.

But Formula 1, one of the world's richest sports, will likely be seen as Kagame and Rwanda's biggest sporting coup to date.

The hidden treasure in discarded wearable and fast tech

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sales of fast tech like mini fans and disposable e-vapes, as well as wearable health tech such as heart monitors and smartwatches are soaring. They're creating a new e-waste problem but are full of hidden treasure.

From mini fans and the cheap chargers to smartwatches bought as part of a New Year's health kick, some 44% of Europeans own at least six "fast-tech" gadgets. And more than half say they keep unused and broken e-waste items languishing at home or throw them in the regular trash instead of recycling.

That's according to a new report by Brussels-based e-waste association WEEE Forum. It describes fast tech, which is booming, as charging and lighting devices under €10 (circa $11), high-tech items like power banks and fitness trackers, and gadgets and lifestyle appliances like cheap mini blenders and fans, as well as single-use electronic vapes.

"So, these electronics are a bit like fast fashion," Magdalena Charytanowicz, WEEE Forum's Communication Manager, told DW.

Nearly 40% of buyers recalled discarding a fast-tech item without using it at all or within a month of buying it, and nearly 60% said they had used a product for no longer than six months. Yet the vast majority of respondents were generally satisfied with their gadgets despite short-lived functionality.

This suggests consumers accept the "fast-tech bargain" of poor quality that "does the job for a while" and is easily replaced — similar to fast fashion — according to the report.

About 62 million tons of electronics are discarded worldwide annually. "That's the Great Wall of China of e-waste that is produced every year," noted Charytanowicz, adding that the figure is set to grow to 82 million by 2030.

In Europe, roughly 40% of electronic waste is recycled, yet the rate worldwide is just 17%. This means valuable materials are lost, while electronic waste ends up polluting the environment.

In 2022, a million tons of critical raw materials were embedded in the 10.7 million tons of e-waste generated across Europe, including manganese, gold, copper and aluminum.

These materials are at "the center of geopolitical tensions," Charytanowicz said, referring to escalating global tensions over access to rare earths and other critical minerals.

"Even the smallest" devices are worth recycling if they contain a circuit board or battery, she said.

Groups like WEEE Forum say more awareness raising is needed on how to dispose of cheap electronic devices, and that recycling should be made as easy as possible.

But the best way to reduce a product's environmental impact is to extend its lifespan through better design and repair, as recycling raw materials is energy intensive.

Charytonawicz said clearer, harmonized EU rules now under consideration could help improve e-waste collection. The bloc's 20-year-old directive is "not really adapted to the changing reality," she said.

Another growing area of e-waste is digital healthcare. While devices such as diabetes sensors and blood pressure monitors are "transforming healthcare," many are designed to be disposable.

A study published in Nature in December 2025 found sales of wearable healthcare electronics, for example, could reach 2 billion devices annually by 2050, and generate a million tons of e-waste.

Jeremy Faludi, associate professor of sustainable design engineering at TU Delft in the Netherlands, says making health tech — particularly digital devices used in, or issued by hospitals — recyclable or reusable comes with some "much bigger challenges" than normal electronics.

That's in part due to the potential for patient contamination, particularly with digital surgical devices that come into contact with blood or other bodily fluids.

"The way that a lot of things are designed now, you can't recover the stuff because it can't be easily sterilized without the electronics," said Faludi.

He worked on the Digital Health in Circular Economy (DiCE) project, an EU-funded initiative, to help redesign digital healthcare devices to "recover critical materials and reduce environmental impacts without compromising patient safety."

A modular design, for example, allows electronic components to be separated from the parts that need to be replaced between patients, making devices easier to reuse.

The four-year DiCE project, which included citizens, hospitals and manufacturers across nine EU countries, found reusable ECG cables — which connect patients to heart-monitoring equipment — cut environmental impacts by up to 81-94%, for example. The sets are usually designed for single use.

Rules around medical waste can also make reuse and recycling more difficult, particularly when requirements differ between countries and often come down to perceived rather than actual risk, Faludi added. He says some 85% of hospital trash is not infectious but everything ends up in the same bins. Better sorting as well as EU-wide regulations around treating non-infectious hospital waste would help here.

Like with other tech, consumer health devices such as blood pressure and glucose monitors often risk being forgotten in drawers or ending up in the wrong waste, simply because people do not know how to dispose of them, said Charytanowicz of WEEE Forum, which is also involved in DiCE.

Pilot schemes in Belgium, Spain and Slovenia that offered free postal returns and collection machines in public places found that more than 60% of people were willing to return unwanted devices if it was a straightforward process.

"What's really important is the convenience so that it can be integrated in your daily routines," said Charytanowicz, adding that "nudges" such as messages that recycling a device could help the health of another person motivate people to "do the right thing."

FIFPRO report highlights growing pains in women's football

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Expected global revenue of over $1 billion and new faces at the U17 World Cup show women's football is booming, but a recent report also highlights need for better structures globally.

At first glance, women's football is healthier than ever. A recent report by FIFPRO, football's global players' union, said worldwide revenue is expected to reach over $1bn (€894m) in 2027. The World Cup in Brazil, a first in South America, will be the last with 32 teams and is expected to be the most watched and biggest revenue-generating women's tournament ever.

The good news continues with the U17 Women's World Cup in Morocco, where the tournament is being held every year for five straight years. Germany, France, Spain, Brazil, the United States and defending champions North Korea are all at the 2026 edition of a competition whose reputation continues to grow.

"You can just see where the game is going, which is so exciting," US head coach Ciara Crinion told FIFA. "The level has just gone up. We have to continue to stay with that and compete as well. [The U-17 Women's World Cup] almost feels like that first stepping stone in a major tournament for young players, and now you see how serious it is taken."

Puerto Rico, Australia and Argentina are making their debuts. For Puerto Rico, qualification is historic, as no football team in the men's or women's game has ever reached a World Cup tournament before.

"We've embraced this idea of being Puerto Rico's first team to qualify for a World Cup," head coach Andres Mirabelli told FIFA. Alongside national development plans, player recruitment was aided by a diaspora of nearly four million in the United States, perhaps the strongest place for women's football in the world. According to FIFPRO, the regional football average salary in North America is $5,940 (€5,547) per month, which is far higher than any other continent.

But even for the handful of players who make it out of these youth tournaments to a professional career, creating a living out of the beautiful game remains a real challenge. The aforementioned FIFPRO report details that many professional female players across the world barely earn minimum wage, with some even falling below it.

The report, which sourced its data from player surveys across the world, found that only 66% of professional players are paid for 12 months a year. The global average salary is $2,600 (€2,300) a month, but the range varies drastically across the world. In Eastern Europe, for example, that average is $880 per month. In Africa, it is $432, but in North America it is just under $6,000.

"I think what you also find in the women's game is the gaps are not just between the top five leagues and the rest. There is a gap there, but even within leagues themselves [there is one]," FIFPRO head of research and strategy for women's football Frankie Brown told DW.

"In some of the other leagues you're dealing with teams that are operating professionally, so will be paying their players on a reasonable wage, and playing in the same league as them you'll have players that are on amateur contracts and not being paid at all."

With economic and job security an issue for many, the report reveals that psychological stress factors are now the second main reason many consider leaving football.

"This is one of the most concerning parts of the report," Alex Culvin, the director of women's football at FIFPRO, said in a statement.

"Of the 49% of women’s football players considering leaving the sport early, 46% cited their mental health as the reason. In interviews, players say the sport's growth brings new pressures — including extra workload and social media abuse — but that clubs do not have the necessary safeguards in place to support these changes. We want clubs and national associations to provide mandatory mental health support focused on wellbeing, which is independent of sporting performance objectives."

FIFPRO's report is asking FIFA to set a so-called floor for the women's game so that minimum standards in key areas such as contracts, pensions and health can be enforced. Perhaps inspiration can be taken from the labor deal the WNBA agreed in 2026. It was long-negotiated deal that ultimately sent a message to female athletes everywhere to push for better pay, conditions and a greater share of the revenue they help generate.

"It is positive that average salaries are increasing for some players and that more are earning a satisfactory wage, but it’s important to note that there is a huge disparity in pay across regions," said Culvin.

"There is also an imbalance in conditions, not only across the globe but also within regions; in Europe, for example, the difference in standards between the east and west is enormous. This is a gulf we need to work hard to narrow; the growth in the most advanced markets must not be completely isolated from the rest of the industry.

With high hopes for the 2027 World Cup in Brazil and recent moves to increase professional football domestically, Germany is aiming to become an example for structural improvement in women's football. Hosting the Euros in 2029 certainly gives them an incentive to improve standards.

While salaries are in the upper tier — in the 2023-24 season, a women's Bundesliga player earned an average base salary of around €48,000 per year — the process of structural development in the country is still in its infancy. A new German football association rule shows that even basic professional structures are still being established. Only since the 2025–26 season have teams in the Women's Bundesliga been required to employ assistant coaches, goalkeeper coaches and athletic coaches on a full-time, professional basis.

"The facilities and the staff members," Nicole Anyomi told DW, were a factor in her making a recent move to London City Lionesses. "We have 30-plus staff members here whereas in Frankfurt we had like seven or eight. You can learn from each person and there is something new to learn every day. They are looking for crimes committed by refugees, migrants and foreigners living in Germany.

But studies by media scholars show that 95% of the AfD's press releases focus on non-German suspects, while crimes involving German suspects are almost completely ignored.

However, the common narrative of the AfD — that non-Germans are inherently much more violent — is simply not true.

In a new research paper, Susann Prätor, a sociologist, criminologist and professor at the Lower Saxony Police Academy, has taken a very close look at crime data in Germany, together with her colleagues from the University of Zurich and the Lower Saxony Criminological Research Institute.

She looked not only at officially reported crime — that is, all offenses that appeared in police crime statistics — but also at unreported crime: These are all offenses that are not reported or detected, and therefore do not appear in any official statistics but are identified through representative surveys.

"According to police crime statistics, people who do not have German citizenship commit crimes more frequently than German citizens. However, studies into unreported cases show that non-German nationals are twice as likely to be reported. Differences between the two groups are also evident in crimes that go unreported, but they are significantly less pronounced," Prätor told DW.

Crimes committed by foreign nationals are therefore twice as likely to appear in police crime statistics as crimes committed by Germans.

These statistics record all reported cases and do not specify whether the allegations prove to be true.

When the statistics are adjusted for the bias in the reporting of crimes, foreigners are no longer 2.6 times more likely to commit crimes than Germans, but only 1.7 times more likely. This figure is also consistent with the results of studies on unreported juvenile violent crime. According to these studies, foreign youths are about 1.6 times as likely to commit violent crimes as German youths.

Other factors affecting crime include age and gender. Young men make up a larger proportion of the foreign-born population, and young men are the group most prone to criminal behavior across all societies.

According to Prätor, "categories such as being young, male, poorly educated and having a history of violence are factors that increase the likelihood of violent behavior. A certain concept of masculinity also plays a role, wherever that may come from. But we do not see culture as a factor, contrary to the prejudice that masculinity and violence play a greater role in other cultures."

Prätor points to a survey on attitudes toward violence. According to the survey, 5% of German youths view violence positively. Among those without a German passport, the figure is 10%.

The criminologist has identified a crucial factor: "Family upbringing plays an important role in shaping attitudes toward violence, because these attitudes are formed in the home. If I experience violence in my own home, I will have a different relationship to violence than if I hadn't experienced it," said Prätor.

Germany is, however, on the right track, says Prätor, even if it doesn't always feel that way. "Fifty or sixty years ago, parental violence was much more common in child-rearing than it is today. Acts of violence committed by young people are also far less frequent than they were in the 1990s or the early 2000s."

Prätor emphasizes that it is important to bring objectivity to the debate. A good illustration of this is a look at police records of serious violent crimes — such as aggravated assault, manslaughter, or murder. "Of every 100 German citizens, 0.2% commit violent crimes; among non-German nationals, the figure is 0.6%."

However, Prätor points out that "more than 99% of the people living here in Germany — whether of German or non-German origin — the overwhelming majority in this country do not commit any serious violent crimes at all."

From action cameras and smartphones to EVs and robot vacuums, Chinese brands are gaining ground worldwide, despite privacy and security concerns. What's driving their rise, and can European rivals keep up?

Do you remember the days when GoPro was synonymous with action cameras?

At its peak in 2015, the US company reigned supreme with around 6.6 million units sold that year and a market share of over 70%. Today, the pioneer is fighting for survival.

GoPro's biggest rivals, DJI and Insta360, entered the action camera market in 2019 and 2021, respectively. Today, they account for more than 90% of global smart handheld camera sales.

Alexander Hannemann, who regularly uses action cameras to film his motorcycle rides, says he switched from GoPro to Insta360 around two years ago.

"The quality is much better than a GoPro, even when I'm speeding up and going into corners at like 80 kilometers per hour," he told DW.

GoPro's case is not an exception. Since around 2010, many Chinese brands have not only caught up but surpassed once-dominant Western rivals across the consumer technology industry, from smart TVs to robot vacuums.

Gone are the days when "Made in China" stood for cheap, mass-produced products.

"China is still making affordable products today," says Chris Pereira, a Canadian entrepreneur and author with over 20 years of experience in China. "But the products are also high quality."

This combination has led to well over half of new consumer tech products globally coming from Chinese suppliers, Pereira adds.

BYD, Xpeng and other Chinese car manufactures sold almost two-thirds of all electric cars last year, surpassing incumbents like Tesla. In robot vacuums, Chinese brands dominate the global market, leaving former pioneer US company iRobot struggling to compete.

So why do Chinese companies increasingly outperform their Western rivals?

Pereira, whose consultancy iMpact helps Chinese companies gain traction overseas, attributes China's success to fierce domestic competition in cities like Shenzhen, Hangzhou and Shanghai; highly integrated supply chains and a can-do business culture that allows companies to innovate; and respond to problems quickly.

Other key reasons for China's competitive edge include unparalleled industrial automation, the strategic use of AI in manufacturing, responsiveness from regulators as well as relatively cheap labor, which together leads to significantly lower production costs and much faster innovation cycles than in the world's most advanced economies.

Joey Cheng, who moved to the US for his PhD studies over 20 years ago, is familiar with both the American consumer and the Chinese producer sides.

While his startup is based in the US, almost all the manufacturing is done in Shenzhen, where a large share of the world's hardware and consumer electronics are made.

"The supply chain in Shenzhen is basically inevitable for cost cutting and quick product development," Cheng says. "Especially as a startup you must get the product out quickly, or you'll get blown out of the water."

Although consumers are flocking to Chinese companies, some reservations persist.

A recent poll found that more than 90% of Europeans do not trust Chinese technology companies with their data, while about 80% distrust US firms. Germans expressed the highest level of mistrust.

Nadine, a German travel blogger who uses DJI and Insta360 products, says she wouldn't use any Asian products at home.

"My home robot vacuum is from a German company for precisely this reason: So the data remains compliant with German regulations. After all, manufacturers can connect directly to the camera," she said, referring to camera and audio hacking incidents involving robot vacuums.

Still, Pereira says that Chinese companies expanding abroad have to pay a "trust premium."

"This trust deficit means that they need to be extra competitive in terms of product and price in order for the market to choose them," he says, adding that the gap can be narrowed by a presence in overseas markets like BYD's first European passenger-car plant, expected to open in Hungary later this year.

Chinese companies say they comply with EU data protection rules. However, domestic security laws in the US and China can require firms to hand over data to authorities, and German officials note limited oversight of whether data is transferred from European servers abroad.

In addition to privacy concerns, Chinese consumer tech firms have come under scrutiny over espionage and other risks, most recently regarding connected devices like EVs.

In light of security concerns, trade deficits and China's overall dominance, the US and several other Western countries have hit Chinese imports and companies with tariffs, blacklists and strict requirements. In late July, the Trump administration banned the import of humanoid robots manufactured outside the US.

A representative of Chinese robot maker Primebot told DW that all Chinese consumer tech companies are feeling the effects.

"We might seek cooperation with a local US company. Another potential solution is building a factory there," he said.

European countries and the EU are also weighing more tariffs against Chinese companies.

Chris Pereira foresees that the tariffs will accelerate two trends among Chinese companies: Moving more manufacturing overseas and an increasing focus on challenging top-tier Western brands with premium goods.

"Chinese officials and companies recognize that manufacturing needs to be localized to a certain degree. One reason is to assuage the challenges of exporting, another is to create trust locally," Pereira sums up.

"The other trend is that once duties wipe out the price advantage at the bottom, the margin left is at the top, so Chinese brands are increasingly competing directly with established Western names, where trust matters most."

As China's dominance keeps growing, Western companies are scrambling to find ways to stay competitive.

Kaz Merchant, who works for a UK-based company that imports and exports IT hardware, says smaller companies, in particular, must carve out a geographic niche.

"They need to have their own unique selling point for a certain product, which China might not have for that region. Or they need to partner with a Chinese company," he says.

To Pereira, a key issue Europe needs to solve is regulators' responsiveness.

"Chinese regulators tell you very clearly and quickly what you can do. That speed helps companies iterate and succeed overseas," Pereira says.

"If you're waiting weeks for a response, you're going to lose business."

Brazil's elections matter for the planet. This is why

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Brazil is heading back to the polls, but the impact could be felt worldwide. With the Amazon vital to the health of the planet, the election could help shape its future.

Few national elections carry as much environmental weight as Brazil's presidential race. And this one is being watched from far beyond the country's boundaries.

Home to the largest section of the Amazon rainforest, Brazil is guardian of a carbon sink of vital importance, making the country's conservation policies globally relevant.

Environmentalists have already voiced alarm at Flávio Bolsonaro — son of right-wing former president Jair Bolsonaro — coming out 2% ahead of incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in the first round of elections.

"It's going to be a second coming of Bolsonaro, therefore a more radical Bolsonaro," said Claudio Angelo, international policy coordinator at Climate Observatory, a network of Brazilian NGOs.

"At the risk of sounding alarmist, I would say Lula's re-election is the only thing standing between us and the end of the Amazon."

The Amazon rainforest stores huge amounts of the carbon dioxide (CO2) released into the atmosphere from the burning of fossil fuels. As a result, it helps regulate the Earth's climate.

For decades, the tropical forest has acted as one of nature's most powerful defenses against climate change. But it has also suffered from widespread deforestation.

During Jair Bolsonaro's presidency, from 2019 until 2023, environmental agencies were weakened and enforcement efforts curtailed, meaning tropical forest was cleared at unprecedented rates.

"The environment agenda was disregarded and the level of control the government had on pollution overall, but specifically on deforestation, was reversed," said Claudio Forner, head of climate policy at Berlin-based think tank Climate Analytics.

Over Bolsonaro's four-year term, an estimated 45,586km2 of the Amazon was destroyed, an area bigger than the size of Denmark. And the forest is not as resilient as it once was, with scientists warning that parts of it are now emitting more carbon than they absorb.

Lula came to office with a message of protection for the Amazon. Besides calling for an end to deforestation by 2030, he increased monitoring of the forest and strengthened Brazil's environmental enforcement agency IBAMA.

Deforestation has plummeted under his leadership, with 2026 set to be its lowest level in a decade. That represents a 41% drop overall compared to Jair Bolsonaro's tenure.

Experts have already criticized Flávio Bolsonaro's record on forest protection. As a senator in 2019, he authored draft legislation that could have stark impacts for the Amazon. While landowners are currently required to preserve 80% of their property for the growth of native plants, the bill would allow them to use their holdings as they please.

"The signal from the president will say 'now you can deforest' or 'it's harder to deforest,'" said Ana Yang, director of the Environment and Society Center at London-based think tank Chatham House.

"The moment that you decrease enforcement capability, deforestation grows," she told DW.

Bolsonaro has also pledged to end illegal deforestation by 2029, a year before Lula. But Claudio Angelo is critical of that message.

"He will end illegal deforestation not by enforcing the law, but by making it legal," he said.

More generally, environmental experts fear Bolsonaro might dismantle many of the protections strengthened under the current president.

"Unfortunately, climate change has become a partisan issue, so rather than offering a solution from their perspective, the right pictures climate change as an invented problem...that regulation goes against economic growth or it goes against competitiveness, that it's the aim of the left to control the means of production," Forner said.

In an interview with Brazilian media in August, Bolsonaro said he regarded extreme weather events to be cyclical and did not believe they were solely linked to human influence.

A Bolsonaro win is likely to see Brazil move away from its position as a climate leader, a role the country embraced last year as it hosted the COP30 climate conference in the Amazonian city of Belem.

But even a Lula victory would not represent a straightforward climate success story, say experts. While the current administration has overseen significant reductions in deforestation, it has also backed plans to expand oil production.

Angelo said the decision to allow exploratory drilling was a "betrayal" of Lula's mandate but added that the president's overall record on climate had been "quite impressive".

"Lula is the world's worst climate leader, except for all the others," he told DW.

The current president will face a runoff election with Bolsonaro on October 25. But while experts say the outcome could determine the fate of the Amazon and the wider efforts to curb global warming, for many Brazilians climate is not high on their list of priorities.

"Ultimately what people care about is access to education, sanitation and health," said Yang. A road built through the Amazon to access those services, while criticized from outside Brazil for destroying parts of the rainforest, would be desirable to voters, she added.

"They don't have time to care. Not because they don't want to. They care about their own survival," she said.

Lawmakers from Germany's Left Party turned their backs on a far-right AfD politician after he questioned the historical consensus that Germany had committed genocide in what is now Namibia. The protest came during a heated debate over reparations for descendants of the victims.

Germany and France reached a provisional agreement on Thursday that backs a scaling down of European emissions rules for new cars largely in line with the wishes of German carmakers, according to a draft document seen by news agencies.

The remaining 10% was to be covered by compensatory measures like low-carbon ​steel and alternative fuels.

Europe's car industry, led by Germany, has been struggling in recent years amid the switch to electric vehicles and competition from China. The draft agreement still needs official confirmation. Then it faces approval by Germany's governing coalition led by the Christian Democrats (CDU), who have pushed for more watered-down emissions regulations than their Social Democratic Party (SPD) partners.

Then Germany and France will still need to convince other EU members and the European Parliament for the agreement to take effect.

Alcohol bans came into force at all German train stations when the clock struck midnight and Thursday began.

All the roughly 5,700 stations across Germany, including the train platforms themselves, are now booze-free zones.

Pubs, restaurants and other such hospitality businesses at train stations are exceptions to the rule, though, and can still serve alcohol. Consumption of alcohol will also remain legal on many trains, with long distance ones still serving beers and other tipples at their on-board bistros.

Rail operator Deutsche Bahn says the change aims to make train stations safer and cleaner, and more pleasant places to spend time waiting for a (who knows, perhaps delayed) train.

Various misdemeanors and incidents had prompted discussions on alcohol at stations over the years, but two this year in particular brought the issues into sharp focus.

A 26-year-old security staff member for Deutsche Bahn was beaten and kicked by a drunken passenger when trying to check their ticket. During the struggle the train door buckled and opened, and the Bahn employee fell from the train, suffering severe injuries. Earlier in the year, a train conductor was killed when attacked by a man without a valid ticket.

The new rules are part of DB's house rules, rather than being criminal law — meaning that at least in the first instance it is a matter for Bahn security staff to enforce rather than police.

"The alcohol consumption ban applies at stations every day, also on days with major events," Bahn spokeswoman Anja Bröder said when asked about often beer-fueled train usage days, like when football fans flock to Bundesliga games.

Around 40 stations piloted the project for some time prior to its implementation, including some of the largest like Cologne, Munich, Hamburg and Berlin. DB says it has had positive experiences with the early experiments, reporting more peaceful stations with less litter and travelers and employees saying they preferred the atmosphere.

A federal group dedicated to helping the homeless has criticized the measure, however, saying that for homeless people with addiction illnesses, train stations often served as "important, weatherproof and comparatively safe places" to shelter. A spokeswoman also told the Rheinische Post newspaper that if they were driven from sites like train stations, it would be harder for social workers to locate them.

Thousands of German gamblers could sue for their money back

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

German gamblers may finally be able to beat the house, thanks to new court rulings in the EU and in Germany. The decisions could help gambling addicts — but the legal road might be difficult.

A German court has followed the European Court of Justice in finding that gamblers can seek compensation from gambling companies if they lost money when online casinos and fruit machines were illegal in Germany.

The verdicts could have major consequences for gambling addicts, as thousands of people could potentially bring civil lawsuits against gambling companies.

A series of court decisions at regional, federal, and European level have found in favor of gamblers in the last year, beginning with the Higher Regional Court Stuttgart in September 2025, which agreed with a plaintiff in the state of Baden-Württemberg who sued the Malta-based online betting company Tipico Games.

The plaintiff lost around €10,000 ($11,200) between 2020 and 2022, but because online gambling was prohibited in Germany until 2021, the court found that operators had therefore received the winnings without legal basis and must return them.

Tipico Games then appealed to the Federal Court of Justice (BGH), the highest civil and criminal court in Germany. In an initial verbal hearing in mid-September this year, the court followed the principle of the plaintiffs' case on one key point: That gamblers have the right to claim back losses if the companies did not have a license in Germany at the time the losses were incurred. But a final decision on the case is only expected to be made in January 2027.

Online casinos and fruit machines were banned in Germany from 2008 to 2021, but several gambling operators were active in the country because they had gaming licenses in other EU countries.

In April this year, the European Court of Justice ruled that EU law does not preclude a member state from "prohibiting certain services," or from "attaching civil-law consequences to that prohibition." In other words, even if a gambling company has a legitimate license in one member state, that doesn't override national gambling laws elsewhere.

Thomas Dünchheim, a business lawyer at global law firm Greenberg Traurig who specializes in gambling law, said law firms were now gathering clients — sometimes trawling the internet with the help of AI tools — to prepare massive collective lawsuits. Dünchheim himself has people who have lost tens of thousands of euros come to him seeking representation.

"The pendulum is swinging more and more towards the consumer. It's a bit like in America — we're seeing a mass phenomenon like the Dieselgate lawsuits against VW," he told DW. "And there are lawyers who will collect all these little cases, bundle them, and then maybe even drive it forward with the help of a legal financing firm. It's a good business for all involved, and the ones that will have to pay are the gambling firms who operated unlawful gambling in Germany."

The decisions could bring a measure of help for addicts, as online gambling can be particularly dangerous for people with addiction issues. According to Tobias Hayer, a psychologist at the Institute for Public Health and Nursing Research at Bremen University, that comes down to four main features of online gambling: Anonymity and the consequent lack of social control, 24/7 accessibility, the frictionless nature of cashless transactions, and the high frequency of games played.

"These court verdicts are not surprising at all," Hayer told DW, but he added that he had an ambivalent attitude to them. "On the one hand I can imagine that this will be a huge relief to many gamblers who had an addiction problem. On the other hand, if these people suddenly get a lot of money in their hands, but they haven't worked through their problems, then of course there's a danger of recidivism."

But Dünchheim suggested that the gamblers may not have such an easy ride when making their claims. It's true that German law has now concluded that if the gambling contract that the player makes with the company turns out to be unlawful in the country where they live, the player can claim their losses back. But only if the player themselves "did not act illegally," as Dünchheim put it.

"You also have to say: Many of these players deliberately went to games in this gray zone because the winnings are potentially bigger," he said. "Because the companies don't pay taxes."

"Then the defense team of the gambling company will of course say: 'You took part in an illegal game yourself, so you were acting illegally.' And this question of interpretation is not simple," he added. "It's a very interesting intellectual question, but it's also existential for the gambling companies."

The Tipico case is perhaps particularly interesting in the German context because, although the company is based in Malta, it was founded in 2004 by a group of German lawyers. The company is a big employer, with several thousand employees worldwide, and has been a sponsor of more than one top German football club. Tipico has also said that it has rigorous consumer protection measures in place to identify and prevent dangerous betting behavior.

But there was some legal ambiguity about its online operations in Germany up until 2021.

"They always had lawyers who could attest that what they are doing conforms to European law, because it is permitted in a European Union member state," said Dünchheim. "But that is a false assumption, as it has now been established that a gambling permit in another EU state doesn't apply in Germany, because unlike other areas, gambling law isn't harmonized across the bloc."

"Tipico was started by two businessmen and grew big in a gray zone," as Dünchheim put it. "They had professional lobbying firms on board and they hired experienced lawyers, they had private equity firms on board. But of course they are carrying a huge financial risk."

AI-assisted dying? Doris Dörrie's new film addresses prospect of care robots

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

While the world debates the dangers of artificial intelligence, filmmaker Doris Dörrie explores how humanoid robots could change the way we deal with aging, caregiving and how people choose to die.

Hamburg in the near future: Instead of human drivers, robots deliver parcels. In cafes, robots roll between the tables, serving diners. On the streets, one encounters humanoid robots walking dogs — or the other way around, people taking their robot dogs for a walk.

This is the setting of Doris Dörrie's new film, "Frau Winkler verlässt das Haus" ("Mrs. Winkler Leaves the House"). At the core of the plot is an elderly woman who can no longer live on her own after a fall. With her only daughter unable to provide permanent care, the solution seems simple enough: a care robot with a built-in EOL program, or "End of Life" mode. This robot not only assists with everyday life but can also provide assisted dying under certain conditions, namely when the person explicitly wishes to end their life and meets the relevant medical criteria.

Dörrie's film tackles several major social issues with remarkable lightness. These include the challenges facing aging societies such as Germany, Japan and China, the question of dying on one's own terms and a mother-daughter relationship that raises questions of guilt and responsibility. Even though the story is set in Germany, such issues resonate globally.

"I write about what personally moves and interests me," Dörrie told DW.

One of the best-known contemporary German film directors, Dörrie achieved her breakthrough in 1985 with the comedy "Men…" She gained further international recognition with films such as 2008's "Cherry Blossoms" and "Greetings from Fukushima" (2016).

Assisted suicide is one of the most complex ethical questions of our time. Laws vary around the world. Though some countries permit active euthanasia under certain conditions, others prohibit and criminalize all forms of assisted dying.

In her film, Dörrie asks what happens when artificial intelligence begins calculating the cost of caring for an elderly person and weighing whether that cost is still "worth" bearing for society.

The underlying idea isn't new, Dörrie said, pointing to the Japanese legend of Narayama, which is also mentioned in the film. According to the legend, the eldest children were responsible for taking their ageing parents to a mountain and leaving them there to die because there was not enough food for everyone. "And that's also the debate we are currently facing to a certain extent: Can we really afford to let older people live longer and longer?"

In Dörrie's film, elderly people are allowed to make their own decisions once they reach a certain state of health — but they can't change their minds afterwards. Yet what does it really mean to make a decision freely? "It's a tricky situation," says Dörrie, because the question is to what extent one is also pressured into making this decision, "not only because it makes more economic sense, but also because one is no longer a burden on one's loved ones — in this case, one's daughter..."

The film often raises these questions indirectly and with plenty of dark humor. At one point, a care adviser presents the EOL feature to Mrs. Winkler as an attractive and, above all, straightforward additional option, making it sound as though there would be nothing more for her to worry about.

Dörrie is not interested in demonizing AI in her film — quite the opposite. In the film, the care robot, which Mrs. Winkler names Pavlovich, helps the elderly woman rediscover some of her zest for life and get out of the house.

"When you travel through Germany, you see small houses everywhere, and statistics show that an elderly person lives alone in every other house," Dörrie said. "If AI can help in these circumstances, why not use it?" She said she sought to explore this ambiguity in her film. Dividing things into black and white would neither be interesting nor, in her view, accurate.

Dörrie and her crew were surprised during filming by how quickly people can develop emotional bonds with robots. She attributes this to the human brain. "We regard anything that moves and speaks as a living being. We can't help it," she explains. And that is precisely what we need to pay attention to when interacting with AI, Dörrie says. "How often are we truly aware that we are talking to a machine rather than a living being? These are things we still have to learn."

Resignation of Bärbel Bas shines light on harsh era of German politics

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

German Labor Minister Bärbel Bas has stepped down, citing her need to grieve a loved one and leave behind the harsh realities of government. So how did German politics get so heated?

For a brief moment, a sense of quiet sympathy could be felt in Berlin's often unforgiving political arena: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas, Germany's labor minister and the co-leader of the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) announced her resignation from all government and party positions. The 58-year-old politician cited a personal tragedy as the reason for her decision: "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I have come to realize that I need time to work through my grief," Bas wrote in a statement.

Bas also wrote that sustained criticism from the conservative governing parties, the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU), had contributed to her decision to step down. In her statement, she wrote that she had been forced to "conclude that public attacks on me, unfortunately also from within the ranks of our coalition partner, continued to intensify during this personally difficult time."

The labor minister had recently been responsible for implementing complex changes to Germany's public pension system — including increasing the retirement age — and had been urged by some conservative politicians, in rather harsh terms, not to delay the project any further.

According to a report in the Süddeutsche Zeitung newspaper, one remark in particular appears to have weighed heavily on her: During an off-the-record briefing with journalists, Chancellor Friedrich Merz, of the CDU, reportedly criticized the pace of her efforts despite knowing that she was working through a personal tragedy.

Did Bas feel so overwhelmed by pressures both personal and political that she chose to step down rather than continue to endure them?

Mirco Liefke, a researcher with the Institute for Media and Communication Studies in the Department of Political and Social Sciences at the Freie Universität Berlin, told DW that unfairness and harshness have always been part of politics. However, he added: "What I do think is new is the potential for escalation and the speed that social media have brought to public debate. Along with that has come an incentive for individual actors to adopt ever more radical positions."

There are few public spaces left in which politicians can speak with the expectation that their remarks will not be reported. Merz is said to have made critical remarks of Bas during an off-the-record briefing whose contents were not intended for publication.

Though he was fully aware of her personal circumstances, Merz reportedly said Bas was barely visible in public and failing to put forward proposals of her own.

"It has effectively become a business model to leak information that is meant to remain confidential and thereby generate attention," Liefke said.

A spokesperson for Merz declined to confirm or deny the report.

Felix Banaszak, the co-leader of Germany's center-left Greens, has known Bas fior years, with both hailing from Duisburg, in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia, albeit born a couple of decades apart.

"Toughness and ruthlessness are nothing new in politics," Banaszak told DW. "But we still need to find a different way of doing things if we do not want to keep wearing people down."

Banaszak said the SPD was partially responsible for the treatment of Bas. "The fact that Bärbel Bas encountered so little understanding for her personal situation from within the ranks of the coalition partner, and that apparently no one in the SPD was willing to step forward, defend her, and take her out of the line of fire, is simply not a good sign," he said.

"It would be naive to pretend that the attacks on a man would have unfolded in a similar way," he added.

Liefke offered a similar view: "Women politicians are subjected to an extraordinary degree of misogynistic and sexualized abuse — and they face a level of public exposure that is entirely new."

The debate over how Bas and her grief should have been treated soon took on some of the same characteristics as the attacks that had preceded her resignation. Manuela Schwesig, the SPD state premier of the northeastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, described the attacks by the CDU/CSU as "inhuman," drawing sharp criticism from many conservative politicians. Merz publicly expressed his regret over the labor minister's resignation and wished her all the best.

Liefke said this was another tremnd in German politics. Once a politician decides to step down, he argues, public expressions of concern are often far more intense than they used to be and come from all sides: "This is a facade of concern," he said. "There is something ritualistic, almost reflexive, about it. People react this way because they are following another major trend: Emotionalization."

The attacks on Bas are just one example of the increasingly harsh tone of political debate. Formal reprimands in the Bundestag have risen sharply, particularly since the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) entered parliament in 2017. High-profile resignations have also made headlines, including that of Kevin Kühnert, the SPD's former secretary-general.

Kühnert stepped down in 2024. In April 2025, however, he told Die Zeit newspaper that he had felt constantly threatened by far-right extremists. Political debate, he said, is showing "an increasing lack of respect for the views of those who think differently."

Liefke said politicians once managed to maintain two distinct modes of communication: one public and one private. "When you think of earlier generations of politicians, what comes to mind is the distance they maintained, the relative lack of emotional display," he said. "Take Helmut Schmidt, for example. He did not openly grieve over things that came crashing down on him. At least, he did not show it publicly." Schmidt, of the SPD, served as chancellor from 1974 to 1982.

"Today, politicians such as Bärbel Bas benefit from drawing on their own life stories when they say, 'I come from a working-class background and worked my way up,'" Liefke said. "That can work to their advantage — but it also makes them far more visible, and far more vulnerable, as individuals."

Bas, who served as president of the Bundestag from 2021 to 2025, intends to retain her parliamentary seat. Life is likely to become quieter for her now as other politicians move into the spotlight, both in public and, perhaps, in private as well.

Formula One in Rwanda: Sportswashing claims vs. hopes for African legacy

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Formula One will return to Africa for the first time since 1993. There is concern Rwanda are using it as another sportswashing opportunity, but there is also hope the event's legacy can impact wider African society.

Rwanda's sports portfolio has grown after confirming it will host Formula One races from the early 2030s. The track is to be built in Bugesera near Kigali International Airport, which Qatar Airways, an F1 sponsor, owns 60% of.

"To be an African and to see it happening in real time, for me in my lifetime because Kyalami [the track in South Africa where F1 was last hosted in Africa] was before I was born, I'm more than happy to have it on the continent," South African presenter and content creator Nqobile Khwezi told Sky Sports.

"To be able to give that taste back to the rest of the world and the audience of Formula One would be an absolute delight."

F1 have also highlighted the benefits for the host nation.

"Through this race we have the opportunity to inspire and bring the passion of F1 to our more than 100 million fans across Africa and firmly believe that sport can be a positive force for good," an F1 spokesperson told The Athletic.

DW contacted Rwanda's Ministry of Sports for comment, but did not receive a promised reply.

For the country's President, Paul Kagame, another move into sport is hardly a surprise given his ventures into European football and the NBA in recent years. Rwanda even sponsored Bayern Munich, but fan and media backlash over the deal caused the Bundesliga club to drop the commercial element with the country's tourist arm, Visit Rwanda, last year.

With many critics accusing Kagame and Rwanda of sportswashing, the arrival of F1, a sport valued at approximately $34 billion (€30 billion), according to sports business website Sportico, brings fresh questions.

"This deal may have positive economic and social impacts across Africa, but we should be realistic about circumstances on the ground in Rwanda," Lewis Mudge, Central Africa director at Human Rights Watch, told DW.

"Human Rights Watch wrote to Formula One in February 2025 to ask how it is addressing the human rights impacts of its activities, including regarding Rwanda and other countries’ bid to host a F1 Grand Prix. We did not receive a response," said Mudge, adding that F1 should only approve racing in the country after addressing the human rights impacts of hosting a race there.

"Rwanda, like other countries, has also used high-profile sports deals and races in the past to distract from its abysmal human rights record. Rwanda is currently the power behind the abusive M23 rebel group in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo and its military forces have been implicated in unlawful displacement and deaths of civilians in violation of international humanitarian law."

For Mohamed Keita, returning the sport to Africa is overdue, but the choice of country is telling.

"The fact that F1 excluded the world's second largest continent from its race series for three decades is indefensible in its own right and is actually a stain on a sport which claims to be global," Keita, Senior Policy Officer, Africa, at the Human Rights Foundation told DW.

"The choice of Rwanda, however, is deeply controversial," Keita continued. "Rwanda is a brutal dictatorship. Its regime is under partial US sanctions for its military occupations of territories of eastern DRC. Rwanda is arming and financing a militia involved in rapes, massacres, forced recruitment and violent plunder of natural resources."

Formula One has history on the continent. In 1958, there was a race in Morocco and more recently, in South Africa, where the last F1 race in Africa took place at the Kyalami racetrack in Johannesburg in 1993. Many previous races in the country were overshadowed by the policy of apartheid.

Despite Rwanda securing the deal first, South Africa believes it will host before them. When Rwanda announced their hosting, South Africa's President Cyril Ramaphosa sent his congratulations in a social media post, adding: "This contract is a recognition of the capabilities of the Rwandan nation and of our continent more broadly."

South Africa's Sports Minister Gayton McKenzie also congratulated Rwanda, but pivotally added that South Africa "will get F1, even before 2030."

There is no denying that hosting a race in South Africa would cost significantly less time and money, given the existing infrastructure.

In either country, comes a legacy opportunity. Sports such as Formula One, where the majority of decision-makers, drivers, team principles and engineers are white men, are a living example of the unlevel playing field. The hope of racing in Africa is that it will go some way to changing that.

"I think that's the kind of aspirational drive of hosting a Formula 1 race. It's leveraging STEM [science, technology, engineering, and mathematics] education, technology, aspiring young girls or women, leveling the playing fields, if it's for all those other reasons and we can make it work then I say yes to Formula 1 in Africa," Dr. Kamilla Swart-Arries, an expert in South Africa's events industry and the legacy of sports events, told DW in 2024.

"I hope F1 will consider further investment in developing African talent in motorsport," said Keita. "There's a lot of good that F1 can do if it genuinely wants to grow the sport in Africa, but Rwanda does not make sense at this moment unless you're doing it purely for the benefit of one dictator and the beauty of the hilly terrain."

"The proposition here is to take the world's most expensive sport to Rwanda, which is one of the world's poorest countries. Rwanda is consistently listed among the UN's list of the 25 least developed countries in the world," said Keita.

The return of the fastest show in sports is a huge moment for both Rwanda and Africa. It raises questions about President Kagame's use of sport, but it also offers an opportunity to shape the event's legacy. Unlike the race Rwanda wants to host, this is not about being the fastest but about making the most of the chance.

At an international AI summit in Dublin, EU leaders promoted trusted and independent artificial intelligence. Critics question whether the bloc can close the gap with the US and China while maintaining strict regulation.

However, he decried the fact that the European Union controls little of the value chain.

"Being cut off from AI, once the economy runs on it, would be more like being cut off from the US financial system," he wrote in an opinion piece for the Financial Times. "The effects would be catastrophic."

Draghi's spirit was on full display in Dublin on Wednesday at an international AI summit the Irish government convened as part of its six-month stint hosting the rotating EU presidency.

The summit's motto, "Harnessing AI to Revolutionize Europe's Competitiveness," might as well have been dreamed up by the Italian former prime minister.
