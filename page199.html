George Lucas's Skywalker Grill Reminds Me of Applebee's in the Best Way

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Skywalker Grill sits on the top floor of the new Lucas Museum of Narrative Art, a project that cost over $1 billion to build. With its plush interior and sweeping views of the Los Angeles skyline, it seems like the kind of place that would serve fussy little plates with foams, sauce squiggles, and $500 tasting menus. Instead of any special effects, the restaurant features a menu of crowd-pleasers.

Among the undulating walls (there are zero right angles), soaring ceilings, and completely custom furniture, diners dig into familiar favorites like spinach and artichoke dip and grilled salmon. Of course, the ruffled potato chips that arrive with the dip are made in-house, and the fish is lacquered with a honey miso glaze. Imagine Applebee’s had a cousin that grew up in Bel-Air and insisted on only the best ingredients.

Legendary filmmaker George Lucas and business executive Mellody Hobson, his wife, founded the museum with the ethos that everyone should feel welcome. It’s why many spaces at the Lucas Museum are open to the public without needing a ticket. Entry to the museum is free for people under 17 and anyone who lives in the same zip code, and the lobby cafe has a $2 grilled cheese. The dress code on Skywalker Grill’s Resy page says it all: “Come as you are. Stay for the experience.”

Diego Cardoso, the culinary director for the museum, developed a menu that nails the balance of high-low. Chicken tenders are served with a generous ramekin of caviar-topped ranch. The pigs in a blanket are the best I've ever had—snappy Wagyu beef hot dogs wrapped in the most buttery puff pastry, sprinkled with everything-bagel seasoning, and served with a truly exceptional grainy mustard—also made on-site.

Wagyu beef pigs in a blanket show off Skywalker Grill's approach to homey hors d’oeuvres.

Every leaf of the Caesar salad is perfectly coated in a garlicky dressing. The sizable chicken milanese is crispy and golden. And the mac and cheese (made with shells, the ideal pasta shape) is so creamy it deserves to be promoted from a side to a main. A chicken and wild mushroom crepe is an ode to a Lucas favorite from a long-shuttered chain called the Magic Pan.

Not every dish is a hit: a vegetarian pasta that featured patty pan squash and zucchini was reminiscent of plane food, and a side of brussels sprouts arrived limp and soggy.

Those looking for something spendier can order a $35 blue crab cake, a $52 lobster salad, and a $38 Wagyu burger made with grass-fed beef raised on Lucas’s Skywalker Ranch. Most items still manage to feel like a value given the generous portion sizes for the price point and many tables around me had leftovers boxed up to take home.

Shells go into a standout mac and cheese.

Since it opened in late September, Skywalker Grill has remained a tough table to get. The highly anticipated museum is already popular among locals and tourists, and the restaurant is no exception. According to its Resy page, there are zero tables for two available for the rest of the year.

Skywalker Grill is part of a growing trend of museum restaurants that are as much of a draw as the institutions that house them. In LA alone, the Hammer Museum is home to Lulu from David Tanis and Alice Waters. Fanny’s, in the Academy Museum of Motion Pictures, is a scene-y 10,000 square feet. LACMA has a café from Erewhon.

In its mission to be one of the most welcoming restaurants, Skywalker Grill is definitely one of the most comfortable. Velvet chairs feature sturdy back support and plush cushions, and sleek black marble tables are spaced out so that it’s nearly impossible to hear another party’s conversation. The wave-like architecture of the banquets means each nook of the restaurant feels private. The extensive use of cherrywood, renowned for its durable patina, speaks to Lucas’s desire to build a place that will outlive him.

The ruffled potato chips are made on-site.

The restaurant has been busy, but it doesn’t feel packed. Thanks to felt under the tables and the liberal use of an acoustic plaster that absorbs sound, it’s easy to forget that there’s a rambunctious toddler two tables over treating the space as his personal jungle gym.

While Skywalker Grill isn’t Star Wars themed, there are subtle nods to the franchise throughout. The curved bar, constructed from cast-glass beams covered in silver leaf, takes on an intergalactic glow when illuminated at night. In the private dining room, the chandelier is modeled after a specific dust cloud in space. The more obvious tributes come from diners who proudly show up in their most prized Star Wars shirts.

For all the cosmic flourishes, the restaurant’s greatest trick is unpretentious cooking: the room may transport you to another galaxy, but the food is down-to-earth.

Homebuilder sentiment is at a 14-year low

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI boom is sucking up all the capital. The Federal Reserve is raising rates to try to cool inflation. And one sector is bearing the brunt of the pain from both: housing.

The big picture: The situation is lose-lose for both homebuilders and homebuyers. Builders are facing more expensive capital and bidding for scarce construction-related resources. Would-be buyers are being quoted some of the highest mortgage rates of this century.

  • New September housing starts data out Tuesday morning puts an exclamation point on what has been a slow-moving disaster for a sector that plays an outsized role in the economic cycle and in Americans' basic living standards.

By the numbers: The number of housing units started by builders fell 5.2% last month, the Census Bureau said, to 1.22 million. It's down 7.4% year over year and near post-pandemic lows.

  • Starts of single-family homes fell 4.6% to their lowest level since March 2023.
  • Pending home sales fell 7.3% last month, the National Association of Realtors said Tuesday morning, to the lowest since the data was tracked going back to 2001.
  • Homebuilder sentiment fell this month to its lowest level since 2012, the National Association of Home Builders said Monday.

State of play: The average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage rate was 7.4% earlier this month, Freddie Mac said in its most recent weekly reading. The rate was briefly higher than that in 2023, but has not been consistently above that level since the fall of 2000.

  • That has kept homebuyers on the sidelines — while a parallel rise in borrowing costs for developers has made it harder for new projects to pencil out.
  • In effect, the bottomless demand for capital from AI hyperscalers and the U.S. government has to squeeze something, and that something has been the housing sector.
  • Residential investment has subtracted from overall GDP for five of the last six quarters.

Zoom out: Housing affordability is the crux of Americans' broader dissatisfaction with economic conditions, and all signs point to it getting worse, not better.

  • For all the bipartisan emphasis on increasing housing supply — most prominently in the passage of a law to encourage more building earlier this year — conditions are weighted against a building boom.
  • Housing developers are competing with data center builders for labor and materials like copper, concrete and HVAC equipment.

Of note: Restrictionist immigration policy has also crimped the supply of construction workers.

  • "Nationwide, more than one-third of builders reported immigration enforcement activity in their market areas during the past month, causing construction delays and discouraging legal workers from reporting to work," said NAHB chairman Bill Owens, an Ohio homebuilder, in the sentiment release.

Between the lines: An unfortunate side effect of relying on Fed interest rate policy for macroeconomic stabilization is that interest-sensitive sectors, particularly housing, take it on the chin when the economy shows signs of overheating.

  • It's particularly problematic now, when persistent undersupply of housing is a defining economic challenge of our age.

The bottom line: The current alignment of interest rates, housing supply and construction costs isn't making anybody happy.

  • Builders can't afford to build, buyers can't afford to buy, and even the people sitting pretty with pre-2022 mortgages can't afford to move.

The Terrifying Future of States’ Criminalizing Pregnant People Is Already Here

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The idea of charging people who have abortions with murder is gaining traction on the right. But a report finds that people are already being prosecuted for their pregnancy outcomes.

Anti-abortion rights demonstrators march to the Supreme Court for the 52nd annual March For Life in Washington, DC, on January 24, 2025.

Since the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade in 2022, the idea of charging people who have abortions with murder has gained terrifying new traction. During her brief stint as White House press secretary, Katie Zacharia became just the latest prominent conservative to draw attention for her ties to a radical wing of the anti-abortion movement that wants to charge people who have abortions with murder. This year alone, state lawmakers floated 14 bills to charge people who have abortions with homicide. (The idea is still so unpopular across the political spectrum that none of those bills passed.) Yet this whole debate is, in a sense, a red herring, because hundreds of people a year are already being prosecuted for their pregnancy outcomes. The vast majority of those cases involved a live birth where the mother was suspected of using drugs, but in a rising minority of cases since Dobbs, people are also being criminalized for pregnancy loss.

I spoke with Dana Sussman, senior vice president at Pregnancy Justice, about a report the organization published today finding that in the first three years after the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, prosecutors have initiated at least 902 cases across the country charging individuals with crimes related to pregnancy, pregnancy loss, or birth.

I’ve edited our conversation for brevity and clarity.

Amy Littlefield: What’s new in this report, which looks at pregnancy criminalization in the first three years after Dobbs?

Dana Sussman: When Roe was overturned, we suspected that pregnancy loss would become an increasingly criminally suspect event, and we unfortunately are starting to see that play out in the data. This report finds a significant acceleration in pregnancy loss prosecutions. We now have documented 70 pregnancy loss prosecutions in the first three years post-Dobbs.

AL: I was struck by the breakdown of allegations prosecutors made in the cases you found. There were 871 allegations of substance use during pregnancy, 77 allegations of a lack of prenatal care, and just 13 related to obtaining, attempting or researching an abortion. What does this tell us about who is being criminalized for pregnancy post-Dobbs?

DS: This is the continuation of a trend that we’ve been seeing for decades. The way that pregnancy is policed and prosecuted in the United States is through the lens of substance use. The foundation for prosecuting pregnancy is based in the war on drugs from the 1980s, and the way in which Black women, in particular, were targeted for charges of child abuse when they were deemed substance users, primarily with crack cocaine and this obsession over a “crack baby epidemic” that has been thoroughly debunked. Three state supreme court decisions used pregnancy and substance use as the way to redefine who a person is [thereby extending child abuse protections to fetuses] under their state laws: Alabama, South Carolina, and Oklahoma, which consistently rank among the top three states that criminalize pregnancy.

AL: What did you find about the race and socioeconomic background of the folks who were criminalized for their pregnancy outcomes in this report?

DS: In the ’80s, ’90s, and early 2000s, we were seeing disproportionately Black women being charged with crimes in connection with pregnancy, and that has shifted since the mid-2000s until the present. The majority are white women at this point, but poverty is a very big indicator, as it is across the criminal justice system. So we’re looking at poor white women as the majority in the data set at this point. There are a few different theories from scholars about why this is. [Legal scholar] Michele Goodwin has said that Black women were the canary in the coal mine; cruelty by the state is tried out on Black women, and then it spreads more broadly. There’s also a lot of scholarship showing that when a victim is white, the perpetrator of the crime is treated more harshly, and so we are talking about the assumption that these are white babies, and so they are held in higher esteem, frankly, than non-white babies, and so their mothers are treated more harshly, potentially. There’s also shifting patterns in policing around nonviolent drug crimes; we see less of it in city centers and more of it in rural areas, and also the patterns of drug use, as well.

AL: So it’s mostly low-income white women in a small number of states. I’m looking at the map: 381 of the cases in South Carolina, 222 in Oklahoma, 204 in Alabama, and then there’s states like my home state of Massachusetts where there’s zero.

DS: Each one of those three states has proclaimed by state supreme court decision that fetuses are children under the child abuse laws. In South Carolina, it was in the late ’90s; in Alabama, it was 2013; and in Oklahoma, it was 2020. So that kind of unleashed permission for prosecutors across the state to apply laws in this way. In other states, we see prosecutors doing this, but those cases have not yet gone to the state supreme court. In Mississippi, for example, the state has no fetal personhood law or judicial decision, and yet we see 27 cases in that state of prosecutors just choosing to reinterpret laws that were never intended to be applied in this way.

AL: So what impact did Dobbs actually have on these types of prosecutions, and is Dobbs really the culprit here, or are there other, more long-standing forces at play?

DS: Two things are true here. One is that the foundation for these prosecutions was laid way before Dobbs, and it’s a continuation of a decades-long trend. Fetal personhood is at the root of these cases, and fetal personhood has existed in state law or in state judicial decisions since the 1990s. That is, ultimately, the anti-abortion movement’s goal. It wasn’t the overturning of Roe. It wasn’t sending issues back to the states. It is the federalization of fetal and embryonic personhood, because that would ban abortion without question, without exception, and it would allow for increased criminalization of people who are not performing pregnancy in the way that the powers that be might want them to. At the same time, we suspect that we are seeing more pregnancy loss cases in our data year upon year post-Dobbs, and I don’t think that should be a surprise, either. If we ban abortion and we make pregnancy loss suspicious because in some places provision of abortion has been made illegal, then we are creating an environment in which we are inviting law enforcement in to investigate how some of these pregnancies ended. That criminal response varies depending on one’s identity, one’s behavior, and one’s access to healthcare.

AL: After the Supreme Court decision in Dobbs in 2022, there were predictions that we would see a mass number of criminal cases related to abortion, because all of a sudden providing abortion was a crime in about a dozen states. How does the reality compare to those expectations?

DS: We are seeing what we expected: that this was going to fall most harshly on people who use substances during pregnancy and were going to be charged with child abuse or neglect, and that pregnancy loss would be an increasingly suspect event. I remember getting lots of questions about period-tracking apps and wiping that from your phone, and I was like, if they want to criminalize pregnancy, they have other ways to do it, and they’ve been doing it. This is not going to be some new dragnet, it’s going to be the same old ways that they’ve been doing it for a very long time, and that has played out. And the circumstances are often through healthcare, that is, the people who are reporting to law enforcement or child welfare [are often healthcare providers]. That was the case before Dobbs, and it remains the case now.

AL: Katie Zacharia recently made headlines for the fact that she was on the board of White Rose Resistance, which is part of an extreme wing of the anti-abortion movement that has called for “equal protection” laws that would treat embryos as people and abortions as murder. I’m wondering if you could reflect on the fears generated by these efforts versus what the data already show about criminalization?

DS: There are 17 states that redefine who a person is to include an embryo or a fetus right now. So this movement to provide “equal protection” to embryos and fetuses already exists in a slightly different form under many state laws.

AL: What would you tell someone who lives in a state where abortion is banned, and is ordering abortion pills to self-manage an abortion?

DS: These are an incredibly small number of cases [criminalizing self-managed abortion in a ban state] when it comes to how many abortions have happened post-Dobbs. You can still access abortion safely and confidentially, and if you are experiencing medical concerns around abortion care, you can call the Miscarriage and Abortion Hotline. You can get care that you need and you do not need to say anything about abortion. People should keep their community of people who know as small and as trusted as possible, because the most common scenario [triggering criminal prosecution] is getting reported by other people.

Amy Littlefield is The Nation’s abortion access correspondent and a journalist who focuses on reproductive rights, healthcare, and religion. Her latest book is Killers of Roe: My Investigation Into the Mysterious Death of Abortion Rights (Hachette).

Kann die EU mit ihrer KI-Agenda die USA und China einholen?

Auf einem KI-Gipfel in Dublin setzen sich die Top-Entscheider der EU für eine vertrauenswürdige und unabhängige künstliche Intelligenz ein. Kritiker bezweifeln, dass die EU so zu den USA und China aufschließen kann.

Wäre die Vorherrschaft im Bereich der künstlichen Intelligenz eine olympische Disziplin, gäbe es nach dem derzeitigen Stand der Dinge nur zwei ernsthafte Anwärter auf die Goldmedaille: die USA und China. Die Europäische Union spielt, wie schon in vielen Bereichen der Spitzentechnologie in jüngster Zeit, in der KI-Debatte kaum eine Rolle.

Der ehemalige Präsident der Europäischen Zentralbank, Mario Draghi, der in seinem Bericht im Jahr 2024 forderte, dass die EU innovativer werden muss, um wettbewerbsfähiger zu werden, sagte kürzlich, KI sei "vielleicht der vielversprechendste" Weg, den die EU heute einschlagen könne, um das Wachstum anzukurbeln. Er kritisierte jedoch, dass die Europäische Union nur einen geringen Teil der Wertschöpfungskette kontrolliert.

"Von der KI abgeschnitten zu sein, sobald die Wirtschaft darauf basiert, käme eher einer Abkopplung vom US-Finanzsystem gleich", schrieb er in einem Gastbeitrag für die Financial Times. "Die Auswirkungen wären katastrophal."

Draghis Haltung kam vergangenen Mittwoch in Dublin bei einem internationalen KI-Gipfel deutlich zum Ausdruck, den die irische Regierung im Rahmen ihrer sechsmonatigen EU-Ratspräsidentschaft einberufen hatte. Das Motto des Gipfels, "KI nutzen, um Europas Wettbewerbsfähigkeit zu revolutionieren", hätte genauso gut vom ehemaligen italienischen Ministerpräsidenten stammen können.

Der Umgang mit KI ist deutlich komplizierter geworden. Eine ganze Reihe von Kontroversen rund um außer Kontrolle geratene KI-Agenten haben für Alarm gesorgt: vom "Hugging Face"-Angriff im Juni, bei dem 1200 Agenten eine Online-Plattform hackten, bis hin zu einem von Anthropic entwickelten Agenten, der der US-Polizei einen falschen Hinweis zu einem ungelösten Mordfall schickte.

Viele Vertreter der Tech-Branche, darunter Microsoft-Gründer Bill Gates und die führenden KI-Forschungslabore selbst, haben eine Entwicklungspause gefordert, damit notwendige Sicherheitsmaßnahmen angepasst werden können.

Trotzdem war die Stimmung auf dem Gipfel weitgehend positiv: Führende Persönlichkeiten aus der Tech- und KI-Branche besprachen gemeinsam mit irischen und EU-Politikern das wirtschaftliche und gesellschaftliche Potenzial der KI. Sie betonten dabei auch die Notwendigkeit strenger Regulierung und Sicherheitsstandards.

Der irische Premierminister Micheal Martin machte deutlich, dass Irland voll und ganz auf KI setze. Er schloss sich der jüngsten Aussage von EU-Kommissions-Chefin Ursula von der Leyen an, die die Technologie als "Wendepunkt" sieht. Wiederholt betonte der irische Regierungschef, wie wichtig der EU dabei der Sicherheitsaspekt ist.

This pastina soup recipe is the best kind of comfort food. Filled with tiny pasta (pastina, in Italian) and tender vegetables, it’s cozy and flavorful, but nourishing too. On a chilly fall or winter day, it’s a delicious way to warm up.

Pastina soup is sometimes called Italian penicillin. You can think of it like chicken noodle soup’s Italian cousin, a perfect soothing meal for days when you’re under the weather. Most versions call for chicken or at least chicken broth, but I wanted to develop a vegetarian take to have in my back pocket during cold season.

After three rounds of testing to get the ratio of noodles to veggies and broth just right, I couldn’t be happier with this pastina soup. It’s simple to make, bright with lemon, and filled with aromatic flavor from bay leaves, parsley, and thyme. I hope you love it as much as I do!

Pastina means “little pasta” in Italian. It doesn’t refer to one specific pasta shape, but rather any very small pasta (less than 1/4 inch in size is a good rule of thumb). Some common types of pastina are stelline (tiny star-shaped pasta), acini di pepe (little pearls), and ditalini (small tubes). Any of these shapes will work nicely in this soup!

This pastina soup recipe has four main steps:

1. Sauté the onions, carrots, and celery until they soften, 8 to 10 minutes. Stir in the garlic, then add the thyme, parsley stems, bay leaves, Parmesan rind, and broth.

Countries have failed – for the sixth time running – to agree on the timeline for publishing the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s (IPCC) next set of influential reports.

The IPCC’s 65th session (IPCC-65) was seen by many to be the final opportunity for countries to agree on a schedule where its seventh assessment report (AR7) would be published in time for the UN’s second “global stocktake” of climate action.

Before last week’s meeting in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, IPCC chair Prof Sir Jim Skea had warned that releasing the reports in time for the stocktake in 2028 would depend on a timeline being agreed by the end of 2026.

But the week-long meeting descended into fraught disagreements between governments, resulting in Skea “suspending” the talks on Friday evening with no decision made.

Countries were also unable to agree on the IPCC’s budget for the rest of the AR7 cycle, leaving the body with a “serious funding crisis”.

The IPCC has pledged to reconvene the meeting at the “earliest opportunity”; however, it has not yet given an indication of when this will be.

In his concluding remarks, Skea described the Addis Ababa summit as a “profound disappointment” and “among the most difficult IPCC meetings”, according to reporting from the Earth Negotiations Bulletin (ENB).

Some countries have reacted with dismay to the breakdown of negotiations, warning that a failure to have the IPCC’s latest assessments on climate adaptation and mitigation ready for the UN stocktaking exercise would undermine global climate action.

A climate envoy from a Pacific island state said it was a “travesty that a handful of countries continue to delay the IPCC’s reports – the science we need back home to help us save lives and livelihoods”.Below, Carbon Brief explores the key developments, debates and disagreements at the IPCC meeting.

Top of the agenda during the Addis Ababa meeting was a decision on the timeline of the publication of AR7’s three working group reports – also known as the “work plan”. The three reports cover climate science (WG1), impacts and adaptation (WG2) and mitigation (WG3).

Countries failed to reach a resolution on the scheduling of the influential reports at five consecutive IPCC meetings since 2024 – despite drafting being well underway.

(For more, see Carbon Brief’s write-up of discussions in Istanbul, Sofia, Hangzhou, Lima and Bangkok.)

Reporting from the ENB from inside these meetings has highlighted that countries are split, broadly, into two groups – those that want all three reports to be published in time for the UN’s second “global stocktake” in 2028, and those that one, or more, to be published afterwards.

A key pillar of the Paris Agreement, the global stocktake is a formal assessment of the world’s collective progress on tackling climate change. The exercise is designed to guide future global climate action, including decisions on national emissions targets and climate finance.

The second global stocktake is due to culminate in late 2028 at the COP33 climate summit.

The Addis Ababa meeting was the final IPCC plenary where countries would be able to agree on a stocktake-aligned timeline. In April, Skea told Carbon Brief that publishing reports by the end of 2028 would only be possible if a decision was taken before the end of 2026.

India, Kenya, Russia and Saudi Arabia have emerged as among the loudest voices calling for a timeline where the IPCC’s latest assessment of climate impacts and mitigation are published after the stocktake. They have argued that developing countries need more time to prepare, review and approve working group reports.

A coalition of developed nations and small-island developing states, meanwhile, have argued in favour of all three reports being published in time for the stocktake, so that the latest climate science can inform future climate action.

(For more on the standoff around the reports and why it matters for climate action, read Carbon Brief’s explainer.)

Over several days of fraught talks in Addis Ababa, the number of timeline options in contention ballooned to seven – before being reduced down to four.

On Monday, Skea established a “contact group”, chaired by Brazil and Ireland, to focus on the timeline issue.

By Friday morning, the group reported back to the plenary that they were still a “long way from consensus”, according to the ENB.

By that afternoon, four options still remained – the three set out by the IPCC ahead of the meeting and a fourth, proposed by Haiti, which would see WG1 approval in April 2028, WG2 in July 2028 and WG3 in October 2028.

After one delegation said they were uncertain how to engage with the draft text, Skea clarified the text had three elements – a preamble, timeline and a section on inclusivity and avenues for providing input.

He said that “it was clear the panel would not be able to take a decision on the timeline at IPCC-65” and explained the idea was to include the four timeline options that emerged from the contact group in an annex “in order to capture progress made during IPCC-65”.

After countries were not able to agree on the draft decision, Skea suspended the meeting at 8:29pm on Friday.

He noted that it would no longer be inclusive for the meeting to continue, given that a number of delegations from developing countries had left.

Earlier in the plenary, Antigua and Barbuda’s delegation had emphasised that 6pm was its deadline and noted that representatives of other small-island developing states had departed. Nepal’s negotiating team had also stated it was leaving.

The ENB’s report details how the five-day meet was beset by disagreements between country delegations on the timeline issue.

As proceedings kicked off on Monday, a significant number of countries set out their position on whether publication of the reports should occur before, or after, the global stocktake.

Both camps cited inclusivity concerns. Those in favour of a stocktake-aligned timeline said that “inclusivity is a priority and should be addressed, but said delaying delivery of the report would further disadvantage the countries most vulnerable to climate change”, according to the ENB.

In discussions on Monday, the Pacific island nation of Vanuatu underscored that “every year of delay is a year in which communities on the frontlines bear losses that cannot be measured or claimed”, the ENB says. The delegation pointed to the International Court of Justice’s 2025 advisory opinion that countries have binding obligations to protect the climate.

The Netherlands delegation said it was “inconceivable” that two years before the conclusion of second global stocktake, the “body created to produce scientific advice accepts it will not deliver working group reports on mitigation and adaption, while the [IPCC co-chairs] say it is possible”, according to the ENB.

But other countries emphasised that the global stocktake could draw from other sources of science if the reports were not ready in time, the ENB notes.

China’s delegation opposed the IPCC holding more than three plenaries each year and said IPCC reports did not need to be linked to the global stocktake. Kenya said that publishing reports after the stocktake “did not mean science would not inform policy”.

Also among those to argue for a longer timeline were Iran and Iraq, with the latter underscoring the importance of participating in IPCC processes on an “equal footing” with other governments, the ENB says.

There were also disagreements over the specific mandate of the contact group set up by Skea on Monday to advance the timeline question. This debate endured well into the end of the week and days after the contact group had started its work.

Countries pushed back on each others’ characterisation of the different timeline options. Proponents for a longer timeline described plans where reports were published in 2028 as “rushed”, whereas those who wanted all reports published in time for the stocktake referred to the longer timeline as “delayed” or “significantly extended”, according to the ENB.

Countries also failed to reach consensus on the draft timeline decision presented to plenary on the last day of the meeting.

While some countries – including Antigua and Barbuda, Costa Rica, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Kiribati, Marshall Islands, Mexico, the UK and Uruguay – detailed their preference for particular passages of the IPCC secretariat’s text, others rejected it outright.

For example, Algeria, India, Iraq and Saudi Arabia stated the draft text could not serve as a basis for work or decisions, according to ENB. India, meanwhile, argued that it could not engage in the decision as the contact group reached “no clear conclusion”.

In an analysis of developments at IPCC-65, the ENB states that discussions in Ethiopia had revealed “room to compromise” between countries.

It continues that “most countries” in attendance had stressed the importance of not deviating from a tradition where the IPCC follows the advice of working group co-chairs when it comes to the schedule for production of assessment reports.

In this case, that would mean agreeing to publish all three working group reports in 2028 “on a timeline that the co-chairs consider feasible and meets all the criteria for IPCC reports” (options one and two in table above).

In a statement shared with Carbon Brief after the meeting, Marshall Islands climate envoy Tina Stege said that it was a “travesty that a handful of countries continue to delay the IPCC’s reports – the science we need back home to help us save lives and livelihoods”. She continued:

“We were here to work, but found every proposal dismissed – on inclusivity, on the relevance of the science for our national planning, on the necessity of evidence to inform the next global stocktake. Progress was blocked, down to the end – a shameful play for time when there is no time to waste.”

Hamid Abakar Souleymane, a negotiator from Chad, tells Carbon Brief the situation was “not helping” countries such as his, which are on the “frontlines” of climate change.

The African nation faces a host of climate impacts, including drought, heatwaves, severe rainfall, desertification and biodiversity loss, he says. As a result, it wants AR7 reports to be ready by the global stocktake so that “[climate] mitigation action can be accelerated”. He continues:

“It is only about two or three countries that are opposing every single element of the agenda – and this is really disappointing. We are in a very uncomfortable situation now because the global stocktake is coming. We don’t have a timeline [for reports] – and we haven’t yet adopted a budget.”

Abakar Souleyame also notes that Chad was at a disadvantage during negotiations because it was only able to send one representative to the meeting:

“A lot of meetings take place at the same time…The participation of a single delegate puts us in a very uncomfortable situation where we cannot contribute adequately to the ongoing process.”

Inextricably linked with the AR7 timeline is the IPCC’s budget, which – among other things – allocates money for its author meetings and approval sessions, including support for experts from developing countries.

Throughout the meeting in Addis Ababa, governments and the IPCC leadership repeatedly emphasised that the IPCC is “facing a serious funding crisis”, the ENB says.

At the start of the meeting, the IPCC’s secretariat presented a proposed budget for 2026-29, which would cover the core work of AR7. This was submitted “against the backdrop of growing annual deficits and a progressively declining cash balance, raising significant concerns about the financial sustainability of the IPCC trust fund”, the document says.

The trust fund is made up of ongoing contributions from its parent organisations, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and UN Environment Programme (UNEP), in addition to voluntary contributions from its member governments and the UNFCCC.

The budget proposal notes that an “insufficient level of voluntary contributions” now “risks the successful completion of the AR7 cycle”.

Contributions in 2025 were 5.3m Swiss francs (£4.8m) lower than 2023, while costs have risen, the secretariat notes. As of the start of July, just 16 countries and organisations had contributed in 2026, totalling 2.8m Swiss francs (£2.6m).

These donors include the EU, UK, WMO, UNFCCC and UNEP, as well as Australia, Canada, Hungary, Luxembourg, New Zealand, Norway, Peru, South Africa, Sweden, Switzerland and Trinidad and Tobago.

Despite being its largest donor historically, the US has not contributed financially to the IPCC since 2024 – before the second term of US president Donald Trump.

According to its proposed budget, the secretariat says it needs approximately 33m Swiss francs (£30m) to complete AR7 – equivalent to just over 8m Swiss francs (£7.2m) per year. At the level of contributions received in 2025, the IPCC trust fund would be “depleted by the end of 2028, preventing the IPCC from delivering the AR7 reports”, the document warns.

As a minimum, the IPCC needs 5.8m Swiss francs (£5.3m) per year in contributions to “enable the successful completion of the cycle”, the secretariat adds, although this would leave “no financial buffer for unforeseen circumstances”.

At the meeting, “many countries expressed concern” about the IPCC’s financial situation and “called for existing donors to increase their contributions”, says the ENB.

Iceland and Ireland said they “anticipated” making contributions soon, the ENB says, while others – including Azerbaijan, China, Finland and the EU – “assured” the IPCC of their continued support.

However, according to the ENB summary, the meeting struggled to agree on a number of points, including whether budget discussions should be paused until a timeline for AR7 had been agreed, and the work of the “Financial Task Team” (FiTT).

An FiTT is assembled for each assessment cycle for “reviewing income and expenditures, assisting in preparing the budget proposals and developing other recommendations related to finance for consideration by the panel”, according to the IPCC.

The IPCC established the FiTT for AR7 at its 60th session in Istanbul in January 2024. It is co-chaired by Kenya and Australia, with core team members from the Bahamas, Norway, Saudi Arabia and the US. Other government representatives can also participate.

Midway through the meeting, FiTT co-chair Patricia Nying’uro from Kenya reported to the plenary that they had been “unable to advance” on reviewing the budgets beyond 2026, with disagreements on whether “budget lines should remain or be deleted”, reports the ENB.

The attempts to remove whole budget lines drew expressions of “deep concern and disappointment” from other countries, the ENB says, with many noting that the work of the FiTT is “purely technical and has never been controversial”.

Belize, France, Germany, Panama and the Netherlands specified that “two delegations have threatened to have no budget if their conditions are not met”, the ENB says, with even budget lines for activities already agreed being “taken hostage”.

The ENB notes that both Saudi Arabia and India expressed concern that certain lines in the 2027 budget “favoured one option” for the AR7 timeline. Both countries opposed using the proposed budget as a basis for discussion in the FiTT, the ENB says.

The ENB quotes Prof Skea, who noted that most of the budget lines apply to all options for the timeline and was puzzled that members seemed to “disagree about things they don’t disagree about”.

A way forward was found for the FiTT to continue its discussions – despite Saudi Arabia’s accusing the secretariat of “blatant bias”, says the ENB. However, further disagreements arose over budget lines for approval meetings in 2027 to extend financial support for developing countries to bring two representatives rather than one.

Skea noted that despite “almost universal” support for increased participation of developing countries in IPCC sessions, the plenary discussions had continued for two and a half hours without agreement. He asked the timeline contact group to take up the issue.

Discussions on the rest of the budget continued in the FiTT, but “there were no further reports to plenary” before the meeting was suspended, the ENB says.

In the evening of the meeting’s final day, with no agreement on the timeline, Skea asked delegates to consider approving the proposed budgets for 2026 and 2027 to allow work on AR7 to continue. According to the ENB, Saudi Arabia, India and Russia objected.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo warned that the delay to agreeing the budgets was an “immediate danger to the viability” of the panel.

According to the ENB, Skea said in his closing address that if there were stronger words than “profound disappointment,” he would use them.

He noted that recent months had been “some of the most turbulent for the panel” and characterised IPCC-65 as “among the most difficult IPCC meetings”, it says.

It remains to be seen when discussions on the AR7 timeline and budget will restart.

A statement from Skea released after the meeting detailed plans to reconvene the plenary “at the earliest opportunity so the panel can effectively conclude its business”.

The IPCC declined Carbon Brief’s request for an interview with Skea, with a spokesperson noting it could not “discuss a plenary that had not yet finished its work”.

On Monday after the meeting, nearly 120 scientists currently drafting the AR7 reports had signed an open letter where they pledged to “keep working” on the reports. By Wednesday, more than 200 scientists had added their names.

The report authors said they remained committed to delivering the assessments “in a manner that is rigorous, timely and inclusive”. The letter also stresses the “world should not have to face” the second global stocktake without the IPCC’s latest reports.

In a statement, Dr Bill Hare, CEO of Climate Analytics, called for discussions to be resumed this year. He said:

“Urgent agreement is now needed to give planning security to the voluntary [report] authors, more than half of which are from developing countries and to safeguard a timeline that sees all WG reports published in 2028, in time to inform the second global stocktake.

“A resumed IPCC-65 session still this year, whichever way possible, is essential to at the very least agree to the IPCC’s 2027 budget to allow work to continue.”

Prof Fabrice Lambert, a contributing author of WG1 and Chilean government delegate, wrote on LinkedIn that there was now a “clear need for professional negotiators and experienced facilitators” from UN bodies to help organise and run panel sessions. He continued:

“Discussions have moved too far beyond the scientific questions that should be the IPCC’s mainstay. Bringing in that expertise could facilitate breakthroughs that have eluded us for so many sessions during AR7.”

The IPCC has also not yet confirmed the date and location of its next plenary session, IPCC-66.

After every IPCC meeting, the organisation writes a report summarising the discussion.  However, the IPCC has failed to sign off on the reports for its past four sessions.

The first item on the agenda for last week’s IPCC meeting was the approval of these four draft reports. To open the discussion on Monday, Skea proposed developing guidelines for producing these meeting reports, the ENB says.

Belgium, France and the Netherlands suggested publishing full transcripts of the meetings in addition to the meeting reports. Panama supported the idea, “emphasising the need for transparency”, the ENB says. However, it adds that Saudi Arabia opposed the suggestion.

Saudi Arabia said it objected to the “highly selective manner” in which the draft meeting reports outlined certain topics in more detail than others, arguing that this “distorted [their] balance”.

Meanwhile, Belgium and Germany opposed the use of the passive voice in the meeting reports, arguing this gave an impression that all opinions are “equally supported”. They said that this resulted in a “skewed way of presenting discussions”, the ENB says.

IPCC vice-chair Prof Diana Ürge-Vorsatz led a huddle to continue the discussion. She reported back at the end of the week that the group had reached a “preliminary agreement” for the “principles for producing meeting reports”, the ENB notes:

“She said the discussion focused on how to record diverging views, as some panel members felt separating out two or three countries invited unwelcome attention or misinterpretation.

“Ürge-Vorsatz said the huddle agreed to group arguments, first identifying countries representing one perspective and elaborating on their argument, then identifying countries representing the other side of arguments. The secretariat will decide on the granularity of reporting.”

The panel requested that the IPCC secretariat prepare revised draft meeting notes in accordance with the new guidance. It decided to “submit the revised draft reports for consideration by the panel at its next working session”.

The IPCC also agreed to defer consideration of a review of its principles and procedures to a future meeting, after India, Iran, Russia and Saudi Arabia argued that there was not enough time to discuss the issue thoroughly, the ENB reports.

These documents are typically reviewed every five years. However, the last review was delayed due to the Covid pandemic.

The co-chairs of each AR7 working group updated governments on how the reports themselves were progressing.

All co-chairs highlighted their working group’s contribution to the special report on climate change and cities, scheduled to be published in 2027.

WG2 co-chairs Prof Winston Chow and Prof Bart van den Hurk explained to the conference floor that the second order draft of the special report was circulated for review earlier this year and received more than 25,000 comments. They added that the fourth lead author meeting was held in India in August and a final draft version is currently being prepared.

In his closing address on Friday, Skea said the progress reports presented by working-group chairs were evidence of the “ongoing achievements” of the IPCC, according to the ENB.

Noting that the IPCC’s scientific work was progressing, Skea reportedly stated that, despite challenges on the timeline issue, “the engine room of the IPCC is working – and it’s healthy”.

The sentiment was echoed by WG1 co-chair Dr Robert Vautard, who wrote on LinkedIn after the meeting that disagreements across country delegations on the AR7 timeline were “so disconnected from the actual collaborative spirit across authors in the report making”.

Nevertheless, the ENB’s meeting summary cautions that the ongoing inability of governments to reach agreement on “critical issues” was now “spilling over to authors”, with “serious potential consequences both for the IPCC and for the science the world depends on”.

It continues that, “while the work of scientists and governments is separate, the friction that has characterised AR7 plenary sessions is now directly affecting the ability of the scientists to carry out their work”.

On Tuesday afternoon, Mxolisi Shongwe, programme officer at the IPCC secretariat, told the plenary that a workshop on IPCC “methods of assessment” was held in February 2026, which featured discussions on artificial intelligence (AI).

He added that the IPCC bureau – made up managerial roles including the IPCC chair, vice-chair and co-chairs of working groups – decided to establish an “informal group” on AI that would provide advice on the “responsible use of AI and emerging digital technologies in IPCC processes”, reports the ENB.

India and Russia “expressed concerns about lack of transparency in [the] use of AI in scientific research”, according to the ENB. Burundi and Iran highlighted the need for disclosure and human oversight in AI-produced material, while Congo asked how AI has so far been used by the IPCC.

After discussions about the terms of reference for the informal group on AI, the panel agreed that any guidance or recommendations that it produced will be “subject to subsequent confirmation by the panel”, reports the ENB.

Separately, vice-chair Ürge-Vorsatz reported on the progress of the IPCC’s gender action team, explaining that the group had finalised the report of the IPCC expert meeting on gender, diversity, equity and inclusivity.

The gender action team “aims to enhance gender equality in IPCC processes through monitoring, accounting for gender in decisions, including diversity in nominations and appointment”, according to the IPCC website.

Dozens of countries commented on the update. Luxembourg, supported by nine other countries, invited the panel to consider expanding the mandate of the gender action team to include “gender diversity, equity and inclusion”, reports the ENB.

However, Saudi Arabia opposed expanding the mandate of the gender action team, arguing it risked “diluting” the team’s work.

To close the discussion on Tuesday, Skea suggested the gender action team consider expanding its mandate and bring suggestions to IPCC-66.

Verkaufsshow für Deutschland: Kanzler wirbt um Investoren

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Der Bundeskanzler hat zum ersten deutschen Investitionsgipfel geladen. 200 internationale Unternehmen sollen in KI investieren. Zuversicht verbreiten war angesagt, auch wenn es etwas hakte am Anfang.

Typisch deutsch? Lange Warteschlangen an der Sicherheitskontrolle beim Einlass zum ersten Investitionsgipfel der Bundesregierung in Berlin. Einige Damen in Kostümjacke und Herren mit Krawatte, die sonst Unternehmen im Ausland leiten, wirken ungeduldig.

Zeit ist Geld. Erste Witze werden gerissen, dass die organisierte Langsamkeit viel über Deutschland aussagte. "In China ginge das schneller", meint ein jüngerer Mann im dunkelblauen Anzug lächelnd.

Die Schlange am Eingang wird so lang, dass der Gastgeber, Bundeskanzler Friedrich Merz, warten muss, bis alle 200 eingeladenen internationalen Firmenchefs den Saal erreichen. Drinnen im großen Saal des ehemaligen DDR-Staatsratsgebäudes, das heute eine private Wirtschaftshochschule beherbergt, gibt sich der Gastgeber mit Verspätung alle Mühe, die kleine Panne zu überspielen.

Der Kanzler spricht von den Klischees über die Deutschen, ihren Fleiß, ihre Pünktlichkeit, ihre Verlässlichkeit, ihre Angewohnheit, mit Handtüchern sehr früh am Morgen Liegen im Urlaubshotel zu reservieren.

"Manche Klischees tendieren dazu, wahr zu sein", sagt Friedrich Merz am Rednerpult und fügt hinzu, diese Eigenschaften würden Deutschland helfen, mit großen Herausforderungen fertig zu werden. Deutschland sei finanziell stabil, wirbt der Kanzler. Schließlich habe das Land als einzige der führenden Industrienationen in der G7 noch ein solides AAA-Rating bei den großen Bewertungsagenturen.

"Deutschland hat eine starke industrielle Basis und ist politisch stabil", wirbt der Kanzler. Das Land sei als Anker in die Europäische Union mit ihrem einzigartigen Binnenmarkt eingebettet.

Es würden 500 Milliarden in die Infrastruktur und jedes Jahr 150 Milliarden in die Rüstung investiert in den kommenden Jahren. Das sei eine enorme Chance für Investoren, lockt Friedrich Merz, der früher selbst den US-Investor Blackrock in Deutschland gemanagt hat. Hindernisse für Investitionen sollten beseitigt, die Verwaltung schneller und digitaler, Energie preiswerter werden.

Der Chef des größten deutschen Bankhauses, das viele Investoren betreut, findet, die Deutschen sollten optimistischer sein. "In Deutschland reden wir oft über Probleme", sagte Christian Sewing, Vorstandvorsitzender der Deutschen Bank.

"Viel seltener reden wir aber über die Möglichkeiten und die vielfältigen Potenziale. Wenn ich mit Investoren in Nordamerika, Asien und im Nahen Osten spreche, fällt mir immer ein großer Unterschied auf. Viele blicken mit mehr Zuversicht auf Deutschland als wir selbst." Ausländische Investoren sähen die Chancen oft besser als die deutschen Unternehmer, meint der Deutsche-Bank-Chef.

Einer dieser Investoren ist der Chef der weltweit wertvollsten Firma, Jensen Huang, CEO des Chip-Konzerns Nvidia. Er bot dem Kanzler, lässig in Lederjacke gekleidet, beim Plausch am Kamin am Vorabend der Konferenz eine Partnerschaft mit seiner Firma an, die die Hochleistungsprozessoren für die Anwendungen von Künstlicher Intelligenz (KI) herstellt. Alle große Internet- und Datenkonzerne sind auf Nvidia angewiesen. Staaten weltweit buhlen um Investitionen des Konzerns.

Die Bundesregierung hat mit Jensen Huang eine Zusammenarbeit vereinbart, in dessen Rahmen rund 10.000 Super-Chips für KI-Anwendungen für die deutsche Industrie bereitgestellt werden. Diese Anwendungen sollen KI in die Produktion von Maschinen, Fahrzeugen, Robotern integrieren und damit in der realen Welt praktisch nutzbar machen.

Industrielle KI wird ganze Fabriken entwerfen, designen und testen, bevor sie in der wirklichen Welt gebaut werden, schwärmt der CEO des Siemens-Konzerns, Roland Busch, in Berlin: "Industrielle KI ist die Jahrhundertchance für Deutschland, für hochwertige Jobs sowie für neues Wachstum. Deutschland muss und kann das Land sein, in dem neue Technologien entwickelt und skaliert werden. Unser entscheidender Vorteil bei künstlicher Intelligenz sind die Kombination von KI mit deutscher Ingenieurkunst und industriellen Daten."

Sechs Ministerinnen und Minister, von Wirtschaft über Wohnungsbau bis Verkehr, hat die Bundesregierung als oberste Wirtschaftswerber bei diesem Gipfeltreffen aufgeboten. Sie halten Seminare ab und treffen sich zu Einzelgesprächen mit Konzernlenkern aus aller Welt. "Im 15 Minuten-Takt, eine Art Investitions-Speeddating", meint ein Mitarbeiter des Bundeswirtschaftsministeriums.

Finanzminister Lars Klingbeil versucht, die Investoren mit einem Scherz in seinen Workshop zu locken. "Ich will ihr Geld, lassen sie uns darüber reden", sagt er mit breitem Grinsen.

Wirtschaftsministerin Katherina Reiche fordert die Zuhörer im Saal zum Mitmachen in einer neuen Ära auf: "KI-Sprachmodelle gibt es genug. Jetzt muss KI in Industrieproduktion umgewandelt werden." Deutschland werde dabei eine führende Rolle spielen, gestützt auf hoch ausgebildete Arbeitnehmer und einen innovativen Mittelstand, versichert Reiche.

Bauministerin Verena Hubertz wirbt für Investitionen in "Betongold", also Immobilien und Wohnungsbau in Deutschland. Mit Blick auf die Pläne einer möglichen neuen linken Landesregierung in Berlin, Wohnungen großer Wohnungsbauunternehmen zu vergesellschaften, sagte Hubertz, die Bundesregierung wolle Investitionen mit einem neuen Bundesgesetz absichern und schützen.

Will Marshall, Geschäftsführer des Satellitenherstellers Planet Labs PBC, gefällt dieses erste Gipfeltreffen der ausländischen Investoren mit der Regierungsmannschaft. Er glaubt an den deutschen Standort und produziert bereits in Berlin. Die Deutschen seien zuverlässig und gut ausgebildet. Er produziere hier für den europäischen Markt, um Europa bei Satelliten und Geo-Daten unabhängiger zu machen.

Insgesamt investierten laut Bundesbank ausländische Investoren im vergangenen Jahr 86 Milliarden Euro in Deutschland. Diese Summe hat sich gegenüber 2024 fast verdoppelt. Der Bundeskanzler erwartet bis 2030 weitere direkte Investitionen ausländischer Unternehmen in die deutsche Industrie von rund 800 Milliarden Euro.

Der Wirtschaftsgipfel werde helfen, diese Summen locker zu machen, meint ein Mitarbeiter des Wirtschaftsministeriums. Am Ende des Tages sei aber nicht das Ziel gewesen, eine konkrete Summe von neuen Investitionen zu nennen.

Auf der Berliner Tagung würden keine Verträge geschlossen, sondern investitionsbereite Unternehmen motiviert und überzeugt. Allerdings gab der US-Konzern Amazon am Rande des Gipfels bekannt, dass er im kommenden Jahr Investitionen in Höhe von 16 Milliarden Euro in seine Infrastruktur für Cloud-Computing und KI-Anwendungen in Deutschland plant.

Labor and the Coalition are poised to take a reckless gamble on AI. Haven’t they realised community support has collapsed? | Edward Santow and Sarah Sacher

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Australian workers expect the government to act to protect them from AI harms. There is a clear reform solution at hand

Get our new political email, free app or daily news podcast

Australia’s major political parties are edging towards rare consensus. Labor and the Coalition increasingly want to take a major bet on artificial intelligence. Yet, just as the parties are coming together, the Australian community is running the other way.

Last year, only 30% of Australians believed the benefits of AI outweighed the risks. In 2026, community trust in AI appears to be falling even further.

It was an unusual step for our parliament to form a joint select committee on AI. It signalled that the decisions being made now on datacentres and how we adopt AI could define our economy, environment and society for generations.

As a fresh round of hearings starts this week, the committee members must confront the gulf between the politics of AI and the stark reality on the ground. Some parts of the business sector are urging us to prioritise innovation and AI adoption, which is often another way of saying: “Do a better job of selling the upside of AI.”

Whether you’re an AI optimist or a sceptic, this is a terrible idea. Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and the other titans of Silicon Valley have a golden megaphone to spruik the benefits of their technology. Australians don’t want our elected representatives to do the same thing – especially when the technology is already bringing both opportunity and harm.

This doesn’t mean being blind to the benefits of AI. On the contrary, Australia is among the highest users per capita of Anthropic’s Claude and other generative AI applications. But we must also be clear-eyed about potential risks. And, crucially, we don’t see a compelling response to our concerns from government or the AI companies themselves.

Take the impact of AI on work. Our community is asking practical questions. How will AI impact our jobs and the jobs of our children? What is the government doing to ensure the AI systems used in Australian workplaces are safe?

Our new report, Drawing the Line, lays out how AI is propelling the rise of harmful tech-enabled worker surveillance. Often workers are unaware of the extent to which they are being monitored – including through their work devices and phones, and via software such as Microsoft Teams.

Unlike some of the hypothetical (and frankly terrifying) risks that the joint select committee on AI has already covered, such as AI-enabled biological weapons and engineered pandemics, this is happening today.

Australian supermarket workers are already being pushed to meet AI-generated productivity metrics that force them to work harder and faster under the threat of punitive repercussions – putting them at risk of injury and workplace accidents.

Remote workers are already being subjected to intrusive surveillance software capable of watching everything they do on their screen – including audio recording conversations they have with their families.

Rideshare drivers are already being penalised for road conditions and customer complaints that are unrelated to their performance, which can result in their account being deactivated and days of work lost.

Workers are being quietly crushed by commonplace AI-powered surveillance tools that have crept into our workplaces and home offices. And the impact is real. Think, for example, of the young woman who reported being sexually harassed by a boss that required her to be on video at all times. Or warehouse workers driven to suicidal ideation due to relentless micromanagement by machine.

Our workplace laws don’t protect workers from unreasonable surveillance. Nor do they give employers clarity about what types of monitoring are lawful.

Unlike existential risks from AI, which are outside the Australian government’s direct control, this problem could be addressed through straightforward law reform.

We urge the government to update the Fair Work Act to require any worker monitoring be “fair and reasonable”. This builds on the government’s commitment to require all personal information covered by the Privacy Act to be handled fairly and reasonably.

Australian workers expect the government to act to protect them from AI harms. There is a clear reform solution at hand. This is not a hypothetical future risk – let’s address it now.

Prof Edward Santow is co-director of the UTS Human Technology Institute and served as human rights commissioner from 2016-2021. Sarah Sacher is HTI’s senior responsible technology policy specialist
