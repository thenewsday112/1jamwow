Das Grazer Klanglicht wagt sich nach Wien: Lichtkunst zwischen Bäumen und Balustraden im Park [premium]

Das Grazer Klanglichtfestival gastiert erstmals in Wien: Mit 16 Licht- und Klanginstallationen wird der Schwarzenbergpark beim Schloss Belevedere in Szene gesetzt.

Es sind nur ein paar Lichterl“, sagt ein älterer Herr kurz vor dem Einlass, „das wird wahrscheinlich nicht so lange dauern.“ Nun: Vermutlich wird dieser Ehrengast, der am Montagabend unter den Ersten war, die das neue „Klanglicht im Park“ beim Schloss Belvedere vorab besuchen durften, seine Meinung ordentlich revidiert haben.

Tatsächlich steckt hinter „Klanglicht“ natürlich viel mehr als „nur ein paar Lichterl“, die hübsch in dunkle Herbstabende hinein arrangiert werden: 2015 hat man in Graz zunächst einmal nur „für fünfzehn Minuten“ die Rückseite der Oper mit einer Lichtinstallation ganz unerwartet frisch in Szene gesetzt, wie sich der Klanglicht-Erfinder und Geschäftsführer der Bühnen Graz, Bernhard Rinner, erinnert. Das kam so gut an, dass Klanglicht zu einem mehrtägigen Kunst- und Klangfestival wuchs, mit dem man prominente Gebäude in der Innenstadt wie den Uhrturm oder die Burg mit Lichtinstallationen teils sehr spektakulär in Szene setzen ließ. In Graz ist das Festival längst ein Fixpunkt im Herbst, heuer allerdings aus budgetären Gründen nicht mehr kostenlos im öffentlichen Raum, sondern kostenpflichtig im Schloss Eggenberg sowie im Schlosspark (siehe Infobox).

Nun wagt man erstmals auch den Weg nach Wien, wo man im Schwarzenbergpark beim Schloss Belvedere einen Rundgang mit 16 Licht- und Klanginstallationen geschaffen hat: Der Wiener Ableger – der am Freitag öffnet und bis 10. Jänner und damit deutlich länger dauert als das Grazer Original – ist wiederum Georg Hoanzl zu verdanken, der ebendiese Location im Sommer mit dem „Theater im Park“ bespielt und Graz um das Klanglicht „ehrlich beneidet“ hat. „Er hat“, erinnert sich Rinner, „dann bei uns angerufen und gemeint, dass er keinen Girlandenpark braucht, sondern auch gern eine Kunstposition hätte und gefragt, ob wir das nicht gemeinsam machen wollen.“ Das war Anfang diesen Jahres, „wir haben uns dann schnell gefunden“, sagt Rinner.

Für Kuratorin Birgit Lill-Schnabl galt es dann, mit relativ wenig Vorlaufzeit ein eigenes Klanglicht für Wien zu konzipieren. „Der Park ist ein wunderschönes Setting, er wurde im späten 17. Jahrhundert als Barockgarten angelegt“, sagt Lill-Schnabl. „Ich habe die Künstlerinnen und Künstler gebeten, den Park weiterzudenken. Es geht nicht darum, die Vergangenheit zu rekonstruieren, sondern eine Brücke zu schlagen zwischen dem barocken Garten von damals und Licht- und Klanginstallationen aus dem Heute.“

Auf die Natur im Park wird jedenfalls in einigen der 16 Installationen Bezug genommen, so widmet die slowenische Architektin und Lichtkünstlerin Katja Paternoster ihren Beitrag „Domestic and Wild“ den Wildtieren: Quer durch den Park kann man ihre Tierskulpturen entdecken – manchmal muss man dafür durchaus genau schauen. Das Duo „Hartung und Trenz“ flutet mit seiner Installation „Lichtwege“ Balustrade und Bäume mit leuchtender Sprache. Hauptattraktion wird vermutlich der Mond („Museum of the Moon“ des Briten Luke Jerram) sein, der schon in 30 Ländern Station gemacht hat und sicher ein beliebter Fotospot werden wird – wie generell die Klanglicht-Installationen von manchen wohl auch (vorrangig?) als fesche Kulisse für Instagram-Stories gesehen werden könnten.

Was Rinner und Lill-Schnabl nicht stört: Man will bei allem künstlerischen Anspruch möglichst viele Menschen ansprechen. Wie das gelingt? „Das ist der steirische Mischsatz“, sagt Rinner. „Es muss eine gewisse Massentauglichkeit haben und dazwischen aber auch Herausforderungen bieten. Ich glaube, damit differenzieren wir uns schon ganz stark von anderen Lichterparks.“ Und, sagt Lill-Schnabl: „Für viele Menschen ist das wirklich der erste Berührungspunkt mit Kunst und Kultur, weil es eben so niederschwellig ist.“

Mit „Klanglicht im Park - The Sound of Light“ gastiert das 2015 gegründete Grazer Klanglicht-Festival erstmals in Wien, konkret im Schwarzenbergpark beim Schloss Belvedere. Zu sehen sind im Park 16 Licht- und Klanginstallationen. Von 23.10. bis 10.1.. Eine frühe Buchung eines Zeitfensters empfiehlt sich. Mystery mailings put Congress and election offices on edge

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Over three dozen congressional lawmakers and election offices in at least 32 states have been targeted with suspicious letters, many containing a white powder.

Why it matters: The letters have forced evacuations and disrupted election offices just weeks before the 2026 midterms, raising concerns about political intimidation.

Driving the news: Rep. Adam Smith's (D-Wash.) district office received a letter Monday, prompting staff to evacuate.

  • In an emailed statement, Smith's office confirmed it "received a suspicious letter containing a powdery substance, like others sent to Congressional offices across the country."
  • Staff returned to the office Tuesday.

What they're saying: "The FBI and U.S. Postal Inspection Service are investigating a series of suspicious mailings sent to various addresses nationwide, including Secretary of State offices and their related election offices, and Congressional offices," an FBI spokesperson said in an emailed statement.

  • "Some of the letters contained an unknown substance and we are working closely with our law enforcement partners to respond to each incident."

Catch up quick: The U.S. Capitol Police, the House and Senate sergeants at arms and other officials sent notices to congressional offices last week warning about letters containing suspicious substances and urging staffers to be vigilant.

  • Over 100 letters were estimated to be circulating around the country, law enforcement sources told ABC News.
  • The USCP referred Axios to the FBI for comment.

Here's what we know about the suspicious letters:

Who was targeted

At least three dozen lawmakers have been targeted with the letters, including both Democrats and Republicans.

  • That includes prominent leaders such as Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) and Sens. Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.), Susan Collins (R-Maine) and Pete Ricketts (R-Neb.).

Election offices in at least 32 states were targeted with similarly suspicious letters, according to WIRED.

  • Authorities intercepted many of the letters, including those addressed to election offices in Ohio, Minnesota and Connecticut. But in places like Oklahoma, Arizona and Delaware, the letters arrived and prompted evacuations.

What's inside

Many letters contained a white powder-like substance.

  • So far, preliminary testing of the powder has found no hazardous materials.

Rep. Pramila Jayapal (D-Wash.) and Sen. Mike Rounds (R-S.D.) also reported receiving suspicious letters but did not disclose their contents.

  • Nebraska officials received a letter that they didn't open and instead turned over to the FBI for further analysis.

Flashback: The Capitol Police's notice went out on the 25th anniversary of the anthrax attacks that terrified Capitol Hill in the wake of 9/11.

  • On Oct. 15, 2001, a staffer opened an anthrax-laced letter in then-Senate Majority Leader Tom Daschle's office, forcing the Hart Senate Office Building to close for months.
  • Anthrax attacks killed five people that year.

Where are they coming from

Law enforcement has not yet determined where the letters are coming from or if they're part of a coordinated attack.

  • The FBI declined to say if the letters are linked, nor are they confirming "specific instances or letters."
  • However, the bureau told Axios in an emailed statement it is working to identify whoever sent the letters.

Worth noting: Some of the letters claimed to come from the Democratic Socialists of America, the left-wing insurgent organization that denies any involvement.

  • Some letters bore a New York City return address that DSA previously used but has not occupied since 2013, a spokesperson said.
  • DSA did not respond to Axios' request for comment.

Jalen Hurts Deserved Better. We All Do.

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In a country that treats workers as expendable parts, Hurts’s playing through a blood clot is just the highest-profile example of the price workers pay in an oligarchy.

The Philadelphia Eagles’s Jalen Hurts during the NFL match between the Eagles and the Jacksonville Jaguars at Tottenham Hotspur Stadium on October 11, 2026, in London, United Kingdom.

In a world where the attention spans of sports commentators and fans are roughly the same as that of a cat chasing a laser pointer, sometimes a story breaks through that demands our undivided attention. Philadelphia Eagles quarterback Jalen Hurts could have died this week, and, despite the relentless churn of the news cycle, we must not look away.

The 2025 Super Bowl MVP was told by the Eagles’ medical staff that he had a “superficial blood clot” after an opponent’s cleat smashed into his calf on October 8. Hurts found himself the night before game day in a London hospital until 3 am, where he was told that sleeping could cause the clot to spread and risk his life. Hurts then, according to reports, attempted to pull an all-nighter, though he may have taken a nap at the stadium before game time—this part of the story is unclear. He then played in the game, where he attempted 40 passes. The Eagles lost 19–17, and Hurts took another transatlantic flight home. After he attended practice for another two days, an ultrasound revealed that Hurts’s condition had worsened. He was diagnosed with deep-vein thrombosis and bilateral pulmonary emboli, meaning he now had multiple blood clots in both lungs. Hurts is out for the season, taking blood thinners, and fortunate that he avoided a pulmonary embolism.

When the story first broke, “NFL insider” Tom Pelissero had the following hot take: “How committed is Jalen Hurts to football?”

So committed, Pelissero wrote, that “after initially being diagnosed with a superficial blood clot in London, doctors told him not to sleep as a precaution, so he had been awake for roughly 36 hours when he started against the Jaguars, throwing for 220 yards and a TD.” (Pelissero later walked back the “awake for 36 hours” part, which was disputed by Hurts’s agent.)

It’s exactly that kind of tough-guy “committed to football” narrative that puts Hurts’s life—and the health of all players—at risk. As former NFLPA chief DeMaurice Smith would remind people, “This is the only job with a 100 percent injury rate.” These injuries invariably get exacerbated because football players, with their non-guaranteed contracts, will always play unless stopped. This is more than a financial concern. It is built into the toxic, gladiator ethos of the sport.

But moments like these are precisely when a team’s medical staff is supposed to intervene. Without their involvement, coaches will do whatever they need to do to keep their jobs, and a quarterback willing to go through a wall to win a game will agree to it, even if it means playing with a blood clot in front of an indifferent London crowd. This is especially the case in Philadelphia, where the Eagles’ perpetually enraged fans were already calling for Coach Nick Sirianni’s job—despite his winning the Super Bowl just 20 months before—because of an early-season losing streak.

What happened with Hurts also makes one wonder where the NFL Players Association, the union that represents players, was as this news broke across the sports world. It should be up in arms, demanding answers for how this could have possibly taken place. Instead, it’s MIA, having not put out—as of this writing—even a boilerplate statement about health and safety guidelines or how players must be protected. The Nation has received no comment from the union, but I have received messages from former players frustrated that it has become so toothless and unwilling, even rhetorically, to shout from the rooftops that such an incident is utterly unacceptable.

The Hurts story feels bigger than one player avoiding a fatal tragedy. It is a microcosm of larger issues within the league, which treats its players—even star quarterbacks—as equipment. These aren’t “gladiators.” They are workers: workers whose average career is three and a half years. The league, despite all of its rhetoric about player safety, treats them like they’re disposable.

But the Hurts microcosm doesn’t end on the football field. This is about a worker who risked his life, with the approval of company doctors, and without the assistance of a union, which chose instead to cover its eyes and ears as long as the National Football League’s golden goose of app gambling and streaming services keeps laying eggs. In a country that treats workers as expendable parts of a production line, with many unions unworthy of the struggles that created them in the first place, Jalen Hurts is just the highest-profile example of the price workers pay in an oligarchy. In this oligarchy, private-equity overlords in the owners’ box look down on their expensive toys and think of themselves as the emperors of ancient Rome. In reality, they represent the worst of us, watching others inflict pain for their own profit and amusement.

Jalen Hurts deserved better than this. So do the rest of us. Now the NFL and its media partners are trying to move on to the next topic, hoping our addiction to gambling and Fantasy Sports will allow us to “thingify” and dehumanize Hurts enough that we can still enjoy the spectacle without considering the price paid. We can’t let them. This is a story of corporate and medical malfeasance, which is also a story too many of us know all too well in a country where cruelty has become more than “the point.” It has become currency.

Dave Zirin is the sports editor at The Nation. He is the author of 11 books on the politics of sports. He is the author of a new book, The People's Historian: The Outsized Life of Howard Zinn.

Is the Military Low-T?

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Last week, a research letter published in the Journal of the American Medical Association—using data from a sample of nearly five hundred military members—reported that approximately twenty-five per cent of participants had at least one measured testosterone level that could qualify them as deficient. The letter is something of a Rorschach test, offering new information that could either bolster or undermine Pete Hegseth’s effort to establish a “high-T Department of War.” Universal screening for testosterone deficiency among men thirty and older has become one of Hegseth’s signature policies: “No fatties, no trannies, no beardos, no weirdos,” he crankily told a crowd of mid-level military members last month, during his “State of the Force” speech. “The patriotic cowboys are in—with testosterone testing on top.”

Every relevant medical society recommends against screening asymptomatic men for testosterone deficiency, because it is akin to scouring a hotel room with a black light: you’re going to find a lot of stuff of indeterminate significance that you wish you hadn’t seen. The data in the JAMA letter, collected across five U.S. military sites between August, 2024, and January, 2026, suggest that more than a hundred thousand men in current service could qualify for testosterone-replacement therapy (TRT) if their screening results are treated as diagnostic. Other military data collected between 2018 and 2025 had estimated the prevalence of diagnosed testosterone deficiency in male military members at less than one per cent.

This variation reflects that a diagnosis of testosterone deficiency can be made rigorously, with a desire to rule out, or capaciously, with a desire to rule in. Decreased testosterone is often cited as an explanation for an array of nonspecific symptoms, including depressed mood, poor libido, fatigue, and spotty memory. Serum testosterone levels vary with stress, sleep, body shape, and time of day; borderline low levels are common and are hard to interpret outside the context of a careful clinical evaluation. Different organizations even use different thresholds to determine what a “low” value is: the Centers for Disease Control uses two hundred and sixty-four nanograms per decilitre, whereas the American Urological Association and the military use three hundred.

These ambiguities make the condition a perfect container for all manner of dysphoria and shame, and set patients and doctors up for conflict. Before prescribing TRT, evidence-based doctors will require multiple confirmatory tests, disregard symptoms that are vague and easily attributed to other causes, and recommend other interventions to boost testosterone production. Patients who are intent on starting injections may then flee to wellness influencers and direct-to-consumer prescribers. “I’ve been experiencing lack of energy, motivation, and 0 sex drive,” an aspiring T user lamented on Reddit’s r/Testosterone forum, dismayed that his physician would not offer him a prescription because his blood work looked fine.

TRT might restore a man’s sex drive, build lean muscle, and boost energy, but it can also accelerate hair loss and lead to abnormal blood counts and clotting. (It also carries a risk of infertility; wanton TRT use among America’s soldiers risks doubling as an inadvertent sterilization effort.) Nonetheless, testosterone prescriptions have accelerated wildly in recent years, both from licensed sources and on the gray market. The fastest growth has been among young people, who sometimes inject doses intended to push testosterone levels far beyond the usual physiologic range.

In the MAHA/MAGA manosphere, the epithet “Low T” stands in for a failure to meet increasingly punitive standards of maleness. “It’s about restoring and optimizing your natural capabilities, protecting your longevity, and insuring you have the biological foundation required to sustain the fight,” Hegseth said when he announced the policy in July, emphasizing that America’s soldiers should remain on “the leading edge of lethality.” But optimization is a goal with no clear threshold for success: one can always look better, feel better, or, I suppose, be something closer to a killing machine. Hegseth is promoting gender-affirming care for cis men, encouraging them to better align their bodies with his idea of what they should be.

When I learned about testosterone deficiency during medical school, twenty years ago, the term referred to a circumscribed diagnosis requiring the presence of specific signs and symptoms: absent libido, infrequent erections, scant body hair, and blood work demonstrating low morning testosterone levels on multiple occasions. It was considered uncommon, especially in young men, yet under-recognized in many instances. (The most common genetic cause of primary testosterone deficiency is the presence of an extra X chromosome; it’s a disorder called Klinefelter syndrome, which goes undiagnosed in upward of fifty per cent of cases.) I’ve never diagnosed low T, although I’ve had patients request an evaluation. This means I’ve probably missed it, but not in the patients who thought they had it.

Separately, doctors have long prescribed TRT to older men for quality-of-life reasons. Men’s testosterone production decreases with age, and older men might experience its lack in much the same way that menopausal women experience decreased estrogen production: fatigue, loss of libido, increased frailty. In both of these cases, hormone prescriptions are not recreational, but they are elective; the drug is being used to supplement rather than restore, and “the real question is benefit” rather than diagnosis, Brad Anawalt, an endocrinologist and men’s-health expert at University of Washington School of Medicine, told me.

Over the past decade, however, several factors have shifted the norms around who seeks a testosterone prescription and who gets one. “Hormones have become magical,” Anawalt said, and testosterone is “the most magical of all. It’s genuinely hard to have a candid conversation about the science of testosterone with almost anybody, because there’s so much emotion in it.” In part, this is because low testosterone, which was previously treated as a source of individual suffering, has been cast as a social crisis. Testosterone levels have dropped worldwide by as much as fifty per cent since the nineteen-seventies—a decrease associated with the rising prevalence of obesity, because excess fat tissue decreases the production of testosterone and its binding proteins.

Low T due to obesity is best treated through weight loss and life-style modifications, but because testosterone supplementation can help shed pounds, many men would rather start there. Members of online testosterone forums often introduce themselves by sharing their age, their weight, and, sometimes, a photo. After a period on testosterone, they’ll post an “after” shot—washboard abs, straining traps, bulging pecs. “You look superb, man” is a typical encouraging response; “kind words, bro, thank you” a customarily humble reply.

In addition to obesity, some researchers posit that hormone-disrupting chemicals, such as the phthalates and bisphenol A (BPA) found in certain plastics, may have a role. This is a favorite preoccupation of Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., and who himself has copped to taking testosterone supplements to maintain his ropey physique, and who calls the global testosterone drop an “existential problem.”

A worldwide decrease in testosterone is notable, and might be a problem, but its public-health significance isn’t yet clear. Testosterone has been measured differently over time and across studies, often without controlling for relevant concerns such as participant B.M.I. or presence of symptoms. Kennedy told a Fox News host that “a teen-ager today, an American teen-ager, has less testosterone than a sixty-eight-year-old man,” which is a dramatic overread of current trends: with the data we have, we can say with confidence only that average testosterone levels have decreased over time, but not much about in whom, why, or what it means. Lower testosterone on average across a cohort doesn’t equate to meaningful clinical deficiency in an individual, since a fifty-per-cent drop might still land someone comfortably within the normal range. Still, some researchers—and many men seeking testosterone prescriptions—argue that “normal” should be an age-adjusted variable. “To optimize your overall physical (sexual/athletic/aesthetic) performance, a man 40s and 50s needs to be in the 700-1000 range,” one r/Testosterone regular advised his comrades.

Low testosterone is often diagnosed without any measured testosterone recorded for the patient at all. Data from 2018 suggest as many as one quarter of men are prescribed testosterone without recent blood work. That’s what we know from the doctor-to-pharmacy world; we have much less insight into the prescribing habits of direct-to-consumer (D.T.C.) platforms, and even less clarity on how much testosterone is being purchased on the gray market with no prescription at all. In one study, D.T.C. providers were happy to prescribe testosterone to a secret shopper with a level of 675 ng/dl. Whatever we might think the rate of testosterone use is, it’s almost certainly an undercount.

The Trump Administration draws a sharp distinction in legitimacy between the use of hormone therapy in the case of transgender-affirming care versus in cisgender-affirming care. I asked Anawalt to help me puzzle over why I consider these interventions discontinuous with one another, rather than variations on a theme. “The trans population I take care of—a fair number of them aren’t looking to become something different than the gender they feel they are,” Anawalt told me. “They’re not looking to become supermen or superwomen.” This was a provocative suggestion: that transgender care, by offering harmony between a body and an identity, is, in a sense, naturalizing, whereas TRT used to compensate for insecurity and enhance masculinity might be the more unnatural intervention.

Testosterone’s current cultural ubiquity “reduces masculinity to a single number,” Anawalt said, even as that number fluctuates and means little out of context. Hormones work via exquisitely calibrated feedback systems, sensitive to all sorts of stresses and rhythms, acting as messengers across our internal ecologies to keep things in balance—a balance that can easily be disrupted by circumstance. In 2020, a group of researchers led by Christopher Frueh, a clinical psychologist at the University of Hawaii at Hilo, published a description of Operator syndrome, a cluster of symptoms—including sleep disturbances, psychiatric illness, chronic pain, and hormone disruptions, and symptoms of traumatic brain injuries—observed in some special-operations personnel after prolonged periods of physical and emotional stress. Testosterone deficiency is a feature of Operator syndrome, and Hegseth has cited the condition as a rationale for his screening program.

Yet it seems reductionist to think of the individuals who fit this profile as primarily “testosterone deficient.” Rather, testosterone production is suppressed in men living under stressful conditions as “an adaptation to energy scarcity, not a disease, and it typically resolves within days of restored sleep and intake,” as the military’s own document on testosterone screening acknowledges. Evidence that TRT helps men with Operator syndrome is mostly hypothetical, and no prospective study has been conducted. In one small case series comparing a handful of special-operations personnel who were on testosterone with those who weren’t, there were no observed benefits in terms of mood, sleep, or sexual function in the men taking TRT. Instead, men on testosterone reported higher pain scores, and researchers noted anecdotal reports of increased irritability and aggression. Several study participants also apparently expressed a belief that, as special forces, they ought to have testosterone levels well above those of a civilian.

There are approximately seventy thousand troops classified as special ops in the United States military, out of a force that contains some two million people. Nonetheless, in twenty-first-century pop culture, depictions of Navy SEALS and other élite fighters in the diffuse and secretive war on terror have come to stand in for the American military as a whole. Hegseth has advocated for operators accused or convicted of war crimes to be pardoned, and routinely references special ops as the embodiment of the “warrior ethos” he strives for. “When the broader force has strayed from the path, has strayed from sanity, has strayed from standards, you have been keepers of the flame,” he told a room of special-ops members in 2025.

Operators are supposed to look a certain way that is decidedly high-T—“You don’t have to be six-foot-three, two hundred and twenty-five pounds, and be able to bench four hundred pounds, but it helps,” the Montana congressman Ryan Zinke, a former SEAL, told Politico. But the American military as a whole looks much more like the country it serves. In 2022, two-thirds of active-duty American service members were overweight or obese, and twenty-one per cent qualified as obese. Given this context, JAMA’s finding that a similar proportion of men might be found to have borderline low testosterone comes as no surprise.

“I think there’s a decent amount of this which is related to just a kind of feeling of humiliation,” the writer Phil Klay, a former marine, told me. “We’re in the wake of failed wars, right? And in the wake of failed wars, there’s always some asshole who is gonna want to say that the reason that we bailed is because we weren’t hard enough, we weren’t cool enough,” he said. Recurrent failures in the Middle East, of course, are low-T; “no fatties” is a plank in Hegseth’s response to those failures.

To live in a state of high T, conversely, is to project a kind of indestructibility. Kennedy once reported that Mehmet Oz had told him that President Trump has “the highest testosterone levels that he’s ever seen for an individual over seventy years old,” and remarked that the President has “the constitution of a deity.” Recently, a young man named Gabe Poirot was interviewed on Fox News about the eighteen days he spent in a coma, in 2021, during which he claims to have met Jesus. “Talk about testosterone!” he marvelled to Kayleigh McEnany. He’s a mustang on my behalf.” In a 2024 survey, nearly eighty per cent of Republican men reported that they thought America had become “too soft and feminine.” Rising anxiety that the future feels suspiciously female may be precipitating the demand for T prescriptions. But the desire also reflects an increasing faith that medicine can ameliorate more ancient types of dread.

In 2012, Stephen Colbert joked that “low T” was “previously known as getting older.” Indeed, in the past decade, death itself began to seem like the ultimate low-T activity. Lower testosterone levels have been associated with higher age-adjusted mortality in some studies, though no trial has demonstrated that higher testosterone leads to a longer life. Still, the idea has stuck. “People are, like, ‘Well, you’re not a younger man anymore’—well, duh? Like, people just want you to wither away and die,” the podcaster and testosterone evangelist Joe Rogan once said. It’s hard to picture how a guy like Rogan, Hegseth, or Kennedy eventually decides to stop juicing. One imagines them as forever brawny, neck veins popping, even as the mortal coil snakes tighter around their chests. ♦

Un negocio con muy buen olfato

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Más de la mitad de los hogares españoles cuenta con una mascota, un indicativo de la pujanza de un sector que mueve a 15.121 empresas, da trabajo a 91.700 personas y que trata de atender el creciente interés de los dueños por la calidad de vida de sus perros y gatos

Los nuevos hábitos de vida de los animales de compañía ya no caben en una tienda de mascotas tradicional... Se extienden por las clínicas veterinarias, los laboratorios farmacéuticos, las carnicerías, incluso por las guarderías y los salones de belleza. Cada vez más propietarios, o tutores, como prefieren llamarlos en el sector, tratan a sus perros y gatos como miembros de la familia y ya no buscan únicamente cubrir las necesidades básicas, sino mejorar su bienestar. Y esto se traduce en un auge del sector, explica Kike Domingo, fundador de Wild Balance y uno de los protagonistas de este capítulo de El poder de las empresas, el pódcast de los que hacen. Su compañía, que comenzó en 2020 con un pequeño local cerca de la estación de Joaquín Sorolla, en Valencia, se ha especializado en hamburguesas para perros que siguen la dieta BARF (por sus siglas en inglés, biologically appropriate raw food, que significa alimento crudo biológicamente apropiado), un modelo de nutrición a partir de ingredientes frescos y crudos.

La revolución en el cuidado animal ha comenzado por la alimentación. El pienso común ha dado paso a otros que no tienen saborizantes ni colorantes artificiales, con conceptos como el NøPienso®, de la propia Wild Balance, producto en el que se observan a simple vista los ingredientes, como la carne y las verduras, y a dietas alternativas sin cereales, hipoalergénicas o que replican la de los humanos. Y recientemente se ha extendido al campo de la cosmética, para incluir productos ecológicos y con formulaciones más respetuosas con las mascotas, señala Alba Ciordia, fundadora de The Doog Life, marca ecológica que comercializa champús, dentífricos, espumas de limpieza en seco y suplementos alimenticios para perros y gatos, quien comparte protagonismo con Domingo en el pódcast de Banco Sabadell.

Cuenta Ciordia que se inició en el sector gracias a una de sus dos perritas, Claire, a la que los productos convencionales le producían reacciones cutáneas debido a la presencia de determinados ingredientes sintéticos. Encontrar alternativas no era sencillo. Por eso, Ciordia se formó en campos como la nutrición para perros y gatos y la fitoaromaterapia, un tipo de tratamientos complementarios a partir de aceites esenciales y extractos vegetales. Con ese conocimiento se embarcó en el desarrollo de soluciones junto a laboratorios especializados. Lo que comenzó como una búsqueda personal terminó por convertirse en una marca que hoy comercializa más de 30 referencias.

La conversación entre los empresarios sirve como hilo conductor para explicar un sector que hoy representa el 0,38% del producto interior bruto (PIB) español, cuenta con 15.121 empresas y da trabajo a 91.700 personas, de acuerdo a las estimaciones de la Asociación Española de la Industria y el Comercio del Sector del Animal de Compañía (AEDPAC). Su secretario general, Ignasi Solana, celebra, en conversación telefónica, el éxito de esta industria que, según él “aún se encuentra dando sus primeros pasos”.

Un sector en expansión. El negocio del cuidado de los animales de compañía crece cada año, de acuerdo con el Informe sectorial 2026 de la Asociación Madrileña de Veterinarios de Animales de Compañía, que analiza su evolución a nivel nacional. La facturación de las clínicas veterinarias, los laboratorios farmacéuticos y los fabricantes de alimentación aumentó un 7,4% en 2025 respecto al año anterior. Una cifra similar a la que ofrece el informe Pet-money: la economía de las mascotas y su impacto en el hogar español, de EAE Business School, que cuantifica la expansión en el 8,3%. Además, el sector exporta cada año más, en 2025, el 30% del valor del mercado de la producción de alimentos fue a Europa, Magreb, América y Sudeste Asiático, destaca Santiago de Andrés, secretario general de la Asociación de Fabricante de Alimentos para Animales de Compañía (ANFAAC).

Basado en productos de primera necesidad. Se caracteriza por una demanda constante en artículos de alimentación y atención sanitaria, lo que genera actividades con ingresos recurrentes, como las suscripciones de alimentación para recibir la comida de la mascota en casa, los planes veterinarios y los seguros.

Diversificado y con enorme potencial para la innovación y la especialización. El sector se expande continuamente hacia nuevos modelos de negocio como la cosmética canina y felina, el ocio y los viajes. También está abierto al desarrollo de productos nuevos en campos como la nutrición especializada, la telemedicina y los dispositivos inteligentes.

Con consumidores que buscan lo mejor para los animales a su cargo. La cesta de la compra para perros y gatos cada vez se parece más a la de los humanos. A veces, incluso, la supera en calidad en determinados artículos, especialmente en los alimentos, explica Ignasi Solana, de AEDPAC. “El ejemplo más claro es que los responsables de perros y gatos apuestan por comida de marca antes que por las genéricas, conocidas como marcas blancas, porque la asocian a mayor calidad. Es un fenómeno que se extiende a otros campos como la cosmética y que nosotros denominamos como premiumización”, indica para describir la estrategia comercial que consiste en ofrecer productos o servicios que son percibidos por su presentación o su precio como de mayor calidad.

Luchar contra la desinformación. Internet y, en especial, las redes sociales han multiplicado la información que reciben los propietarios o tutores, lo que puede generar confusión y dificulta la capacidad para distinguir los contenidos basados en evidencia científica, en publicidad, en opinión o en pseudociencia. Santiago de Andrés, de ANFAAC, señala que aún hay margen de mejora en la comunicación entre veterinarios y tutores, con datos precisos y basados en ciencia.

Atender a la sostenibilidad. La fabricación de alimentos implica un gran consumo de recursos a través de la producción ganadera. Además, genera gran cantidad de emisiones contaminantes. Los piensos, aperitivos y productos de higiene requieren de envases plásticos muchas veces difíciles de reciclar. La industria ya trabaja en proteínas alternativas y en el aprovechamiento de productos de la industria alimentaria para desarrollar formulaciones de menor impacto. Igualmente, investiga nuevos métodos de almacenamiento y distribución de los productos que permitan reciclar y reutilizar envases.

Por otro, la irrupción de la telemedicina y los dispositivos inteligentes abren nuevas oportunidades en el sector sanitario, pero requieren de grandes inversiones.

Contribuir a la creación de espacios adaptados a las mascotas. Pluralistic: The death of history's most successful publisher (20 Oct 2026)

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

I published my first story in 1988, when I was 17 years old. Today, at 55, I've published more than 30 books, with three more coming in the next year or so. In the intervening years, I've spent a lot of time thinking and talking about publishing.

I've had plenty of opportunity to reflect on publishing: I've been a bookseller and worked in libraries, I wrote a column for Publishers Weekly, self-published a few books of my own, published the first-ever book licensed under Creative Commons, and broke several Kickstarter records for audiobook projects. But most of all, I've worked with publishing professionals: publicists, marketing directors, operations people, copyeditors, and, above all, editors.

My first novel editor was the magnificent Patrick Nielsen Hayden, who supported my publishing career from the very start, donating to help send me to the Clarion science fiction writers' workshop and inviting me to come read slush at Tor Books whenever I was in New York. Patrick didn't teach me everything I know about publishing, but he sure taught me a lot about publishing, and hardly a day goes by without my having cause to reflect on one of our many discussions over our decades-long professional and personal association.

Out of all the things I learned about publishing from Patrick, one truth stands out above all: publishing is "the act of making something public." Publishing may or may not involve editing, layout, printing, binding, marketing or other activities that we associate with publishing houses, but publishing always involves identifying a work, identifying an audience, and finding ways to connect that work to that audience.

This definition does a lot of useful work. For one thing, it locates publishing as part of the hard problem of human society, namely, "getting people to care about something that matters to you." Beyond publishing, this is the crux of politics, religion and commerce:

https://pluralistic.net/2021/07/04/self-publishing/

For another, this definition lets us understand that (for most of its existence) Google was the most successful publisher in human history. No other institution of any kind connected more works with more audiences. Lots of people have heard of Google's wildly unsuccessful motto "Don't be evil," but far fewer are familiar with Google's wildly successful mission statement, "organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful."

But all of that is in the past tense. With its AI projects – AI summaries, the Gemini chatbot – Google has abandoned the practice of "connecting works with audiences." Today, Google's function is to plunder all the works on the internet and chop them up and arrange them in an unimaginably vast, multidimensional array for the explicit purpose of preventing audiences from connecting with those works.

For most of its history, Google operated in a virtuous cycle with publishers whereby they allowed Google to crawl their sites and Google located audiences and connected them with the pages on those sites. Today, that cycle has turned vicious, with Google acting as a parasite that extracts value from the web's publishers and offers nothing in return.

This is entirely in keeping with the enshittification hypothesis: that once a platform is liberated from the disciplining forces of competition, regulation, labor and interoperability, it will squeeze its suppliers and users and harvest all the value for itself:

https://pluralistic.net/2023/01/21/potemkin-ai/#hey-guys

But AI represents a new phase in enshittification. Google has assayed enshittificatory gambits in the past, like deliberately worsening its search results in order to force you to search repeatedly, which gave the company more chances to show you ads:

https://pluralistic.net/2024/04/24/naming-names/#prabhakar-raghavan

But as sleazy as those gambits were, they were profitable. In some important sense, they were sustainable. Assuming Google could carry on indefinitely scamming its users in this way, they'd continue to bring in more cash than they spent, and continue to be the world's most successful publisher, connecting works and audiences (albeit in a deliberately degraded way).

Contrast this with AI, the money-losingest endeavor in the history of human civilization. Google is losing hundreds of billions of dollars on AI. The more AI Google does, the worse the company's financial footing becomes. If Google does this for long enough, it may cease to exist altogether. This collapse can only be prolonged by Google's ongoing extraction of value from web publishers, who no longer have their works connected with audiences via Google search results. Instead, Google usurps those audiences, shows them as many ads as it can, and still loses money in the bargain.

In Tim Wu's Age of Extraction, Wu lays out a thesis about how intermediaries – middlemen who perform a useful function connecting different groups, firms and individuals – are prone to developing "main character syndrome," abandoning their role as facilitators for others and abusing their position to make themselves the prime beneficiary of the transactions they facilitate:

https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/691177/the-age-of-extraction-by-tim-wu/

Google has a scorching case of main character syndrome, and nowhere is that more obvious than in its AI-fueled transformation into web-scale parasite. The fact that this gambit might well destroy Google and the publishers the company once served – rather than merely enshittifying search with worse results and more ads – makes the whole thing look more like a Mafia bust-out than the enshittification of the first half of the 2020s:

It's certainly an ignominious end for history's most successful publisher (as was).

‘Too big to not fail’: Google outage exposes UK reliance on a handful of cloud providers

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Our dependence on internet services of a few key tech companies leaves us vulnerable to cloud failures, bugs and more, experts say

It happened again. A power outage hit a datacentre in London, and things cascaded from there: multiple banking services faltered, court hearings were put on hold and patients reported temporary glitches on the NHS app.

The datacentre in question was in Google’s europe-west2-b zone, one of more than a dozen “zones” that the US search giant maintains in Europe to service its clients.

The outages – which have affected, according to the website Downdetector, Royal Mail, Revolut, HSBC and Lloyds bank, among others, are so far not of the scale of a similar cascade this time last year, when Amazon Web Services went down for nearly half a day.

That outage hit a far bigger node – Amazon’s us-east-1 – in a concentrated region of Virginia, which hosts the greatest density of datacentres on Earth. It saw cascading effects worldwide: taking out the Roblox game and Signal messaging app and causing smart mattresses worldwide to suddenly heat overnight and toast their occupants.

Today’s problems appeared to peak at about 9am, and by mid-afternoon were somewhat resolved, according to reports submitted to Downdetector. Because Google Cloud stores data and runs operations for a number of businesses, there were widespread knock-on effects – mostly confined to UK businesses.

Revolut confirmed “a service disruption affecting multiple services”, but added that “all our customers’ money and data are safe”. Lloyd’s said its app was working, but “we understand some [people] are having issues with certain online services this morning”.

HM Courts and Tribunal Service, which handles remote video hearings in courts across England and Wales, said it was “aware of an issue affecting Cloud Video Platform,” and was “working with our technology partners to restore service”.

Like the far bigger outage last year, it’s a reminder of the physical infrastructure – often creaking and provisional – that underpins most of the virtual world.

Michał “rysiek” Woźniak, an internet infrastructure expert, said the outages were a reminder of the increasing concentration of the internet and vital services on the infrastructure of a few key companies – the “hyperscalers”.

“Hyperscalers have become too big to not fail. But worse, they’ve become so deeply ingrained into the very fabric of our digital lives that switching away from them seems unimaginable.”

The Guardian has reported that British police keep vital data on Microsoft’s Azure cloud, despite being apparently aware that this exposes sensitive information to US government insiders, hostile foreign actors and outages like today’s. Up to 60% of the UK government’s infrastructure is hosted on cloud services – mostly Amazon, Microsoft and Google.

“I am sure Google’s engineers are doing everything they can to mitigate them, but it is unreasonable to have a handful of gigantic companies whose outages time and again cause public institutions or large swaths of the digital economy to stop working en masse,” said Woźniak.

Last year’s AWS outage was later revealed to have been caused not by a power outage, but by a bug in automation software. A separate outage was caused by an AI tool that Amazon had adopted after laying off tens of thousands of staff last autumn.

Woźniak pointed at an announcement this year from Google indicating that it was also embracing AI, with 75% of all new code “now AI-generated and approved by engineers”.

“One can only wonder how that affects the reliability and quality of Google’s services. Multiple large outages at another hyperscaler, AWS, a year ago have been linked to internal AI tooling.”

A Google spokesperson said: “We have resolved a disruption to Google Cloud services at a third-party facility in our europe-west2 cloud region due to a power event. Services have been restored. We will update our public status dashboard with an analysis of the incident.”
