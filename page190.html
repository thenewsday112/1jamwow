Pluralistic: Adversarial interoperability is a team sport (21 Oct 2026)

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

Enshittification is particularly galling because (in theory, at least), digital technology should be really hard to enshittify.

That's because the only digital computer we know how to build is the "Turing-complete, universal von Neumann machine," which is to say, a calculating engine capable of computing every valid program. In some deep, important, foundational sense, every computer is the same as every other computer. Sure, they vary in terms of speed, storage and RAM, but given enough time and electricity, every computer can run every program.

This has profound implications for enshittification. Say a tech boss takes away a feature you value (as when Sonos pushed an update that removed nearly all the functionality from its "smart" speakers), or adds a feature you hate (like when Chamberlain blocked third-party apps from controlling its garage doors so it could force its customers to view seven ads before they could open or close their garages). At that point, a programmer can write an "interoperable" program that restores the good feature or blocks the bad one.

This program can be costlessly and instantaneously distributed to every affected technology user, and those users will likely pay something for it. After all, enshittification is a way to shift value away from technology users to tech companies, which makes the case for you paying for disenshittification technology that wrestles that value back.

All of that means that, in a state of nature, companies that are contemplating enshittification need to worry that their greed will tempt new companies to enter the market and poach their customers and suppliers. This alone might be enough to frighten companies off from enshittificatory gambits, and if companies press ahead regardless, then they open market opportunities for better companies that can costlessly, instantaneously usurp the original manufacturer's relationship with their entire affected customer base.

That's how it should work. But in 1998, Bill Clinton signed the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, and Section 1201 of the DMCA establishes a felony (punishable by a five-year prison sentence and a $500,000 fine) for reverse-engineering and modifying technology without the manufacturer's permission. Then, the US Trade Rep spent the next 25 years making sure that every US trading partner had an identical (or even worse!) "anticircumvention" law:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/05/26/babyish-radical-extremists/#cancon

The result is a grotesquely asymmetrical digital world, in which companies are free to use the flexibility of digital computers to downgrade or brick your digital devices, but where you commit a crime if you avail yourself of a countermeasure to get more out of something you own. This is the core technological factor at play in enshittification.

Enter AI. Thanks to vibe-coding, everyday people are rolling their own software for their devices and building the tools to shatter the digital locks that manufacturers use to block disenshittification. An excellent article by 404 Media's Jason Koebler runs down some of these hacks, including drop-in replacements for smart TV software that blocks the endless ads and surveillance that have made TVs all but unusable:

https://www.404media.co/reverse-engineer-anything-is-the-new-wild-west-of-ai/

These AI tools mix several techniques to disenshittify users' devices. One major component is automating the installation of existing alternative firmware for these devices that have been (possibly illegally) created by hobbyists and uploaded to various code repositories. Installing these alternative OSes is typically a gnarly task, requiring the user to do a lot of complex, fiddly, highly technical work to bypass their devices' digital locks.

In a world without anticircumvention law, a company that sold alternative OSes could hire a ton of programmers and UX developers to smooth over this process, but this is beyond the reach of a typical hobbyist or even a community of hobbyists, especially if they must operate in the shadows, under the threat of civil and criminal liability.

There's a useful analogy here to the trajectory of desktop versions of GNU/Linux. The first time I tried to install Linux (on an original iMac, in the late 1990s), the installation process included a multi-week post-installation wrangle that saw me (often unsuccessfully) compiling key modules from source code and then painstakingly configuring them to get the computer's graphics and I/O working reliably.

Fast forward 30+ years and installing Linux is trivial, thanks to highly capitalized companies like Canonical (makers of Ubuntu, the flavor of Linux I've used for nearly 20 years now). Installing Ubuntu today merely requires that you download the OS to a thumbdrive, reboot your computer using the drive as a startup disk, and click "install." Canonical's installer automatically hunts down the correct software for your hardware and configures it. Canonical writes and contributes to a lot of parts of the Linux OS, but the most important work it does is the fiddly, painstaking, labor- and capital-intensive work of perfecting that installer so it Just Works.

Much of what a vibe-coded jailbreaking tool does is stand in for Canonical, but for hobbyist OSes for smart TVs and speakers and many other devices. These LLMs patiently research every weird error and peccadillo and parse through message boards and logfiles detailing other hobbyists' struggles to get the code working and methodically apply and adapt those solutions until your device Just Works.

Some of these vibe-coded interop tools go farther than that. According to Koebler's coverage, some device owners had their coding assistants scratch-build entirely new, heavily customized OSes for their devices, complete with bypasses that allow this new code to be installed despite the manufacturer's countermeasures which are designed to prevent this. Delightfully, many of these users have created software that revives their bricked, discontinued devices. This poses the possibility of a world in which manufacturers no longer have the last word in your use of your own property: the mere fact that the manufacturer lost interest in a device and killswitched it does not mean that you must consign that valuable device to a landfill to become immortal, toxic e-waste.

All of this is good news. Some of the most sophisticated jailbreaking tools (such as those on the super viral "Reverse Engineer Anything" Github page) run locally, on your own computer, without invoking the "frontier models" that the big, awful AI companies have created:

This is good even if you're just modifying your own devices to get them to more closely conform to your own use-case. LLMs are genuinely good at coming up with the abstruse and obscure configuration lines and other incantations to get your technology working. Recently, I used a local model to fine-tune the power-management on my new laptop in ways that go beyond the options that are exposed through user-friendly graphic interfaces. The LLM spat out a grub "GRUB_CMDLINE_LINUX_DEFAULT" line that (again) Just Worked.

The last time I had to do this, I spent days painstakingly trouble-shooting options by trial-and-error. Using a chatbot to fill in the gaps left by decisions about which configuration options to place within reach of regular users and which to reserve for power-users has made my computer much more useful to me for the things I want to do, like writing and making collages.

The reality is that it's just not practical for technology developers to turn every conceivable function of your computer into a simple-to-use, accessible tool. A large part of technology design is about leaving things out (or, as my favorite Eno Oblique Strategy goes, "Be the first person to not do something that no one else has ever thought of not doing before"). No matter how thoughtful and diligent a technology designer is, they will never fully anticipate the needs and exigencies of their users. That's why the motto of the disability rights movement is "Nothing about us without us."

The users of technology know more about their use-cases than can ever be captured through a requirements gathering exercise and user testing. One of the best and proudest legacies of technology is the lineage of "user-friendly" programming tools that let nontechnical people design their own applications that exactly met their needs, from Hypercard to Visual Basic to Scratch, a lineage that vibe-coding tools are arguably part of:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/25/other-people/#right-there

But these tools are not perfect (nothing is). For all that Hypercard lets you do many things that would otherwise require a skilled programmer, it was also always limited relative to, say, writing MacOS code in Objective C. Your Hypercard program might do exactly what you needed it to, but slowly, and sometimes unreliably, and with serious limitations, such as a relatively low ceiling for the number of records you could add to a database.

Vibe-coded personal apps aren't necessarily subject to these limitations, but they come with some significant risks. Vibe-code can have serious security vulnerabilities that expose the user to things like identity theft or sextortion. It's conceivable that a vibe-coded garage-door opener app might make it possible for attackers to open your garage door when you're not home (or worse, when you are!). Depending on your risk-tolerance and expertise, these might be acceptable prices, or they might be too high.

But it's important to clearly identify the problem here: if the alternative OS you install on your smart TV exposes you to privacy-invading hack attacks, the problem is the defect in that software. The problem isn't that you had the temerity to rip out the manufacturer's own software and replace it with code of your choosing. You bought that TV, it's yours, and you don't owe the manufacturer an obligation to expose yourself to its own, built-in, intentional surveillance and invasive ads.

Here's a parallel: you have the right to put any shoelaces in your running shoes. Sure, Nike might prefer you to lace up your Jordans with its expensive OEM laces, but that's Nike's problem, not yours. Now, Nike might argue that by using no-name laces, you run the risk of having a lace break while you're running, which could also break your ankle. That's certainly a possibility: shoelaces do break, and Nike might be justifiably proud of the quality of its laces. But if your generic laces snap (and so does your ankle), the problem is that the shoelaces you chose were defective. The problem is not that you chose someone else's laces.

Your stuff is yours. You can put anyone's milk on your cereal, not just the milk that Kellogg's endorses. If the milk you choose is sour and you have to pour your cereal down the toilet, that's too bad, but it doesn't mean that Kellogg's should have the right to shame you for having the temerity to choose the milk you preferred, and it certainly doesn't militate for DMCA-style enforcement to coerce you into using Kellogg's official milk in the name of protecting you from food wastage.

I raise this because there's a live debate over this subject, which is well represented in the latest 404 Media podcast:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UbG1qwxMtU0

In the podcast, Koebler and his 404 co-founder Joseph Cox express misgivings about various possible uses of "slop interop." Koebler discusses the fact that John Deere sells the same physical tractor at many different price-points, depending on which software a farmer chooses, and raises the possibility that a farmer might vibe-code software that replicates the high-end functionality without paying for it. Koebler discusses two possible downsides to this: first, that the vibe-coded software might be defective and expose the farmer to risk; and second, a question about whether it's fair or just for a farmer to get those additional functions of their tractor without paying for them.

I think the first objection is entirely legitimate. The software you run on your expensive tractor should be secure, both for your own benefit and for the security of our food supply. That's actually a pretty good reason to ditch John Deere's own software, which is very insecure:

https://pluralistic.net/2022/05/08/about-those-kill-switched-ukrainian-tractors/

But I think the second question – whether it's legit to get this functionality without paying John Deere for it – is spurious. Sure, if John Deere provides you with that functionality, then they can send you the bill for it. But if you provide yourself with that functionality, using software you procured independently of John Deere, then John Deere has no right to get paid for it, and they certainly shouldn't have the right to stop you.

That tractor is yours. The fact that John Deere manufactured it is irrelevant. If you pay a contractor to build a house for you and later on pay a different contractor to add another bathroom to it, you don't owe the first contractor anything. It's your house (and if that bathroom has a leaky toilet, that's a matter between you and the second contractor, and not a reason to force you to only use your original contractor).

The law defines a narrow slice of functions that manufacturers get to control: those ideas that a patent examiner deems "novel, useful and nonobvious." It's legit (and good) for commercial operators to copy and improve the ideas of their rivals and competitors. It's why skilled tradespeople cluster together in the same geographic area.

Take Clerkenwell, the neighborhood in London where precision machining was invented by the jewelers and machinists who clustered there during the Longitude rewards. The reason all those competitors sought such close promixity to one another was because they wanted to copy the new techniques their rivals were developing (even if that meant that their rivals would be able to copy them):

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_rewards

If you're old enough to remember the advent of spreadsheets and the rollout of Visicalc, Lotus 1-2-3 and Excel, you'll know that every time a software vendor invented a new spreadsheet feature, all the other ones immediately copied it. This wasn't bad for spreadsheets: it was good for spreadsheets, just as copying your competitors was good for precision machining in Clerkenwell.

Everything from GNU/Linux (a feature-for-feature clone of AT&T Unix) to SAMBA (a plug-compatible clone of Microsoft's SMB) to iWork (file-compatible, feature compatible clones of MS Office's apps) came about through this "adversarial interoperability." What's more, the heyday of this kind of adversarial interop was also the heyday of disenshittification, the era in which any anti-feature installed by your existing vendor could be countered by a disenshittifying patch from a new one:

https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2019/06/adversarial-interoperability-reviving-elegant-weapon-more-civilized-age-slay

The question of whether vibe-coded software is any good is separate from the question of whether it's good to replace the software in your devices, even if you replace it with software that has features that the manufacturer would normally charge a premium for. You are under no obligation to arrange your commercial affairs to the financial benefit of manufacturers, and anyone who claims to be surprised that someone they sold a device to used it to benefit themselves at the manufacturer's expenses is either disingenuous…or unserious. You bought it, it's yours.

But just as this vibe-coded adversarial interop has drawn both valid and spurious criticism, it's also drawn excitement that is partially justified and partially overblown.

When it comes to reviving bricked devices or rooting out the surveillance and ads from your smart TV, vibe-coded interop tools are genuinely exciting. These are the kinds of enshittification that can truly be remediated with individual jailbreaks.

But the real, meaty examples of enshittification can't be addressed on an individual level – they require systemic fixes. If you jailbreak your iPhone so that it can use alternative app stores, that fact is not sufficient to cause those alternative app stores to come into existence. If we want to stop Apple from levying a $100b "app tax" on the digital economy, it's not enough for individuals to vibe-code their own iPhone patches. We need to repeal the law that bans jailbreaking iPhones, so that a would-be alternative app store can raise capital and pay programmers and tech support staff and salespeople to assemble rival catalogs of software and help customers figure out how to get up their phones to use it.

The same goes for jailbreaking your printer so it accepts generic ink. Sure, this may work for you if you're up for refilling your own cartridges, and I'm sure that you can find someone on Alibaba who will sell you ink by the gallon. But the mere fact that a few users have secretly and privately jailbroken their inkjets won't conjure up a generic inkjet cartridge manufacturing sector. For that to happen, we need to make jailbreaking printers legal, not just possible.

Indeed, if we want to improve the quality of all that jailbreaking code the chatbots are writing and circulating, we need to be able to put those jailbreaks in public repositories for public inspection and public critique. It's possible for some of the code produced by an LLM to be slop, and for other code to be fine, and for yet a third tranche of code to be remediable slop that can be made robust and reliable through auditing and refactoring. You can't figure out which is which if you have to keep that vibe code a secret on pain of a long prison-sentence.

I think there's good cause to be excited at the prospect of getting your devices working better, or just working at all. I think that even if the code stops working after a while, that's still fine – either you got more use out of your device than you would have without the new code, or maybe you just make more code that runs for a while for you, and then you do it again.

These individual improvements to your devices and technology are worth celebrating. But they are no substitute for systemic intervention. If we're going to have a new, good internet, a post-American internet that truly disenshittifies America's defective tech exports and replaces them with robust, transparent digital public goods, we need to make reverse-engineering legal again. Once we fix our policy, then we can fix our code:

Meta hit with $25 billion penalty for misleading Facebook users

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion in penalties Wednesday after a jury found in September that the social media giant misled users about their data privacy.

Why it matters: The penalties come nearly a decade after the Cambridge Analytica scandal first broke and signal that Big Tech can face significant financial consequences for misleading users about their privacy.

Driving the news: Judge Francis Mathew determined the penalty amount Wednesday, handing New Mexico a win as the only state to take its claims to trial after 48 others settled.

  • Mathew concluded New Mexico was entitled to $219.5 billion based on the state's maximum $5,000 penalty multiplied by 43,899,720 violations. He reduced that to $25 billion.

What they're saying: "This ruling, and the Attorney General's approach to this enforcement, makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico," C.J. Mahoney, Meta's chief legal officer, told Axios in an emailed statement.

  • "At its core, this lawsuit seeks to impose a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online. We are confident we will prevail on appeal. In the meantime, Meta will continue to build a safe and free platform to connect millions of Americans, grow small businesses, and help the country maintain its competitive edge."

Catch up quick: In September, a New Mexico jury said Meta had committed 43 million "willful violations" of the New Mexico Unfair Practices Act.

  • Mathew found that Facebook's privacy controls failed to give users meaningful control over their data and that third-party app developers could access their information through the platform's systems.
  • Mathew found Mark Zuckerberg knew users didn't understand how data privacy works, and exploited that, noting that the CEO had once written that users "probably don't understand their current privacy settings or care about who sees their updates."
  • Zuckerberg also wrote that the company should give users "a chance to change their privacy settings but obviously don't encourage it."

Zoom out: The suit followed reporting that revealed data firm Cambridge Analytica had harvested data from more than 50 million Facebook users to craft political ads during the 2016 election.

  • After the scandal, Facebook promised more transparency, but the court found Meta failed to consistently inform users when their data was misused.

Japan is Making it Harder for Non-Citizens to Become Permanent Residents

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tourists from around the world continue to flock to Japan’s bustling megacities and quiet onsen towns, generating a record-setting (and sometimes unmanageable) influx of travelers: an increase from 25.1 million in 2023 to 42.7 million in 2025, according to the country’s tourism board. In the face of this boom, Japan is setting more boundaries with visitors, including levying an increased tourist tax and, as of October 1, making it harder for non-citizens to obtain residence status.

The number of foreign residents in Japan reached a record 3.96 million people in June 2025, prompting Japan’s immigration agency to tighten its eligibility guidelines for the country's permanent residency permit, which is available to foreign workers who have continuously lived in Japan for at least 10 years. The new rules are just one part of a larger framework of policies adopted by the Japanese government on January 23, 2026 called the “Comprehensive Measures for the Acceptance and Orderly Coexistence of Foreign Nationals.”

The government says the policies are designed to address “anxieties and feelings of unfairness felt by some citizens as the number of foreign residents increases,” Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi wrote in a September 26 post on X. That means applicants to Japan's foreign residence permit will soon face stricter language proficiency requirements, more expensive fees, and a higher income threshold.

If you're pursuing permanent residency or a long-term stay in Japan, here's what you need to know.

The new requirements dictate that applicants must earn more than the average Japanese household income (which was about $36,350 per year in 2024, according to a survey by the country’s health, labor, and welfare ministry). In addition, applicants must now show pension benefits that match what they would have received after 30 years in the country’s employee pension program. (If the applicant falls short on the pension requirement, the guidelines say they can supplement with additional “financial assets," but does not provide specifics beyond that.)

The government also announced a dramatic 20-fold hike in the application fee, which is now 20,000 yen, or about $1,260. That said, the cost is still relatively affordable compared to permanent residency-related applications in countries like the UK, which cost 3,226 British pounds (about $4,261), and the US, which can cost upwards of $10,000, according to Arton.

Qualifying for Japan's permanent residency visa isn't just about financial eligibility. Applicants will also need a proficient understanding of the Japanese language, which the guidelines define as at or above a B1 level on the Japanese-Language Proficiency Test; however, it’s not yet clear how applicants will be asked to demonstrate their language ability.

The income changes will retroactively affect applications filed from April 2026. Other changes, such as the pension requirement, will be implemented starting April 1, 2027.

“For Americans thinking of making Japan their long-term home, the message is clear: financial security alone is no longer enough,” Armand Arton, CEO of citizenship firm Arton Capital, tells Condé Nast Traveler. “Applicants must also demonstrate a genuine commitment to living and working in the country, as well as integrating into Japanese society.”

My path to seeking reproductive healthcare outside the US began in the same way it would have at home: It started with life not going according to my neatly organized plan.

The writer in Oaxaca, where she went to freeze her eggs

In summer 2025, I found myself freshly divorced and looking out over the proverbial fertility cliff of turning 35. I suddenly felt the need to act fast. I was unsure about having children, but I wanted to keep that door open for the future.

As a self-employed writer with health insurance that leaves much to be desired, I knew going abroad for a more affordable option was my only choice—at least the only financially responsible one. That’s how I found myself in the office of Doctor Daniela Paz Cervantes, an OBGYN in Oaxaca, Mexico, who oversaw my care for two rounds of egg freezing just a few months later.

I’m not unique in my search for medical care abroad. Although exact numbers are difficult to track, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that “millions of US residents travel internationally for medical care each year,” listing reproductive tourism as one of the main categories of services that they seek. While I went to Mexico for my treatments, Spain, Greece, Cyprus, and Barbados are also particularly popular for fertility tourism.

Dr. Mario Vega Croker, Medical Director of Panama Fertility located in Panama City, Panama, has noticed more Americans knocking on his door. Panama Fertility opened nearly 40 years ago as a small clinic catering to local families; fast forward to today, and it’s grown into one of the most prolific clinics in Central America, with half of their patients hailing from the US and Caribbean.

“American patients are one of our fastest-growing groups, especially in gestational surrogacy, [receiving] egg donations, and IVF,” says Dr. Vega Croker. “Many come to us after putting their dreams on hold because treatment at home felt out of reach […] Finding the same expertise and quality of care at about half the cost often gives them the chance to finally begin, or to try again.”

According to FertilityIQ, a data-based fertility education platform, the average egg freezing cycle costs $16,000 in the US. Meanwhile, I spent about $7,000 for one round, which is inclusive of what I paid for flights, food, accommodations, and activities for the three weeks I was in Mexico for my treatment. And I appreciated the quality of my care: My entire cycle was incredibly thorough and personalized, even before I touched down in Oaxaca. It was a no-brainer.

Alisa Purdie traveled to Barbados Fertility Centre for her IVF treatment.

Alisa Purdie, a California-based online business coach, faced a similar set of choices when she got divorced at 31 with the added challenge of managing premature menopause, which can cause fertility issues—though her journey, from ideation to undergoing treatment, took more than a decade to come to fruition.

“After my divorce, my plan was to do embryo adoption because I was in menopause, so I didn’t have any of my own eggs,” Purdie says, referring to the option to acquire an embryo from another couple and carry it herself. “I was just sort of collecting information about all the different options as a single woman and what that [path to motherhood] might look like.”

Then she learned of a friend who had frozen her eggs in Greece, and it sparked a lightbulb. After looking into options in Turkey, Greece, and the UK, Purdie settled on the Barbados Fertility Centre not only because of the 50% cost savings compared to the US, but also because of the warmth and compassion of the staff she met during a visit and exploratory phone calls.

“The information and the data, of course, is really important,” Purdie says in reference to the clinic's 72% success rate with donor egg IVF treatments. “But it's a pregnancy. You're trying to bring a baby into the world. There also has to be kindness, patience, and positivity.”

Life took a few more turns for Purdie, and she didn't begin her fertility treatment for 15 more years, though she knew the clinic would be her path forward. By the time she reached Barbados at age 46, she had married her best friend of 12 years and arrived with a new plan: fertilize a donor egg with her husband, instead of adopting an embryo as she'd initially intended.

KI - teuer und ineffizient? Konzerne fahren Nutzung zurück

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Künstliche Intelligenz soll menschliche Arbeit automatisieren und Unternehmen produktiver machen. Doch fehlerhafte Ergebnisse, steigende Betriebskosten und überzogene Erwartungen zwingen viele Unternehmen zum Umdenken.

Der globale Arbeitsmarkt steht vor enormen Umwälzungen. Ein Bericht des Weltwirtschaftsforums zur Zukunft der Arbeitaus dem vergangenen Jahr besagt, dass bis zum Jahr 2030 weltweit rund 92 Millionen Arbeitsplätze durch Künstliche Intelligenz und damit verbundene Automatisierungsprozesse wegfallen oder sich sehr stark verändern werden.

Gleichzeitig sollen rund 170 Millionen neue Arbeitsplätze entstehen. Entsprechend hoch sind die Erwartungen vieler Firmen an die KI: Sie soll Prozesse beschleunigen, Kosten reduzieren und damit die Effizienz steigern.

Doch mittlerweile rudern einige Unternehmen schon wieder zurück. Nachdem sie bereits viel Zeit und Geld in KI investiert hatten, um diese möglichst flächendeckend einzuführen, rücken nun Fragen nach dem tatsächlichen Nutzen, den laufenden Kosten und den Risiken stärker in den Mittelpunkt.

Ein extremes Beispiel ist das globale Beratungsunternehmen Accenture. Eigenen Angaben zufolge hat Accenture insgesamt drei Milliarden US-Dollar gezielt in den Ausbau seines Data- und KI-Bereichs investiert. Jährlich soll mehr als eine Milliarde in die Fortbildung der Mitarbeiter geflossen sein - ein erheblicher Teil davon in spezielle KI-Trainings.

Accenture soll dabei eine besonders aggressive KI-Strategie verfolgen: Eine Beförderung in Führungspositionen erhalten nur Angestellte, die regelmäßig interne KI-Tools nutzen.

Dieser enorme Druck führte jedoch dazu, dass Mitarbeiter die teuren Werkzeuge auch für relativ einfache Arbeitsschritte genutzt haben sollen, um die Nutzungsanforderungen zu erfüllen: etwa für das Erstellen von Präsentationen aus Textdokumenten, das Zusammenfassen von Berichten oder für einfache Programmierungen.

"Vor ein paar Jahren waren die Anwendungen noch relativ günstig, weil die Währung, mit der man sozusagen bezahlt hat, die Daten waren", erklärt Anne-Sophie Mayer, Professorin für Digitale Arbeit an der Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität in München.

"Das hat sich jedoch grundlegend geändert. KI wird immer besser, weshalb die Lizenzkosten für hochspezialisierte Modelle stark gestiegen sind."

Für das Nutzen dieser KI-Tools müssen oft teure Tokens gekauft werden - so auch bei Accenture. Dort sind die Kosten förmlich explodiert, weshalb das Unternehmen nun die Reißleine gezogen hat.

Künftig gibt es pro Kopf nur noch ein Budget von 29 Dollar pro Monat für KI-Anwendungen, darüber hinaus gehende Kosten müssen erst von der Unternehmensführung genehmigt werden.

Doch nicht nur die Nutzung teurer KI-Tools für einfache Arbeiten wurde zum Kostenfaktor. Heraus kamen oft KI-generierte Inhalte, die zwar auf den ersten Blick professionell wirkten, bei genauerer Prüfung aber oberflächlich oder fehlerhaft waren, oder kaum zu neuen Erkenntnissen führten.

Anstatt Zeit zu sparen, wurde erheblicher Mehraufwand generiert. Mitarbeiter mussten die KI-Informationen aufwändig überprüfen, Fehler korrigieren und Ergebnisse in einen brauchbaren Zustand bringen. Dieses Phänomen, das auch schon in vielen anderen Unternehmen aufkam, ist unter dem Namen "Workslop" bekannt geworden.

Dies beklagte jüngst auch Ryan Roslansky, einer der stellvertretenden Vorstandsvorsitzenden beim Softwareriesen Microsoft, auf seinem öffentlich zugänglichen LinkedIn-Konto.

"KI-Schrott gibt es nicht nur auf Social Media. Es gibt ihn auch im Arbeitsalltag. Jemand hat mir ein Dokument geschickt, das eindeutig KI-generiert war. Ich habe es gelesen, erkannt, dass alles KI ist, es durch meine KI zusammenfassen lassen und am Ende nichts gefunden, das irgendwie neue Ideen einbrachte ... Es ist ein Teufelskreis."

KI-Expertin Anne-Sophie Mayer spricht in diesem Zusammenhang von einer problematischen "Homogenisierung von Inhalten". Bei einfachen Zusammenfassungen oder Routinearbeiten, bei denen es nicht um Kreativität gehe, seien KI-Tools durchaus sinnvoll, erklärt die Professorin.

"Aber wenn es um Handlungsempfehlungen geht oder darum, Dinge zu interpretieren, dann muss das immer noch der Mensch tun, damit Mehrwert generiert wird und auch neue Erkenntnisse zutage kommen und ich nicht meinen Wettbewerbsvorteil verliere."

Arbeitsmarktforscher sehen langfristig noch ein weiteres Problem: den Verlust von Fachwissen. "Das Outsourcing des Denkens führt zu kognitiver Verkümmerung", warnt etwa Eric So vom Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). Wenn Beschäftigte sich zu stark auf KI-Inhalte verlassen, könnten die eigenen fachspezifischen Fähigkeiten nachlassen.

"Das ist tatsächlich eine große Gefahr", sagt auch Anne-Sophie Mayer, "weil KI ja auch immer suggeriert, dass man recht hat. Weshalb man ja nicht angeregt wird, Dinge zu hinterfragen. Natürlich werden die KI-Tools immer besser. Und dann wird man als Mensch natürlich sehr bequem."

Noch problematischer wird es, wenn dann fehlerhafte Informationen nicht kontrolliert und in interne Prozesse übernommen werden und sich dort weiterverbreiten. "Das muss man einfach erkennen: KI macht Fehler. Und die Verantwortung für einen Output bleibt immer beim Menschen."

Ein weiteres Problem vieler Unternehmen: die oft nur unzureichende Einbindung der Beschäftigten bei der Einführung von KI. "Wenn ich ein firmenspezifisches KI-Tool einführe, bin ich darauf angewiesen, dass meine Mitarbeiter helfen, dieses KI-Tool auch zu trainieren", erklärt Anne-Sophie Mayer.

Dies schüre jedoch auch viele Ängste, "etwa dass Mitarbeiter denken: Mensch, wenn ich jetzt helfe, diese KI-Tools zu trainieren, dann mache ich mich vielleicht in ein paar Jahren obsolet."

Mayer erklärt, sie selbst habe bei einigen Unternehmen miterlebt, dass Mitarbeiter sogar bewusst falsche Informationen eingepflegt hätten, aus Angst, das KI-Tool könne sie eines Tages überflüssig machen.

Zuletzt hatten Meldungen über den Facebook-Mutterkonzern Meta aufhorchen lassen, der rund 8000 Mitarbeiter entließ, nachdem man ihre tägliche Arbeit heimlich für das firmeninterne KI-Training aufgezeichnethatte.

In Deutschland schrecken noch viele Unternehmen davor zurück, KI einzusetzen. Einer Erhebung des Instituts für Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB) zufolge haben zwei Drittel aller Firmen des Landes bis Ende 2025 auf den Einsatz von KI-Tools verzichtet .

Häufig wurden von den Firmen keine geeigneten Einsatzmöglichkeiten gesehen. Einige gaben aber auch fehlendes Knowhow, inkompatible IT-Systeme oder Datenschutzhürden als Grund an.

Anne-Sophie Mayer kann diese Zurückhaltung zumindest zum Teil nachvollziehen. Ihrer Meinung nach sollten Firmen vier Punkte erfüllen, damit ein Einsatz von KI auch erfolgreich sein kann.

"Erstens: Es muss ein Anwendungsbereich definiert werden: Wo hab ich überhaupt ein Problem, und wie kann die Lösung aussehen? Der zweite Punkt ist die Datenqualität. Wenn ich einen schlechten analogen Prozess habe, werde ich auch einen schlechten digitalen Prozess haben. Wenn ich bestimmte Daten gar nicht besitze, nicht ordentlich aufbereite und dann eine KI darüberspiele, dann bringt das nichts."

Drittens, sagt die Expertin, sei es trotzdem wichtig, sich eine gewisse Offenheit gegenüber der neuen Technologie zu bewahren - "die Balance zu finden zwischen den Potentialen von KI, ohne gleichzeitig jedem Trend hinterherzurennen und sie völlig blind einzusetzen.

Und viertens: Die Befähigung von Mitarbeitern. Sie dazu anzuregen, selbst gute Anwendungsbereiche zu finden, bei denen auch sie den Mehrwert von KI erkennen können."

Las amenazas a la democracia no se manifiestan únicamente en las instituciones ni se reducen a los grandes debates parlamentarios: también se hacen visibles en la vida cotidiana, en las condiciones materiales de la población y en la manera en que una sociedad trata a sus colectivos más vulnerables.

"Trabajar y no poder vivir": la vivienda pone a prueba los derechos conquistados con la democracia

Esta ha sido una de las ideas que han atravesado el encuentro 'Democracia: derechos y libertades en peligro', organizado por elDiario.es en el café A Tres Bandas de Palma con el apoyo de MarSenses Hotels & Homes. A través de dos mesas redondas, 'España, país de libertades y derechos conquistados' y 'Colectivos en peligro: una lucha constante', representantes del ámbito sindical, universitario, empresarial y de la creación de contenido han examinado qué está poniendo en riesgo las conquistas sociales y qué herramientas existen para defenderlas. De la vivienda a la diversidad sexual, el debate ha dejado una advertencia común: los derechos no son irreversibles y su reconocimiento legal no basta para garantizar que puedan ejercerse.

En la apertura, la directora de elDiario.es en Balears, Angy Galvín, ha recordado que libertades hoy asumidas como cotidianas -casarse, divorciarse o acceder a los servicios públicos- son el resultado de conquistas históricas. La democracia, ha defendido, no se limita a votar cada cuatro años, sino que exige una ciudadanía activa y dispuesta a movilizarse cuando sus derechos están amenazados. “Esto nos enseña, al fin y al cabo, que la calle también puede legislar”, ha afirmado, aludiendo a la capacidad de las protestas para influir en las instituciones.

La primera mesa ha debatido sobre una contradicción cada vez más presente en la vida de muchas personas: tener un empleo ya no garantiza poder vivir de manera autónoma. La periodista y creadora de contenido Elena Reinés, el secretario general de UGT en Balears, Pedro Homar, y el profesor de Derecho Constitucional de la Universitat de les Illes Balears (UIB) Amir Al Hasani Maturano, moderados por Galvín, han abordado la crisis de la vivienda como un problema que desborda el mercado inmobiliario y pone a prueba la capacidad de los derechos sociales para traducirse en condiciones de vida dignas.

Reinés ha llevado al debate su experiencia en la acampada por el derecho a la vivienda en la Puerta del Sol de Madrid, una movilización que ha descrito como expresión del malestar acumulado ante las dificultades para emanciparse. “No es normal que hayamos normalizado trabajar y no poder vivir”, ha denunciado. La comunicadora ha cuestionado que se considere habitual que el alquiler absorba una parte desproporcionada del sueldo o que compartir piso siga siendo una obligación económica incluso cuando las personas esperan poder vivir de forma independiente.

La acampada también le ha servido para cuestionar la imagen de una juventud desmovilizada. Reinés ha destacado la organización de los participantes, las comisiones de trabajo, las actividades culturales y las redes de solidaridad que permiten cubrir necesidades básicas. Además, ha denunciado la respuesta policial a las protestas y ha reivindicado el derecho a manifestarse como parte de la salud democrática. “Una democracia también se mide en cómo viven las personas”, ha resumido.

Homar ha coincidido en situar la vivienda en el centro de la emergencia social. El responsable de UGT ha reivindicado la negociación colectiva y la huelga como instrumentos que han permitido conquistar derechos laborales y equilibrar la relación entre trabajadores y empresas. Sin embargo, ha advertido de que las mejoras salariales pierden capacidad para garantizar una vida digna cuando el precio de la vivienda se dispara y obliga a trabajadores de sectores como la hostelería a compartir piso e incluso habitación.

Por ello, ha reclamado sacar la vivienda de la lógica especulativa y apartarla de los intereses de los fondos de inversión. “Hay que sacarla de la concepción de bien de consumo”, ha sostenido. La reflexión ha conectado el derecho a la vivienda con el resto de las conquistas sociales: disponer de un empleo, de un convenio o de un salario no resuelve por sí solo las dificultades para acceder a una vida autónoma si las condiciones materiales impiden ejercer esa independencia.

La distancia entre el reconocimiento formal y el ejercicio efectivo de un derecho ha llevado la conversación al terreno jurídico. Al Hasani ha explicado que la Constitución y los tratados internacionales reconocen derechos fundamentales, pero que su desarrollo requiere en muchos casos leyes específicas, políticas públicas y mayorías parlamentarias capaces de impulsarlas.

El profesor ha recordado que determinadas materias vinculadas a los derechos fundamentales se regulan mediante leyes orgánicas, que necesitan mayoría absoluta en el Congreso. También ha detallado el recorrido parlamentario que puede seguir una norma, desde el debate de las enmiendas hasta su paso por el Congreso y el Senado. El proceso evidencia que las reivindicaciones ciudadanas no se convierten automáticamente en derechos efectivos: necesitan organización social, voluntad política y apoyo institucional.

La vivienda es uno de los ejemplos más claros de esa brecha. Al Hasani ha advertido de que la Constitución no la reconoce como un derecho fundamental, sino como un principio rector de la política social y económica, una diferencia que limita las posibilidades de exigir directamente su cumplimiento ante los tribunales. El profesor ha considerado que habría sido deseable dotarla de una mayor protección constitucional.

El debate sobre la vivienda y los derechos sociales ha conectado así con una idea transversal a toda la jornada: el reconocimiento formal de un derecho es importante, pero no basta si las condiciones materiales o las decisiones políticas impiden ejercerlo.

Tras la primera mesa redonda, la jornada ha puesto a prueba los conocimientos del público con el trivial “¿Cuánto sabes sobre la Constitución española?”, presentado por Albert Xamena, divulgador científico y creador de contenido en la cuenta Entre fones i neurones. Una propuesta participativa para acercar la Carta Magna a los asistentes y poner a prueba cuánto saben sobre los derechos y libertades que recoge. El ganador ha conseguido dos noches de hotel para dos personas en la cadena hotelera MarSenses.

La segunda mesa, 'Colectivos en peligro: una lucha constante', ha trasladado esa advertencia al terreno de los derechos LGTBI+ y de los discursos contrarios a la diversidad. La presidenta de Ben Amics, Tatiana Casado, ha recordado el camino recorrido desde la persecución legal de las personas homosexuales hasta el reconocimiento del matrimonio igualitario y de las familias diversas. También ha aludido a los avances relacionados con la atención sanitaria y la autonomía sobre el propio cuerpo.

“Los derechos que tanto nos ha costado conseguir pueden desaparecer de un plumazo”, ha advertido. Casado ha expresado su preocupación por la posibilidad de que parte de la juventud perciba esas conquistas como definitivas y por la confianza de quienes creen que determinadas opciones políticas no afectarán a sus propias familias. Esa sensación de seguridad, ha planteado, puede impedir reconocer que los derechos alcanzados tras décadas de reivindicación también pueden sufrir retrocesos. La advertencia ha situado la defensa de las minorías en el centro de una discusión más amplia sobre la calidad democrática. No se trata únicamente de conservar normas escritas, sino de impedir que la discriminación vuelva a ganar terreno o que los avances se debiliten mientras una parte de la sociedad los considera intocables.

La visibilidad y la igualdad también han centrado la intervención del director general de MarSenses Hotels & Homes, Rodrigo Fitaroni, quien ha alertado de que cada vez más personas LGTBI+ vuelven a ocultar su orientación sexual o identidad de género en el entorno laboral. El directivo ha descrito una empresa de casi 500 trabajadores como un reflejo de las distintas sensibilidades que conviven en la sociedad y ha cuestionado que la existencia de protocolos y formaciones sea suficiente para erradicar la discriminación. “Un curso de una hora no cambia la mentalidad de nadie”, ha señalado.

A su juicio, las medidas contra la discriminación deben traducirse en actuaciones concretas y en un compromiso efectivo de las empresas. Fitaroni ha explicado que MarSenses colabora con Ben Amics en iniciativas de sensibilización y en la incorporación de personas trans a través de su bolsa de empleo, además de haber incorporado educadores sociales a su plantilla. También ha defendido la necesidad de recurrir a profesionales especializados para avanzar hacia entornos laborales inclusivos. Fitaroni ha trasladado el debate al ámbito laboral y ha alertado de que cada vez más personas LGTBI+ vuelven a ocultar su orientación sexual o identidad de género en el trabajo. Al describir una empresa de casi 500 trabajadores como un reflejo de la sociedad, ha cuestionado que los protocolos y las formaciones sean suficientes si no existe una voluntad real de aplicarlos.

La defensa de los derechos también ha centrado la conversación sobre educación, redes sociales y convivencia. El profesor Pau Torres ha reivindicado la transmisión de los valores democráticos a través de la educación y ha recordado la experiencia de la asignatura de Educación para la Ciudadanía durante el Gobierno de José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero. A su juicio, que un derecho figure en la Constitución no significa que la sociedad pueda olvidarse de él: es necesario explicarlo, reivindicarlo y formar a nuevas generaciones capaces de comprender su importancia.

“Tenemos que aprender a soportar a quien no nos cae bien, a escucharlo y a debatir”, ha afirmado. Torres ha advertido de que la confrontación política se vuelve especialmente peligrosa cuando los dirigentes presentan al adversario como alguien dispuesto a destruir el modo de vida de sus votantes. Esa dinámica alimenta la sensación de amenaza y debilita los vínculos necesarios para sostener una sociedad plural. El diálogo, ha defendido, no implica aceptar los discursos de odio ni renunciar a los derechos fundamentales, sino aprender a gestionar las diferencias sin deshumanizar al otro.

Constança Ramis, graduada en Economía y Relaciones Internacionales y creadora de contenido, ha coincidido en la necesidad de recuperar la capacidad de debatir con quienes mantienen posiciones distintas desde el respeto a los derechos humanos. También ha alertado del papel de las redes sociales en la difusión de bulos y mensajes extremistas: desmontar una falsedad requiere más tiempo y esfuerzo que propagarla, lo que permite que los contenidos engañosos alcancen una gran difusión mientras las explicaciones contrastadas tienen más dificultades para abrirse paso.

La discusión ha cuestionado, además, que la juventud esté inevitablemente escorada hacia la derecha. Casado ha recordado el crecimiento de las manifestaciones del Orgullo en Palma y ha destacado el potencial de la universidad para formar personas con espíritu crítico. Reinés, por su parte, ha relacionado parte del malestar juvenil con la precariedad y las dificultades de acceso a la vivienda, y ha reprochado a los sectores progresistas haber desatendido durante años las redes sociales, un espacio en el que los discursos de extrema derecha han ganado presencia.

La experiencia de Brasil ha servido a Fitaroni para ilustrar los riesgos de la expansión de la extrema derecha. El directivo ha advertido de la evolución de discursos dirigidos abiertamente contra las minorías y de la facilidad con la que pueden integrarse en la conversación pública hasta dejar de percibirse como una amenaza. Durante su intervención, ha cuestionado la normalización de mensajes políticos que parecen inofensivos cuando se presentan como bromas. Ha criticado la frivolidad con la que, a su juicio, se asumen algunas declaraciones de Vox y ha advertido del riesgo de acostumbrarse a expresiones que deberían generar rechazo. La repetición de esos mensajes, ha planteado, puede desplazar los límites de lo socialmente aceptable y hacer más difícil combatirlos cuando ya han arraigado.

La desinformación, los algoritmos y la apelación a las emociones han aparecido como factores que agravan esa dinámica. Frente a ellos, los participantes han reivindicado la responsabilidad de los partidos, las empresas, las instituciones educativas y las plataformas digitales, así como la necesidad de ofrecer referentes capaces de conectar con las nuevas generaciones sin recurrir al insulto ni a la simplificación.

La mesa también ha abordado las desigualdades históricas entre el castellano y el catalán. Torres ha cuestionado que la Constitución establezca la obligación de conocer el castellano sin fijar una exigencia equivalente para las demás lenguas oficiales en sus territorios. El profesor ha relacionado esta diferencia con una situación de diglosia que ha asociado tradicionalmente el castellano a los ámbitos formales y de prestigio, mientras el catalán quedaba relegado a los espacios cotidianos. También ha defendido que el conocimiento del catalán debería ser obligatorio en territorios como Balears.

La jornada ha concluido con una reivindicación compartida: los derechos no se mantienen únicamente porque estén escritos en una ley, sino porque la sociedad los conoce, los ejerce y está dispuesta a defenderlos. La movilización ciudadana, la acción sindical, la educación y el pensamiento crítico han aparecido como herramientas fundamentales para impedir que las libertades se erosionen de manera gradual y sin una respuesta colectiva.
