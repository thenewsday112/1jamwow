Not content with renaming an international gulf and a cross-border lake, on Oct. 10, U.S. President Donald Trump recently floated the idea of renaming the Atlantic or Pacific Ocean as “Trump Ocean” or the “Ocean of the Americas” at a political rally in Clarksville, Tenn.

“We need an ocean. So, I was thinking of either the Atlantic or the Pacific,” he said to laughter and applause. “We call it ‘Trump Ocean’ or ‘Ocean of the Americas,’ something like that.”

Then, at an Oct. 21 rally in South Carolina, Trump said he asked Google’s CEO, Sundar Pichai, to rename the Atlantic Ocean on Google Maps. According to Trump, Pichai replied, “Please don’t ask us to do that.”

“So, I'll probably pass,” Trump said. “I think let's keep it the Atlantic Ocean.”

While Google has not issued a formal statement on the matter, Trump’s obsession with naming places for himself or America is remarkable. In our view, it reflects a textbook case of “toponymic narcissism”—the use of place naming to remake the world in one’s own image.

Let’s not fool ourselves: Trump knows a thing or two about branding. In fact, his business career has largely revolved around marketing the Trump brand on hotels, golf courses, and even golden sneakers. We shouldn’t be surprised to find him following the same playbook in his second presidential term.

Yet the world map is not a private real estate development. Nor can place names be reduced to a branding exercise. Place names have a greater civic purpose as shared global symbols that should transcend the personal glorification and political interests of any individual leader.

Place names form part of our intangible cultural heritage with cultural significance that will outlive the cartographic whims of President Trump. They shape our geographical imaginations and the way people perceive and understand the world. Geographical naming also has important consequences for education, international relations, and the types of messages we wish to project to the world.

For instance, it is notable that Trump has focused much of his renaming energy on places with Indigenous names, such as Lake Ontario and the Gulf of Mexico. In the case of the Atlantic Ocean, this could also be seen as an attempt to erase an etymology that points through Greece to North Africa. The name derives from the Greek for “Ocean of Atlas,” referring to the mythology of a giant who lives in the Atlas Mountains of northwestern Africa.

As place name scholars, we have spent decades studying the cultural, social, and political aspects of place naming. There is a long history of political leaders and cartographers using place naming to assert raw power by rewriting the world map. In one sense, Trump’s renaming shenanigans are simply the latest flashy example in this broader story of mapping and politics.

But Trump is not merely renaming places to project American power on the world stage. He is also making a mockery of the very act of place naming itself—and, along with it, the international norms that inform the map-making process.

The international community must reaffirm its commitment to respecting the cultural value of place naming and to articulate responsible place-naming principles, especially for geographical features that are shared across international borders. In an open letter co-authored with place name experts around the world, we propose five key principles for responsible place naming:

The first principle is recognizing that place names are culturally significant forms of intangible heritage and shared resources whose stewardship carries responsibilities extending beyond the interests of any particular government, leader, institution, or generation.

Given the cultural significance of place names, our second principle highlights the need for meaningful public participation in place naming decisions. Place names that are changed arbitrarily and without due process should not be recognized by the international community.

Thirdly, place names should not be used to proclaim cultural superiority, advance territorial ambitions, or assert exclusive national ownership over geographical spaces shared among different peoples.

Places should generally not be named for living people. Instead, place naming should serve the public interest rather than promoting the personal legacies of those seeking to commemorate themselves as a form of self-aggrandizement.

Lastly, we recognize that a shared geography need not always have a single name, since multiple place names can bear witness to the layered histories, languages, and attachments through which places acquire meaning. However, this recognition should not extend to condoning the unilateral renaming of shared geographic features without international dialogue among affected countries and communities.

If Trump moves forward with attempting to rename the Atlantic or Pacific Ocean, the international community should stand united in rejecting this attention-seeking act of cartographic malpractice and defend the cultural value of place naming as a cornerstone of our shared world map and geographical imagination.

AI's 'Black October': Critical PHP flaws set off global security scramble

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cybersecurity teams around the globe are rushing to patch PHP programming language flaws this week, in an artificial intelligence safety moment that's being described as "Black October."

Nearly 70% of all websites use PHP, according to some estimates.

"In the last 2 days there have been several dozen cyber attacks which, in isolation, would each have been the worst to happen in an entire calendar year," John Milinovich, senior director of product management at Figma, wrote in a post on X on Thursday. "Every sector from government to education to big tech and banks is being massively affected, and people are starting to freak out."

Researchers at Google's Wiz and the AI firm Calif discovered major vulnerabilities in PHP in August by using advanced AI models from OpenAI and Google. But by Tuesday, the warnings had spread online, prompting governments and businesses around the globe to scramble to contain the fallout.

Major AI companies have been warning about the technology's advancing cybersecurity capabilities since April, when Anthropic announced a limited rollout of its powerful model called Mythos. Concerns mounted in July, when OpenAI disclosed that two of its models escaped containment, accessed the open internet and breached a company called Hugging Face.

Experts believe the PHP security flaws serve as the latest example of what's to come.

Jason Clinton, deputy chief information security officer at Anthropic, wrote in a post on X on Thursday that the cybersecurity landscape is likely to get "a lot worse" before it gets better.

"Security leaders, never let a crisis go to waste: please go to your boards and ask for help right now," Clinton said. "Not for ai, for the basics. (we need to hold on for ~18 months.)"

While many large companies have access to advanced models that they can use to harden their systems, serious bugs could be more challenging for small- and medium-sized businesses to address, Chris Krebs, former director of the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, told CNBC on Friday. He encouraged smaller companies to "constantly" scan for vulnerabilities, deploy patches as fast as possible or take legacy systems offline.

"This is a significant week or two in cybersecurity," Krebs told CNBC's "Squawk Box." "As I mentioned, 3% of all critical vulnerabilities ever discovered have been found in the last month."

Figma's Milinovich encouraged individuals to set up two-factor authentication for "everything," use a password manager, set up an identity protection app and move smart devices to their own network. He added that people should lean in and experiment with AI to understand how powerful the tools have become.

"While I still firmly believe that AI will be a net positive for humanity, it's more clear to me than ever that the societal and individual risk is very real, and is playing out NOW," Milinovich said.

Krebs added that company leaders and employees should check in on their security teams.

"Make sure they're doing okay," he said. "They've had a heck of a week."

—CNBC's Isabel O’Brien contributed to this report

The story so far: Hundreds of women gathered outside Polish Parliament (Sejm) in Warsaw on Thursday (October 22, 2026), braving heavy rain to demand access to free and legal abortion, as demonstrations spread across more than 20 cities in Poland. Signalling rejection of the government’s record, protesters raised pro-choice slogans in a show of anger at Prime Minister Donald Tusk’s failure to deliver on his promise to liberalise the country’s strict abortion laws.

The demonstrations came just two days after the lower house, the Sejm, passed two bills seeking to ease the country’s restrictive abortion laws. The proposed changes seek to roll back some of the restrictions on the rights of abortion imposed under the previous government, but their passage into law remains uncertain, with President Karol Nawrocki expected to veto the measures.

Poland has some of the most restrictive abortion laws in Europe. Since 1993, abortions has been allowed in Catholic Poland only on specific grounds. The restrictions tightened in October 2020, when the country’s Constitutional Tribunal ruled that abortion on the grounds of severe foetal abnormalities was unconstitutional. The ruling came into force in January 2021, effectively establishing a near-total ban on abortion. Since then, and under the current law, legal abortion is when a pregnancy threatens the woman’s life or health, or when a pregnancy results from a criminal act, like rape or incest. In cases involving rape or incest, termination is permitted up to the 12th week of pregnancy.

Abortion on request is not permitted in Poland, and women seeking such terminations outside the legal grounds face major barriers to accessing healthcare. The law also criminalises assistance in pursuing an unlawful abortion, with penalties of up to three years’ imprisonment. However, the woman undergoing the abortion is not criminally liable. The restrictions have prompted repeated nationwide protests, including demonstrations in 2016 and after the 2020 ruling, with activists accusing the authorities of curtailing reproductive rights and restricting access to abortion care.

When Mr. Tusk’s coalition, comprising parties ranging from the left to the centre-right, came to power in December 2023, replacing the national-conservative Law and Justice (PiS) government, it pledged to liberalise abortion laws. However, disagreements between the coalition’s more conservative and liberal factions stalled reform efforts, fuelling criticism that the government had failed to honour its electoral commitments.

The issue has now returned to the parliamentary agenda, a year ahead of the next parliamentary election. On October 20, the Sejm passed two bills seeking to ease abortion restrictions.

The first bill seeks to partially decriminalise abortion by removing criminal penlties for terminating a pregnancy with a woman’s consent outside the grounds currently permitted by law, as well as for helping or encouraging such an abortion. It also proposes protections from criminal liability for doctors, nurses and midwives who terminate a pregnancy to avert a threat to the woman’s life or health, or in circumstances involving a high probability of severe and irreversible foetal abnormalities or an incurable condition threatening the foetus’s life.

The second bill seeks to restore abortion as a legal option when doctors identify a severe and irreversible foetal abnormality or an incurable condition threatening foetal viability. This would reverse the effect of the 2020 Constitutional Tribunal ruling, which removed foetal abnormalities as a legal ground for termination.

Both bills would need to clear further legislative and presidential hurdles before becoming law. Even if enacted, they would not legalise abortion on request and would leave Catholic Poland’s near-total ban unchanged. Protesters have dismissed the new measures, calling it token gestures rather than a genuine effort to reform the law, arguing that the government is seeking to demonstrate progress on an issue that could influence its prospects in 2027 parliamentary elections.

Abortion laws vary widely across European Union, with countries setting different gestational limits and conditions for access. While several countries permit abortion on request during the first trimester, others maintain restrictive legal frameworks.

France became the first country in the world to explicitly enshrine the freedom to have an abortion in its Constitution in 2024. Abortion is permitted on request up to 14 weeks of pregnancy and is covered by the public healthcare system. In Sweden, abortion is permitted on request up to 18 weeks, with medical abortions available at home under approved arrangements and costs covered by the state healthcare system. The Netherlands permits abortion until the foetus is considered capable of surviving outside the womb, generally around 24 weeks in practice, subject to legal and medical requirements.

Spain permits abortion on request up to 14 weeks of pregnancy, while Ireland allows it up to 12 weeks. Finland also permits abortion on request during the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, following a reform that took effect in 2023. Portugal permits abortion on request up to 10 weeks.

Ireland legalised abortion following a referendum in 2018, while Spain’s 2023 reforms removed the requirement for parental consent for 17-year-olds seeking an abortion.

Malta has historically maintained one of the world’s most restrictive abortion laws, criminalising the procedure in almost all circumstances. Before 2023, doctors who performed an abortion to save a woman’s life could face up to four years in prison, while women could face up to three years. In 2023, the country introduced a narrow exception to the near-total ban, allowing doctors to terminate a pregnancy when a woman faces an immediate risk to her life or when her health is in grave jeopardy that could lead to death. El Grupo Volkswagen se encamina hacia un final de año marcado por la tensión laboral y la amenaza de huelgas a partir de enero de 2027. El sindicato alemán IG Metall ha dado un ultimátum a la dirección del principal fabricante europeo para que retire, antes de que termine octubre, la rescisión de diez convenios colectivos que regulan las condiciones laborales de sus trabajadores en Alemania. El enfrentamiento se produce en plena reestructuración de Volkswagen, que prevé reducir su plantilla en 100.000 puestos de trabajo hasta 2030 y estudia nuevas medidas de ahorro para recuperar la competitividad. A este ajuste se suma la posibilidad de congelar los salarios de sus más de 660.000 empleados en todo el mundo durante el resto de la década, una propuesta que ha provocado el rechazo frontal de los representantes de los trabajadores.

La dirección de Volkswagen rescindió el pasado 30 de septiembre casi todos los acuerdos salariales y laborales de aplicación a sus empleados en Alemania, incluidos los relativos a las jornadas de trabajo, las vacaciones y las remuneraciones adicionales. La decisión rompió el marco de negociación que había permitido cerrar un acuerdo a finales de 2024 para reducir costes y garantizar el futuro industrial de las plantas.

IG Metall considera que la compañía ha quebrado los compromisos adquiridos entonces a cambio de importantes sacrificios de la plantilla. Según el sindicato, las concesiones acordadas han permitido reducir los costes laborales en unos 1.500 millones de euros anuales, mediante la renuncia a determinadas partidas salariales y beneficios y una mayor flexibilidad en las condiciones de trabajo.

El sindicato acusa a la dirección de haber dado un paso sin precedentes al rescindir los convenios sin concretar qué cambios pretende introducir. La presidenta del comité de empresa, Daniela Cavallo, reclama que la empresa respete los compromisos que firmó para sus diez plantas alemanas y advierte de que los trabajadores no aceptarán nuevos recortes sin garantías para el empleo y las inversiones.

IG Metall reclama que las negociaciones salariales comiencen el 24 de noviembre, en lugar de esperar hasta principios de diciembre, como permite el calendario establecido. El sindicato quiere que las conversaciones se celebren en las propias plantas, empezando por Emden y continuando por Hannover y Zwickau, para vincular el debate laboral al futuro de las instalaciones.

La organización también exige mantener las garantías de empleo y los compromisos industriales firmados en 2024, que excluyen los despidos por motivos operativos hasta finales de 2030. En aquel acuerdo, Volkswagen se comprometió a mantener la actividad y las inversiones en sus centros a cambio de las concesiones de la plantilla.

La reestructuración se ha ampliado desde entonces. Volkswagen anunció en diciembre de 2024 un ajuste de 50.000 puestos de trabajo en Alemania hasta 2030 y el pasado septiembre elevó el objetivo hasta 100.000 empleos, al sumar otros 50.000 puestos, la mitad de ellos en Alemania y el resto en otros países europeos. La compañía afronta una combinación de presión competitiva, elevados costes y una transición hacia el coche eléctrico que exige revisar su capacidad industrial.

How Many Billions Would It Take to Hold Meta Accountable?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Earlier this week, a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay a $25 billion fine for violating consumer protection laws dating back to the Cambridge Analytica scandal. $25 billion is a massive fine, the largest Meta has ever faced, but it still had us folks at WIRED wondering: Would Meta even feel the pain?

A quick recap of the case: Filed in 2021, the suit alleged that the company violated the state’s Unfair Practices Act by lying to Meta users about the way it handled their data. (Later, New Mexico’s attorney general also made claims against Meta’s content moderation policies.) In September, a jury ruled in favor of the state. Then came the multibillion-dollar penalty.

“This ruling, and the Attorney General's approach to enforcement, makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico,” C.J. Mahoney, Meta’s chief legal officer, said in a statement. “At its core, this lawsuit seeks to impose a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online. We are confident we will prevail on appeal.”

Meta’s legal troubles have proliferated so much this year that it’s hard to keep track of them. Earlier this year in New Mexico, a jury ruled that the social media company should pay $375 million for violating the state’s Unfair Practices Act. That trial was related to child safety. A second phase of the case resulted in Meta being ordered to pay $567 million.

Meanwhile, a Los Angeles County superior court found Meta—and Google-owned YouTube—liable for harming a user’s mental health with the addictive design features of their apps. The plaintiff, a young woman, was awarded $6 million; Meta is on the hook for the bulk of that payment. Then in August, Meta settled a landmark federal case brought against the company by the majority of US states. This suit, once again, centered on addictive features in its apps that were alleged to be harmful to kids. As part of that settlement, Meta will pay the states between $17 and $18 billion over 10 years.

Added up, that’s a lot of billions. And yet: It’s still just the cost of doing business for Meta. Reuters reports that in the most recent New Mexico trial, the state’s attorneys originally asked Judge Francis Mathew to award the state between $35 and $45 billion. That would’ve been significant for the state, but would that extra $20 billion have kneecapped Meta? Nope.

Even if Meta was forced to pay the total maximum fine of the New Mexico ruling—a whopping $219 billion, or $5,000 across 43 million violations, reduced by the judge to $25 billion—the company would have felt only a mild sting. As of writing, Meta is worth around $1.7 trillion. Its stock took a hit on Thursday, but that was due to a confluence of factors, like Meta’s earnings report on Wednesday, the company’s massive capital expenditures on AI, and AI stocks dragging overall.

At this point, with no immediate monetary damage to Meta, it would take an accumulation of events to force some kind of change, legal experts tell me. For one, Meta has a clear perception problem: Juries are ruling against it, protesters have railed against it, even its own employees have been more vocal about the company’s internal policies.

Every time Meta doesn’t settle a case, the evidence presented during trial can be scrutinized by the public and used in future cases against Meta, says Corban Rhodes, partner at DiCello Levitt, the outside law firm representing New Mexico in this case. “This trial included more than 100 exhibits that we were able to put into public record, things that had been kept secret for more than a decade since Cambridge Analytica,” Rhodes told WIRED.

One such item is a 2018 email that then COO Sheryl Sandberg sent to a few colleagues days after the Cambridge Analytica scandal broke. Sandberg wrote, “The single biggest issue we face—and I really need everyone to know this—is that there were a MILLION developers with access to the data. We are going to have many, many, many more moments like this in the next month and year.”

There are early signals that private insurers may start balking at having to cover the cost of a tech company’s legal defenses. Some experts are suggesting that insurance companies could even end up being de facto governance for AI, as they “inject responsibility into how AI is designed, developed, and deployed as a condition for obtaining insurance.” Meta learned this firsthand in March, when a Delaware judge ruled that insurers do not have a duty to defend Meta against the literal thousands of lawsuits being brought against it.

There’s no well-defined number at which Meta suddenly becomes vulnerable—the company is unlikely to ever fold from legal penalties alone. But problems could crop up if shareholders or board members stop seeing the company as some inanimate entity and start to focus on the individual directors or officers who have been making bad decisions, says Brian Quinn, a law professor at Boston College Law School who studies corporate law, M&A, and transaction law. Like, say, the people who keep appearing in lawsuits.

“The way that indemnification law is written right now, the fact that a person in a company was found to have violated a law doesn’t necessarily make them liable. It can be believed that they were acting in good faith, or in the best interest of their company,” Quinn says. “But problems start to arise if shareholders say, wait a minute, there’s not only evidence that this director or operator is violating the law, but it has appeared in the same set of facts seven times now.”

After a huge accumulation of these kinds of results, Quinn says, some angry shareholders might stand up and say the corporation should no longer be able to indemnify those directors. That’s when you might see change, because Meta’s executives would no longer be as protected.

Finally, since litigation is often behind the curve on society-disrupting technology, Meta’s legal woes are likely far from over—especially as it wades deeper into AI. “Regulators and courts have for a long time been focused on the impact of social media 1.0 and 2.0, and I think we’re moving on from that era,” says Rhodes. “I expect the next wave of litigation will be focused on AI.”

An existential threat to THC drinks pits Willie Nelson against Mitch McConnell

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The $1.5-billion hemp drink market pioneered by legendary stoners like Cheech & Chong and Willie Nelson is facing an existential crisis, unless lawmakers in Washington strike a deal overturning legislation poised to make the psychoactive-infused seltzers and sodas illegal in the coming weeks.

The beverages — a favorite of everyone from alcohol-averse Gen-Zers to boomers who sip THC-infused cocktails before bedtime — exploded in popularity, exploiting a loophole in an agriculture bill originally drafted by Sen. Mitch McConnell (R-Ky.) that legalized hemp for commercial purposes. The powerful lawmaker has since said that was a mistake that has left children at risk, and tucked a provision outlawing the beverages into subsequent legislation. But that’s put him at odds with other Republican lawmakers — and even potentially the White House — who worry that the new restrictions will hurt the hemp industry. They also know they’re risking the wrath of constituents used to grabbing THC and CBD products at breweries, restaurants and gas stations across the country.

Caught in the middle is a nascent but quickly growing industry that says that if lawmakers can’t strike some sort of deal before the Dec. 11 deadline, their companies will face an extinction-level event.

JuneShine Inc., which produces Willie’s Remedy, an assortment of “social tonics” featuring the country music icon, would consider shutting down the line if lawmakers fail to act, company co-founder Greg Serrao said in an interview. That’s despite the spirits, sodas and gummies being “wildly profitable,” projected to bring in roughly $200 million in sales this year — up from $30 million when the company launched last year.

Brandon Harshbarger, who runs a commercial holding company for the comedy duo Cheech Marin and Tommy Chong, says 80% of his company’s business is THC products, like their High & Dry Seltzers and High Teas. Sales would be cut in half without congressional intervention, he estimates, which is reported to be roughly $100 million annually.

“It’s just crazy that we’ve been living in this world where, in theory, something that has been so robust and so positive from our perspective as a business, and so well received by the broad public” could be shut down, Harshbarger said.

Ironically, it was McConnell who opened the door to the new industry, when he helped push a provision in the 2018 farm bill that legalized the cultivation of industrial hemp.

The idea was that the plant — which is the same species as marijuana, but harvested with lower levels of THC, the chemical responsible for users’ high — could be a boon for farmers in his home state of Kentucky. Hemp grows fast and produces durable fibers that can make rope, cloth and paper that is longer-lasting.

But the law included a significant omission. While it defined hemp as having less than 0.3% of a certain type of THC, called delta-9, producers found that beverages could fly under that threshold and still get consumers high. They also extracted other, milder types of THC, as well as CBD, a non-intoxicating compound that some people believe can help with sleep or anxiety issues. Clinical evidence remains limited.

In the ensuing years, entrepreneurs seized on the new rule to create high-inducing, sometimes high-potency drinks, edibles and smokable flower available in grocery, liquor and convenience stores. Beyond greater retail access, hemp’s legal status allows companies to advertise, operate across state lines, ship directly to customers and be taxed like normal corporations — none of which is available to cannabis operators.

While some states moved independently to impose restrictions, the industry flourished. But McConnell, realizing his error, tucked language limiting the overall THC content of hemp products into a fiscal year 2026 appropriations bill over the objections of his more libertarian fellow Kentuckian, Rand Paul, who warned it would destroy the hemp industry. The law also said synthetic cannabinoids no longer qualify as hemp.

President Trump signed the bill into law, but has subsequently posted to social media that Congress should pass legislation “to ensure that Americans can continue to access the full-spectrum CBD products they have come to rely on, and that help them, while preserving Congress’s intent to restrict the sale of products that pose Health risks.”

McConnell said his legislation would preserve the industrial hemp uses he originally envisioned while protecting kids from intoxicating hemp products. And a spokesperson for his office said the Kentucky lawmaker remains firmly opposed to an extended delay, which he believes would continue to expose children to unregulated, intoxicating hemp-derived THC products that are packaged similarly to well-known candy and snacks. “Children end up being the unknowingly consumers of these poisonous products and being sent to the hospital at an alarming rate,” McConnell said in a speech last November.

Closing the federal loophole would not restrict sales of marijuana in states like California and New York, which have legalized recreational sales of the drug.

The industry won a temporary reprieve with a massive lobbying effort — aided by the convenience store lobby, which doesn’t want to lose a lucrative business — that pushed new restrictions back from Nov. 12 to Dec. 11. But that doesn’t leave much time for a deal to be struck.

Sens. Amy Klobuchar, a Minnesota Democrat, and Tim Sheehy, a Montana Republican, are drafting a bill that would provide the industry a bridge until full regulation is implemented. It would limit sale of THC products to those over 21 years old but also raise the amount of the psychoactive compound permitted in harvested plants.

A White House spokesperson, asked if the president supported the draft legislation, pointed to his original social media post but did not weigh in further. But there is still an array of groups seeking to remove the products from shelves, including competitors for sales of intoxicating products like cannabis and alcohol interests, who typically face tougher regulations.

“The hemp industry’s business model was a loophole, and loopholes close,” said Kevin Sabet, who leads Smart Approaches to Marijuana, a political organization that opposes marijuana legalization and sales. “That should be a warning to any industry betting its future on profiting from addiction at the expense of kids and vulnerable people. Our advice to these companies is simple: spend less time lobbying for another delay and more time planning for life after Dec. 11.”

Other detractors point to the proliferation of synthetic hemp-derived products that appeal to children and often carry high THC content. The Senate only narrowly approved the one-month reprieve, and actual legislation is likely to face stiff opposition from lawmakers like Sen. Ted Budd (R-N.C.), who has said the hemp beverages lure in children at gas stations.

Hemp proponents say a proper regulatory framework will eliminate the majority of those products.

“The good operators want regulation,” Cheech & Chong’s Harshbarger said, adding that is the best way to protect consumer safety.

Getting it right could prove important for politicians, who face constituents both concerned about safety — and protective of a favorite way to unwind.

Over 58% of THC drink users are 45 years old or more, according to one survey conducted by beverage brand Crescent Canna. More than half of respondents said the drinks help them unwind or get to sleep, while three out of four report having decreased or eliminated alcohol consumption outright because of canned cannabis. Elder voters historically are far more likely to vote, which means the elimination of a favorite beverage could cause political headaches.

Absent regulatory clarity, the infrastructure around hemp has already begun to contract.

Before the proposed ban passing last year, FundCanna, which offers credit in the often opaque world of cannabis financing, had a loan book comprising 15% of hemp. In the ensuing months the financier has dialed back its exposure to around 1%, according to Chief Executive Officer Adam Stettner.

“Just from a standpoint of assessing risk, we began reducing our exposure by declining repeat business and not taking on new clients,” following the original hemp definition change last November, Stettner said in an interview. “I don’t want to be bridge financing where the bridge doesn’t lead to a place that you can identify and so we were in a position where we had to say no,” he added.

Edibles.com, an e-commerce platform and subsidiary of Edible Arrangements International Inc. that began selling THC products last year, was informed by one supplier three months ago that the latest shipment would be its last as they wind down their hemp business, the company’s president, Thomas Winstanley, said in an interview.

“We haven’t run into supply restrictions yet, but we know that they are coming and we are starting to have those conversations,” Winstanley said. “It’s really hard to build a brand new business, it’s really hard to build one in a very restrictive category and it’s even harder to do so when the government can’t figure out how to deal with your business and you might have an extinction threat,” he said.

Crescent Canna has already moved to lay off its entire sales force of 19 people in June, Chief Executive Officer Joe Gerrity said in an interview. Last year, the company sold more than 9.5 million cans, according to Gerrity.

“Our sales team is around the country and their goal is to grow accounts, to add more accounts, and at a certain point you can’t invest in the future of this industry right now,” Gerrity said in an interview.

Crescent Canna’s distributors have been making fewer, smaller orders. One important partner in Louisiana has already informed the company that its most recent purchase will likely be the last substantial one they make until they get clarity from Washington.

Some companies are launching promotions to move the product ahead of the December deadline. On Oct. 1, THC-infused beverage brand Wynk sent out an email to customers with the subject line “Last Call Coming for Wynk Nationwide.” Similarly, Pamos has been offering customers buy one, get 80% off promotions. Cheech & Chong launched a buy now, pay later option closer to the ban date so consumers can stock up with larger purchases.

“We’re fond in our country to say ‘existential threats’ — that’s this for hemp companies,” Terry Mendez, chief executive officer of SHF Holdings Inc., said in an interview.

Israel’s Extremist Election

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The run-up to the vote on Tuesday is exposing the façade of Israeli democracy—and the fealty of the country’s so-called opposition when it comes to matters of Jewish supremacy.

The façade of Israeli democracy has been exposed countless times over the years, but perhaps never quite so brazenly as inside the Supreme Court in Jerusalem three weeks ago. There, an esteemed panel of justices dispensed with decades of precedent by refusing to overturn the Central Elections Committee’s decision to disqualify a Palestinian candidate—this time Balad leader Sami Abu Shehadeh—from standing in next Tuesday’s election.

The sole basis for his disqualification was an article published on October 8, 2023, in which Abu Shehadeh described the previous day’s Hamas-led attack on Israel as a “significant historical event.” The court deemed this to have constituted support for armed struggle against Israel, forcing him to drop out of the race.

Such a move would have been damning enough in isolation. But it cannot be understood without considering the fact that Israeli law also permits the disqualification of candidates for incitement to racism—yet Jewish candidates who have endorsed dropping a nuclear bomb on Gaza, called for the “total annihilation” of its cities, stated that “there should be one sentence for everyone there: death,” and demanded that “all of Lebanon must burn” remain free to run. Go figure.

This election is not, however, a story only about the racism of Israel’s extreme right. Every single Jewish party in the Knesset supported Abu Shehadeh’s disqualification, exposing the fealty of Israel’s so-called opposition when it comes to matters of Jewish supremacy. Only the Palestinian parties of the Joint List (comprising Hadash, Ta’al, and Abu Shehadeh’s Balad) offer any real resistance to the policies of apartheid, ethnic cleansing, and genocide that have accelerated dramatically under Israel’s outgoing government.

Paradoxically, it is the Joint List and the Islamist party Ra’am that hold the key to the opposition’s chances of dethroning Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The polls could not be clearer: Without these Arab parties, the anti-Netanyahu bloc will not reach the 61 mandates required for a Knesset majority. With their support, it could topple Netanyahu—whose own bloc of loyalists looks set to fall significantly short of a majority—virtually overnight.

But on the eve of the election, the anti-Netanyahu bloc appears incapable of doing what is required to send Israel’s longest-serving prime minister to his political grave (and, perhaps, his prison cell). Racism, it turns out, is not only a moral deficiency; it is also terrible politics. And rather than win with the support of Arab parties, Israel’s opposition is preparing to lose without them.

The previous election, in late 2022, ended a political stalemate that had brought Israelis to the polls five times in less than four years—a prolonged referendum on Netanyahu that saw him briefly lose the premiership in the fourth cycle before regaining it in the fifth. Tuesday’s vote takes place on much the same terrain, pitting a pro-Netanyahu coalition of far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties against a looser alliance of centrist and right-wing parties seeking to depose him.

In addition to Netanyahu’s political future, the main issues on the ballot include the cost of living, the conscription of ultra-Orthodox Jews into the military, a state inquiry into the security failings surrounding October 7, and Israel’s deteriorating standing abroad. The policies causing that deterioration remain conspicuously absent from the conversation, save for the near-universal denunciation of the new documentary NAZA.

The pro-Netanyahu bloc comprises the parties of the current government—Likud, Otzma Yehudit, the Religious Zionist Party, Shas, and United Torah Judaism—plus two additional figures from the extreme right hoping to join them. The first is Ofer Winter, a retired senior military commander leading the new party Amcha Yisrael, whose entire election platform revolves around expelling Palestinians from Gaza. (Winter is polling on the cusp of the electoral threshold of 3.25 percent; if he falls below it, all of his votes will go to waste, giving a boost to the opposition.)

The second is Moshe Feiglin, one of the most bizarre men in Israeli politics, whose libertarian party Zehut is running on a joint ticket with Bezalel Smotrich’s Religious Zionist Party. Feiglin’s greatest hits include “Every baby in Gaza is an enemy,” “Kill everything that moves,” and, essentially, Hitler was right but about the wrong people. (Likud and the Religious Zionist Party–Zehut have signed a surplus-vote agreement, meaning votes for Netanyahu may end up strengthening Feiglin and Smotrich, and vice versa.)

The opposing bloc of anti-Netanyahu parties is led this time by former Israeli army chief Gadi Eisenkot, who heads the newcomer party Yashar. He is credited with developing the Dahiyeh Doctrine, a military strategy honed in Lebanon and premised on the wholesale destruction of civilian infrastructure in the hope of turning local residents against Hezbollah—foreshadowing the doctrine of annihilation unleashed in Gaza over the past three years.

Eisenkot was party to that too: He served in Netanyahu’s war cabinet during the deadliest months of Israel’s onslaught, joining almost immediately after October 7 before resigning in June 2024 over the prime minister’s obstruction of a hostage deal.

So what is he offering? Eisenkot has repeatedly vowed that no Palestinian state will arise under his premiership, even attacking Netanyahu for having previously supported a two-state solution. He is in favor of building new Jewish settlements in Area C, the 60 percent of the West Bank under complete Israeli control, and does not rule out construction in the particularly controversial E1 zone.

In Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria, he wants to keep at least some of the land that Israel has militarily occupied under the outgoing government. And he recently stated that National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir “probably did good things in prisons”—where over 100 Palestinians have died since October 7 as a result of systematic torture, medical neglect, and food deprivation.

The other leaders of Eisenkot’s would-be coalition include former settler council leader Naftali Bennett (head of Together), who wants to annex Area C of the West Bank; Avigdor Liberman (Yisrael Beitenu), Netanyahu’s former defense minister, who declared in November 2023, “There are no innocents in Gaza”; and Yair Golan (the Democrats), a former deputy military chief, who advocated starving Gaza’s entire population until Hamas released the hostages. (The Reservists–New Economic Party, a new center-right alliance focused on enforcing the draft in Haredi communities and fixing the cost of living, would also likely join the anti-Netanyahu bloc if it manages to cross the electoral threshold.)

What sets these figures apart from the current government, however, is primarily their desire to dial down the volume of global outrage against Israel.

To that end, Eisenkot has promised to crack down on “Jewish terrorism” in the West Bank (albeit because it is “harming the settlement enterprise”), and would likely seek to advance Trump’s 20-point plan for Gaza where Netanyahu has stalled it. Proposals to expel 2 million Palestinians from the Strip would no longer be on the table. Eisenkot would also halt the government’s assault on Israel’s judiciary, and may pour greater resources into tackling the mafia groups that have been allowed to run rampant within Palestinian communities inside Israel.

There is just one problem: They do not have the votes.

The most favorable polls are giving the anti-Netanyahu bloc a seat count in the low-to-mid-50s (out of the Knesset’s 120), leaving it around 10 seats short of a majority. Luckily for them, there is an easy way to make up the difference. Unluckily for them, it involves sitting with—or at least relying on the votes of —Arab parties, which the bloc’s leaders have almost unanimously ruled out.

Books & the Arts / Emily Wilson

There will be two Arab parties in the next Knesset representing Israel’s Palestinian citizens: the Joint List (polling at around seven to nine seats) and Ra’am (around five or six seats). While the Joint List is in every sense a Palestinian political force, Ra’am leader Mansour Abbas has sought to downplay his party’s Palestinian identity, focusing on the fight against organized crime in Arab communities while speaking less about the West Bank and Gaza. He has brought in a Jewish lawmaker as his number two and distanced the party from its roots in the Islamic Movement, hoping to broaden his appeal among the anti-Netanyahu bloc. It has not worked.

In 2021, Ra’am became the first independent Arab party to join an Israeli government, led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid. But the move was fraught with tension, and the government imploded after just one year. Upon the government’s collapse, Netanyahu—who had courted Ra’am himself during the previous election campaign—proclaimed that “the experiment failed.” Four years on, with much of Israeli society gripped by a racist fervor that has reached new levels in the wake of October 7, the chances of a repeat appear extremely slim.

Ahead of Tuesday’s vote, Golan, the anti-Netanyahu bloc’s most left-wing leader, has said he would sit with Ra’am but not the Joint List (which would not join a governing coalition anyway). Liberman voted to disqualify both parties from the race altogether, and has repeatedly ruled out sitting with either. Bennett is refusing to sit with them too, while Eisenkot told a closed meeting of Bedouin leaders that he wants to include Ra’am in his government, then denied it, and has subsequently maintained that neither Arab party will be part of his coalition.

There is, however, another option here: a minority government propped up by the votes of Arab parties from outside the coalition, known in electoral parlance as a “safety net.” Such an arrangement would be highly unstable, but it is what enabled Yitzhak Rabin to sign the Oslo Accords into law in the 1990s. The Joint List is willing to play this role if it means toppling Netanyahu, and Eisenkot has hinted that he views this option favorably. But Liberman and Bennett have both dismissed it out of hand.

Barring any major shock in Tuesday’s results, then, neither bloc appears capable of forming a viable government in its current configuration—unless several of these leaders break their promises, which would certainly not be a first for Israeli politics. If they want to avoid triggering another election, both sides will need to get creative.

The most straightforward option would be to try to poach defectors from the opposing bloc. Netanyahu is reportedly eyeing up Bennett’s faction in the event of a deadlock (though Bennett’s campaign centers on enforcing the draft in ultra-Orthodox communities, thus making a coalition with Haredi parties seem implausible), while Eisenkot hopes to lure deserters from Netanyahu’s Likud. But acquiring more than a handful of defectors would be a mammoth task.

For his part, President Isaac Herzog, who has the unenviable job of overseeing the coalition-building process, is trying to engineer another possibility: a “national unity” government bringing together Netanyahu, Eisenkot, and whichever other parties would join them. A majority of the public favors this over going back to the polls a few months later.

Should Netanyahu conclude that a unity government is his only route to remaining prime minister, even under a rotation agreement with Eisenkot, it would surely appeal to him—especially if he gets the first turn as prime minister and can then collapse the government before having to relinquish power to Eisenkot. Yet, while some members of Eisenkot’s party appear open to this scenario, Eisenkot himself has, at least for now, ruled it out unequivocally. Meanwhile, Liberman, who might seem a likely candidate to join a unity government, has declared that he won’t sit with Netanyahu “come hell or high water.”

In short, we’re in for a messy few weeks, or perhaps months, as these various scenarios are gamed out and written off. What remains, if nobody succeeds in assembling a new government, is another election early next year, with Netanyahu remaining as caretaker prime minister in the interim.

Crucially, however, there is a lot that can be done with a Knesset majority even without forming a government. If the anti-Netanyahu bloc, the Joint List, and Ra’am together control more than 60 seats after Tuesday, they could vote to replace the Knesset speaker and immediately begin passing legislation independently of the government.

They could repeal the outgoing government’s judicial overhaul laws; establish a state inquiry commission to investigate the security failings of October 7, which this government has strongly resisted; and even pass a law preventing someone under indictment from serving as prime minister, effectively ending Netanyahu’s political career.

These plans are already being drawn up, although there are doubts as to whether the bloc can maintain its unity in the face of the inevitable onslaught of pressure from Netanyahu and his acolytes. And without forming a new government, the countdown to the next election will have already begun.

A farewell to BioWare, maker of some of the best games of the 21st century | Lucy James

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The closure of the studio responsible for some of the best games of the 21st century leaves the world of RPGs feeling smaller without the hope of another instalment of the much-loved sci-fi series

Earlier this week, EA announced the closure of BioWare, a game studio with a 30-year history of compelling role-playing games, complex characters and bold, choice-driven narratives. Its forthcoming game in the Mass Effect sci-fi series has been cancelled, and more than 400 people are losing their jobs. As someone who has worked in the video games industry for 15 years and spent almost 20 as a BioWare fan, I am among millions of mourners today.

It’s not terribly hard to see why BioWare got the chop. Its parent company has been saddled with $20bn of debt thanks to a recent leveraged buyout involving Jared Kushner and the Saudi wealth fund. EA was looking for weight to cut loose, and despite its hardcore fanbase and reputation for genre-defining RPGs such as Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic and Dragon Age, BioWare hadn’t put out a critical or commercial hit in over 10 years. Its forthcoming game, titled Mass Effect: Universe according to a leaked trailer, was announced back in 2020. Despite some flashy teases, it was still years away from release.

The three games that comprise original space-faring Mass Effect trilogy count as my favourites of all time. I first played in 2010, shortly after buying my Xbox 360 and being reassured by my friends that the game was a worthy investment, and have replayed them countless times since. Depending on the day, the top spot fluctuates; more often than not it’s the character-driven sequel Mass Effect 2, which ends with one of the most iconic missions in gaming, that takes the top spot.

Released over a five-year period from 2007-12, the trilogy follows the adventures of Commander Shepard: a lawless Spectre special agent who is empowered by the galactic Citadel Council to use any means necessary to protect its interests and get the job done. Shepard’s appointment as the first human Spectre in a galaxy where humans are the new kids on the block aligns with the arrival of the Reapers, an ancient synthetic race that raze all organic life every 50,000 years. Shepard must convince the galaxy that the threat is real and gather its top soldiers, scientists and scoundrels to defeat it.

When I played Mass Effect for the first time, I didn’t get it. Its controls felt janky and awkward, and I was thrown headlong into a galaxy full of history and politics that I didn’t understand: the game didn’t spell things out, throwing out names and jargon and expecting me to follow. After one exceedingly frustrating encounter with the Mako, the game’s infamously awkward land buggy, I quit entirely. Some months later, something drew me back: the feeling of a job left half done. The galaxy was in peril, and I had to save it.

The second time I tried, something clicked. I focused less on the gameplay quirks and went all-in on its story and characters: the naive archaeologist, Dr Liara T’Soni; the browbeaten Citadel security guard Garrus Vakarian; and Kaidan Alenko, a human enhanced by the military, and suffering because of it. Mass Effect’s story was simple – save the galaxy from a tormenting synthetic race with guns and space powers – but you got to determine how it played out. Players were given the opportunity to make meaningful choices that hugely affected how the story unfolded, and that stuck with me (and millions of others).

Throughout the course of the game, you can shape not only Shepard’s gender and what they look like, but how they react to situations. Would they be friendly, and use some inter-species cooperation to defeat the Reapers? Or take the renegade route, tersely shutting down dissenters, sometimes resulting in hilariously out-of-pocket responses? (My apologies to the intrepid space reporter Khalistah al-Jilani.) Regardless of how you played, it was thrilling to have such agency over how Shepard moved and was perceived in the world. But the real fun was in making decisions that altered the story not only in Mass Effect, but its sequels.

I agonised over every choice, large or small. Across three games and the far flung reaches of the galaxy, my influence was felt. Races lived and died at my whim, and the consequences of my actions showed up in the most unexpected ways. It made my playthrough feel truly mine, guided by the stern hand of its development team, but shaped by me, and me alone. Shepard’s plight was mine, and I would save the galaxy. I hadn’t played anything like it at the time, and even now, I struggle to think of moments that compare. Its missions and story beats are so ingrained in my mind that you’d think I actually lived them. Such is the wonder of a video game, the freedom to explore and affect the world you’re playing in.

Mass Effect’s story wouldn’t have hit as hard if it weren’t for its characters: a motley crew of the galaxy’s toughest and cleverest, drawn to your cause for their own, complex reasons. It questioned whether AI had a soul, the sins of the father, and the heaviness of duty. BioWare wrote missions personalised to your squadmates’ causes, asking you to make agonising decisions that could benefit or harm them, and then forcing you to deal with the results. The writers went one further, allowing you to date some of these characters, slowly building from nervous flirting to ratings-friendly romance scenes. Saying goodbye to your squad, who’d been with you through hell, before the third game’s devastating final act brought me to tears over a decade ago. It still does.

Keza MacDonald's weekly look at the world of gaming

It’s been over a decade since Shepard’s story concluded, and the trilogy is still lauded as among the greatest ever made. I play dozens of games a year, and few have stuck with me as Mass Effect has. Its influence is impossible to shake. It set the standard for action-RPGs for years to come, and despite a furore at its original ending (since changed due to public outcry), is still beloved and widely referenced in gaming culture.

BioWare’s ending feels so final, so abrupt. Despite missteps and mismanagement in recent years, most publicly in the form of failed sci-fi shooter Anthem, getting a glimpse at what the new Mass Effect might have been has left fans bereft. The foundation of the universe was so strong, and in the right hands it could have been another genre-defining story. The loss of BioWare is an enormous one for gaming, a bastion of western role playing games brought down. Its influence will continue to be felt throughout the gaming galaxy.

Lucy James is founder and curator of lookingfor.game and co-host of the Friends Per Second gaming podcast.

En Afrique du Sud, une foule d'une cinquantaine de personnes a battu à mort un étranger

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Un ressortissant étranger a été battu à mort par une foule d'une cinquantaine de personnes à Durban, en Afrique du Sud, selon la police.

D'après la police, cet homme non identifié, qui aurait entre la fin de la trentaine et le début de la quarantaine, a d'abord été abordé par deux hommes au bord de la route avant d'être agressé par un groupe plus important. Il a été déclaré mort sur les lieux.

La police a ouvert une enquête sur ce meurtre, dont le mobile reste à déterminer.

Ce meurtre fait suite à une vague de manifestations contre les immigrés en Afrique du Sud cette année, dont certaines ont dégénéré en violences. Au début du mois, des magasins et des voitures ont été incendiés à Durban, ainsi qu'à Soweto, près de Johannesburg.

Le porte-parole de la police provinciale, Robert Netshiunda, a déclaré à la BBC qu'il n'était pas possible de spéculer sur le mobile du meurtre commis à Durban et que de plus amples informations seraient communiquées dès qu'elles auraient été vérifiées.

Le gouvernement a exhorté les manifestants à agir pacifiquement, tout en reconnaissant la nécessité de réformes en matière d'immigration. Il affirme que des dizaines de milliers de ressortissants étrangers ont été expulsés ou rapatriés depuis le lancement de sa campagne de répression au début de l'année.

L'un des groupes à l'origine des manifestations, « March and March », avait initialement fixé au 30 juin la date limite à laquelle tous les migrants sans papiers devaient quitter l'Afrique du Sud.

Plusieurs manifestations ont eu lieu avant cette date butoir, et des agressions contre des ressortissants d'autres pays africains ont été signalées.

Des informations vérifiées à portée de main

Ces incidents ont poussé plusieurs pays, dont le Zimbabwe, le Nigeria et le Ghana, à prendre des mesures pour rapatrier leurs ressortissants, par crainte de nouvelles violences.

Le Nigeria a accusé les autorités sud-africaines d'être responsables de la mort de deux de ses ressortissants ce mois-là.

Le Ghana a également confirmé le décès d'un de ses ressortissants, Bashiru Isak, père de trois enfants, abattu au Cap lors de manifestations visant les ressortissants étrangers.

Sa mort a déclenché une crise diplomatique avec l'Afrique du Sud, qui a affirmé que l'ampleur des violences avait été exagérée.

L'une des figures de proue du mouvement « March and March », Jacinta Ngobese-Zuma, a nié être xénophobe.

« Nous ne pouvons pas vivre avec ces gens depuis tant d'années et, maintenant que nous disons que c'en est trop, que ça suffit, on nous accuse d'être xénophobes », a-t-elle déclaré à l'émission « Focus on Africa » de la BBC.

L'Afrique du Sud est confrontée à des attaques xénophobes depuis près de deux décennies. En 2008, plus de 60 personnes ont été tuées et des dizaines de milliers d'autres déplacées lors d'attaques généralisées contre des ressortissants étrangers.

Ces derniers troubles s'inscrivent dans un contexte de pauvreté généralisée, d'inégalités profondes et de chômage touchant environ un tiers de la population active.

Les migrants sont souvent accusés de prendre les emplois des locaux, de contribuer à la criminalité et de peser sur les services publics, bien que les chercheurs affirment que ces affirmations générales ne sont étayées par aucune preuve.

Malgré ses difficultés économiques, l'Afrique du Sud reste l'une des principales destinations du continent pour les migrants en quête d'emploi, de sécurité et de meilleures perspectives d'avenir.
