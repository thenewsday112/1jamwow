Dollarregen für deutsche Wertarbeit: US-Investoren auf Einkaufstour

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mehr Deals mit Deutschland: Immer mehr Geld US-amerikanischer Investoren fließt in traditionelle deutsche Unternehmen wie Birkenstock. Die schwierige Suche nach Firmennachfolgern begünstigt den Übernahmetrend.

Was haben die deutschen Traditionsunternehmen Birkenstock, Viessmann, Linde und EBM-Papst gemeinsam? Kurze Antwort: Sie alle wurden von US-amerikanischen Investoren aufgekauft.

Traditionelle deutsche Familienbetriebe und mittelständische Unternehmen scheinen auf US-Unternehmen und Investoren eine besondere Anziehungskraft auszuüben. Nach einer Auswertung des Finanzdatenanbieters Pitchbookgab es im vergangenen Jahr insgesamt 76 Übernahmen und Beteiligungen an deutschen Industrie- und Fertigungsunternehmen mit US-Beteiligung.

Insgesamt summierten sich den Angaben zufolge die Transaktionen 2025 auf rund 29,5 Milliarden US-Dollar. Das ist fast doppelt so viel wie im Jahr 2022. Bis zum 19. August dieses Jahres kamen bereits 23,9 Milliarden US-Dollar hinzu.

"Das Interesse von US-Investoren ist definitiv weiter vorhanden", erklärt Samar-Ahmad Albrecht-Mahmood von Veracity Capital Investmentsaus Frankfurt am Main auf DW-Anfrage. Die Firma berät Unternehmen, die in Deutschland investieren wollen.

Deutsche Unternehmen seien insbesondere im Industrie- und Fertigungssektor attraktiv, "weil viele von ihnen hoch spezialisiert sind, starke Kundenbeziehungen haben und sehr gutes technisches Know-how besitzen."

Außerdem erreichten viele deutsche Unternehmer ein Alter, in dem sie über den Verkauf ihres Unternehmens nachdenken. "Da es oft keinen Nachfolger innerhalb der Familie gibt, kommen Unternehmen auf den Markt, die vor ein paar Jahren vielleicht nicht zum Verkauf gestanden hätten", so Albrecht-Mahmood.

Prominente Übernahmebeispiele in diesem Jahr waren der Mulfinger Ventilatoren Spezialist Ebm-Papst, für den das US-Unternehmen Madison Air im August 5,4 Milliarden Dollar zahlte.

Außerdem wurde im Juni 2026 das Augsburger Traditionsunternehmen Everllence, vormals MAN Energy Solutions, an Bain Capital verkauft. Die US-amerikanische Investmentgesellschaft erwarb 51 Prozent der Anteile.

Nach Angaben des Branchendienstes Die deutsche Wirtschaftbesitzt kein Land mehr deutsche Unternehmen als die USA. Von den insgesamt 7.861 ermittelten Unternehmen in der Datenbank "Deutsche Unternehmen in Auslandsbesitz" befinden sich 1.750 in US-Besitz.

Auch die Wirtschaftsprüfer von PricewaterhouseCoopers (PwC) sehen die "Destination Deutschland" im Trend. Laut gleichnamigen Reporthaben zwar nicht die Anzahl der Übernahmen und Beteiligungen zugenommen, wohl aber das finanzielle Volumen (siehe Grafik).

Der Bericht hebt ausdrücklich die massiven Investitionen in Höhe von 7,7 Milliarden Euro der US-Investmentgesellschaft Carlyle, in Partnerschaft mit der Qatar Investment Authority (QIA) beim Kauf des Unternehmens BASF Coatings hervor. Der Deal mit der traditionellen Firma für Fahrzeuglacke und Oberflächentechnik wurde am 26. Oktober 2025 vereinbart und am 30. Juni dieses Jahres vollzogen.

Laut einer aktuellen Umfrage der amerikanischen Handelskammerin Deutschland planen die meisten der befragten US-Unternehmen, ihre Aktivitäten in Deutschland auszuweiten. 62 Prozent gingen davon aus, dass sich die Bedingungen in den nächsten drei bis vier Jahren verbessern werden.

Mehr als die Hälfte (52 Prozent) der befragten US-Firmen gaben an, ihre Aktivitäten in Deutschland in diesem Zeitraum ausbauen zu wollen, ein leichter Anstieg gegenüber 49 Prozent im Jahr 2024.

Die Zunahme von US-Firmenübernahmen- und Beteiligungen scheint allerdings auf den ersten Blick nicht zu den rückläufigen US-Auslandsinvestitionen in Deutschland im vergangenen Jahr zu passen. Denn laut Bundesbankfloss 2025 deutlich weniger Investitionskapital aus den USA nach Deutschland als im Jahr zuvor.

Zwar sind die Auslandsinvestitionen in Deutschland insgesamt gestiegen, von 57,4 Milliarden Euro im Jahr 2024 auf insgesamt 86,1 Milliarden Euro im vergangenen Jahr. Doch aus den USA kamen nur noch 11,8 Milliarden Euro, knapp 44 Prozent weniger als 2024. Dagegen stiegen die Investitionen aus Großbritannien auf rund 26 Milliarden Euro.

Experte Albrecht-Mahmood versucht, den Widerspruch aufzulösen. "Der Trend setzt sich fort, auch wenn das nicht einfach bedeutet, dass es jedes Jahr mehr Deals gibt", erklärt er. Das allgemeine Interesse an guten deutschen Industrieunternehmen, besonders an starken Mittelständlern, bleibe hoch.

Ein weiterer Punkt, so Albrecht-Mahmood, sei die Bewertung. "Im Vergleich zu den USA sehen wir immer noch Situationen, in denen deutsche Unternehmen moderater bewertet werden, besonders im KMU-Segment (KMU = kleine und mittelständische Unternehmen - Anm. d. Red.). Das kann Übernahmen für internationale Käufer ziemlich attraktiv machen."

The Supreme Court Is Coming for Affirmative Action—Again

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The high court has just accepted a case that takes aim at efforts to increase diversity at high-performing public schools.

One might think that mediocre white folks and the minority of AAPI parents who have decided to join forces with them would be happy after their victory over affirmative action in the 2023 case known as Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. They succeeded in their goal of reinterpreting the 14th Amendment as “colorblind”—notwithstanding the fact that the 14th Amendment was a direct response to over 200 years of slavery and oppression of Black people in the New World—and with it ushered in a new era of “white makes right” hiring practices.

The SFFA case has been extremely effective at limiting opportunities for Black and Latino students. Enrollment at the top 50 most selective colleges and universities plummeted for Black students during the year after the Supreme Court’s decision, falling by 27 percent. Enrollment for Latinos fell 10 percent. These people have won.

And yet, the winners are not happy or satisfied. They feel they haven’t won enough, especially the people on the Supreme Court who are primarily responsible for making it harder for Black and Latino students to get into school. Having succeeded in cratering enrollment in colleges and universities, the court has moved on to its next target… fifth graders. Apparently, some Black and Latino fifth graders dared to get accepted into a few middle schools in Maryland and, well, parents of white and AAPI children can’t have that, now can they?

On Monday, the Supreme Court agreed to hear a case called Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education. The case arose when a group of mostly Asian American parents sued a Maryland school district over its admissions process for four highly selective magnet schools.

The policy, adopted in 2021, during the Covid pandemic, involved placing all fifth graders who met a set of criteria—grades, reading comprehension, and, yes, the beloved standardized test scores—into a pool. The kids’ scores were compared with other kids’ scores at similar elementary schools (for instance, children who came from schools with high poverty rates were compared with other children from schools with high poverty rates). From within that pool, students were then chosen randomly to attend one of the four magnet schools. It makes sense that kids should be compared with other kids from similar schools in order to get into the pool of potential lottery winners, since how you do relative to the educational environment you find yourself in should be the thing that matters when we’re talking about freaking elementary school. But if you think like that, then you’re not thinking like a parent who is sure that any opportunity that goes to a kid who is not their own is an affront to the natural order of racial supremacy and the Constitution.

In any case, during the time period this plan was active, AAPI enrollment actually went up at two of the four schools, and AAPI students were overrepresented in the magnet schools relative to their population in Montgomery County.

So you have ostensibly “race-neutral” criteria. You have a literal random lottery. You have enrollment going up for AAPI students in some schools, but the Maryland schools were still occasionally admitting the Black and Latino fifth graders by allowing those kids to compete against their peers instead of on an unequal playing field, so that meant some Asian American parents could still be pissed off enough to file a lawsuit.

While we’re here, I think it’s important to point out that, although I certainly blame the litigating parents for their participation in racial discrimination against kids from impoverished schools, I am able to see why they think they have to drip this bile over other people’s children. It is, fundamentally, ridiculous that we are made to think there are only four good middle schools in an entire county. Why isn’t every school as good and desirable as these four magnet schools? Why do we make parents feel like educational resources are scarce and must be competed for in a zero-sum environment.

One solution to the problem of equal opportunity in education is to make every school a palace. Getting into a good school shouldn’t be like winning a lottery. It should be the default expectation in this society. But… we don’t want to do that. We’d rather send AAPI kids and Black kids and Latino kids and even white kids into some kind of Dickensian tragedy where there’s not enough education to go around so some kids get gruel.

Be that as it may, the lawsuit was dismissed by the lower courts because, obviously, when you have race-neutral criteria, a literal lottery, and your group does better than its proportional representation, it’s pretty hard to allege racial discrimination against you with a straight face. Moreover, the lawsuit should be moot because the Montgomery Board of Education uses some other system now. There’s no reason for the Supreme Court to take up the case other than to overrule the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals,

For a mercy, I guess, the Supreme Court seems only to be interested in a narrow question: whether the fact that the system was race-neutral is enough to dismiss a claim for discrimination against AAPI students out of hand. My guess is that the Republican supermajority will say “no,” and that the case will therefore move forward, allowing the lawyers to keep searching for evidence that Black fifth graders shouldn’t get into magnet schools as long as there is a white or Asian kid who wants to go to the same school.

But in some sense, that’s the most dangerous part of a lawsuit—the fact that, despite victory, the victors are not satisfied and will not be satisfied until Black students are drummed out of the system. We see the strands of this kind of mentality from the functional spokesperson for the entire white and AAPI grievance movement, legal gadfly Ed Blum. On Monday, Blum was given an entire column in Jeff Bezos’s Washington Post to argue that colleges and universities are still discriminating against white and AAPI students by admitting Black people. Blum’s main point of contention was, as always, test scores: “The median LSAT scores of rejected Asian and White applicants in the 2024-2025 cycle at Duke far exceeded the median scores of admitted Black applicants, who were 3½ times more likely to be admitted than a similarly qualified Asian applicant,” Blum wrote. He then went on to decry the fact that schools (Blum’s column was mostly focused on law schools) continue to use personal essays and other factors to make admissions decisions.

People like Blum would have you believe that standardized test scores are the only thing that should matter when it comes to admissions—as if every applicant in the country should take a test and the person who scores “the best” should get into their first-choice school and so on and so forth, until the person who scores the worst can either go to Trump University or kill themselves.

But that’s not how admissions have ever worked or should ever work. Schools are going to take a number of factors into account when building their applicant pool. Blum, who has pushed these cases and organized groups of AAPI parents to do the same, has succeeded in convincing the courts that race is the one factor, different from all others, that schools cannot look at—but admissions committees are still free (as of now, at least) to look at a number of other things, including: geographic distribution, gender, economic factors, leadership qualities, legacy status, and whether an applicant “fits” with the school’s mission and ethos. Some law schools, for instance, place a heavy emphasis on work experience; others value people who are likely to stay local and serve the community where the school is located. Blum sees only one test to rule them all, but people smarter than Blum appreciate the (trigger warning) diversity of an admitted class with a mix of skills and strengths to bring to the classroom.

Blum’s solution to the apparent problem that Black people are still getting into school despite his organization’s best efforts is Orwellian in scope. He demands “mandatory public disclosure” of all of a school’s applicants. “This would include,” he wrote, “standardized test scores, GPAs, family income, Pell Grant eligibility, AP coursework and other metrics, broken down by race and ethnicity.”

Can you imagine applying to a school and, as a result, watching your family’s personal income become a matter of public record? Can you imagine having to share whether or not you are eligible for a Pell Grant with the entire world? Can you imagine having to do all of this because some guy like Ed Blum doesn’t think you belong in school? In three years, Blum has gone from “universities shouldn’t consider race” to “universities should make public personal information, broken down by race and ethnicity, for my edification.”

The goal isn’t just to keep Black and Latino kids out of schools; it’s to discourage them and their parents from even applying. It’s not enough that Black and Latino enrollment should fall off a cliff. If there is one Black kid in a selective school, there’s a white or AAPI parent who thinks their kid should have gotten in instead. No matter that the white or AAPI kid couldn’t outcompete a kid with similar education privileges who did get in. No matter that a test score isn’t the only or best measure of a person’s academic worth or intellectual potential. If a Black or Latino kid exists at a school, people like Ed Blum will always argue that they shouldn’t.

For these people, one Black kid will always be one too many. They think the Constitution doesn’t protect equal protection and equal access to opportunity. They think it protects overrepresentation for their kids.

How the energy crisis could become a food security crisis

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Iran war and the Ukraine-Russia conflict are creating a "multidirectional squeeze on world agriculture" that could spiral into a crisis, a new report finds.

Why it matters: Wars and extreme weather are colliding to threaten global food supplies and drive up prices, especially in vulnerable economies.

  • The report explores how the El Niño phenomenon could compound the problems of restricted supplies of key fertilizer ingredients, higher fuel costs on the farm and more.
  • The S&P Global Institute report is an unusually holistic look at the topic.

Driving the news: The analysis comes alongside new warnings about food from the International Monetary Fund.

  • Its World Economic Outlook last week sees an overall 10% increase in fertilizer prices this year and food costs rising by 5% in 2026 and 2.4% in 2027, though it depends on the region.
  • "Higher energy and food prices could have severe effects on poverty and food security" and threaten political stability in vulnerable economies, the IMF warned.

State of play: Food producers need steady supplies of natural gas and sulfur for fertilizer, diesel for farm machinery and food transport, and open sea lanes for trade, S&P notes.

  • "Conflict is now testing all of them at once, just as El Niño tests the harvest," states the report from the research and consulting firm.
  • "The risk is increasing, especially for low-income countries, where food costs amount to about half of household spending," it states.

Zoom out: The disruptions could affect agricultural production worldwide, from soybeans and corn in Brazil to wheat in Australia, S&P finds.

The intrigue: The S&P report also warns of something that has received somewhat less attention.

  • The diesel supply crunch doesn't just raise costs for farmers. It also works alongside pro-biofuels policies to make biodiesel production more attractive.
  • "The knock-on effect is that, in some regions, crops for fuel become more valuable than crops for food."

Zoom in: A few stats via S&P illustrate the overlapping threats.

  • Middle Eastern prices for exported sulfur, a fertilizer ingredient, reached a record $1,000 per metric ton in July, after starting the year at a historically high $500 per metric ton.
  • 47% of the global sulfur trade normally goes through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • In Indonesia, industrial use of palm oil, largely for biodiesel, is expected to be roughly five times its 2015-16 level.

The bottom line: A global food crisis is by no means certain.

  • But if wars keep disrupting energy and fertilizer supplies, and weather cuts harvests in key regions, "the elements of such a crisis will be falling into place," S&P warns.

Albanian Student Strikes Spur a Political Crisis

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new round of protests against the ruling party’s corruption has Prime Minister Edi Rama giving cops bonuses to crack down on dissent.

Police clash with protesters last week in central Tirana.

For the past week, students in the Albanian capital of Tirana have been on strike—an action that’s spurred major clashes with police and widespread damage to government buildings and public property. In response, Albanian Prime Minister Edi Rama has sought to step up the government crackdown, offering security forces extra money to suppress the worst political crisis of his 13 years in office.

The specter of a more intensive backlash does nothing to address the issues that sent Albanian students into the streets: increased education fees, poor school conditions, and a lengthy list of government corruption scandals, including the possibly illegal sale of land to Jared Kushner for luxury property development. In lieu of endorsing reforms on all these issues, Rama chose violence.

The latest turn in Albanian protest politics will likely just ratchet up the mood of growing frustration among dissenters. For 130 days this summer, Tirana saw a wave of peaceful protests against Rama’s approval of the Kushner-backed resort project—a boondoggle that detractors have argued only benefits powerful oligarchs close to Rama’s government.

But what happened last week took everyone by surprise. The Albanian student union called for school strikes and planned a series of demonstrations at the Education Ministry in central Tirana. The protests kept growing, and eventually a handful of black-masked provocateurs began tearing out CCTV cameras and stoplights in the city center. The confrontation escalated from there: Restive protesters threw rocks, exploded fireworks, and burned dumpsters. The cops responded with tear gas, water cannons, and baton beatings. Not surprisingly, the response pushed more demonstrators into anger.

Toward the end of the week, things got ugly. Demonstrations and vandalism paralyzed the city; banks, government ministries, police stations, and Rama’s political party headquarters were battered and burned by bricks and Molotov cocktails. A pattern emerged: Daytime protests mixed peaceful protesters with teenage Albanians throwing and breaking things; and nightfall brought pitched street battles between cops and more aggressive, older, and angrier crowds. Now cars and buildings were burning—and in one fracas with protesters, a cop was briefly set aflame.

By Sunday night, downtown Tirana scarcely had any stoplights, streetlights, bus stops, or dumpsters that weren’t damaged. Streets were covered with rocks, broken glass, and smoke, while clouds of tear gas besieged rioters and confused tourists. At least 100 protesters and 50 police have been treated for injuries, and dozens of protesters have been hospitalized or arrested.

“You cannot negotiate with this,” said Rama, displaying a piece of broken concrete at a Monday meeting with police officers. “The moment the state negotiates with this, it has fallen.”

In the closed meeting—Rama has limited public appearances since the protests began in June—the president said he was willing to discuss complaints about his government’s track record. But he stressed that he wouldn’t entertain the demands of protest leaders.

“Dialogue at any hour, on any day, anywhere, with anyone, except the political forces behind the violence that refuse to distance themselves from it,” he said. Given how long Albanians have been demonstrating against his government—and how widespread the protests have grown—Rama is essentially saying he’ll only negotiate with elites who are already likely to be in accord with his government anyway. Then came his bid to keep the security forces in crackdown mode: a pledge of across-the-board bumps in salary and a one-time bonus of about $2,800 to cops clashing with rioters. Most protesters saw the offer as a flagrant bribe designed to shore up his increasingly unpopular government.

“Rama doesn’t have the money to educate students because politicians steal public funds and he sells our land to his friends and foreigners without debate,” said Agim, a 22-year-old barista and student who comes out to protest when his schedule permits. “But somehow he’s got millions of euros to bribe the police to protect him.”

Despite Rama’s resounding election victory last year over opposition leader Sali Berisha, any residual sense of his governing under a popular mandate has succumbed to the overt corruption of his regime—and his refusal to confront popular dissent is only compounding his isolation from the struggles of ordinary Albanians.

And amid conditions of government impunity and corruption, public cynicism mounts. “Berisha and Rama are seen in the same light,” said a local journalist who covers crime and corruption, and asked not to be named because of mounting threats against regime opponents. “They compete with each other to control a rotten system for their gain and to repay their oligarch backers and the cocaine mafia that controls Albania’s economy. There’s no good guys on either side. But one bad guy [Rama] is more palatable to the European Union.”

In Albania’s relatively free press and political system, overt political repression is rare. But the journalist is far from alone in worrying about a crackdown. Lorenzo, a 24-year-old waiter and protester, explains that face masks and avoiding arrest are crucial.

“If the police arrest you, you can lose any scholarship or government job,” he said. “My mother is a nurse at a government hospital in Korča. If my face is photographed and the police identify me, she could lose her job. They’re threatening our families.”

In the 1990s, after Albania broke free from a brutal communist regime that ruled for 45 years, crushing dissent and immiserating the populace, the country fell into chaotic violence. Local gangs, former security commanders, and an influential cohort of organized crime bosses effectively lorded over the streets until peacekeepers from nearby Italy helped restore government control in 1999.

From that low point, Albania was able to transition into a fairly calm, peaceful, and democratic state, even as its economy floundered. As the government managed to deliver basic services, and secure basic freedom of movement and speech, street crime also abated, and a small but growing tourist economy took hold, seeking to exploit the country’s pristine beaches and stunning mountain ranges.

After Rama came to power in 2013, he was able to leverage Albania’s more stable footing into an investment-friendly economic agenda. He also embarked on the long path to Albania’s admission to the EU in 2030—a plan that most citizens of this former pariah state eagerly support.

Yet Rama’s reputation as a competent technocratic reformer was short-lived. Despite Albania’s progress toward a more open society, the country has never managed to break the feudal control of its economy by powerful families and clans. Corruption moved behind closed doors as officials and oligarchs divided public funds among them, and employed bribes to secure overpriced government contracts. The economy, particularly for young people, completely stagnated, leading to an exodus over the last decade of at least a half a million Albanians out of a total population of just under 3 million.

Albanian crime lords, meanwhile, acquired a major interest in Europe’s lucrative cocaine trade. Albanians played an outsize role in transporting cocaine from South America to ports in Europe, which they then leveraged into control of much of the wholesale trade across Europe. Albanian gangsters ran much of the traffic in cocaine and heroin in the United Kingdom after waging a bloody gang war across London to drive out rival English and Kurdish gangs.

The proceeds and criminal fallout of this drug coup haven’t directly affected life in the Albanian homeland—most Albanians can’t afford cocaine, and the local marijuana trade is peaceful enough. But the country is plagued with rampant money laundering, as Albanian drug lords seek to transform billions in cocaine-related revenue into bank accounts and legal assets.

This means that there’s been a paper boom in the construction of restaurants, business, and apartments and resorts across the country. Tirana’s under a massive construction boom, with cocaine money springing for an estimated 12,000 new luxury apartment units that few Albanians can afford.

If Tirana were able to harness all the incoming drug money into something like a viable development plan, Rama might have been able to purchase a measure of social peace and stability. But this spending and development hasn’t led to better jobs or increased public funding of basic services. Instead, Albanians are facing the major signs of oligarch-bred inequality: soaring housing prices, expensive bars and restaurants that clearly lose money, and the surreal sight of twentysomething guys with Manchester and London accents driving Bentleys around Western Europe’s poorest capital—all in the shadow of luxury apartment towers that no reasonable person expects to be inhabited.

Albanian students aren’t dumb; the corruption and predation of Rama’s regime have greatly disillusioned the rising generation of Albanians. The luckiest among them have emigrated to other European countries, settling in the UK, Belgium, Netherlands, Germany, Greece, and Switzerland to work ordinary jobs that offer a future; the country’s émigré population is well into the hundreds of thousands. A 24-year-old truck driver named Luisi who’s turned out for the protests says he’s also thinking of leaving the country for Malmo, Sweden, where a cousin of his lives—and where Balkan organized crime has established a major hub. Luisi now supplements his modest $600-a-month salary by selling small amounts of marijuana and cocaine in clubs that he can’t afford to visit unless he’s working. His cousin assures him that “there’s work and I can make a lot of money.”

When I ask him what his cousin does, Luisi laughs. “He’s a gangster bro, he wants me to come help him. I don’t know if I should. But I’ll never get a good job or buy a house in Albania.”

Luisi then starts to interview me: “How much does a commercial truck driver get paid in America? I don’t want to be a gangster with my cousin, but I can’t stay here.”

The Future of CSS ✨

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

For the past few years, all of the major browser vendors (Google, Apple, Microsoft, and Mozilla, along with Igalia and Bocoup) have been working together on Interop(opens in new tab), a project to improve universal browser support on web technologies. Scientists finally uncover why statins can cause muscle pain

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Statins are among the most important medications for preventing cardiovascular disease. By lowering cholesterol, they have helped save millions of lives and reduce the likelihood of heart attacks and strokes. However, these benefits can come with unwanted effects. Some patients develop muscle pain or weakness, while a small number experience serious muscle breakdown that can potentially cause kidney failure.

Scientists at the University of British Columbia (UBC), working with colleagues at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, have identified a molecular mechanism that helps explain these complications. Their findings, published in Nature Communications, could guide the development of safer statins that maintain their cholesterol-lowering benefits while minimizing muscle damage.

How Statins Trigger a Harmful Calcium Leak

To investigate what happens inside muscle cells, the researchers used cryo-electron microscopy, an advanced imaging method that allows scientists to examine protein structures in remarkable detail. Their investigation centered on the ryanodine receptor (RyR1), a protein that regulates the movement of calcium within muscle cells.

Under normal conditions, this protein functions as a controlled gateway, releasing calcium when muscles need to contract. The researchers found that statins can interfere with this process by holding the channel open. As a result, calcium escapes continuously into areas where it should be carefully regulated, creating conditions that can injure muscle tissue.

"We were able to see, almost atom by atom, how statins latch onto this channel," said lead author Dr. Steven Molinarolo, a postdoctoral researcher in UBC's department of biochemistry and molecular biology. "That leak of calcium explains why some patients experience muscle pain or, in extreme cases, life-threatening complications."

Three Statin Molecules Reveal the Mechanism Behind Muscle Damage

The researchers concentrated on atorvastatin, a commonly prescribed medication for lowering cholesterol. Although this particular drug was the main focus, their results suggest that similar interactions could occur with other medications in the statin family.

One of the most striking discoveries was the unusual way statin molecules attach to the calcium channel. Rather than acting individually, three molecules occupy a specific pocket within the protein. The first binds while the channel is closed, making it more likely to open. Two additional molecules then enter the pocket and hold the channel open, allowing calcium to continue leaking.

"This is the first time we've had a clear picture of how statins activate this channel," said Dr. Filip Van Petegem, senior author and professor at UBC's Life Sciences Institute. "It's a big step forward because it gives us a roadmap for designing statins that don't interact with muscle tissue."

This detailed understanding could give drug developers a way to address the problem at its source. By modifying the specific features of statin molecules that interfere with muscle proteins, scientists may be able to reduce unwanted effects without compromising the drugs' ability to control cholesterol.

A Potential Path Toward Safer Cholesterol Drugs

More than 200 million people worldwide use statins. Although severe muscle injury occurs in only a small percentage of patients, less serious problems such as soreness, weakness, and fatigue are considerably more widespread. These symptoms can be disruptive enough that some people discontinue their medication, potentially losing important protection against cardiovascular disease.

The discovery could eventually help researchers create treatments that are easier to tolerate, allowing more patients to continue taking medications that reduce their risk of heart attacks and strokes.

Achieving this level of understanding required exceptionally detailed imaging. Working with the high-resolution macromolecular cryo-electron microscopy facility at UBC's faculty of medicine, the team was able to observe precisely how statin molecules interact with the muscle protein. These structural insights connect a longstanding drug safety concern to a specific molecular process, providing valuable information for future medication development.

"Statins have been a cornerstone of cardiovascular care for decades," Dr. Van Petegem said. "Our goal is to make them even safer, so patients can benefit without fear of serious side effects."

Although additional research and drug development will be needed before these findings translate into new treatments, they offer a promising direction. For millions of people who depend on statins to protect their hearts, future medications with fewer muscle-related complications could make treatment more comfortable and improve everyday quality of life.

Hurricane warning maps are improving, but a dangerous problem remains

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

An updated hurricane forecast map is making it easier for people to identify areas threatened by storms, but new research reveals a concerning limitation. Even when people can recognize where hurricane watches are in effect, they may not feel more at risk or become more inclined to take precautions.

Researchers at Florida State University examined whether changes to the National Hurricane Center (NHC) forecast graphics improve public understanding of hurricane threats. The study involved faculty from the School of Communication within the College of Communication and Information and the Department of Geography.

The researchers evaluated an experimental graphic that expands the traditional hurricane forecast cone to include tropical storm and hurricane watches and warnings for inland areas. The familiar cone has historically placed greater emphasis on coastal regions, even though dangerous storm conditions can extend far from the shoreline.

The findings revealed mixed results. Although the revised graphic helped people without specialized weather knowledge recognize inland areas under hurricane or tropical storm watches, it did not significantly influence how dangerous they believed the storms would be or whether they intended to take protective measures. Participants also showed no improvement in identifying areas covered by storm warnings.

The updated format was developed by the NHC in 2024, and the findings were published in the American Meteorological Society journal Weather, Climate, and Society.

"The National Hurricane Center has made a number of thoughtful changes to improve how hurricane information is communicated," said Assistant Professor Elizabeth Ray, the study's lead author. "We had a simple question. Do those changes actually help people understand what the graphic is telling them about the risk they face?"

Why People Misunderstand Hurricane Forecast Maps

To investigate how the public interprets hurricane forecasts, the research team surveyed residents of Florida and Georgia. Participants were shown either the conventional hurricane cone graphic or the newer experimental version that includes additional information about inland storm threats.

People who saw the revised map were more successful at identifying locations under hurricane or tropical storm watches. However, that improvement in recognizing watch areas did not lead to greater concern about the potential danger or stronger intentions to take safety precautions.

The results also highlighted a persistent misunderstanding surrounding the familiar hurricane "cone of uncertainty." Many people incorrectly interpret the width of the cone as an indication of a storm's physical size, rather than understanding what the graphic is intended to communicate about the uncertainty in its projected track.

"For years, research has shown that people often mistake a wider cone for a bigger storm," Ray said. "Our findings suggest that even with an updated graphic, that misconception can be hard to shake."

Why Better Hurricane Warnings May Not Be Enough

The NHC introduced its expanded forecast graphic in 2024 to help people recognize that hurricane dangers are not limited to communities along the coast. Flooding, tornadoes, and destructive winds can threaten areas well inland, making it important for residents beyond coastal evacuation zones to understand their potential exposure.

By adding inland hurricane and tropical storm watches and warnings to the traditional forecast cone, the updated graphic provides a more complete picture of where dangerous conditions could develop.

However, the study suggests that adding useful information to a forecast does not automatically make people more aware of their personal risk or more likely to respond appropriately.

The researchers stressed that their findings do not call into question the quality of the NHC's weather predictions, which they consider highly accurate. Instead, the results underscore the difficulty of presenting complex meteorological information in a way that people can readily interpret and use when making safety decisions.

"Our question wasn't whether the science is good," said co-author Patrick Merle, professor and director of the School of Communication. "The science is excellent. The question is how we communicate that science so people who aren't meteorologists understand the risk they're facing.

If people find information credible but still don't understand it well enough to take protective action, then communication has fallen short. The goal is to empower people with information they can use to stay safe."

The findings point to the importance of looking beyond a single forecast image when preparing for a hurricane. Although the cone can provide valuable information about a storm's expected path, it cannot communicate every hazard that a particular community might experience.

The researchers recommend using hurricane forecast graphics alongside local weather predictions and detailed information about possible impacts. Understanding the specific threats to an area, as well as the protective steps residents should consider, can provide a more useful basis for making decisions.

"Our findings are a reminder that one graphic can only tell you part of the story," Ray said. "Don't rely on the cone alone. Pair it with clear, local information about what impacts to expect and what actions to take. That gives you a fuller picture of what the storm could mean where you live and what to do next."

The study received funding through an internal Florida State University SEED grant. The research was also informed by discussions with the NHC, which supplied the experimental forecast graphics evaluated by the team.

Astronomers uncover new secrets of the Universe’s first five minutes

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have measured the amount of helium produced during the Universe's first five minutes with unprecedented precision, offering a clearer picture of the conditions that existed shortly after the Big Bang. The achievement, made by an international research team that includes scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, represents a major advance in precision cosmology.

The findings, presented in five papers published in The Astrophysical Journal, are based on 130 hours of observations using the Large Binocular Telescope. By analyzing distant galaxies, the researchers reduced the uncertainty in this fundamental measurement to just 0.5%, making it three times more precise than previous estimates.

The improved accuracy provides scientists with a powerful new tool for investigating the origins of the Universe and testing the Standard Model of physics. Such fundamental tests have a long history of advancing scientific understanding and contributing to technological breakthroughs.

"This is a physics experiment on a grand scale and one of the biggest findings in my entire 40-year career," said Evan Skillman, University of Minnesota College of Science and Engineering Distinguished Professor in the School of Physics and Astronomy. "It's a fundamental number that tells us specifically about the conditions of our Universe in its first five minutes. It has diagnostic power that speaks directly to the Standard Model of Physics."

According to the Big Bang theory, the Universe began expanding from an extraordinarily hot, dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago. Three major lines of evidence support this explanation: the continuing expansion of the Universe, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and the amounts of light elements such as helium and deuterium that formed during its earliest moments.

Although cosmic expansion and the microwave background have been studied extensively, measurements of primordial helium have historically received less attention. Determining its original abundance more accurately allows researchers to investigate the physical conditions that prevailed when the Universe was only minutes old.

To achieve their new level of precision, the team departed from the conventional method of estimating primordial helium. That approach involves observing large numbers of galaxies and extrapolating the results to determine how much helium existed before later generations of stars altered their chemical composition.

Instead, the researchers selected 15 exceptionally pristine, small, distant galaxies. These galaxies have undergone relatively little chemical evolution, making them valuable cosmic "time capsules" that preserve clues about the Universe's early composition.

Powerful Telescopes Deliver Record Precision

The team used sophisticated spectrographs developed at Ohio State University to examine more than 10 helium spectral lines and 15 hydrogen lines simultaneously. Studying these signals together made it possible to identify and correct subtle sources of measurement error that earlier studies could largely overlook.

Such small effects become especially important when researchers are trying to reduce uncertainty to less than 1%. Accounting for them was essential to achieving the new 0.5% precision.

"The MODS spectrographs took 12 years to build from conception to first light on sky," said Richard Pogge, College of Arts and Sciences Distinguished Professor of Astronomy at Ohio State University. "This is the kind of project we designed them to do, and to see them deliver is enormously satisfying. It's not every day you can help build instruments that measure something fundamental about our Universe."

Beyond refining estimates of the Universe's earliest chemical composition, the new measurement provides an important test of the Standard Model of physics. The amount of helium created shortly after the Big Bang depends on the physical conditions at that time, including the types of elementary particles present.

Using their precise helium measurement, the researchers were able to calculate the number of neutrino families in the early Universe. Neutrinos are extremely light subatomic particles, and their behavior plays an important role in scientists' understanding of fundamental physics.

The results therefore do more than strengthen existing evidence for the Big Bang. They also offer a more precise way to check whether established physical theories accurately describe the Universe during its first few minutes.

"We promised a half-percent uncertainty in our proposal, and we got there," added Skillman. "In the unpredictable world of science, that doesn't happen very often."

The international collaboration brought together scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, Ohio State University, Gonzaga University, Northwestern University, The University of Texas at Austin, Indiana University, University of California Santa Cruz, University of Illinois, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, and TRIUMF.

I’m a CTO. AI still needs your human expertise | Madhu Shashanka

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI is increasingly getting good at doing some tasks, but it can never replace human workers’ judgment

Autonomous cars, which may be the safest we have built, keep getting easily avoidable parking tickets. While Waymos crash far less often than human drivers over tens of millions of miles, they’re still terrible at performing basic tasks – keeping out of disabled parking spots and tow-away zones, or steering clear of a church garage during Sunday service, according to the Wall Street Journal.

That is the state of AI today: impressively capable at something complex, yet embarrassingly clumsy at something easy. And it has implications for what work will remain human and what will be automated by AI.

People expect AI to lead to fewer jobs in 34 of the 37 countries surveyed by the Pew Research Center. In wealthy countries like the US, Australia and South Korea, about seven in 10 expect job losses over the next 20 years. And across countries, concern among young people is rising. While fears focus on job numbers, the bigger change will be what jobs become.

The nature of human work will move towards judgment. Experts refer to AI as “jagged”, superhuman at certain tasks but weak at others, with no clear boundary between the two. AI rapidly improves where work can be automatically checked and examples are endless. But it’s shaky where no automated test can determine whether the answer is right. Even if AI is correct a majority of the time on a task, it often doesn’t tell you when it’s inaccurate. If you’ve used AI, you’ve probably experienced how it can be confidently wrong.

Some of these errors will solve themselves as models get better. But jaggedness is a symptom of how these models learn. I see this often as a technology leader building with these systems.

A smarter model still needs a right answer to learn from. When one gap is closed, it shows up somewhere else no one thought to check. But many business decisions don’t immediately have a clear right answer. Whether a choice is good depends on a future no one can see and data no one has. That’s where judgment comes in. So AI will keep mastering tasks that have checkable results but struggle where a person has to decide what a good outcome looks like.

As a result, a new type of work is starting to appear. Someone tests whether the output of AI is correct based on real work, not just on benchmarks. Someone sets limits on what AI is allowed to do before it runs, so the worst mistakes are avoided. Someone runs and manages several agents the way a shift manager runs a crew. Picture an airport control tower. Radar and software track flight paths, with humans judging the few that matter to avoid collisions. No one is manually watching every screen. That would never scale.

Setting AI’s limits is harder than it sounds. In a 2026 study, after an agent was told to protect a secret, it destroyed its own email server, deleting every single email. It was given a goal, without any limits on what it could do. The researchers described it as the right values paired with terrible judgment. Deciding what is off limits and what a mistake would cost takes someone who knows the work and will answer for the result.

Here is the good news for people worried about their own jobs. The people best suited for doing this new type of work aren’t computer programmers. They are the ones who know how the real work is done. Catching what a confident AI left out or got wrong takes someone who knows the complete answer. That knowledge is uncheckable, unwritten and takes the form of judgment honed over years of doing the task and watching it break.

Ford recently learned the value of human expertise. In recent years, it leaned hard on automating its quality control, cutting thousands of jobs. But earlier this year, it had to rehire 350 veteran engineers to oversee the process and hunt for failure points before a part reached the factory floor. They brought the judgment of watching parts fail over years. With humans at the helm, Ford’s quality ranking then hit first among mainstream brands for the first time since 2010.

But we are making a serious mistake with the jobs we’re eliminating due to AI: the entry-level, routine ones. That routine work is how judgment gets built over time. Junior employees work under the supervision of senior experts until their experience turns into instinct. AI eliminates the friction related to learning how to do the job. But the demand for judgment did not go away. We are just destroying the path to developing it.

The real question is not whether machines will take over our work. It is whether we will have the people who can tell when the machines are wrong. Right now, we are automating the training ground where judgment is learned. If you have hard-earned expertise about anything, the next few years will only make you more valuable. And if you are just starting out, the old apprenticeship model will not hand it to you. You have to go and earn it, deliberately, on tasks the machines cannot yet judge.

Sixteen years after an explosion on the Deepwater Horizon oil rig unleashed more than 4 million barrels of oil into the Gulf of Mexico, life in the deep sea has yet to recover.

A new study finds that in the first few years after the 2010 disaster, populations of octopus, squid, krill, shrimp, and lanternfish declined by at least 60 percent. The research, based on acoustic surveys and thousands of netted samples across a broad swath of the Gulf of Mexico, found little evidence that life in the deep sea has bounced back.

The study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, described the losses as “the fastest and most severe” decline of animal life in the ocean layer that extends from about 600 feet deep to more than two miles down. While researchers stopped short of concluding that the oil spill had caused the declines, they said it was “the most parsimonious explanation.”

Since the disaster, scientists have found oil-related compounds in the flesh of squid and octopus and in fish eggs deep underwater, contamination that they say can impair reproduction and development or lead to an early death. Lingering pollution may make it harder for deep-sea fish that are guided by their sense of smell to find mates.

North Korea publishes photos of Kim Jong Un - why do rumours of his death keep coming back?

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

North Korean state media released photos of Kim Jong Un shortly after rumours of his death started spreading online

Kim Jong Un has appeared in North Korean state media looking remarkably healthy for a man rumoured to have died from a brain haemorrhage.

Photographs published by state newspaper Rodong Sinmun show the 42-year-old dictator touring what the paper said was a clothing and footwear exhibition with his daughter.

The images emerged hours after a Chinese dissident who lives in the US claimed, without evidence, that Kim had been taken to a hospital in Beijing in a coma and had died.

For those who watch North Korea closely, this kind of rumour has become almost routine.

In one of the world's most secretive states, facts are often difficult to verify, creating fertile ground for speculation.

This latest claim originated with Wanjun Xie, who identifies himself as chairman of the Chinese Democratic Party, a group banned by Chinese authorities. He has previously circulated unverified claims, including speculation about the death of Chinese leader Xi Jinping.

Xie alleged that Kim suffered a severe brain haemorrhage and was transferred to Beijing because North Korea lacked the medical facilities to treat him.

He claimed Kim later died and that his body had been secretly returned to North Korea, with the regime concealing his death.

Yet the photographs tell a different story.

Kim is seen examining displays, accompanied by Kim Ju Ae and his usual entourage of officials.

While there is no way to know exactly when the images were taken, there are other reasons to doubt the claim.

There would probably be signs beyond North Korea's borders if there were a sudden collapse in Kim's health.

China could tighten security along its frontier to prepare for potential instability or refugee flows. Activity around North Korean diplomatic missions might also increase. Defectors in South Korea often receive information through informal networks along the Chinese border. None of this has happened.

South Korean intelligence officials, meanwhile, told Yonhap they had heard nothing to support the reports.

The rumour also echoes previous false alarms.

In 2020, a high-profile North Korean defector claimed he was "99% certain" Kim had died following cardiovascular surgery. Not long afterwards, Kim appeared in public and the claim unravelled.

So why do these rumours keep resurfacing? The first reason is succession.

North Korea has been ruled by the Kim family for three generations, but what happens after Kim Jong Un remains less certain than it was for his predecessors.

Kim Il Sung spent decades preparing his son, Kim Jong Il, for power. By the time Kim Il Sung died in 1994, the succession plan was firmly established. Kim Jong Il's transition was relatively smooth.

Kim Jong Un's rise was faster and more precarious. After Kim Jong Il suffered a stroke in 2008, his youngest son was rapidly promoted through the ranks. By the time his father died in 2011, he had been publicly identified as the future leader.

Even so, Kim Jong Un spent years consolidating power, purging rivals and eliminating perceived threats.

Today, South Korea's intelligence agency believes he may be preparing his daughter as a successor. She has increasingly appeared at high-profile events and military displays.

But she is thought to be only 13 years old. North Korea has never been led by a woman, and she has not been formally designated as heir. Her youth would also leave her vulnerable in any power struggle.

All of this uncertainty helps explain why rumours about Kim's health attract so much attention.

The second reason is that the rumours are not entirely implausible.

Kim is overweight and a heavy smoker. South Korea's National Intelligence Service has repeatedly warned that his lifestyle puts him at higher risk of cardiovascular disease. Heart problems and strokes also run in the Kim family.

If anything were to happen to Kim, the stakes are high for neighbouring countries and the wider international community. Any sudden leadership crisis raises difficult questions about control of North Korea's nuclear weapons and the prospect of political instability.

Would a power struggle tempt hardliners to act aggressively? Could unrest trigger a refugee crisis along the Chinese border? These are questions that concern not only Beijing and Seoul but also Washington, which maintains around 28,000 troops in South Korea.

Intelligence agencies watch Kim Jong Un closely so they are prepared for any news.

Because North Korea remains an information black hole, it is also an easy target for false reports. Only a tiny circle has regular access to its leader. Even intelligence agencies struggle to obtain reliable information, and much of what emerges is heavily caveated as unverified or highly likely rather than confirmed fact.

The regime itself is also suspected of deliberately encouraging confusion, leaking misleading information to obscure what is really happening inside the country.

So it is hard to believe that Wanjun Xie had somehow stumbled across such significant news, when it had eluded those whose job it is to stay across it.

He may simply have been seeking attention. Or he may have wanted to embarrass Beijing by highlighting its ties to Pyongyang, although those are well-established for the world to see.

Whatever his motive, Xie is far from the first person to exploit the uncertainty surrounding North Korea's leadership.
