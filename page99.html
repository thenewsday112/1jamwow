Dog owner is fined £100 for parking six hours while pet was being put down after spending £1,500 on treatment

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published: 16:07 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 17:00 GMT, 25 October 2026

A heartbroken dog owner was slapped with a £100 parking fine for spending six hours at the vets while his beloved pet was put down.

David Atkinson, 49, rushed his nine-year-old bulldog Reggie to the vet after he had started wheezing and struggling to breathe on September 19.

Once at Vets for Pets, inside Pets at Home in Canterbury, he spent £1,500 on emergency scans and treatment, but tragically, the vet said Reggie had to be put down.

After the lonely journey home without the family pet, Mr Atkinson was struck with another blow when a parking charge arrived in the post a few days later.

Mr Atkinson said: 'It's just an insult. To think that a parking ticket is warranted in that situation is beyond belief.'

The bereaved owner had spent six gruelling hours tending to his dying dog - but he forgot there was a three-hour time limit in the car park outside, which he had more than doubled.

Despite explaining the circumstances, parking firm UK Car Park Management (UKCPM) initially rejected his appeal, leaving Mr Atkinson furious at what he called a complete lack of compassion.

Bosses have now confirmed they have cancelled the charge as a 'gesture of goodwill'.

Mr Atkinson's bulldog Reggie was discovered to have a kinked windpipe after a walk in the woods and tragically had to be put down

'I genuinely believed once they received my appeal and the reasons why I was there for so long, they would understand and just cancel the parking fine,' Mr Atkinson said before the fee was rescinded.

'I thought with any kind of human compassion, you'd read the appeal and understand why I was there for six hours.

'It's not like I'd parked my car and walked into town. I was inside the vet's.

'It didn't cross my mind at any stage that I would have to come and move my car because I was passing the three-hour time limit.

'When my dog's dying, it wasn't really on my mind to be doing that.'

The ordeal began when Mr Atkinson took Reggie for a walk in Blean Woods, near Canterbury, on Saturday, September 19.

But on their way home, the bulldog began struggling to breathe.

'I went home and just thought he would lie in the garden and calm himself down,' Mr Atkinson recalled.

'But he never did, so I took him to the vets as his breathing seemed to get worse. You could see he was struggling.'

ANPR cameras recorded Mr Atkinson arriving in his Porsche Taycan at 2.21pm and leaving at 8.55pm, a total stay of six hours and 34 minutes, more than double the car park's three-hour limit.

During that time, vets sedated Reggie and carried out X-rays and CT scans, but Mr Atkinson, who lives in Canterbury, was eventually told his windpipe was bent and his heart was inflamed.

With no prospect of recovery, Reggie had to be put to sleep.

Mr Atkinson - who works as a director of an online jewellery retailer - remained at the practice throughout Reggie's treatment, briefly sitting in his car while scans were carried out.

When Pets at Home closed at 7pm, he had to leave the building temporarily before being allowed back inside to spend a final hour saying goodbye.

After receiving the parking charge and having his appeal rejected, Mr Atkinson telephoned UKCPM to check whether the company understood his circumstances.

But the company's initial refusal to back down prompted him to contact local media.

'It just seemed very non-sympathetic to the situation that my dog passed away, and I was there for six hours with him in intensive care,' he said.

'Then to get the fine just seemed very upsetting, and it angered me.'

Mr Atkinson stressed he had no complaints about the veterinary practice, which he said offered to contact the parking company with proof of how long Reggie had been receiving treatment.

But he questioned why a three-hour restriction was in place at a car park serving a veterinary practice where emergency treatment could take much longer.

'Maybe if it's just a supermarket, you don't need to be there for three hours. I understand that,' he said.

'But for a vet, you might have to be there for that long. Why would you have a parking limit on an area which could be as sensitive as that?'

Mr Atkinson said the loss of Reggie has been a devastating blow for his family, particularly his three children, aged 11, eight and five, who had grown up alongside 'the fantastic dog'.

'He had a great temperament, just a fantastic dog,' Mr Atkinson said.

'Before I left for the vets, I told the kids to come and give him a stroke and say goodbye to him, just in case. I didn't want them not to have a chance to say goodbye.

'They got extremely upset, and I said, "It's okay, dad is going to be coming home with him. Just give him a stroke and tell him you love him."

'And then, of course, he never did come home.'

In a statement, a spokesman for UKCPM said the company is 'very sorry to hear about Mr Atkinson's loss and recognises the difficult circumstances surrounding his visit'.

'When the original appeal was reviewed, we did not have supporting evidence relating to the emergency veterinary treatment and the decision was therefore made based on the information available at the time,' they said.

'Having reviewed the circumstances, we recognise this was an exceptional situation and have cancelled the parking charge as a gesture of goodwill.'

UKCPM's decision comes just days after Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced tougher rules for private parking firms, including a 10-minute grace period and the cancellation of charges in certain medical emergencies.

However, the new rules have yet to come into force, and it remains unclear whether the provisions covering medical emergencies would extend to veterinary treatment.

Pets at Home has been contacted for comment.

Ciężki weekend dla Brytyjczyków i Norwegów. Boelo nie odpuszcza

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ponad 82 tys. gospodarstw domowych w Wielkiej Brytanii zostało w niedzielę pozbawionych prądu. To efekt niżu Boelo, któremu towarzyszą gwałtowne wichury i ulewne deszcze. Z powodu burzy odwołano wiele lotów, wstrzymano kursowanie pociągów i promów. Nie odbyło się także wiele zaplanowanych na weekend imprez. Burza uderzyła również w południową Norwegię - spowodowała odwołanie rejsów promowych, liczne wypadki drogowe i przerwy w dostawach prądu.

60-letni mężczyzna zginął po tym, jak spadło na niego drzewo w pobliżu Castle Douglas w hrabstwie Dumfries and Galloway - informuje BBC.

Brytyjski urząd meteorologiczny (Met Office) ostrzegł w niedzielę, że Boelo nadal stanowi zagrożenie dla życia i zalecił mieszkańcom, aby w miarę możliwości pozostali w domach. Alerty objęły Szkocję, Irlandię Północną i północną Anglię.

W niedzielę rano przez północne regiony kraju, a zwłaszcza przez Szkocję, przeszły wichury z porywami wiatru osiągającymi prędkość do 135 km na godzinę.

W Szkocji oraz w północnej Anglii obowiązuje pomarańczowy alert w związku z silnym wiatrem, drugi w skali Met Office stopień zagrożenia, który pozostanie w mocy do późnych godzin wieczornych.

Organizacja ENA poinformowała, że po przejściu burz Austen (w ubiegłym tygodniu) i Boelo przywrócono dostawy prądu do 345 tys. odbiorców. Ponad 82 tys. gospodarstw domowych jest bez prądu.

Według zarządcy infrastruktury kolejowej Network Rail Scotland w Szkocji doszło do poważnych zakłóceń w transporcie. Powalone drzewa, zerwane linie energetyczne oraz podtopienia, w tym zalanie torów na jednej ze stacji kolejowych w Glasgow, spowodowały zamknięcie wielu dróg i linii kolejowych.

W Irlandii Północnej, według operatora sieci energetycznej, w niedzielę przed południem bez prądu pozostawało 7 tys. gospodarstw domowych. Konieczne było również odwołanie rejsów promowych z Belfastu do Szkocji i Anglii.

W niedzielę rano na międzynarodowym lotnisku w Belfaście odwołano ok. 15 lotów. Z kolei na lotnisku Belfast-George Best anulowano połączenia m.in. z Londynem, Glasgow i Leeds.

W całej Wielkiej Brytanii z powodu Boelo odwołano wiele wydarzeń zaplanowanych na ten weekend.

W Liverpoolu nie zostały włączone iluminacje w ramach festiwalu „River of Light”. Na północy Anglii – m.in. w Salford i Darlington – odwołano targi rolnicze. W Paisley w środkowej Szkocji i Cookstown w Irlandii Północnej nie odbyły się festiwale z okazji Halloween. W Edynburgu władze miasta zamknęły w sobotni wieczór popularne ogrody Princes Street Gardens. Nie można było też zwiedzać edynburskiego zamku.

Z powodu wiatru dochodzącego w porywach do 135 km/godz. przerwana została komunikacja morska między Norwegią a Danią. Armator Color Line odwołał wszystkie rejsy do duńskiego Hirtshals co najmniej do poniedziałku do godz. 16.30. W podróż nie popłynęło co najmniej 1000 pasażerów. Służby meteorologiczne podniosły alarm pogodowy do drugiego najwyższego poziomu. W okolicach Bergen i Stavanger na zachodzie kraju prognozowane są fale o wysokości do sześciu metrów. Najwyższy poziom morza spodziewany jest w poniedziałek przed południem.

TIRANA, Oct 25 - Protesters poured onto the streets of Tirana on Sunday hours after Prime Minister Edi Rama announced new measures to stem a wave of anti-government demonstrations.

On a rainy afternoon, protesters gathered in front of the Education Ministry and then walked to Rama's office, setting on fire chairs he had placed in front of it as an invitation to a dialogue with the government.

Student-led demonstrations, fuelled by anger over perceived government corruption, a lack of jobs and poor education standards, have engulfed central Tirana and spread to other cities this month.

Demonstrators demanding Rama's resignation have set fire to government buildings and banks, smashed shop windows and pulled up paving stones to throw at police.

"I believe that those who demand my resignation are mistaken if they think they can impose it through violence or any other means, but I do not believe that everything they say or demand is wrong," Rama said in a video address.

The unrest, which followed months of largely peaceful summer protests against a luxury coastal development backed by US President Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner, poses the biggest challenge yet to Rama, who has governed since 2013 and won a fourth term in office last year.

Earlier on Sunday, Rama introduced new measures to curb violence including fines of 500,000 lek ($6,188) for everyone who participates in a protest that turns violent even if he or she does not commit acts of violence. REUTERS

U.S. federal safety regulators continue to tackle the sticky problem of autonomous vehicles, this time with a proposal that could signal to the public, quite literally, when a vehicle is operating autonomously.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has proposed new lighting requirements for autonomous vehicles that include an option (dare I say a strong suggestion?) to use cyan-colored lights to signal when a vehicle is operating autonomously without a human occupant monitoring the road.

The agency is not proposing a mandate for blue-green lights. Importantly, under the proposal, the lights would be used when an AV is operating in autonomous mode.

Still, federal regulators are clearly seeking some uniform way of identifying autonomous vehicles.

The proposal establishes equipment specifications, including where the lights are mounted, the color they emit, and how they’re activated. It would also remove requirements for manually operated controls for turn signals, high beams, and hazard lights.

Under the proposal, the NHTSA suggests the lights be cyan (blue-green), remain steadily illuminated rather than flash, and be visible from the front, rear, and both sides.

This latest proposal is one piece of a bigger effort to update regulations that help speed up the adoption of AVs while maintaining safety, according to NHTSA administrator Jonathan Morrison.

The agency is already working on a federal automated vehicle framework and is trying to update federal motor vehicle safety standards, which already govern automakers today. For instance, FMVSS rules were not designed for vehicles like the Tesla Cybercab and Zoox robotaxi, which lack traditional, federally required equipment like pedals, steering wheels, and design features like side mirrors and rearview mirrors.

Kinderhochburg Prenzlauer Berg: Was ist aus dem einstigen "Pregnancy Hill" geworden?

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Der Name Prenzlauer Berg stand einst für volle Spielplätze und Latte-Macchiato-Mütter. Inzwischen hat sich das Bild in "Pregnancy Hill" geändert. Was sind die Gründe?

Prenzlauer Berg, das war lange der Inbegriff von Kinderreichtum und Gentrifizierung. Das Bild von Latte macchiato trinkenden Prenzlauer-Berg-Muttis, die mit ihren Kinderwagen nebeneinander schiebend die ausladenden Gehwege verstopften, verbreitete sich auch weit über die Hauptstadt hinaus. Im Gegensatz dazu standen ähnliche Stadtteile anderer Großstädte wie Ottensen im hamburgischen Altona oder Sülz in Köln-Lindenthal.

Dabei bekamen nicht etwa die Prenzlauer-Berg-Mütter mehr Kinder - es lebten einfach mehr Menschen im Alter zwischen 25 bis 45 Jahren dort, die Jahre, in denen man typischerweise Kinder bekommt. Dadurch war auch die Zahl der Kinder hoch: Vor knapp 20 Jahren lebten mehr als 9000 Kinder unter sechs Jahren in Prenzlauer Berg, heute sind es etwa 7800, bei deutlich gestiegener Bevölkerungszahl - allerdings auch deutlich gestiegenen Mieten.

Für Eltern war es jahrelang schwierig, Kitaplätze zu bekommen. Man nahm, was man kriegen konnte. "Goldstaub" seien Kitaplätze vor einigen Jahren noch gewesen, sagt Ansgar Oberholz, Co-Gründer von St. Oberholz, einem Anbieter von Co-Working-Spaces. Er wohnt seit 1999 in Prenzlauer Berg.

Mittlerweile sei das umgekehrt: "Die Kitas bewerben sich auf die Kinder", sagt der 53-Jährige, der mittlerweile auch ein Enkelkind hat. Im Sommer hingen die Spielplatztore über Wochen voller Schilder, auf denen Kitas mit freien Plätzen warben. Fast schon verzweifelt, hatte man den Eindruck.

Das bestätigt auch Michael Gueffroy, Unternehmenssprecher von Kindergärten NordOst, einem Berliner Eigenbetrieb, der Kitas in Pankow, Lichtenberg und Marzahn-Hellersdorf betreibt. "Der demografische Wandel führt dazu, dass wir aktuell sehr viel aktiver Familien für unsere Angebote gewinnen müssen", sagt er. Im vergangenen Jahr lag die Auslastung in den Pankower Kitas seines Betriebs bei 79 Prozent. Vor 20 Jahren waren es noch 98 Prozent.

Seit 2019 gehen die Kinderzahlen zurück, sagt auch Ronja Tietje, die Pankower Bezirksstadträtin für Jugend und Familie. Erst im Süden von Pankow, dann auch im Norden des Bezirks. Mittlerweile gebe es nur noch einen kleinen Teil des Bezirks, Blankenfelde-Heinersdorf, wo der Anteil der Drei- bis Sechsjährigen steige, sagt sie: "Da ist nicht mehr wahnsinnig viel Bewegung drin."

Tietje war auch von 2016 bis 2021 Jugendstadträtin, sie erlebte die damalige Situation mit dem Mangel an Kitaplätzen eindrücklich mit, wie sie selbst sagt. Seit 2013 besteht in Berlin ein Rechtsanspruch auf einen Kitaplatz nach dem vollendeten ersten Lebensjahr. Zu Beginn herrschte großer Druck, auch weil diese Kitaplätze teils eingeklagt wurden, wie Tietje erzählt. Damals seien viele Mitarbeiter für zusätzliche Beratung eingestellt worden, auch Babysitter oder Tagesmütter wurden vom Amt bezahlt, damit Eltern wieder arbeiten konnten.

Mittlerweile hat die Situation sich deutlich entspannt. Kitaplätze sind nicht nur ausreichend, sondern in großer Zahl vorhanden. Dafür spüren mittlerweile schon Grundschüler Leistungsdruck, sich gegen Mitschüler zu behaupten und mit guten Noten einen Platz an einer beliebten weiterführenden Schule zu ergattern - dort gebe es weiterhin das Problem, dass die Plätze knapp seien, sagt Tietje. Mittlerweile sei die Aufgabe nicht mehr, Kitaplätze zu schaffen, sondern Orte, an denen die größer gewordenen Kinder Zeit verbringen können: Jugendtreffs und Sportplätze etwa, erzählt Tietje.

Die geringere Auslastung von Kitas sieht Tietje als Chance, um den Betreuungsschlüssel zu verbessern. Und in einigen Kitas sei es auch baulich möglich, nicht genutzte Räumlichkeiten abzugeben. Familienzentren oder etwa die Pfadfinder seien auf der Suche.

Eine Kinderhochburg ist Prenzlauer Berg also nicht mehr, auch wenn die Gegend für Familien weiterhin attraktiv ist: viele Spielplätze, breite Fußwege, relativ zentrumsnah. Aber: Die Mobilität hat nachgelassen, sagt Tietje, und das liegt hauptsächlich an den hohen Mieten.

Viele Bewohner, manche davon in eigentlich zu großen Wohnungen, können oder wollen nicht umziehen, weil sie im Zweifel entweder deutlich mehr Miete zahlen müssten, oder deutlich weniger Quadratmeter zur Verfügung hätten - oder beides. Man ziehe nicht mehr so häufig um, wie früher, sagt die Bezirksstadträtin. Auch Oberholz sieht das: Er kenne Start-Ups, die keine Leute einstellen können, weil sie keine Wohnungen für sie finden - ganz unabhängig vom Bezirk.

Die Lebensqualität in Prenzlauer Berg sei aber immer noch hoch, sagte Oberholz, und gerät ins Schwärmen. Es gebe eine wirklich tolle Mischung im Kiez, verschiedene Lebensentwürfe nebeneinander, gelebte Toleranz, internationale Bewohner. What's at stake as Israel holds its most important election in years

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When Israelis go the polls on October 27, they will face a familiar question: Should Benjamin Netanyahu, the country’s longest-serving prime minister, stay in power?

Nearly every recent election has been a referendum on Netanyahu. This time, many Israelis say the choice is more consequential than ever: not only about who will lead the country, but about where it is heading.

It is the first national vote since the Hamas-led attack of October 7, 2023, the deadliest security failure in Israel’s history, and the wars that followed in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran, which have mobilized much of the country and subsequently deepened Israel’s international isolation.

For many voters, this is the first chance to pass judgment on the leadership that presided over October 7 failures, to decide who should lead the country in conflict and who should carry the burden of increased military service.

Netanyahu’s outgoing government – the first in decades to conclude a full four-year term – relied on far-right and ultra-Orthodox partners, who pushed Israel in a more nationalist and religious direction than ever before. He became the first sitting Israeli prime minister to stand trial on corruption charges as his government pursued a judicial overhaul aimed at weakening the supreme court and other checks on power. The overhaul effort sparked massive protests in 2023.

At stake in Tuesday’s election is whether Israel keeps moving in the nationalistic, conservative and religious direction – or changes course. The answer might not be clear for weeks, because of the way Israeli coalitions are built. Polls suggest neither Netanyahu’s bloc nor the opposition is likely to win a majority alone, meaning the vote may launch Israel’s next political crisis rather than settle it.

Israelis vote for party lists, not individual candidates. In a society divided by nationality, religion, ethnicity and ideology, that produces a crowded field.

Dozens of lists register each cycle – 37 this time around – but only those that pass a 3.25% vote threshold win seats and enter the 120-seat Knesset, Israel’s parliament. Polls suggest 11 to 13 parties will meet the bar; seats are allocated through a proportional representation system.

The two largest parties are Netanyahu’s right-wing Likud and Yashar, a new centrist party led by Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff who has emerged as his principal challenger.

The latest Channel 12 opinion poll puts Eisenkot first with 23 seats, ahead of the Likud with 21, though other pollsters differ. On the pro-Netanyahu Channel 14, Likud is projected to take 30 seats and Eisenkot about 20. In head-to-head matchups about who is better suited to be prime minister, Eisenkot has led Netanyahu by a steady margin.

But coming out as the largest party is not enough to win. A prime minister needs 61 seats to form a government, and no party has ever won that majority alone. So, election night usually settles very little. The real contest is the coalition calculus that follows, which can take days, weeks, and even months.

Most polls put Netanyahu’s bloc, made up of Likud, two far-right parties led by Bezalel Smotrich and Itamar Ben-Gvir, and two ultra-Orthodox parties, at 49 to 55 seats, short of the 61-seat majority.

The opposition, which looks strong on paper, is deeply fragmented: Eisenkot has parties led by former Prime Minister Naftali Bennett and former Defense Minister Avigdor Liberman to his right, and the Democrats party led by Yair Golan on his left. Bennett and Liberman have not endorsed Eisenkot and both have their own premiership aspirations.

According to the opinion polls, any alternative government would probably need the support or the abstention of the Arab parties, and several opposition leaders have ruled out any cooperation.

With no side holding a clear majority, a deadlock, a minority government, a unity government – or another election – are all plausible outcomes.

With neither bloc projected to win comfortably, the outcome may hinge on the margins: which small parties clear the 3.25% threshold and how many votes go to parties that miss it, effectively sending them down the drain.

Turnout matters, since a few thousand votes can determine whether a party disappears entirely or enters parliament, or even becomes the kingmaker that breaks the deadlock between the blocs and reshapes the political map.

Arab voter turnout could be pivotal. Palestinian citizens of Israel – who make up roughly 20% of the Israeli population – have traditionally voted at lower rates than the national average. In the 2022 election, the Arab turnout was 53.2%, while the general turnout was 70.6%. This time, pollsters expect it to exceed 60%. That would raise the threshold and make it harder for small parties to survive.

Netanyahu’s coalition has several small parties at potential risk, including Smotrich’s Religious Zionism and a new right- wing party led by Ofer Winter, a prominent retired military general.

While most opposition parties sit safely above the line, another new, uncommitted small party, connecting a reservist and economic agenda, is polling close to the 3.25% threshold.

Another factor drawing attention is Israelis living abroad. Israel does not allow overseas voting, except for diplomatic staff, and official data suggests more than 11% of the 7.3 million eligible voters, about 810,000 people, live outside the country. Thousands plan to fly to Israel to vote in person, a number that could potentially impact the election.

In a country that has been through years of war, security and accountability are powerful electoral forces. But opinion polls suggest the cost of living is also a leading concern for many voters – particularly for younger ones.

According to the latest Channel 12 poll, 21% of Israelis rank the economy and the cost of living as their top priority in the coming election – ahead of the ultra-Orthodox conscription with 17%, the October 7 failure at 14% and the war with Iran at 10%.

The picture differs sharply by political bloc: among voters of Netanyahu’s coalition – the war with Iran ranks first at 21%, while among opposition voters, the October 7 failure leads at 28%.

A September survey by the Achord research center at Hebrew University found that 58% of voters aged 22-34 year olds said cost of living and housing prices would drive their vote, while 36% cited security issues.

Despite these figures, the economy has not been a central theme in the campaign topic – until this weekend, when Netanyahu moved it to the top of the agenda. In an interview to Channel 10 focused on the economy, Netanyahu addressed the wave of international condemnation of Israeli settlements in the West Bank and said Israel should prepare for possible international banking sanctions by developing “an economy that can circumvent them.” He said crypto currency and blockchain are being considered, and he had requested a plan to deal with it.

The interview sparked harsh criticism of Netanyahu by economists, including a rare rebuke from the Bank of Israel, who publicly rejected the idea, which said that “cryptocurrencies cannot serve as a means of circumventing sanctions.”

Netanyahu’s main rivals portrayed his proposal as evidence he expects Israel to become more isolated. Gadi Eisenkot, Netanyahu’s main challenger, mocked the idea as “BibiCoin” and called it a “threat to national security,” accusing Netanyahu of planning to “dismantle the Israeli economy for generations ahead.”

Netanyahu built his political career as a security hawk, yet he was prime minister on October 7, when Hamas infiltrated the Israeli border, killing more than 1,200 Israelis and taking 251 hostages into Gaza.

He has resisted taking personal responsibility and has rejected demands to establish a state commission of inquiry to investigate the security failures. His opponents have made that lack of accountability a central campaign theme.

The war has also shaped Eisenkot’s personal story. His son Gal, 25, was killed fighting in Gaza in December 2023, a loss that reinforces his appeal to voters focused on security. Other party lists have recruited bereaved families and relatives of former Israeli hostages as candidates. Opposition parties have also added new reservist candidates, fresh from fighting in Gaza and Lebanon, pushing the issue of military service to the top of the election agenda.

Most Jewish Israelis over 18 are subject to conscription, but ultra-Orthodox men have long enjoyed a blanket exemption for religious study. Netanyahu’s ultra-Orthodox coalition partners have sought to preserve and expand the exemption, even as the Israeli military says it is missing more than 12,000 personnel while being stretched by multi-front fighting. Reservists fill the gap, serving up to hundreds of days a year on repeated deployments.

All the opposition parties have made universal service a central campaign pledge.

The outgoing government was among the most hardline on Palestinian issues in Israel’s history, driven largely by two far-right nationalist party leaders.

Smotrich, the finance minister, who also oversees civil administration in the occupied West Bank, pushed to expand settlements dramatically, while withholding tax revenues to the Palestinian Authority (PA), which critics say could bring its economic collapse.

Ben-Gvir, the national security minister, championed a death penalty law in the Knesset, toughened conditions for Palestinian prisoners, and weakened police enforcement against Israeli settler violence in the West Bank. Both opposed ending the Gaza war, both are advocating for Israeli settlements in Gaza, and both envision full annexation of and Jewish control over the West Bank.

If Netanyahu returns to power with his current partners, that approach is likely to continue and intensify. Smotrich wants to cancel the Oslo Accords, dismantle the Palestinian Authority, and go to war in the West Bank. Ben-Gvir, who has said he will demand the defense ministry in a new government, has called for Palestinian emigration from Gaza and the West Bank.

An opposition government would probably not represent a radical ideological reversal, and would change the tone more than the substance. Eisenkot, like Netanyahu, has rejected Palestinian statehood, and any opposition coalition would include right-wing parties resistant to any concession.

Still, a government not dependent on Ben- Gvir and Smotrich would likely act more forcefully against settler violence, drop open advocacy of emigration and PA collapse, and be more responsive and receptive to US and regional initiatives in Gaza and elsewhere.

But it would inherit the same unresolved questions: how to disarm Hamas, who rebuilds Gaza, and who restores law and order in the West Bank.

The wars in Gaza and Lebanon, the conflict with Iran and the surge in settler violence have left Israel’s diplomatic standing at its weakest point in decades. Netanyahu faces an International Criminal Court warrant alleging war crimes in Gaza, and he avoids traveling to most European countries because of the risk of being arrested. US President Donald Trump endorsed him ahead of the vote, but the picture in the wider American public is much less friendly.

A March 2026 Pew Research Center poll found 60% of US adults surveyed held an unfavorable view of Israel, and 59% had little or no confidence in Netanyahu to do the right thing regarding global affairs. Pew’s Spring 2026 Global Attitudes Survey pointed the same way, with majorities in most of the 36 countries it covered holding unfavorable views of Israel and its prime minister.

The shift has been sharpest in Western Europe, where several countries – including the United Kingdom, France and Canada – have recognized a Palestinian state and imposed sanctions and trade restrictions on Israeli settlements and individuals and entities linked to settler violence. Ben-Gvir and Smotrich have been personally sanctioned and are facing travel bans from multiple Western countries.

If Netanyahu’s government is re-elected, that international pressure will likely grow. A government led by Eisenkot or another opposition figure might see some easing of tensions, at least at first. Firmer action on settler violence could open a window to restore warmer ties with some European governments and the US Democratic party.

But it is not likely to reverse the broader trend. The main opposition contenders are not expected to overhaul Israel’s security and foreign policy, so a new government would regain some diplomatic breathing room, but not a clean slate.

Trump immigration win could strengthen admin's hand in looming Supreme Court fight

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Supreme Court justices will begin hearing oral arguments Monday on a wide range of cases this term involving gun rights, religious freedom, immigration detention and the limits of government power.

The Trump administration scored a major full-panel victory at the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals Friday, which ruled that detained illegal immigrants who were never formally admitted into the U. S. are not constitutionally entitled to bond hearings while awaiting deportation.

The ruling comes as the Supreme Court prepares to hear Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha this term, a closely related fight over whether the federal mandatory-detention statute applies at all to longtime immigrants arrested inside the country — setting up the possibility that the justices could either undercut Friday’s ruling or strengthen the government's hand.

The Fifth Circuit answered the constitutional question in Ignacio Sosnava Rodriguez v. Sylvester M. Ortega, finding that three immigrant men detained under the mandatory-detention statute are not entitled to bond hearings under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The court held that their years or even decades of residence in the United States, along with ties to the country and their communities, do not create a constitutional right to a bond hearing.

"The Constitution does not entitle them to a bond hearing," the court wrote.

FEDERAL APPEALS COURT COMPARES TRUMP MIGRANT DETENTION POLICY TO JAPANESE AMERICAN INTERNMENT

Dozens of immigrants are detained by US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents inside the Federal Plaza courthouse in New York City on Thursday, June 26, 2025, following their legal proceedings.  (Lokman Vural Elibol/Anadolu via Getty Images)

Meanwhile, the Supreme Court will consider Rhoney, a case out of the Second Circuit, to determine whether the mandatory-detention law applies to longtime immigrants arrested inside the country in the first place.

At the center of the issue is a 9-2 circuit split over which federal detention law applies to immigrants who entered the country illegally, were never formally admitted and were later arrested by immigration authorities after living in the U.S. for years — sometimes decades after crossing the border.

The Fifth and Eighth Circuits have held that those immigrants remain "applicants for admission" under Section 1225 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, subjecting them to mandatory detention without an ordinary bond hearing while their removal proceedings play out. Nine other federal appeals courts have ruled that Section 1226 applies instead, which provides a path for them to seek release on bond.

The Supreme Court will essentially decide whether immigrants who entered illegally and have lived in the U.S. for years can be subjected to the same mandatory-detention provision that applies to certain immigrants seeking admission into the country.

If the Supreme Court sides with the Second Circuit, Barbosa da Cunha — a Brazilian national who entered the country illegally roughly two decades ago and was detained in 2025 — would have a path to seek release on bond. Such a ruling would likely apply to the three longtime immigrants at the center of Rodriguez as well, substantially undercutting the significance of Friday’s ruling by eliminating the need to answer the constitutional question of whether due process requires a bond hearing.

Judge Leslie Southwick pointed directly to the looming Supreme Court fight in her dissent.

Should the Supreme Court eventually determine that this court was wrong as to the statute, then the constitutional issue we address here will have far less significance. It certainly has significance at the moment," Southwick wrote.

But conservative legal experts say Rodriguez could also strengthen the administration’s hand in Rhoney by undercutting one argument against the government’s interpretation of Section 1225: that applying the mandatory-detention provision to longtime residents would raise serious constitutional concerns.

Michael Morley, a constitutional law professor at Florida State University, said Friday’s ruling could influence how the justices approach that question because courts often consider whether a particular reading of federal law would create constitutional problems.

"The Fifth Circuit’s en banc ruling in Rodriguez today takes the next step by holding that such detention would not violate the U.S. Constitution’s Due Process Clause," Morley said. "This holding may impact the Supreme Court’s reasoning, because an important consideration in disputes over federal laws is whether a particular interpretation would be unconstitutional."

"If the Court agrees with the Fifth Circuit’s analysis that the Due Process Clause allows such detention, it may be more likely to conclude that federal law permits it, as well, Morley continued.

Hans von Spakovsky, a senior legal fellow at Advancing American Freedom, argued that the decision could bolster the administration as the justices prepare to resolve the circuit split.

"I actually think this ruling will help the government in the Rhoney case," von Spakovsky said, pointing to the Fifth Circuit’s position that the applicable immigration statute does not require bond hearings for immigrants who were never admitted to the United States.

"There was already a split in the circuit courts on this issue but the Fifth Circuit has consistently held that the applicable immigration statute does not require bond hearings for aliens who were never admitted to the United States," he said. "I will be surprised if a majority of the Supreme Court disagrees when they ultimately issue a decision in the Rhoney case."

The Supreme Court has not yet scheduled oral arguments in Rhoney, which it agreed to hear earlier this month.
