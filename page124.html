‘I can’t believe I get to see that’: One Born Every Minute is back for a 12th series

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Reality TV show returns to focus on childbirth, diversity and the sharing of stories in a social media age

When One Born Every Minute first aired in 2010, viewers loved its fly-on-the-wall take on life in a maternity unit, from midwives idly chatting about unusual pregnancy cravings to parents squabbling over whether to call their child Zeus.

Almost two decades later, as the show is rebooted for a generation who spend their lives on social media, much has changed. Parents now share far more about their struggles with miscarriages, infertility and mental health; audiences have to be lured away from watching reels on their phones with more rigorous storytelling.

“Quite a bit is different about the way that people have children,” said Will Rowson, executive producer at Dragonfly, which makes the show for Channel 4. “People are so used to sharing. They expect to share, there is a willingness to share, and that is a generational thing.”

The first episode of the show, which airs on Sunday night, tackles topics that have come to the foreground in the years since the Bafta-winning first run ended in 2018. These include caesarean births – it notes that these have doubled over the past decade – as well as health anxiety and pregnancy loss.

The series focuses on parents – a shift from medical staff being the prominent characters in the previous 11 series. Rowson said it would delve into an “enormously diverse range of stories” thanks to a wider approach to casting and a move away on TV from “eclectic, whimsical” scenes. A tighter budget was also a factor.

He said: “We’re recognising that people will get their miscellany and gentle anthropology elsewhere, so we make sure we’re giving the narratives, the backstories of the families, and understanding what the birth means to them. That’s the sort of context and richness you don’t get in a 30-second clip.”

Like its predecessor, the show takes a visceral, no-holds-barred approach to what’s shown, from bellies being sliced open to a baby being pulled out smeared in faeces. Set in St Mary’s hospital in Manchester, which specialises in maternity care, the series will feature more complicated pregnancies and surgical procedures. “It’s an extraordinary thing to witness, you go ‘oh my goodness, I can’t believe I get to see that’,” said Rowson.

Faye, who is shown giving birth by C-section, said the show had given her a way to experience the birth of her son differently. “I remember shaking, and feeling this overwhelming feeling of being cold and quite scared. And actually, when I’ve seen it back, I’m in really safe hands, and I got to see my little boy being born.”

She was also motivated to take part because – as an NHS clinical lead – she wanted viewers to see “what’s going on in the background, and how much hard work people do”. She added: “You always hear the bad stuff and not the good stuff.”

In the first episode, Faye shares the heartbreak of multiple miscarriages, leading her to believe she would not have children until a surprise pregnancy at the age of 38. She said she wanted to help people understand “how many women it happens to, and how difficult it is to experience”.

“I just felt something was wrong with me. I felt this lack of being a woman, and it fed into my [former] marriage, and everything broke down further from that,” she said. When she finally became pregnant, her earlier losses intensified her anxiety: “I said to [my partner] Will ‘if I experience it again, I think I’ll be broken’.”

Peace, who also features on the show, wanted to showcase the experience of having twins, from concerns over their growth, to the decision to have a C-section. “I would love to watch that,” she said, adding that she started watching the show as a child: “I love seeing normal women with normal bodies having normal pregnancies.”

Through sharing her experience of miscarriage, the unexpectedness of having twins, and the health complications her daughter was born with, Peace wanted viewers to understand the importance of “not letting things out of your control get to you”.

She added: “I didn’t want to do it at first, because I didn’t want to open up about [miscarriage], and because I feel it seems like a taboo topic to talk about, and you blame yourself. There is this perception of having a perfect pregnancy.”

Like many other parents who took part, her partner, Kwamdeen, felt the episode would be “a great memento” to show their children.

The casting director, Nsofwa Nwannadi, said she was surprised by how open people were and was moved by the desire among those who experienced challenges for “people to understand that they are not alone, it’s not stigmatised, it’s not your fault”.

Some participants applied to be on the show, with many responding to communications sent out by the hospital, Nwannadi said. Her team sought out a diverse range of participants and experiences by engaging with minority ethnic, LGBT and disability communities.

Unlike reality TV, in which participants are cast for their personalities, “this is real life, we’re reflecting what’s actually there at the hospital”, she said.

This included fathers ruminating on fatherhood and modern masculinity, mothers expressing fears about sublimating their identities to motherhood, and birthing partners discussing their decision to remain child-free.

“It’s been lovely to have been privy to a myriad of different stories, backgrounds and journeys. It’s not a one-size-fits-all situation, and that’s great, actually,” Nwannadi said.

One Born Every Minute airs on E4 from Sunday at 9pm.

Iran’s oil lifeline is drying up. The biggest pain may fall on ordinary citizens

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Iran sanctions expert Miad Maleki explains what could happen if Tehran's crude revenues fall to zero — and why the economic pain may hit ordinary Iranians before the regime's military.

Iran is approaching a financial reckoning as a U.S. naval blockade threatens to wipe out its seaborne crude oil revenues by mid-December — but the economic pain may fall more heavily on ordinary Iranians than on the military Washington is trying to constrain.

Iran’s trackable floating oil inventory outside the U.S. blockade zone has fallen to zero, according to energy intelligence firm Kpler, which projects that Tehran's crude revenues could dry up by the middle of December as outstanding payments are settled.

But the loss of oil revenue would not necessarily translate into an immediate military funding crisis. Tehran could shield military spending while cutting civilian programs, allowing inflation to erode purchasing power and drawing on alternative revenue sources.

That complicates Washington's pressure campaign: The blockade may deprive Tehran of one of its biggest sources of cash without immediately starving the military and Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of the money they need to operate.

Oil directly accounts for roughly 30% of Iran's government budget, according to Gregory Brew, a senior Iran and energy analyst at Eurasia Group, and Miad Maleki, an Iran sanctions and financial expert at the Foundation for Defense of Democracies.

Iran entered the year expecting to export about 1.7 million barrels per day at roughly $54 per barrel, Brew said. Actual exports have fallen short of that target, though higher oil prices allowed Tehran to build some financial cushion.

Maleki estimated that oil and refined petroleum products account for between 50% and 60% of regime revenue when indirect income is included.

Still, the December deadline is not a date on which Iran suddenly runs out of money.

"The December timeline is more of a cash flow kind of disruption, and not really a collapse state," Maleki told Fox News Digital.

Tehran has so far allowed inflation and the weakening rial to absorb some of the pressure rather than abruptly stopping government salaries or allowing widespread shortages to develop.

"The purchasing power of Iranians incrementally declines," Maleki said.

Losing a revenue stream that covers roughly 30% of the government budget would nevertheless force increasingly difficult choices. Tehran can cut spending, borrow more or run larger deficits. Deeper cuts could hit subsidies, pensions and other government programs, while deficit spending risks fueling already high inflation.

"There's already expectations that government spending next year is going to be greatly reduced," Brew said . "A bigger chunk of the budget is likely going to be met through borrowing and deficit spending, which is of course going to add to inflation."

Military spending, however, could prove more resistant.

"There is a broad expectation that non-military spending is going to be slashed significantly, while military spending is going to be maintained and might even increase because of the overall war footing that the Iranian regime has been put on," Brew said.

Iran is already spending resources to rebuild missile production facilities and other military infrastructure, expand underground facilities and repair damage to its nuclear program, Brew said.

Iran's armed forces also have a direct connection to the country's oil trade.

The Treasury Department said last year that Tehran was allocating roughly 200,000 barrels of crude per day to its armed forces, with the value of planned allocations expected to exceed $10 billion annually. Military organizations can sell allocated oil abroad and use the proceeds themselves.

Maleki said those sales have helped finance equipment, chemicals and weapons components and support Iran-backed groups including Hezbollah and Hamas.

The IRGC also draws revenue from smuggling, black-market commerce and sanctions-evasion networks outside the formal state budget. Brew said its economic influence has expanded as sanctions have squeezed Iran's private economy.

"If the IRGC is the single most important institution in the country, and if it largely benefits from sanctions and blockades and various forms of U.S. economic pressure, then there's a world in which it benefits from this new environment rather than being squeezed like the overall government budget," Brew said.

Iran's oil squeeze has been months in the making.

Tehran held roughly 135 million barrels of crude in floating storage before the war began in February, providing a stockpile it could continue selling after U.S. forces restricted new exports. That offshore inventory has now been exhausted.

U.S. Central Command estimates the blockade has already denied Tehran at least $25 billion in oil revenue.

Buyers of Iranian crude typically settle payments one to two months after delivery, creating the lag between the disappearance of Iran's floating inventory and Kpler's projected revenue cutoff in December.

Even if that projection broadly holds, however, Iran is unlikely to stop earning money from hydrocarbons altogether.

Smaller quantities of oil can still move overland by truck and rail and across the Caspian Sea, while refined petroleum and petrochemical products can travel through routes outside the seaborne crude trade targeted by the blockade.

"While the amount of Iranian oil that can be tracked has fallen to zero, it's unlikely Iran's oil exports completely fall to nothing," Brew said. "The amount will likely be fairly small, but it won't be completely zeroed."

Both experts said an agreement with Washington allowing Iranian tankers to resume leaving the Persian Gulf could reverse the trajectory . Without a deal or relaxation of the blockade, the financial pressure is expected to build into next year.

The bigger test may arrive in March, when Iran begins its new fiscal year and must reconcile its spending plans with dramatically lower oil income.

"There's going to be a fiscal squeeze, and there's going to be pressure around the release of the new budget," Brew said. "There's going to be economic unrest, potentially even economic turmoil as the government rolls out spending cuts."

By then, Tehran could have gone months without its traditional seaborne crude income, forcing the regime to decide how much of the financial squeeze it is willing to pass on to its population while preserving spending elsewhere.

Why quitting your job might be your best decision yet

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Below, Lindsay Crouse shares five key insights from her new book, The Case for Quitting: The Surprising Benefits of Opting Out.

Lindsay is a marathon runner and journalist who worked at The New York Times for 15 years. Her reporting helped bring maternity rights to pregnant Nike athletes.

What’s the Big Idea?

Quitting isn’t failure. Knowing when to walk away from a goal that no longer serves us can free up the time, energy, and opportunity we need to build something better.

1. Perseverance can work against us.

Perseverance is essential. That’s why our culture holds it in such high regard. But misplaced perseverance backfires. It can lead us to push too hard against dead ends, pouring ourselves into futile goals we don’t actually have the power to achieve.

When we fail to quit, we waste precious resources—energy, money, time—on pursuits that make us increasingly miserable, even as we fall short of what we want. Focusing on the work keeps us from registering the mounting costs, or even recognizing that we’re failing at all.

I spoke to a woman who poured a decade of her life and thousands of dollars from her savings into fertility treatments, only to finally accept that they weren’t working. She quit, and began rebuilding the life she’d put on hold. A year later, she became pregnant naturally. Despite the promises of those interventions, her fertility goal had never been fully in her control. I also spoke with a woman who pushed toward her dream of becoming an Olympian, even as her diligent training led to overtraining and injury, turning the sport she loved into an ordeal.

Both women had spent their lives learning to do hard things. But they hadn’t learned what to do when perseverance failed them. For both, quitting was the only realistic way out—no matter how much it felt like failure. Continuing would have only brought more failure. Quitting freed them to redirect their energy toward pursuits where they could succeed.

2. Fear of quitting is often misplaced.

When we contemplate quitting, we’re easily paralyzed by fear. Usually that fear centers on judgment: the worry that we made the wrong choice, that others will notice and think less of us.

But this fear is often proven to be unfounded. There’s a cognitive bias called the spotlight effect in which we tend to think that others are paying more attention to our choices than they actually are. I spoke with an actress who quit her career after noticing that everyone around her was putting their lives on hold for a dream they no longer truly believed in—largely because they’d already told everyone back home that they were going to be a star. She felt that pressure too. If she quit, what would everyone think? It turned out they thought the same thing as when she’d set out for Hollywood: Sounds great, we support you. People rarely care about what we do as much as we care about it ourselves.

“We tend to think that others are paying more attention to our choices than they actually are.”

Then there’s fear of our own judgment. What if we regret our decision to leave? But research shows that over the long arc of our lives, we tend to regret inaction more than action. Quitting is an action. It may mean taking a loss, but it also means opening yourself to new opportunities.

We should fear failing to decide more than the act of quitting itself. Kicking these decisions down the road to our five- or 10-year-older selves only leaves them with the same hard questions and even higher stakes. Quitting doesn’t get easier. We just become older.

3. Testing the waters usually beats a cannonball.

We often think of quitting as one blunt, seismic act—the day we’ve finally had enough and storm out in a rage. But that’s rarely the healthiest way to cut the cord. Instead of diving headfirst into a new life, we can wade in slowly.

In one research study, would-be entrepreneurs who kept their steady jobs while building a runway to their next step were more likely to succeed than those who quit outright. Knowing this helps counter the mental pushback of “I can’t quit,” because wading in mitigates the risk.

R&B legend Bill Withers is a great example of this approach. He was born into poverty in the segregated coal country of West Virginia and worked a series of jobs to support himself, eventually landing work in Los Angeles manufacturing toilet seats for airplanes. At the same time, he started playing guitar in clubs, exploring new interests. He discovered he was enormously talented, but he kept his factory job. Even after his breakthrough song, “Ain’t No Sunshine,” became a hit, he hedged his bets and kept making toilet seats. It was only after he was laid off that he realized he was ready to embrace his new career. By then, he had the gift, the confidence, and the financial foundation to succeed on his own terms.

4. Women’s endurance can become a weakness.

Women have specific physical, mental, and social qualities that help them endure more and quit less than men, especially in scenarios where conditions are harsh and the stakes are high. I wrote about this for The New York Times in 2018, after watching a Boston Marathon staged in freezing rain, where women finished at higher rates than men.

“We live longer than men, but we don’t live better.”

But is all this endurance good for women’s life outcomes? We live longer than men, but we don’t live better. Women are more likely to battle chronic pain, lie awake with insomnia, and suffer migraines. Women are catching up to men in rates of excessive drinking, and are more likely to take medication for anxiety and depression. Women graduate from school at higher rates than men, but our incomes are lower and we occupy fewer leadership positions. Motherhood complicates this dynamic further.

Is women’s ability to keep going at all costs something to celebrate? In a world that will demand as much as it can get from us, how can women learn to moderate our ability to persevere, before this strength begins to hold us back?

5. Quitting can be the path to better.

Remember the aspiring Olympian from the first insight—the one whose relentless perseverance led to injury and burnout? After she quit track, she didn’t run at all for years. She started a family. Got a regular job. Found other interests. Moved on. With time, her injuries healed. And then she started running again, just for fun.

Everything was different this time. Instead of running for medals, she was running for herself. Suddenly, she got faster and faster. Eventually, she set the national record in the marathon, reaching heights she’d never approached during her years of grinding pursuit.

The point isn’t that quitting is a comeback tool or a success hack. It’s that quitting deserves to be treated as a genuine option—far more than the never-give-up, keep-showing-up messages we’re so used to seeing blasted around us. We’re mostly conditioned to see quitting as a way to close a door, but that’s only half the story: It often opens doors in ways we can’t predict.

Erst wenn sein Auto richtig blitzblank sauber und wieder trocken ist, fährt der Schützling von Toto Wolff in die Arbeit.

Also klammert sich diese Szene offenbar fester an Riten und Aberglauben denn alle anderen Sparten.

Solch mentale Strategien verfolgt auch Toto Wolff: Er setzt alles daran, bei so vielen GP wie möglich stets im gleichen Hotel und gleichen Zimmer unterzukommen, erzählte er einmal. Wenn er den gleichen Fahrer hat, sei er besonders glücklich – der Routine, der gewohnten Abläufe wegen, die hilfreich sein können zur Optimierung von Leistung und Konzentration.

Conservas de pescado: etiquetado más claro y control del envase

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

La nueva normativa que se está difundiendo en España sobre conservas de pescado y marisco apunta a una mayor claridad en las denominaciones y a una descripción más precisa de lo que contiene cada lata. Aunque los detalles concretos aún deben interpretarse con prudencia, el debate público coincide en una idea: el comprador necesita saber qué pescado o marisco está adquiriendo y qué tratamiento ha recibido. Esto afecta a productos de gran rotación en supermercados y tiendas de alimentación, y también a la industria transformadora gallega y europea, que trabaja con un mercado muy internacionalizado.

El cambio más comentado afecta a la forma de nombrar el producto. Frente a expresiones ambiguas o genéricas, se busca que cada conserva identifique la especie y la presentación con mayor exactitud. En el caso de las sardinas, por ejemplo, la diferencia entre sardina y sardinilla deja de ser una cuestión menor: la talla y el peso pasan a ser criterios relevantes para poder usar una denominación u otra. También se presta atención a términos como ventresca, lomo, filete, cocochas o anillas, que deben responder a cortes y partes concretas y no a simples reclamos comerciales.

Junto a las denominaciones, la norma pone el foco en procesos habituales en la industria conservera, como el ahumado, el escabeche, la pasteurización o la ultracongelación. Estas palabras deben tener un significado comprensible y verificable para quien lee la etiqueta. La idea de fondo es que el consumidor no tenga que adivinar qué hay dentro de la lata ni confiar únicamente en la imagen del envase. En un lineal con muchas opciones parecidas, esa claridad resulta especialmente útil para comparar productos y elegir con más criterio.

La actualidad de las conservas de pescado no se limita a las latas de sardinas o atún. Mejillones, berberechos y almejas cuentan con reglas de calidad que arrastran décadas de historia y que ahora se revisan para adaptarse a los hábitos de consumo actuales. La presentación, la especie y los aditivos empleados deben quedar bien explicados en el envase. Este punto es importante porque no todas las conservas de moluscos se elaboran igual ni con las mismas materias primas, y el consumidor puede encontrar propuestas muy distintas bajo apariencias similares.

En paralelo, las conservas de pescado mantienen un valor nutricional que las hace interesantes dentro de una dieta variada. Las sardinas en conserva son un pescado azul pequeño, asociado a proteínas y ácidos grasos omega-3, y aparecen en recetas sencillas como tostadas con cebolla y pimientos. Su carácter práctico no está reñido con una alimentación equilibrada, siempre que se atiendan las cantidades y se revise el contenido de sal o el aceite añadido. Esa doble condición, alimento cotidiano y fuente de nutrientes, explica que las conservas tengan un hueco propio en la conversación pública.

Galicia es una de las zonas donde la relación entre pesca, transformación y conserva tiene más peso. La presencia de empresas conserveras y de asociaciones sectoriales en ferias internacionales como SIAL París muestra que el producto no solo se consume en casa: también se vende fuera y compite en el mercado europeo. Francia figura entre los destinos más relevantes para las exportaciones españolas de pesca y acuicultura, lo que refuerza la importancia de cuidar la imagen, la trazabilidad y la información del envase.

La internacionalización convive con una industria auxiliar muy especializada. El sellado hermético de latas y bebidas, por ejemplo, forma parte de una cadena técnica que en Galicia tiene historia y presencia global. Aunque el foco principal está en el contenido de la conserva, el envase es la barrera que protege el producto durante meses. Por eso, la calidad del cierre, la integridad de la lata y la manipulación adecuada resultan decisivas para que el alimento llegue en condiciones seguras al consumidor.

A la hora de comprar, conviene revisar que la lata no esté abollada, hinchada o golpeada. Los especialistas en seguridad alimentaria insisten en que estos defectos pueden comprometer el sellado y permitir la entrada de aire o microorganismos. Una lata dañada no debería comprarse ni utilizarse, aunque el producto parezca normal por fuera. También es recomendable comprobar la fecha de consumo, el registro sanitario y los datos del fabricante, además de conservar el envase en un lugar fresco y seco, lejos del sol y de objetos que puedan perforarlo.

En el hogar, las conservas cerradas pueden mantenerse a temperatura ambiente si se guardan en condiciones adecuadas. Una vez abiertas, deben tratarse como cualquier otro alimento perecedero y seguir las indicaciones de conservación del fabricante. La higiene durante la manipulación y la cadena de frío de los productos frescos o congelados completan las precauciones básicas. No se trata de alarmar al consumidor, sino de recordar que la comodidad de una lata no elimina la necesidad de mirar el envase y actuar con sentido común.

El interés por las conservas de pescado combina varias capas: la claridad del etiquetado, la seguridad del envase, el valor nutricional de especies como la sardina y el peso de una industria gallega que exporta a Europa. Revisar la información y el estado de la lata sigue siendo la mejor rutina para disfrutar de un producto práctico, frecuente en la despensa y muy ligado a la tradición conservera española. La próxima normativa, junto con los controles sanitarios y la responsabilidad de los fabricantes, dibuja un escenario en el que el consumidor debería disponer de más datos para elegir con tranquilidad.
