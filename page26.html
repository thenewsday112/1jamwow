Rock collapse causes casualties at Turkish tourist hotspot – media (VIDEOS)

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

At least two people have been killed and another injured after rocks broke off a natural formation at a popular tourist destination in Türkiye’s Cappadocia region, local media have reported, citing provincial authorities.

The incident occurred at around 3:15 PM local time on Thursday following heavy rainfall, Nevsehir Governor Huseyin Kok told reporters.

The collapse took place near the entrance to the Goreme Open-Air Museum, where rocks broke off one of the region’s distinctive fairy chimney formations.

Rescue teams recovered two bodies from beneath the debris, while another person was taken to hospital with injuries, according to the governor.

Police, gendarmerie, medical personnel, and disaster response teams were dispatched to the scene, he said.

Rescuers are continuing to search the debris for anyone who might still be trapped, while the authorities have yet to release the victims’ identities or nationalities.

According to media reports, rain and wind have affected the region intermittently this week, forcing the cancellation of Cappadocia’s popular hot-air balloon tours on Wednesday and Thursday.

Western Europe is falling apart – we should prepare for what comes next

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Timofey Bordachev, Program Director of the Valdai Club

For Russia, developments in the rest of Europe matter primarily insofar as they affect our national interests. Above all, this concerns security given that, for centuries, Russia has faced its greatest challenges from the West, and there’s little reason to assume that this historical pattern has disappeared.

Western Europe’s leading countries now stand at a crossroads, yet the choice confronting them isn’t simply between competing models of domestic development, it’s between war and peace with their neighbors, foremost among them Russia, whose size and historical role have repeatedly made it the principal obstacle to Western European expansion.

The unrest sweeping several European countries this autumn marks a new phase in the continent’s systemic crisis as it reflects the convergence of two long-term problems that much of Europe’s political establishment has proved incapable of resolving.

The first is the exhaustion of the socioeconomic model that sustained Western European prosperity throughout the Cold War and its aftermath. That model depended on Europe’s privileged position in the global economy and the absence of serious competitors beyond the Western world, but China’s rise, combined with increasingly limited resources, has undermined those advantages.

Closer economic integration with Russia, and access to its enormous natural wealth, might have offered Western Europe a final opportunity to preserve its exceptional position, but instead regional leaders allowed their own arrogance to prevent an accommodation with Moscow because they couldn’t accept that Russia wished to live peacefully with its neighbors, but on terms consistent with its own interests.

The second problem is Western Europe’s longstanding absence of genuine sovereignty.

Integration into the American-led NATO system has deprived even major powers such as Germany, France, and Britain of the freedom to pursue fundamental political change and one of the alliance’s practical functions has been to preserve the existing political order within its member states.

Any serious attempt to transform that order would require a reassessment of relations with Washington and such independence is something these European governments have long been unwilling, or unable, to contemplate.

Together, these structural weaknesses have left Western Europe’s political and economic establishments effectively paralyzed.

For nearly two decades, governments have substituted the appearance of activity for meaningful reform. Successive emergencies, involving migration, the pandemic, hostility towards Russia, and preparations for military confrontation, have allowed leaders to avoid addressing deeper problem and this helps explain the temptation to interpret today’s unrest as another exercise in political manipulation. Emmanuel Macron, for example, has repeatedly demonstrated an ability to survive the consequences of disastrous decisions through tactical maneuvering.

Yet it would be a mistake to dismiss the protests as artificial or orchestrated.

In France, the initial demonstrations were driven by schoolchildren and university students demanding more teachers and improvements to an education system suffering from declining standards, but these grievances are hardly unique to France and they reflect broader weaknesses in modern education, including funding arrangements that encourage institutions to compete for pupils rather than concentrate on teaching.

By early October, the French protests had expanded as other groups affected by Macron’s liberal economic policies joined the demonstrations. The authorities blamed the political left, while unrest escalated into rioting and looting.

Similar discontent emerged in Spain and Portugal, where soaring housing costs became the immediate trigger. Both countries have spent years occupying subordinate positions within the European Union, with little influence over its economic priorities, even as their former politicians find comfortable positions in Brussels.

Western Europe has consequently entered a period in which conventional political competition is increasingly accompanied by mass protest and the participation of young people is particularly significant.

In France, the EU’s most important military power and its only nuclear-armed member, it was students who provided the initial momentum. While much of the adult electorate has descended into political apathy under Macron, younger generations retain aspirations for a better future and their grievances are genuine. So, too, is the wider crisis that has brought people onto the streets.

There are therefore grounds to believe that public dissatisfaction may eventually bring new political forces to power, which from Russia’s perspective raises an obvious possibility in that perhaps Western Europe’s next generation of leaders will prove more reasonable on questions of security and relations with Moscow.

Such hopes, however, may be premature because they reflect less our actual experience of European politics than our desire to imagine a Western Europe capable of recovering its political judgement.

Indeed, the objective nature of the present crisis makes its possible consequences especially troubling and European history offers little reassurance that profound domestic upheaval produces peaceful relations with neighbouring countries. On the contrary, revolutionary transformations have repeatedly generated external aggression, and the French Revolution provides one example.

The upheavals of the late 18th century were followed by wars against neighboring states and, eventually, Napoleon’s attempt to dominate Europe.

The extraordinary energy released by revolution demanded an outlet, while new rulers also needed resources and opportunities for enrichment beyond their borders.

As Henry Kissinger observed in his early historical work, the internal foundations of Napoleon’s expansionism helped prevent a lasting accommodation with Russia after the disastrous campaign of 1812.

Germany offers an even more destructive precedent, where the political upheavals of the 20th century ultimately brought to power a regime whose ambitions extended far beyond Germany’s borders. The conquest of neighboring countries was followed by an attack on the Soviet Union and the most devastating war in human history.

Again, internal political transformation was accompanied by an effort to direct national energies towards external expansion and the consequences of that war fundamentally altered Europe’s position as many of its leading states lost much of their effective sovereignty and became dependent on the United States for their security.

Paradoxically, that dependence may now offer some protection against the most dangerous consequences of Europe’s internal crisis because governments that lack genuine strategic independence may also lack the freedom to embark upon adventures of their own choosing.

Nevertheless, Russia can’t base its security on the assumption that European political upheaval will produce more sensible leaders even if the temptation to hope for a peaceful transformation of Europe is understandable. Hope isn’t a strategy, and history provides ample grounds for caution.

Russia must therefore prepare not for the miracle of a Western Europe reconciled to peaceful coexistence, but for the possibility that its internal crisis will once again find expression in confrontation abroad.

Whatever political forces eventually emerge from the present turmoil, preparing for a military confrontation with our European neighbors must remain one of Russia’s central strategic priorities.

Suicide deaths in Germany surge to 27-year high

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Germany has seen its highest annual suicide toll in nearly three decades, with 11,334 deaths recorded last year, according to the Federal Statistical Office. Experts suggest that the surge may be linked to a loosely regulated assisted-suicide system, which allows even healthy adults to seek help ending their lives.

The data set released earlier this month and analyzed in greater detail on Wednesday by Germany’s National Suicide Prevention Program (NaSPro) found a rise of 962 suicides, or 9.3%, in 2025, taking the annual toll to its highest level since 1998.

The total was greater than the approximately 7,677 people killed in traffic accidents, homicides, illegal-drug-related deaths and AIDS combined, the report said, adding that men accounted for 69.2% of suicides.

Since 2021, the annual total has climbed 23% though the number of suicides remains below its historical peak of 18,825 in 1981, NaSPro said.

While men accounted for most suicides, the increase was disproportionately concentrated among women, whose deaths rose 17.9%, compared with 5.8% among men. Most suicides (73.7%) also occur in the over-50 age group.

Although hanging remained the most common method of suicide (4,145), it was followed by medication (2,848 cases), which saw a striking surge compared to 2024, when it accounted for 2,002 deaths. This category alone accounted for nearly 88% of the entire annual increase.

The latest figures also point to an increase in suicide deaths among the German youth: among girls aged 15 to 19, deaths surged from 52 in 2024 to 84 in 2025, while the overall toll among teenagers rose from 188 to 208. Suicide accounted for approximately 18% of all deaths among people aged five to 24.

While the German government did not offer a definitive explanation for the dramatic increase, multiple experts linked it to the expansion of assisted suicides, which are not separately identified in official mortality statistics. Three major organizations providing such services reported 1,287 assisted deaths last year, up from 977 in 2024 and just 346 in 2021; medication is also the most common method used in assisted suicides.

Germany’s legal framework permits assisted suicide even for people who are not terminally ill or suffering from incurable conditions. Individuals willing to go this path are not required to provide any justification, and in principle, even a physically healthy young adult can legally seek assistance if the decision is voluntary, informed, and enduring. However, nobody is obliged to assist, and euthanasia performed directly by another person remains illegal.

Ulrich Hegerl, chairman of the German Depression Aid and Suicide Prevention Foundation, criticized what he described as the country’s “extreme position” on the matter, warning that “when assisted suicide becomes normalized as a service, this threshold is lowered, and the number of tragic, preventable suicides rises.”

Suicide researcher Ute Lewitzka of Goethe University Frankfurt, meanwhile, has offered another explanation, describing the surge as a crisis phenomenon. She suggested that financial hardship and uncertainty can intensify suicidal thoughts among vulnerable people, while social media could also contribute. Lewitzka also criticized what she described as Germany’s inadequate access to psychological care.

Ontario projects bigger deficit, cites U.S. tariffs and higher oil prices

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ontario Finance Minister Peter Bethlenfalvy delivered the province's fiscal update on Thursday, saying the government is remaining prudent.Frank Gunn/The Canadian Press

The Ontario government says its deficit for the current fiscal year is now expected to be $16.9-billion, up from the $13.8-billion it projected in its March budget, as U.S. tariffs and higher oil prices have cut the province’s anticipated economic growth in half.

Releasing his fall economic statement on Thursday, Finance Minister Peter Bethlenfalvy defended the deficit, warning that the spending is needed as the trade war with the U.S. intensifies and President Donald Trump’s threatened additional tariffs, which could take effect Jan. 1, loom over Ontario.

The province’s new larger shortfall was announced just a week before the federal government is to deliver a full-scale budget next Thursday, as Ottawa grapples with the same slowing economic growth and its own struggle to contain spending while mitigating the impact of U.S. tariffs.

Much of the reason for this year’s Ontario deficit figure is a $3.8-billion boost to the province’s contingency fund, which now sits at a total of $4.9-billion. That account could be used for tariff relief, health care costs or other services, Mr. Bethlenfalvy said, and it could be needed if the trade war further darkens the economic picture.

But the fall economic statement also projects additional red ink in future years. The province says next year’s deficit could be $12.7-billion – more than double the prediction in Mr. Bethlenfalvy’s March budget. And for 2028-29, when he had just months ago forecast a $600-million surplus, he now expects a $6.7-billion deficit.

The province’s books, the fall economic statement says, won’t be balanced until 2029-30 – making Thursday the fourth time the government has put off a planned return to black ink since 2024.

When measured as a percentage of gross domestic product, the shortfall projected Thursday isn’t anywhere near as large as those the province ran up in the recessionary early 1990s, under NDP premier Bob Rae, or the record deficits under Liberal Dalton McGuinty in the wake of the 2008 global financial crisis.

But if this year’s projection proves accurate, it would be the largest deficit ever posted – in raw dollars, not accounting for inflation – by the Progressive Conservative government of Doug Ford, even bigger than the $16.4-billion pandemic-fuelled shortfall in 2020-21.

Ford faces Ontario legislature after long summer break, pledges more landfills

In a speech to the Ontario Legislature, Mr. Bethlenfalvy called this “perhaps the most consequential time in our country’s recent history,” adding that “we are living in a post free-trade, protectionist world.”

Speaking to reporters after delivering the statement in the House, Mr. Bethlenfalvy said the spending is needed amid the uncertainty caused by U.S. tariffs and high oil prices owing to the U.S.-Iran war. But he said he dislikes deficits and is still dedicated to balancing the budget.

“While I’d like to balance today, I am also a realist of the world we’re in,” he said. “We’re not going to slow down our investments in health care, social services and other programs.”

He also argued that Ontario has the fiscal room it needs to run deficits as it faces the tariff crisis, noting recent credit-rating upgrades, the province’s low borrowing costs relative to other provinces and its net debt-to-GDP ratio. That key metric for government finance shows that Ontario’s net debt is now projected to be 37.4 per cent of the size of its economy, down from 37.7 per cent in the March budget.

However, that figure is expected to rise, along with Ontario’s net debt, which is projected to grow beyond $483.8-billion by the end of this fiscal year. Net debt is now set to clear the half-trillion mark next year at $514.7-billion. And it’s forecast to be $541.8-billion the year after that, which is $200-billion more than Ontario’s debt load was when Mr. Ford took office in 2018, after an election campaign in which he accused the predecessor Liberal government of bankrupting the province.

The fall economic statement lays out the damage U.S. tariffs have already done to Ontario. The levies on steel have seen exports to the U.S. sink more than 50 per cent so far this year compared with the same time period in 2024, the economic statement says. Auto exports are down almost 14 per cent since 2024. Exports of all kinds to the U.S. made up 77 per cent of Ontario’s total in 2024. That number fell to 72 per cent in 2025, and it now sits at 64 per cent in 2026 so far, the government said.

Carney, Ford pledge billions for tariff-hit sectors, roads to Ring of Fire

Back in March, the Ontario government had predicted modest 1-per-cent growth in real gross domestic product this year, in the face of U.S. tariffs. But now, with the trade war’s escalation, the province expects just half that, based on its conservative read of projections from private-sector economists. Better growth is projected to return next year, though it is still expected to be slow.

Opposition leaders at Queen’s Park panned the government’s fiscal update.

Ontario NDP Leader Marit Stiles said instead of offering people relief on the high costs of groceries or rent, or proposing solutions to overcrowding in hospitals or schools, Thursday’s update was full of “tired announcements and broken promises.”

“It is stunning to me that this Premier and this Conservative government cannot see what is right in front of them. The people of Ontario are getting crushed under the pressure of rising costs,” Ms. Stiles said.

Interim Liberal leader John Fraser said the update showed “the worst fiscal management by any government in the history of Ontario.”

As previously announced, the fall economic statement confirms Ontario’s commitment to match the federal government’s “productivity mega deduction,” a sweeping multibillion-dollar tax move to allow companies to accelerate tax writeoffs for a wide range of expenditures.

Personal and operational details of more than 41,000 US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) employees and contractors, including undercover immigration agents, have been leaked online, reportedly exposing the department’s internal structure and surveillance operations.

The disclosure comes amid growing public anger over President Donald Trump’s mass deportation campaign, with the department’s Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) facing growing criticism over aggressive raids, detention conditions, and the use of force against migrants and protesters.

Released on Wednesday by transparency nonprofit Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets), the files contain names, contact details, job titles, and internal system access groups across ICE, Customs and Border Protection (CBP), and other DHS agencies.

DHS confirmed to the media that it is “aware of a potential breach” and is working to address it, adding that it will protect employees and update the public “when more information is available.”

Dubbed ‘Icepick’, the archive was supplied to DDoSecrets by a hacktivist group called ‘Department of Peace’. It contains around 21,300 CBP records, 6,600 linked to ICE, and 3,400 to Homeland Security Investigations, along with records from immigration services, cybersecurity, and other DHS divisions. Some records match employees’ social media profiles, but others exposed internal organizational charts and previously undisclosed programs and operations.

According to Wired, the files reference undercover investigations, wiretapping, and immigration databases, with internal access groups linked to surveillance tools including Palantir’s Falcon software and Mobile Fortify, a facial recognition system used by immigration agents. Other groups reference AI services including OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, and Elon Musk’s xAI chatbot Grok. Access groups are lists of employees authorized to use particular systems, which means the records could help identify which employees have access to surveillance tools and how DHS operations are organized.

“This dataset is massive in terms of both size and impact,” investigative journalist and hacktivist maia arson crimew, who developed a searchable version of the archive, said in a statement accompanying the release. She called it “a complete game changer for anyone investigating DHS,” arguing that it could help identify agents and their chains of command.

DDoSecrets co-founder Emma Best said the files go beyond an ordinary employee directory.“Rather than an indiscriminate directory of every DHS employee, Icepick identifies many of the architects and drivers of DHS policy and operations,” she said, pointing to officials overseeing covert investigations, efforts to identify government leakers, and deportation programs.

The hackers claim to have obtained the records through DHS’s Okta employee login system, though Okta denied that its systems were compromised.

The hackers linked the leak to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s pledge to intensify immigration enforcement. Speaking after two fatal ICE shootings in Texas and Maine, Mullin said federal agents are “turning up the heat on the streets,” despite growing criticism of their tactics. “On July 17th, Markwayne Mullin said he was ‘turning up the heat.’ This is what happens to ICE when you do that,” a Department of Peace spokesperson said in a statement published by DDoSecrets.

The disclosure comes amid growing backlash over Trump’s immigration crackdown, a central priority since his return to the White House in January 2025. His push for mass deportations, expanded detention, and tighter border controls has sparked protests over aggressive raids, masked agents, and alleged racial profiling.

Anger intensified after federal agents fatally shot US citizens Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minnesota in January, followed by other violent incidents involving ICE officers. Just this month, federal agents shot and wounded a Dominican migrant during an arrest in New York, prompting further demonstrations.

ICE has also faced scrutiny over detention conditions, with at least 52 deaths in its custody during Trump’s first 500 days back in office, according to Human Rights Watch and Physicians for Human Rights.

The administration maintains that its operations are necessary to enforce immigration law and remove dangerous criminals. The Icepick publishers say the records can help journalists, lawmakers, and civil liberties groups investigate DHS activities. Emma Best argued that the disclosure can enable investigations, congressional inquiries, and legal challenges by replacing the administration’s “anonymity with accountability.”

The disclosure adds to a series of US government data security incidents.

In September, hacking group ShinyHunters claimed that it stole personal data on nearly all FBI agents and their spouses, including sensitive job details and medical information. The group said the attack was “not financially motivated” and demanded that the FBI withdraw a cybersecurity advisory published in May that it claimed contained false allegations about it.

Separately, a Pentagon personnel system breach reportedly exposed Social Security numbers, personal details, and military job records belonging to more than 3 million people, including service members and their families. The incident prompted a class-action lawsuit last week.

India’s refining boom finds an opportunity in the global diesel crunch

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

By Zoya Hussain, Senior Correspondent, RT.

India’s long-planned refinery expansions could make it an even bigger diesel supplier in 2027, just as disruptions to Russian and Middle Eastern shipments have created an unexpected export opportunity for Indian refiners.

State-owned refiners plan to add 16.5 million tons of annual diesel production capacity next year, executives said at an industry conference in Goa on Tuesday. The projects were originally intended to meet India’s growing domestic fuel requirements, not the current global diesel shortage.

“The expansion is driven by a portfolio, but the opportunity is in diesel,” XAnalysts founder and chief oil analyst Mukesh Sahdev told RT.Why are global diesel supplies tight even as oil demand is falling?The US-Iran war has disrupted Middle Eastern fuel shipments, while attacks on Russian refineries and export restrictions have reduced supplies from another major producer. Diesel powers transportation, factories, and farms, and the shortages are largely driving the current price inflation and affordability crisis.

Global diesel and gas oil availability fell by 1.7 million barrels per day – equivalent to roughly two-and-a-half times Japan’s daily diesel consumption last year – in the third quarter, compared with a year earlier, according to figures from the International Energy Agency (IEA) and the Energy Institute.

Wholesale diesel prices surpassed $200 per barrel in September, nearly double their prewar levels, and reached around $220 in Europe and the US, the IEA said.

The shortage persists despite weakening overall oil demand, as diesel consumption has remained comparatively resilient and inventories have fallen, the agency said. On October 15, IEA members agreed to accelerate emergency stock releases, including over 20 million barrels of diesel.How are Indian refiners positioned to benefit?Indian Oil Corporation, the country’s largest refiner, expects expansions to add around 8 million tons of diesel a year. Hindustan Petroleum Corporation’s new Rajasthan refinery is expected to produce 3 million tons next year, while the expanded Numaligarh refinery in Assam state will add another 3 million tons, Reuters reported.

Bharat Petroleum Corporation plans another 1.5 million tons of annual output. In all, India’s plans will expand overall refining capacity from 267 million tons annually to 300 million by 2030.

The country already produces more diesel than it consumes.

Refiners produced around 59 million tons from April to September, against domestic consumption of around 46 million, according to Oil Ministry data cited by Bloomberg.

The additional volumes are coming onstream amid a global shortage, creating a commercial opportunity for projects originally designed around Indian fuel demand, ICRA senior vice president Prashant Vasisht told RT.Russian crude ‘a natural fit’

New refining capacity alone does not guarantee more diesel. Refineries need suitable crude oil, while equipment limits how much of it they can turn into diesel, Vasisht explained.

“Indian refineries are also getting the right quality of crude oil,” Sahdev said, noting Russian, Iraqi, and heavier Venezuelan grades as suitable feedstocks for diesel production.

Russian crude is “a natural fit for the Indian system,” he added. The new Rajasthan refinery can process Russian Urals and Iraqi Basrah grades, S&P Global reported in July.

Sahdev said India’s reliance on Russian barrels has also grown as production from traditional suppliers in Malaysia and West Africa declined.Can India fill the global supply gap?India is already the world’s second-largest seaborne diesel exporter after the US, according to data analysis firm Kpler. Exports reached 596,000 barrels per day in September, compared with the year-to-date average of 482,000 barrels per day, the data shows.

Additional Indian production could make a meaningful difference to global supplies, but it cannot replace all the diesel lost because of disruptions affecting Russia and the Middle East, the analysts cautioned.

“We are providing very large quantities to Europe and some of the other regions, but these are still not enough,” Vasisht said.

Not all the additional diesel will be available for export. India’s growing domestic demand, refinery operations, and freight costs will determine how much reaches overseas buyers.

The IEA expects global refinery output to recover in 2027, but warns that depleted inventories could keep diesel markets tight well into next year.

But expanding production alone is not enough, Sahdev cautioned. India also needs stronger commodity-trading networks and financial mechanisms to capture the full value of its growing refining capacity.

“If India does not do this, then somebody else will make money on Indian capacity,” he said.

Possible dwarf planet discovered beyond Pluto

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Astronomers have discovered a large icy object beyond Pluto that could qualify as a dwarf planet, potentially adding a new member to the solar system’s officially recognized group.

The discovery was announced on Thursday by the US-funded Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, which houses the world’s largest 3.2-gigapixel digital camera, built specifically for astronomy.

Researchers estimate that the object, provisionally named 2026 ED138, is between 600 and 1,500 km in diameter and may be one of the largest dwarf planet candidates ever found. It currently sits roughly 46 times farther from the Sun than Earth. Pluto’s average distance, for comparison, is about 40 times Earth’s.

The object likely escaped detection until now because it appeared against a crowded backdrop of millions of stars near the plane of the Milky Way. However, Rubin’s repeated images and tracking software has now allowed scientists to pick out its movement.

If confirmed, ED138 would join five dwarf planets recognized by the International Astronomical Union, which include Eris, Haumea, Makemake, Ceres, and Pluto, which was considered the solar system’s ninth planet until it was reclassified in 2006. Dwarf planets orbit the Sun and have enough gravity to become roughly round, but share their orbital neighborhoods with other bodies.

ED138 may be large enough to meet the roundness requirement, but astronomers still need more research and better measurements before determining its status.

“The main thing we need to do is to establish the physical size of 2026 ED138,” discoverer Matt Holman, an astrophysicist at the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard and Smithsonian, told Reuters. He explained that ED138’s size is currently estimated from the sunlight it reflects. However, a small shiny object can appear just as bright as a large, dark one, Holman said.

To narrow down the true size of the object, researchers could use Chile’s ALMA telescope which can detect radiation emitted by ED138 itself, helping get a more accurate measurement.

The last dwarf planet officially recognized by the International Astronomical Union was Haumea in 2008. Other candidates have since emerged, including 2017 OF201, whose discovery was announced in May 2025, but none have yet been formally added to the list.

Gaza protesters shout down ex-German FM (VIDEO)

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Former German Foreign Minister Annalena Baerbock was shouted down by pro-Palestinian activists during a speech at a Swedish university, Berliner Zeitung has reported.

The heated confrontation happened earlier this week at Uppsala University, where Baerbock was delivering a lecture on international cooperation and multilateral leadership, the outlet said on Wednesday.

Activists repeatedly interrupted her speech, accusing her of defending Israeli attacks on civilian infrastructure.

“You justified the bombing of hospitals and schools in Gaza, and now you come here to talk about peace!” one protester shouted.

Another challenged Baerbock over her talk of democracy and freedom, demanding: “Whose democracy are you defending? Whose freedom do you actually stand for – only that of Israelis?”

Baerbock initially attempted to deflect the accusations, suggesting that protesters raise their concerns during a question-and-answer session after the lecture. However, as the interruptions continued, she snapped: “Now I’m speaking!” The promised discussion reportedly never took place.

Baerbock headed Germany’s Foreign Ministry from 2021 to 2025 and staunchly defended Israel’s military campaign in Gaza following the Hamas-led attack on October 7, 2023. Berlin continued approving weapons supplies to Israel despite mounting international criticism, authorizing around €161 million ($168 million) in military exports in 2024.

The former minister herself sparked controversy in October 2024 when she argued in the German parliament that civilian sites in Gaza could lose their protected status under international humanitarian law if Hamas used them for military purposes. Her remarks drew criticism from those who accused Berlin of providing political cover for Israeli strikes on civilian infrastructure.

Beyond her stance on Israel, Baerbock’s tenure as Germany’s top diplomat drew criticism over a lack of diplomatic pragmatism, inconsistent foreign policy positions, and a string of high-profile verbal blunders. She also called for the protection of Palestinian civilians and stressed that Israel’s military operations were bound by international humanitarian law.

Israel launched its Gaza offensive following the October 2023 Hamas-led attack, which killed around 1,200 people and saw some 250 taken hostage. More than 75,000 Palestinians have since been killed across the occupied territories, including at least 21,500 children, according to a UN Commission of Inquiry.

In a report released on Monday, the commission concluded that Israel had continued committing “genocide” in Gaza despite the October 2025 ceasefire, with Israeli strikes reportedly killing another 1,431 Palestinians as of September 30. It also accused Israel of crimes against humanity in the occupied West Bank. Israel has rejected allegations of genocide, maintaining that its operations target Hamas.
