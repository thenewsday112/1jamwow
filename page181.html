But with the introduction of the new Mac Mini M6, the older M4 is no longer as scarce (there was no M5 Mac Mini), and the base model (256-GB hard drive, 16-GB unified memory) is on sale for $649—$150 off MSRP and $250 cheaper than the M6.

It’s a good deal for a computer that is plenty powerful for most people, and some M4 Mac Mini configurations can even handle AI tasks. But—and it’s a big but—shoppers should be aware that the M4’s original base price was $599 when it launched in 2024. However, with Apple raising prices on hardware across the board this year, driven by chip and memory shortages, the M4 ended up costing $799. In comparison, the new M6 starts at $899.

Depending on how you look at it, it’s either a discount or a hard pill to swallow, knowing that it used to be much cheaper. If you’ve been eyeing a new Mac Mini and don’t need the better performance of the M6, you’re unlikely to get a lower price than this M4.

The M4 Mac Mini may no longer be Apple’s most powerful tiny computer—that title now belongs to the M6 and M5 Pro models—but the M4 is still an incredibly strong and efficient processor. When it was discovered that it could easily run agentic AI locally, people gobbled up the Mac Mini as the affordable computing option.

Of course, for intensive tasks like AI or video editing, you really need more than 16 GB of memory; those who bought it for AI purposes loaded it up with 32 GB of RAM. For most users, however, this base-specs machine is more than capable. While the M6 system on a chip is extra powerful due to having more CPU and GPU cores, Neural Accelerators, and higher memory bandwidth, the M4 Mac Mini isn’t lagging far behind. For things like web browsing and general productivity, you won’t see much of a difference in performance.

If you need the performance, we recommend the newer M6. But for most people, the M4 Mac Mini is more than sufficient for everyday computing, and you're unlikely to find a better deal than this one.

Apple Juice Bread

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

A simple swap from water to apple juice transforms this basic bread recipe into a subtly sweet loaf that is perfect to tear into at breakfast time with a smear of good butter on top, but it also takes a PB&J to the next level. The subtle, but unmistakable, undercurrent of apple sweetness struck me as a bit of a magic trick in a bread that, otherwise, to the eye and hand was just a soft white loaf.

I first encountered the idea at the small four-seat teppanyaki restaurant in the back of the Suiran Hotel in Kyoto, Japan, where chef Tsubasa Yamaguchi served it before my first course; it was a perfect way to start the meal. When I asked him what he did differently to prepare it, he said he just took the same rustic bread recipe he baked elsewhere and used apple juice in place of water. I was incredulous. That was it? The same amount? The same technique? Yes and yes, he said. I was hooked. It turns out the water-to-juice switcheroo gives an added taste of fall without taking anything away from the shape and texture. It’s become an instant favorite at my house both breakfast and lunch, and I expect it will be at yours.

Take care when you’re lowering the bread into the Dutch oven; it is a slightly nerve-wracking moment but a little attention is all that’s needed. You can do it!

Mix 4 cups (500 g) all-purpose flour, 1¾ cups apple juice, 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1 Tbsp. sugar, and one ¼-oz. envelope instant yeast (about 2¼ tsp.) in a large bowl just until flour is hydrated and a very shaggy dough forms, about 1 minute. Cover bowl with plastic wrap and let dough sit at room temperature 15 minutes.

Set a small bowl of room-temperature water on work surface for easy reaching. Uncover dough, dip your hand in water and stretch one side of dough up and over to meet opposite side; turn bowl 90°. Repeat 3 more times, turning bowl 90° after each fold. (This constitutes 1 full fold.) If dough doesn’t always stretch to meet opposite side, just aim for the middle. Wet your hands and shake off excess water any time dough begins to feel sticky. Cover dough and let sit 30 minutes (set a timer). Repeat folding process to make 1 full fold. Re-cover and let dough sit another 30 minutes. Repeat folding process and resting one more time. By the last fold, dough should be significantly smoother and tighter.

Transfer dough to a generously floured surface, dust with flour, and shape into a tight ball. Dust dough with more flour. Dust a kitchen towel with flour and place inside a high-sided bowl to line. Arrange dough, seam side up, in bowl on top of towel. Tightly cover bowl with plastic wrap and let dough sit in a warm spot until puffy, about 45 minutes.

Place a rack in middle of oven, then set a large ovenproof Dutch oven with a lid on it; preheat oven to 400°. Place a 12" square of parchment paper on a flat surface. Cut a 2"-long slit in each corner toward the center. Very carefully, tip dough out onto center of parchment, trying your best not to deflate it or ruin its shape. (It’s easiest to place parchment on a flat plate so you can easily turn dough out of bowl.) Working very quickly and using a pair of kitchen shears lightly coated in vegetable oil, cut an X in center of bread. Remove pot from oven and very carefully lower dough into pot. Cover pot and return to oven.

Bake bread 35 minutes. Uncover pot, reduce oven temperature to 375°, and continue to bake bread until crust is deeply browned, 35–40 minutes more. Carefully tip loaf out onto a wire rack and let cool at least 1 hour before slicing.

Do Ahead: Bread can be baked 3 days ahead. Let cool completely; store airtight at room temperature.

Brazil’s Workers’ Party Is Not Stopping

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With just a few days remaining before the runoff election, supporters of Lula’s party are doing everything possible to rally support behind the president.

Supporters of Brazil’s president and presidential candidate Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva of the Workers’ Party attend a campaign event in São Paulo, Brazil, on October 11, 2026, ahead of the presidential runoff election.

Rio de Janeiro—On Tuesday, October 6, at roughly 5 pm, people wearing red shirts, hats, and stickers started filling the Rio de Janeiro Bankers’ Union office in the center of the city. Still shaken by the results of the first round of Brazil’s elections that previous Sunday, Workers’ Party voters had been called to a meeting by newly elected congressman Marcelo Freixo to strategize the next steps of a national campaign to turn the elections around.

That Sunday, October 4, millions of Brazilians voted to elect seats in the chamber of deputies and the Senate, as well as state deputies, governors, and the president. Democratic socialist President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a member of the Workers’ Party, was facing far-right candidate Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of Jair Bolsonaro, who is currently in jail for an attempted coup d’état in 2022. Contrary to the polls leading up to election day, which gave Lula a lead over the far-right candidate, Bolsonaro captured 1.87 percent of valid votes more than Lula, with 47 percent of total votes against Lula’s 45 percent. Since neither candidate reached over 50 percent of the vote, the race heads to a runoff on October 25. But even if Lula were to win this coming Sunday, Bolsonaro’s party, the Partido Liberal, swept the Senate, winning 28 of 54 available seats, giving the far right a majority in the legislative chamber on top of its control of the lower house.

A Bolsonaro win in the runoff would give unprecedented power to the far right in two of the three top branches of Brazil’s government. And in the 48 hours following Bolsonaro’s advantage in the first round, he announced plans to modify the country’s 1988 Constitution to “redemocratize the country,” proposing to abolish reelections and reform the Supreme Court, the last independent branch of government. The Supreme Court has been key in maintaining Brazilian democracy. But experts warn that a far-right majority in the Senate could be used to impeach and humiliate ministers who voted to convict Jair Bolsonaro. “It’s the end of the Lula era and we will be in power for decades,” Flávio said in a campaign event following the race.

The Lula era began in 2003, when he became president on a democratic socialist platform. He was elected again in 2006, with an approval rating of 52 percent. He won his third election in 2022, ousting far-right incumbent president Jair Bolsonaro. Lula’s governments were marked by a decline in poverty through welfare policies, expansion of investment in higher education, increased access to water and basic sanitation, the delivery of hundreds of thousands of public housing units, and the expansion of workers’ rights.

After the first round of voting, Lula and the Workers’ Party accused Meta of meddling with the 2026 elections through disinformation campaigns and the suppression of left-wing content across Meta’s social-media platforms. Hundreds protested outside of Meta’s office in São Paulo on Monday, and the company announced on Tuesday that is complying with an order from the electoral court, triggered by the Lula campaign’s accusation, “to give equal treatment in its platforms” to both candidates ahead of the runoff, reported the Associated Press.

As Lula campaign supporters race to activate voters ahead of Sunday’s runoff, legal experts remain concerned about Flávio’s intentions to change the Constitution. For Wallace Corbo, professor of constitutional law at the State University of Rio de Janeiro and the Getúlio Vargas Foundation, this proposal should be understood as a dog whistle confirming the likelihood of a return to far-right authoritarianism. “Substituting the 1988 Constitution in a context of the rise of authoritarian governments, with very low popular participation, is very problematic in terms of legitimacy, democracy, and rights for citizens and workers,” Corbo told The Nation, referencing the record number of people—33.4 million people, or 21 percent of the electorate—who did not vote in the October 4 elections. This low participation contrasts sharply with the support for the 1988 Constitution, which involved an overwhelming number of participants from the different historically marginalized groups, including Indigenous and Black people and women, becoming “the most democratic and participatory Constitution in our history,” after 21 years of a far-right military dictatorship supported by the United States, Corbo said, adding, “There’s a danger of its being effectively abolished or significantly disfigured, with the rollback of certain rights for the population.”

Additionally, a reform of the judiciary, as Bolsonaro has promised, might risk rolling back the limited abortion rights in the country. Abortion is largely banned in Brazil but allowed in the cases of rape, life endangerment, or when the fetus has been diagnosed with anencephaly—exceptions that have been protected by Supreme Court rulings. In 2025, for example, Justice Alexandre de Moraes blocked a ban on fetal asystole, a technique used to stop the heartbeat of a fetus before it’s removed from the uterus, which would have ended all abortions beyond 20 weeks in Brazil. “The judiciary is still one of the institutions that most firmly upholds established rights and resists certain setbacks—this could be lost, and it could completely change this paradigm if, for example, there were an expansion of seats on the Supreme Court,” said Angela Boldrini, a São Paulo–based journalist specializing in coverage of reproductive rights.

The next president will be able to name at least four new Supreme Court justices. If Bolsonaro wins, this would give him unprecedented power over the three branches of government. In the Senate, the risk is a conservative majority passing even more draconian anti-abortion legislation. “If we have a chamber of deputies, a Senate, and a president that align with Bolsonarism, we lose all the checks and balances that could, for example, lead to a veto or the overturning of a presidential veto,” added Boldrini.

In the Rio de Janeiro Bankers’ Union office earlier this month, around 2,000 people showed up to strategize ahead of the election runoffs. The sense of urgency was palpable in the room, as was the resolve to do everything possible to bolster Lula’s campaign. Lula is an original founder of the Workers’ Party and has dedicated his life to defending its principles, including the redistribution of wealth through welfare policies and taxing the rich.

“Each leftist militant here has to convince one person who didn’t vote to go vote,” said Alberes Lima, the Rio de Janeiro state president of the Workers’ Party. “We need to instill in people what a Bolsonaro government means. It means the end of workers’ rights, the end of a raise in the minimum salary; it means the end of many social programs that were built throughout many of your lives. Many things were built by people before us and we cannot make a mistake now.” With Brazil’s previous military dictatorship, which suppressed speech and violently persecuted leftists from 1964 to 1985, hanging in the air, Lima concluded, “We need to make a difference and we need to build something new so this bleak reality doesn’t fall upon Brazil again. Nineteen sixty-four, never again.”

While many on Brazil’s left are sounding the alarm on a possible dictatorship that is similar to that established by the US-backed coup d’état of 1964, Corbo says this is a new, more subtle version of a shift to an authoritarian government. “[The Bolsonaro family and their allies] didn’t succeed in installing a violent coup d’etat [in January 2023], so they are trying to install it under a semblance of normality through the control of the three powers of Brazilian democracy,” he said. “With a president controlling all of these powers [the judicial, the legislative, and the presidency], there will be creations of new mechanisms to weaken the opposition, weaken regulatory channels, and establish an authoritarian regime through normal institutional channels.”

But much like 1964, this authoritarian regime would function in service of the interest of the United States, and consequently, could have catastrophic consequences for the whole world.

Flávio and Trump met months before the election, and Flávio’s proposals contain 16 of the 21 demands Trump made of Brazil in October of last year, when the US imposed tariffs on the country. The demands include less regulation of American tech companies; no expansion of Brazil’s digital and free payment system, Pix; a “strategic and long-term partnership” with regards to Brazil’s rare minerals; and an alignment on deforestation of the Amazon, 60 percent of which is located in Brazil.

A Flávio presidential term presents an existential threat not only to Brazil but to a world currently dealing with the accelerating effects of climate change. With the far right running the Senate and Bolsonarista governors across Brazil acting in support of deforestation, the addition of a Flávio presidency would be devastating.

“Flávio Bolsonaro has shown that he will be just as willing as his father to dismantle protections for climate and nature and allow illegal miners, loggers and land grabbers to invade Indigenous territories,” Jonathan Watts wrote for The Guardian on October 7. “As a lawmaker, he backed a 2019 bill to eliminate requirements for landowners in the Amazon to conserve a proportion of native vegetation. In his manifesto, he completely ignored the climate crisis. And he has courted support from some of the most predatory landowners in the rainforest.”

On the ground, left-wing organizers—both inside and outside of the Workers’ Party—are doing everything possible to mobilize support for Lula and Brazilian democracy, hosting rallies and making their presence known. “We need discipline in a moment like this,” congressman Freixo said at the October 6 meeting. “We cannot make mistakes.”

Given that a significant number of voters did not make their way to the polls in the first round—the highest number of abstentions since 1998—the Workers’ Party is focused on finding and captivating these eligible voters to overtake Bolsonaro. They have just three days left to turn out those voters, in what Freixo called “the most important election of our lives.”

Flying Can Use Less Energy Than Swimming, and these Birds Have Been Doing It All Along

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists thought swimming was the most energy-efficient way for animals to travel.

An Andean condor and a trumpeter swan weigh roughly the same: about 11 kilograms. Both can fly, and both have to overcome gravity to stay in the air. So you might be tempted to think that they use up comparable amounts of energy.

The swan spends around 99% of its flight time flapping its wings. The condor, just 0.1%. And according to new research, the condor uses 79% less energy to cover the same distance.

While the swan relies primarily on its muscles, the condor has learned to harness an enormous, invisible source of energy: the atmosphere itself. In fact, it’s so efficient, that under the right conditions, its flight can be more energy-efficient than swimming.

Swimming is generally the “cheapest” way to travel from an energy standpoint. It makes a lot of sense; water supports an animal’s weight, allowing fish and other swimmers to move without constantly fighting gravity.

Flying comes next. Sure, you can “ride the wind”, but you need to generate lift to stay airborne, which can require considerable energy. Running is typically the most expensive. Land animals have to support their own weight while repeatedly accelerating their limbs and bodies.

Scientists describe this relationship using a quantity called the cost of transport: the energy required to move a given distance, often calculated relative to body mass. Think of it as the animal equivalent of fuel economy.

Now, an international team of 68 scientists challenges the classic thought on the cost of transport. Much of our understanding, they say, comes from experiments conducted in laboratories, where fish swim in tanks, birds fly in wind tunnels, and mammals run on treadmills. Those controlled environments make it easier to measure energy expenditure, but nature doesn’t really work like that.

“To us, the sky might just look like background for flying animals, but it is an essential resource that can dramatically change the cost of a journey,” says first author Dr. Martina Scacco.

If you’ve ever watched an eagle or vulture circle around the sky, you’ve probably seen it: they can remain airborne for long periods of time while barely flapping their wings.

These birds are masters of exploiting physics.

When the Sun heats the ground, the ground warms the air above it. In some places, pockets or columns of warmer air rise through the atmosphere. These are called thermals.

A soaring bird can enter one of these rising columns and circle upward, gaining altitude without having to generate all that lift through muscular effort. Then, once they’re high up, it can glide toward its destination, gradually losing altitude until it finds another thermal.

It’s kind of like taking an elevator to the top of a building and then gliding down toward the next one.

This “free” energy comes from the Sun, which heats the landscape and drives atmospheric motion. But from the bird’s perspective, it’s an external energy subsidy.

So far, nothing new. But that’s where the new study comes in.

To find out how much energy birds actually spend travelling, the researchers assembled an unusually large dataset.

They combined 17 years of GPS and accelerometer records from approximately 850 individual birds and bats belonging to 38 species. They also analysed four years of radar observations of smaller flying birds, divided into 12 size categories.

Altogether, the analysis covered nearly 200,000 GPS-based flight segments and thousands of additional radar observations. The animals ranged from tiny birds weighing little more than 12 grams to enormous soaring birds weighing over 11 kilograms.

GPS tracking showed where and how fast animals travelled. Accelerometers, which measure movement, helped researchers distinguish between energetically expensive wingbeats and more economical gliding or soaring.

The team then matched the animals’ movements to information about wind and rising air currents, allowing them to estimate the energetic cost of travelling through real atmospheric conditions.

Across seven species of vultures, the researchers analysed flights representing more than 4.3 million kilometres of travel. That’s enough to circle the Earth more than 100 times. Collectively, the birds accumulated approximately 128,000 days of flight.

Yet depending on the species, they spent only 0.1% to 7% of that time flapping their wings. The Andean condor, the heaviest vulture in the comparison, flapped least of all.

Granted, these birds are exceptional flyers to begin with; but the secret lies in how they use the energy that was already present in their surroundings.

And when the researchers translated their movements into estimated energy costs, some birds proved more economical travellers than swimming animals.

The study uncovered another seemingly surprising pattern. In general, larger species tended to spend less time flapping and were more efficient flyers.

That sounds counterintuitive. Surely a heavier bird needs more energy to stay airborne?

Well, in absolute terms, yes, larger animals generally require more energy. But the question here is how much energy they spend moving a given distance relative to their mass.

Large soaring birds have evolved wings and flight behaviours that let them extract considerable benefit from rising air. Under favourable conditions, they can travel enormous distances with relatively little muscular effort.

But there’s a catch: larger birds are more dependent on finding suitable atmospheric conditions.

Smaller birds are more flexible, they don’t need to rely on thermals as much, but they spend more time flapping their wings and use up more energy as a result.

Perhaps the most surprising finding is that some soaring birds travel more efficiently than swimming animals, overturning the traditional ranking of animal locomotion.

But the findings could also reshape our understanding of migration. A longer route with favourable winds and thermals might require less energy than a shorter journey through difficult conditions.

That’s particularly relevant as climate change alters atmospheric conditions. Changes in wind and rising air currents could affect how much energy birds need to migrate, and potentially where they can travel.

For now, these energy costs are modelled estimates rather than direct metabolic measurements. But the message is clear: the cost of getting somewhere depends not just on the animal, but also on the environment carrying it; and we need more complex studies to truly map that out.

For the past week, President Donald Trump and his aides have traded accusations with Elon Musk, the CEO of SpaceX, over who restricted Ukraine’s access to a military-grade version of Starlink, SpaceX’s satellite network product that Ukrainian forces depend on to intercept incoming Russian projectiles and target Russian launchers.

Musk blamed Trump and the U.S. military for the decision, writing on X, “Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS.” Trump insisted that his administration did not disrupt Ukraine’s access to the network. Secretary of State Marco Rubio argued that decisions about Ukraine’s access to the network were in the hands of SpaceX and Musk and not the White House. The Wall Street Journal and CBS News reported that the decision had been Musk’s.

On Mar. 9, 2025, Musk had promised that, whatever his disagreements with U.S. policy toward Ukraine, “Starlink will never turn off its terminals. We would never do such a thing or use it as a bargaining chip.” Critics have raced to blame Musk alone, casting him as the puppeteer behind the throne, the first business titan to achieve what William Randolph Hearst, Marcus Alonzo Hanna, and J.P. Morgan only aspired to: a latter-day Cornelius Vanderbilt, who is reputed to have scoffed, “Law? What do I care about the law? Hain’t I got the power?”

That gets the story backward. Musk’s businesses are uniquely dependent on the blessings of Washington: a fifth of SpaceX’s revenue comes directly from federal agencies, its satellites require federal authorization to launch and operate, and the possible merger of Tesla and SpaceX will live or die by the decisions of federal regulators.

Confronted with a White House that appeared determined to squeeze Ukraine, whatever its public denials, Musk faced a familiar choice for anyone dependent on political patronage: capitulate, resist, or retreat into ambiguity. He chose ambiguity, revealing the limits of his power. The wealthiest man in the world still answers to someone else.

The finger-pointing creates a haze of ambiguity, giving both men plausible deniability. The apparent aim is to force Ukraine into a settlement by depriving it of the means to resist: a way to secure a quick, short-term peace and lower oil prices at the expense of Ukrainian sovereignty and international law.

But that calculation mistakes capitulation for stability. Rewarding President Vladimir Putin of Russia could encourage him to test NATO’s resolve in the Baltic states, Poland, Finland, or Sweden, raising the risk of a direct confrontation with the alliance. It would also signal to other U.S. adversaries that sufficient pressure can fold Washington’s commitments.

For now, Trump could claim to have brokered peace between Russia and Ukraine, even if the peace he delivers is the silence of a defeated country. Musk can evade responsibility by presenting himself as merely one supplier among many across the defense industry whose failures have weakened Ukraine’s defenses. He could point to weapons manufacturer RTX, whose Patriot interceptors are so scarce that Ukraine reportedly ran out of them this month, or to delays in delivering F-16s manufactured by Lockheed Martin that were promised two years earlier.

Beneath the public quarrel lies a familiar pattern: surrender political independence and retain the freedom to accumulate extraordinary wealth. The historians who have studied Putin’s Russia most closely would recognize the pattern instantly. Timothy Snyder has written that Russia’s oligarchs “were tamed, but not by a neutral state.” Rather, “Putin himself became the head oligarch, the boss of bosses.” Anne Applebaum echoed the insight and described Putin as making himself “godfather to all of the other oligarchs.”

Putin laid out the terms of that bargain on Jul. 28, 2000, when he met with 21 of Russia's richest men in the Kremlin, less than three months after his inauguration. The message was simple. The fortunes they had amassed in the chaos of the 1990s were theirs to keep, as long as they stayed out of politics entirely. Keep your distance from power, and the state would leave you alone.

Those who refused the deal faced immediate reprisals. Boris Berezovsky and Vladimir Gusinsky, whose television networks refused to bend the knee, were driven into exile within the year. Gusinsky’s NTV was seized by state-controlled Gazprom. Mikhail Khodorkovsky, then Russia’s richest man, funded opposition parties and lectured the Kremlin on corruption. In Oct. 2003 he was pulled off his plane at a Siberian airport and arrested. He spent a decade in prison and watched his oil company, Yukos, dismantled and its assets transferred to state-controlled companies.

Those who accepted the deal are the ones who got rich, and they have never been richer. Forbes counted a record 155 Russian billionaires this year, the fourth straight year that number has grown, with a combined fortune of $696.5 billion—up 11% from the previous year, even as Putin’s war against Ukraine entered its fifth year. Their collective wealth reportedly now exceeds the bank term deposits of the rest of Russia’s population: 155 oligarchs with fortunes greater than the savings of roughly 146 million ordinary Russians.

“Oligarchies are unstable,” Snyder has observed. “Members disappear.” For those who might have forgotten the terms of Putin’s bargain, an instructive reminder came in 2023, when Yevgeny Prigozhin, the head of the Wagner Group, did the one thing the bargain forbade: he challenged the Kremlin’s authority. Prigozhin seized a military headquarters in Rostov-on-Don and sent his forces toward Moscow. His column advanced to within 125 miles of the capital before he agreed to stand down under a deal brokered by Aleksandr Lukashenko, the Belarusian dictator.

Putin publicly called the mutiny treason, then appeared to forgive him. Two months later, on Aug. 23, 2023, Prigozhin’s private jet crashed north of Moscow, killing everyone aboard. The Kremlin denied involvement, the U.S. intelligence concluded that the plane had been blown up, and The Wall Street Journal later reported that the operation had been ordered by Putin’s Security Council chief. Putin’s eulogy, delivered the next day, described Prigozhin as a man who had “made serious mistakes in life.” No oligarch in Russia needed the rest of the sentence spelled out.

Prigozhin’s fate is only the most spectacular entry on a longer, murkier list. Since the invasion of Ukraine began in 2022, at least 85 Russian businessmen and officials have reportedly died in unexplained or violent circumstances, many of them figures in the energy industry. Among the dead are three Lukoil executives: board member Alexander Subbotin, who died in May 2022 after a visit to a “shaman” outside Moscow; chairman Ravil Maganov, who fell from a sixth-floor window that September; and vice president Vitaly Robertus, who was found dead in his office in March 2024.

In April 2022, two former energy and banking executives were found dead alongside their families. Vladislav Avayev, a former Gazprombank vice president, was found shot dead in his Moscow apartment with his wife and 13-year-old daughter. Sergei Protosenya, a former Novatek executive, was found hanged at a rented villa in Lloret de Mar, Spain, with his wife and daughter stabbed to death inside. Catalan police concluded that Protosenya had killed his family, then himself. His son rejected that finding, insisting that his father had been in good spirits and had no reasons to kill them and himself.

Transneft vice president Andrei Badalov fell from a 17th-floor window in July 2025. The Moscow Times has counted 18 such fatal falls from windows since 2002. Three days later, Russia’s transport minister, Roman Starovoit, was found shot dead in his car hours after Putin fired him. Another transport official died of supposedly acute heart failure the same day.

Putin’s Russia offers an extreme and grotesque example of the bargain. Yet some critics see an emerging American version, in a far less violent form. Snyder and Applebaum have warned that Western billionaires risk repeating the mistakes of Russia’s oligarchs under Boris Yeltsin, believing they can manage a strongman for profit, only to discover that he is managing them.

Musk spent more than $277 million to help elect Donald Trump, took a desk in the West Wing, and ran the Department of Government Efficiency like a man who believed he was co-president. When that arrangement flamed out in May 2025, he tried to exercise independent political power by turning against Trump, calling his signature legislation, the Big Beautiful Bill, a “disgusting abomination.” Musk threatened to form a third political party and then briefly launched one. He even taunted Trump about Jeffrey Epstein, posting that the president “is in the Epstein files.”

After Trump publicly mused about terminating his contracts, Musk quickly retreated. He deleted the posts, and conceded that they “went too far.” He returned to the fold, shook Trump’s hand at Charlie Kirk’s memorial, accepted the National Medal of Science from him this month, and became the biggest known donor of the 2026 midterms, giving at least $366 million to Republican groups. He has grown vastly richer in the process: SpaceX’s June IPO made him the world’s first trillionaire, while the company remains the U.S. government’s dominant launch provider, with competitors such as Blue Origin winning only a fraction of the contracts.

Musk’s retreat illustrates the bargain: surrender the freedom to oppose the president and preserve the ability to enrich yourself. By that measure, Musk’s political defeat has come with an extraordinary financial windfall. He won by losing. The throttling of Starlink in Ukraine appears to fit this pattern. Last week, after Trump demanded that Ukraine stop striking Russian refineries and Kyiv did not comply, its access to a military-grade version of Starlink was restricted and responsibility disappeared into a haze of accusation and denial.

The costs of such Faustian bargains fall on everyone excluded from them. In Russia, soldiers die at the front by the hundreds of thousands while politically compliant billionaires accumulate obscene fortunes. In Ukraine, drone operators lost contact with their interceptors in mid-flight last week while Russian missiles were in the air; the consequences of a disrupted connection are immediate and potentially fatal. Other customers face the question: can a contract with Starlink be relied upon?

In America, democratic government rests on the principle that neither political office nor private places anyone above the law. Businesses must be free to challenge a president without fearing retaliation, and their owners must remain accountable for their conduct. Those protections help distinguish America from an authoritarian dictatorship like Putin’s Russia.

The Starlink saga does not suggest that America has become Russia. But it raises a troubling question: is political obedience becoming the price of commercial success in America?  Putin’s oligarchs struck that bargain in 2000 and have been collecting on it ever since. Whether Americans accept a version of it will depend on how forcefully our institutions and business leaders defend our freedom to say no.

Van Gogh’s The Starry Night May Have Been Based on a Real Night Sky and Astronomers Found an Astonishing Match

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Astronomers found a remarkable match between Van Gogh’s Starry Night and the actual night sky of July 22, 1889 from the artist's vantange point.

A dark cypress rises like a flame above a sleeping village. The church steeple points toward a sky in motion, where great yellow stars glow inside rings of light and blue currents sweep across the heavens. In Vincent van Gogh’s landmark painting The Starry Night, painted in 1889, the countryside seems still, but the universe above it refuses to rest.

Van Gogh painted it while staying at Saint-Paul-de-Mausole, a psychiatric hospital in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, southern France. He had voluntarily entered the institution in May 1889, hoping to recover from the mental health crises that had troubled him in the preceding months. The hospital stood amid the rolling hills and olive groves of Provence. From his room, Van Gogh could look east across the countryside, a view that would inspire some of his most celebrated paintings.

The village, with a church recalling his native Netherlands, was largely imagined. But the sky had a more tangible starting point.

In a letter to his brother Theo, written in late May or early June, van Gogh recalled: “This morning I saw the countryside from my window a long time before sunrise with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big.”

The “morning star” was Venus, the brilliant planet that can appear before sunrise. Art historians have long identified it with the large, glowing object just above the cypress in The Starry Night.

But what about the other stars? Did van Gogh paint a real and somewhat accurate arrangement of celestial bodies, or did he simply imagine them to suit his artistic vision?

Scholars have long debated how much of the sky van Gogh observed and how much he imagined. New York’s Museum of Modern Art, which owns the painting, dates it to June 1889, largely based on the painter’s correspondence with Theo. But a new astronomical study proposes that the painting’s stars and crescent moon accurately depict the night sky more than a month later, before dawn on July 22, 1889.

If so, The Starry Night may be much more astronomically accurate than perhaps anyone assumed.

For Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim, the stars have been familiar companions since childhood. He was eight when he saw Halley’s Comet in 1986, a mind-blowing experience that helped set him on a path toward astronomy. Now a telescope engineer and founder of the South Korean company SLLAB, Kim has spent more than 25 years developing large telescopes. Over that time, he has become intimately familiar with the night sky, memorizing the positions of stars through countless hours spent aligning instruments.

That experience gave him a rather unusual perspective when he looked closely at Van Gogh’s The Starry Night. Among the exaggerated, glowing stars near the center of the canvas, he recognized the Hyades, a V-shaped star cluster in the constellation Taurus.

“People like me, who guide a telescope through its first alignment with the sky, naturally memorize where the stars are,” Kim told ZME Science. “To my eyes, the star cluster in The Starry Night was the Hyades in Taurus. But earlier studies explained it as Aries.”

Something didn’t sit right. Aries contains relatively faint stars, hardly the sort of brilliant celestial spectacle that might catch a painter’s imagination. The Hyades, by contrast, form one of the most recognizable star patterns in the sky.

“At that moment, I was sure this painting was worth studying,” Kim recalled.

What began as a hunch would eventually lead Kim and his colleagues at Kyung Hee University to challenge more than a century of assumptions about one of the world’s most famous paintings.

Art historian Albert Boime and astronomer Charles Whitney had previously offered conflicting astronomical interpretations in the 1980s, both working from a June date.

The most striking problem was the moon. At the painting’s upper right, it is a slender crescent. But on June 19, 1889, it was 63 percent illuminated — more than half full.

On July 22, the moon was just 28 percent illuminated. Kim’s astronomical simulations tracked the night’s sky back in time from Saint-Paul-de-Mausole’s vantage point and placed the moon above Venus in the eastern sky, with the Hyades, a V-shaped cluster in Taurus, between them. A day earlier, the moon and Venus were farther apart; in the following days, they drew much closer.

“The Moon, the Hyades and Venus all match the painting at once,” Kim said.

Of course, van Gogh did not set out to paint an astronomical chart. His stars are enlarged, haloed and embedded in sweeping brushstrokes. How could researchers distinguish a genuine star pattern from a coincidental arrangement of yellow paint? Aren’t we running the risk of reading too much between the lines when perhaps the artist had no such intention?

The researchers still thought they had something to work with. They not only reconstructed how the sky would have appeared above Saint-Rémy on different mornings in June and July 1889 but also visited the area in person and used an earlier landscape sketch by Van Gogh to estimate how much of the eastern horizon he could have captured in his composition.

The results narrowed the possibilities around dawn on July 22, but the researchers still needed to establish whether the painted stars actually resembled the cluster.

Imagine tracing five stars from a painting onto a transparent sheet, then placing that sheet over a real star chart. You can move the tracing, rotate it or enlarge it until the patterns overlap as closely as possible. What matters is not the absolute position or size of the stars, but the distances and arrangement between them.

That’s essentially what the researchers did.

Before examining The Starry Night, they tested their method on other paintings. In Van Gogh’s Starry Night Over the Rhône, for example, the seven stars of the Big Dipper are recognizable, giving them a benchmark for how accurately the artist reproduced a real constellation. They also examined works by earlier painters, including one with a recognizable depiction of Orion and another whose celestial arrangement could not have existed in the real sky.

Armed with these comparisons, the team measured five prominent points in the central star pattern of The Starry Night. Their arrangement closely resembled the V-shaped structure of the Hyades, with a degree of distortion broadly comparable to what they found in Van Gogh’s other night paintings.

There was another intriguing result. When they repeated the comparison using a black-and-white drawing Van Gogh made after the original painting, the stars matched the Hyades even more closely. The researchers interpreted this as a possible indication that Van Gogh had a real celestial arrangement in mind, rather than merely using an arbitrary pattern of painted dots.

But resemblance alone is of course not proof.

“Procrustes analysis is by no means a cure-all; even a completely different constellation can sometimes show a high degree of fit,” Kim cautioned.

Indeed, the researchers found that other constellations could produce similarly convincing mathematical matches. The trouble was that they failed other tests. Some appeared in the wrong part of the sky; others contained stars too faint to be plausible candidates, or required treating Venus as an ordinary star.

The Hyades, by contrast, satisfied several constraints at once: their shape, position, orientation and relationship to the moon and Venus were all consistent with the July 22 reconstruction.

There remains an inconvenient piece of evidence. On June 18, van Gogh told Theo that he had “a new study of a starry sky.” That is why scholars have long assigned The Starry Night to June. Kim and his colleagues do not dispute that work had begun by then, but that doesn’t mean the letter proves the canvas was finished by then.

The Dutch painter had a debilitating episode in mid-July, partly because his correspondence fell silent after July 16. He subsequently spent weeks unable to work. If that chronology is correct, he would probably have been in no condition to observe the dawn sky on July 22, let alone incorporate it into a painting.

Kim and his colleagues therefore revisited the historical evidence to see whether the episode might have begun later than previously assumed.

One clue came from Van Gogh himself. In an August 22 letter to Theo, he recalled the circumstances of his relapse: “This new crisis, my dear brother, came upon me in the fields, and when I was in the middle of painting on a windy day”.

The researchers compared that description with weather observations from three stations in southern France (how amazing is it that we have this data from the late 19th century?). They found that a strong mistral, the northerly wind characteristic of the region, swept through the area around July 27–29. Another clue appeared in a September letter, in which Van Gogh described having been confined for six weeks. Counting backward places the beginning of his illness around July 25 or 26, broadly consistent with the weather records. Other letters, however, suggest a longer period of illness, so the record remains contradictory.

All of these clues permit a later onset, which may have allowed him to observe the night sky and paint it on July 22.

“The astronomical interpretation stands on its own, regardless of when the seizure began,” Kim said.

All of this does not settle the question of when Van Gogh finished The Starry Night, nor does it diminish the imagination that transformed an ordinary landscape into one of art history’s most recognizable images.

“Vincent found it hard to paint without a model, and he even argued with Gauguin, who believed one should paint from imagination. This background explains the realistic night sky in Starry Night over the Rhône. Why, then, have the stars in Café Terrace at Night and The Starry Night been interpreted as imaginary? Of three night skies painted by the same artist, we have long accepted that one is real and the other two are imagined. Wouldn’t it be more natural to explain all three as real, or all three as imagined?”

“In my view, Vincent always pursued artistic expression grounded in accuracy. Being based on the real sky does not deny his expression. In The Starry Night, I believe Vincent brought together on a single canvas the skies of several days before and after July 22. In fact, no two skies are the same; something always changes.” Kim told ZME Science.

Indeed, the swirls need not be either a transcription of nature or a flight from it. Van Gogh may have started with a real dawn sky and made it unmistakably his own.

Kim hopes his findings will open a conversation between astronomers and art historians.

There is also a rather charming irony to his investigation. Despite spending years studying the painting, reconstructing its stars and retracing Van Gogh’s observations, Kim has never actually stood before the original canvas. It hangs at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, and he jokingly hopes the museum will invite him to see it.

In the meantime, he suggests a different way of experiencing the masterpiece. Anyone can look up at the real night sky and search for the Hyades, whose distinctive V-shaped arrangement appears near the reddish star Aldebaran in the constellation Taurus.

“But seeing the Hyades is much easier,” Kim said. “After that, whenever you look at The Starry Night, you may see the Hyades first.”

The findings were published in the journal Scientific Reports.

The episode has striking parallels with secular debates around AI. It’s worth thinking about why the discussion within a fervently traditionalist community played out so similarly to the one on the secular left. The Lakewood rabbis don’t consider generative AI destructive simply because it is new or because it could easily supply users with blasphemous content (though there is that). They also argue that interpreting the Torah is a fundamentally communal activity that requires ameilut, or toil, a philosophy espoused by foundational scholars like Rashi. They fear a future in which Torah study is fully automated and human intellectual exertion nullified. In this, they’re surprisingly aligned with leftists who oppose data centers and big tech. “They might come up with different sources for explaining it,” anthropologist Ayala Fader told The Forward, “but they are actually articulating some of the objections to AI that you can read about in The Chronicle of Higher Education.”

There are, of course, more prosaic concerns. Haredim, like other groups that enforce strict religious or cultural observances, have long argued about when and how to accommodate the demands of the contemporary world, and whether to adopt or shun new technologies. A decade ago, a debate raged among Hasidim (a subset of Haredim) about the Internet’s potential for sacrilege. Today, there are Hasidic TikTok influencers—although they abide by strictly enforced community standards. Many Orthodox Jews today see AI as a similar gateway to pornography or other impurities, and it remains to be seen whether they will accept it as inevitable and just try to put up some guardrails, or succeed in fully eradicating it. The Satmar Rebbe, the head of the Satmar Hasidic dynasty, put out an emergency broadcast this past May: “On every phone in the home, even on a kosher phone, one can connect to…impurity in a single moment,” he said. “There are many victims—young boys, girls, and even married yeshiva students. They are falling and descending directly into hell.” He called upon Satmar Hasidim to abstain from cell phones that preload applications like Gemini or OpenAI.

Anti-AI Haredim are not alone in considering the risks of this new technology to be existential. The threats it poses to labor are obvious, and yet there’s something about it that’s still more troubling, something that can be hard to put your finger on. The debates around AI often take on a strangely religious tone—after all, the most prominent leader to condemn its encroachment on humanity is Pope Leo. The Lakewood rabbis’ objections may help us better understand why both secular and religious AI refuseniks feel that something elemental is at stake. The Haredim who met in January argued that mitzvot—good deeds performed to fulfill a Torah commandment—cannot be replaced by machines. To eschew ameilut is to forgo the sacred work of Torah study. Outsourcing interpretation of scripture can be seen as an attack on the act of exegesis itself. In this way, AI threatens to undermine their way of life more thoroughly than previous technologies.

While it may be dangerous, AI is also increasingly omnipresent, complicating Orthodox communities’ attempts to rein it in. “You have to create rules that people will follow,” Forward staff reporter Louis Keene told me over the phone. “And if a rabbi is setting rules and nobody’s following them, it doesn’t matter how strict he is because nobody’s observing it.” Yet Keene acknowledges that many people intuit that there’s just something off about AI: “A lot of people are either nervous about it or repulsed by it. I don’t think people generally like AI art. Why do we see so much slop? We’re calling it slop. Everyone agrees.” For religious as well as secular communities, that slop presents a kind of spiritual affront.

There is a remarkably religious flavor to the way even atheists and technocrats talk about AI. Advocates frequently refer to it as a god in the making. “If we ever get AI that is strong enough to basically be God and solve all of our problems, it will need to use the same techniques that the actual God uses in terms of maintaining some distance,” rationalist and AI blogger Scott Alexander told Harper’s Magazine earlier this year. I do think it’s possible that the AI will be like, Now I am God. I’ve concluded that the actual God made exactly the right decision on how much evil to permit in the universe. Therefore I refuse to change anything.” Why are self-styled rationalists leaning so hard into such an esoteric framing?

“[T]here comes a time in every ai policy professional’s life when they realize they have to read the talmud to make further progress,” Dean W. Ball, OpenAI’s head of strategic futures, posted this summer. While that may seem like a completely out-of-left-field remark to anyone not versed in Silicon Valley lore, it sparked extensive conversations among AI researchers. The thinking goes that the Talmud offers a model of interpretation and debate that could teach AI the nature of ethics. Researchers often refer to the “AI alignment problem,” which is the difficulty that human designers face in making sure an AI system’s behavior matches the goals and values humans actually want. This is both a practical and philosophical problem, so loaded that it lends itself more easily to the domain of theology than corporate governance. Judd Rosenblatt, founder of the AI consulting company AE Studios and the AI ethics nonprofit AI Alignment Foundation, told The Jewish Forward that he looks to the Talmud for inspiration as he considers the AI alignment problem, believing its tradition of evolving arguments and adaptations could help us design AI systems that can improve exponentially without going rogue. “I think a tradition that reinterprets nothing is the more fragile one,” he said. “A rule that cannot be bent, cannot adapt to a new world and dies out.” Besides, he argues, Judaism has survived multiple implosions, including the destruction of the temple.

Not all Haredi agree that AI is a modern-day Tower of Babel, tempting the wrath of God with our hubris. Users on imamother.com, an online forum for Orthodox women, discuss their use of AI with a mix of shame and excitement, with some saying it motivates them to see doctors or go to the dentist. As one poster asked, “Am I a horrible person for not listening to das torah [a rabbinic pronouncement]? I can’t believe that HaShem would rather I be completely dysfunctional than use this tool.”

Other Orthodox Jews are embracing these tools wholeheartedly, particularly those identified with Modern Orthodoxy, a broad movement that seeks to reconcile Jewish observance with modern life. Orthodox Union, a large US-based nonprofit, is developing AI-powered Torah Study apps that collate large amounts of revered texts for both professional scholars and curious laypeople. Orthodox Union’s Rabbi Simon Taylor discussed the organization’s reasoning behind developing an app: “We have to figure out how to leverage AI to teach more Torah and to spread Judaism. We knew that we weren’t going to get it right the first time and it wasn’t going to be perfect. We worked very, very hard on safeguarding and guardrails for everything that we do as well, lots and lots of testing.” Rabbi Zohar Atkins has a new venture called Yochai, a chatbot that offers AI study support for the Torah and Talmud. The study tool takes its name from the second-century sage Shimon bar Yochai and its logo is a carob tree—the plant that, according to legend, the sage sustained himself on as he hid in a cave for 13 years to escape Roman persecution.

On the other hand, Rabbi Danya Ruttenberg recently delineated a Jewish case for being exempted from using AI in the workplace. She argues that AI is theft, as large language models like ChatGPT culled their data from countless sources without permission or compensation. Halakha, Jewish law, forbids Jews from benefiting from stolen goods. Similarly, claiming authorship for something one didn’t write oneself also a form of theft—something rabbis using AI to craft sermons may find alarming. She also points to the destruction that data centers are wreaking on the environment, and invokes the ethical principle of Bal taschit, which God reveals to Moses in Deuteronomy 20:19: Do not destroy.

Still, it’s hard to imagine that the Haredim who take a hard-line stance against AI will form a political coalition with secular leftists who share their fears about the technology’s existential risks. After all, Haredim who oppose Zionism on religious grounds and occasionally protest in support of Palestine have not exactly reached across the aisle to outsiders to forge an alliance. Nevertheless, the resemblance between secular and traditionalist Orthodox oppositions to AI is uncanny: Both insist that hard work is its own reward, and abhor the way AI destroys the capacity for human cognition. Ameilut, says The Forward’s Louis Keene, is a mitzvah. Toiling is part of the process. “I’ve found that ameilut-maxxing translates pretty well to non-religious life, too. It’s an imperative to embrace the challenge,” he writes. In other words, nothing worth doing is easy.

Technology is reshaping how the devout practice their beliefs, and it’s changing how everyone interacts with the world. AI garners devotees, and seemingly alters reality—this kind of thing was once the province of religion. For some, this development is value-neutral, and for others it poses a deep threat. The collision of tech and theology brings up a myriad of uncomfortable questions. If we are made in God’s image, and AI is made in our image, where does that leave us?

Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy speaks during a rally with President Donald Trump in Youngstown, Ohio.

There are vile and amoral political actors who will stop at nothing to promote their venal interests—and then there’s Ohio GOP gubernatorial hopeful Vivek Ramaswamy. The biotech billionaire mounted a vanity 2024 presidential run to brand himself as a Gen Z mini-Trump and went on to codirect the nihilist DOGE assault on governance until Elon Musk’s more capacious ego muscled him aside. After both these self-branding efforts flatlined, Ramaswamy decided to emulate the career path of Vice President JD Vance, leveraging his sketchy venture-capitalist fortune into a run for statewide office in his native Ohio.

Ramaswamy has been in a tight and ugly contest with former state health commissioner Amy Acton that, against all odds, became exponentially more repulsive this week with the release of a digital ad featuring Acton’s estranged mother. At the age of 12, Acton and her younger brother had been removed from their mother’s home in 1978 after her stepfather, Cletis Arthur, was arrested and charged with the rape of his stepdaughter. Their biological father, Gerald Stearns, was granted custody of Acton and her brother; her mother, Donna, and her accused rapist then skipped out on bond. Acton told the Youngstown Vindicator that her last contact with her mom came in the courtroom where the custody case was held: “I went to give her a kiss, and she just turned her cheek away. That was the last time I ever saw her.”

I love my daughter, but when I saw her lying about her opponent—and it’s not easy for a mother to say this—but Amy has a serious problem. She is a liar. She lies about her past. She says she grew up living in a tent. None of that is true. She’s lied her entire life. My daughter needs help, and she can’t help you if she needs help herself.

When the ad surfaced as part of an online poll, it met with a proper wave of outrage and disbelief. A MAGA movement that’s long dismissed and excused sexual assault, up to and including President Donald Trump’s special pleading for the defendants in the Cornell gang-rape case, was now attacking the credibility of a 12-year-old rape victim. It’s a textbook illustration of the abusers’ defense tactic known as DARVO—deny, attack, and reverse victim and offender.

Only this sleazy instance of the practice was doubling as a political attack ad. Acton’s campaign responded with a statement from her brother, Philip Stearns, pointing out the obvious: “It’s not surprising that Vivek Ramaswamy has stooped to such a despicable low as to attempt to turn the sexual abuse Amy endured at twelve years old from our birth mother’s husband into a political talking point. After he was arrested and Amy and I were removed from their custody, our birth mother stayed married to Amy’s rapist until he died.” Acton’s campaign then released an ad in which her brother explained, “I experienced a lot of abuse, but Amy got the worst of it…. Shame on Vivek. It’s the contempt he has for Amy. It’s the contempt he has for Ohio. He’s not fit to lead.” Former GOP presidential candidate Nikki Haley and conservative pundit Meghan McCain echoed the same sentiment, and a host of other political figures have denounced the gutter attack.

But Ramaswamy, being Ramaswamy, is unfazed—indeed, after his campaign initially wouldn’t avow responsibility for the spot, it announced that it was spending $4 million to broadcast it during the homestretch of the race. (Ramaswamy’s campaign has so far spent more than $100 million, more than tripling outlays from Acton’s team.) His press flack, Connie Luck, continued playing up the smear, with a disingenuous disclaimer professing that “we don’t know what happened between Amy and her mother, who first spoke out about Amy in 2020,” and then parroting the mother’s attack: “We know that Amy Acton is a serial fabulist who has some serious problems with the truth that the public needs to be aware of before deciding who to entrust as governor.”

Any honest assessment of Ramaswamy’s prospects in the race’s final week and half should begin and end with the 2012 saga of Todd Aiken, the Missouri GOP Senate candidate brought down by his demented comments on how “legitimate rape” rarely produces pregnancies—and therefore its victims should be denied access to abortion.

Foley, the longtime director of the House page program, had long been rumored to have abused that post for sexually predatory ends. After a series of damning messages proving out those charges in unmistakable intimate detail surfaced, Foley resigned from Congress on September 29.

The Democrats had indeed sought to make Republican corruption a central issue in that midterm cycle—but until the Foley scandal broke, their pitch came across as too procedural and removed from the daily lives of most Americans to land effectively; it hinged mostly on Abramoff’s convoluted deals to represent Native American casino interests, and pay-to-play accords in the gilded precincts of the Beltway. But here was the Republican congressional leadership plainly covering up the actions of a sexual predator targeting idealistic young men under his direction. (The reasons for then–Speaker of the House Dennis Hastert’s dilatory handling of the Foley affair would later become painfully clear when he was also exposed as a serial sexual predator, who had exploited the high-school students in his charge when he was a high-school teacher and wrestling coach.)

It’s striking how many parallels this midterm cycle shares with 2006, from a Republican Party at a loss to distance itself from its president’s unpopular war in the Middle East, his mishandling of the economy, and his breathtaking record of corruption. But Ramaswamy’s blunder, like the earlier Foley debacle for the GOP, may well reinforce the party’s justly earned reputation as an enabler of sexual predation and an enthusiastic defender of the manifold abuses of women normalized by the online manosphere. Especially after the uprising over the horrific gang-rape at Cornell, which has led to the predictable doxing and defamation efforts from the online right, the bid to discredit a rape victim who was 12 years old when her predator was arrested looks pathological in the minds of fair-minded observers. It’s now up to Democratic leaders to make sure no one in the country is allowed to forget Vivek Ramaswamy’s moral depravity—or his ambition to be the Trump figure for the next generation of right-wing misogynists—between now and November 3.
