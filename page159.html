"People are tired of theater": The left sours on "performative" Trump impeachment

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Progressive House members and candidates are taking a stark turn away from what they describe as "performative" efforts to impeach President Trump.

Why it matters: This is a drastic shift in tone from Trump's first term, when progressives were some of the loudest voices in favor of impeachment.

  • Rep. Rashida Tlaib (D-Mich.) famously said of Trump in 2019, "We're gonna impeach the motherfucker," a line Republicans have routinely dredged up to dismiss impeachment efforts against the president as premeditated.
  • On Thursday, she told Axios in a statement: "No matter what happens in November, we'll still have a Trump administration for the next two years to hold accountable."

Driving the news: Likely incoming Democratic House members Darializa Avila Chevalier of New York and Melat Kiros of Colorado struck a similar tone in a recent podcast interview when asked about impeaching Trump.

  • "I support accountability, but ... that is something that Trump himself seems to want," Avila Chevalier said.
  • "My question is: Is that actually going to be meaningful accountability for the Trump administration?"
  • Said Kiros: "The accountability has to actually lead to some material changes."

What we're hearing: Rep. Delia Ramirez (D-Ill.), who has advocated a day-one impeachment, told Axios in an interview, "Impeachment can't just be a political messaging strategy."

  • "I think what Melat and Darializa are getting to is that people want to see justice and real accountability. ... We can't just tell people things that some would feel like is fluff," Ramirez said.
  • "I think the American people are tired of theater," said Adam Hamawy, another likely Democratic House member from New Jersey. He told Axios any impeachment proceedings need to be "a real process with substance, rather than just getting up there and just making a show of it."

Rep. Analilia Mejia (D-N.J.) said Democrats "should investigate the heads of these departments and bad actors across the board," but she is "less inclined to the performative and more inclined to identify what's impactful."

  • "The performative may feel nice, but in the end will not bear the kind of fruit that the American people deserve," she added.
  • Tlaib said the House Oversight Committee "must continue pressing for real accountability and consequences for this corrupt administration's crimes and abuses of taxpayers' dollars against our neighbors, public health, environment, safety and democracy."

The big picture: Democrats have had a tortured relationship with impeachment since Trump returned to office in 2025.

  • After impeaching the president twice in his first term only to see him acquitted both times by the Senate and then reelected in 2024, even the most ardently anti-Trump Democrats came into last year recoiling at the mere mention of another impeachment.
  • But they were buffeted by a grassroots base furious about the second Trump administration's precedent-shattering moves and demanding Democrats use every tool at their disposal to fight back.

By the numbers: When Rep. Al Green (D-Texas) tried to force a vote on impeaching Trump in June 2025, 128 of his fellow House Democrats voted with Republicans to block it.

  • By December, only 23 Democrats voted to block it.
  • When Green did it a third time last month, 18 Democrats voted to block it, while 147 voted with him to force the vote and 46, including party leaders, voted "present."

Yes, but: Green and Rep. Shri Thanedar (D-Mich.), who similarly championed impeaching Trump in his second term, have both lost their reelection bids this year.

  • Their losses came as the party base has turned away from anti-Trump #resistance crusaders in favor of more broadly anti-establishment, left-wing ideologues, as Axios previously reported.
  • These incoming progressives are expressing a view more in line with Democratic leaders like House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.) and Judiciary Committee ranking member Jamie Raskin (D-Md.): That impeachment shouldn't be a fait accompli.

What's next: While Trump may be off the board for now, his Cabinet certainly isn't.

  • The House could soon vote on an impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, triggered by Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) just before lawmakers left for October recess.
  • "I do think that holding Pete Hegseth [accountable] and looking into the ways in which our governing bodies have failed to meet the standards, the laws ... is all part of holding this government accountable," said Mejia.

The title of Pawel Pawlikowski’s extraordinary, compact drama Fatherland has a double meaning. In this somewhat fictionalized story of Thomas Mann’s brief 1949 visit to Germany, the Nobel Prize-winning author, after having lived in exile in California for 16 years, is challenged with finding his mooring in a country he can no longer call home; if it’s his fatherland, it long ago turned its back on him and his ideals. But fatherland can also be a state of mind where fathers live, a state both more specific and more elusive than fatherhood. In Pawlikowski’s telling of this portion of Mann’s story, it’s during this trip that Mann learns of the death of his son, Klaus—also a writer, though not as successful as his father—by suicide. If the elder Mann feels any emotion when he hears this news, it’s frozen inside him, a bubble of feeling isolated on its own ice floe. Fatherland, so secure in its rigid borders, is a cold, cold place.

Mann’s freedom from this interior island prison happens in the film’s last five minutes, a wordless reckoning that’s as quietly moving as anything you’ll see at the movies this year. But that moment wouldn’t hold so much power if Pawlikowski—with the help of his cowriter, Hendrik Handloegten—weren’t so skilled at sketching in the feathery details leading up to it. Pawlikowski is also the director of the 2013 Ida, as well 2018’s Cold War, pictures that, like this one, deftly balance postwar Eastern Bloc realities with the truth that all humans strive for connection and belonging. These aren’t dour movies, but serious, lustrous ones, less “political” pictures than movies about people trying to sort out what living really means.

At the beginning of Fatherland, no one—not even the aged Mann, played here in a quietly extraordinary performance by Hanns Zischler—has figured that out. The movie opens with a long-distance phone call: Mann’s semi-estranged son Klaus (August Diehl), living in Cannes and clearly in emotional distress, has reached out to his sister Erika—we’ll later realize it’s Sandra Hüller’s voice we hear on the other end of the line. Erika, an actress, writer, and onetime race-car driver, has become her father’s assistant, and she now urges Klaus to join her and their father on the trip they’re about to undertake, from California to the “new” barely postwar Germany. In the year of Goethe’s 200th birthday, Mann will be accepting two awards, one in Frankfurt, in the West, the other in the East, in Weimar: his justification is that his literary idol was born in one city and died in the other, though it’s obvious that Goethe couldn’t have imagined that chasm that would open between the two within 200 years' time.

Both cities are in transition, and in crisis, though neither wants to admit as much. In Frankfurt, a gray city crumbling at the corners and still finding its footing after the war, though somehow doing its best to pretend all is well, Mann and Erika are greeted by the mayor and hustled off to a press conference. There, the esteemed man himself answers the respectful but probing questions of journalists. Once they learn that he’ll also be traveling to the East, it’s not lost on them that doing so could make him a target at home in the States, where anti-Communist storm clouds have long been gathering. And there are other, more spiritual questions for him to consider. An American journalist, a woman who, it turns out, is an old friend of Erika’s and possibly a former lover (she’s played by Anna Madeley), asks him where he feels his true home is, now that he’s an American citizen. His answer is courtly but honest. He may live in the United States, he says, “but where my Heimat is, my home, is a much more difficult question.” He’s a man between states, both literally and figuratively.

In Weimar, the chief way to welcome a returning hero is apparently to greet him with a somber folk song sung by a choir dressed in dark clothing; at one point, he’s serenaded by a group of children singing the then-newly pened East German national anthem. Mann accepts it all gracefully, but there’s also something a bit quizzical and lost in his expression. He’s jarred by one alarming moment: a wild-eyed man appears at the hotel suite where he and Erika are staying, begging him to reveal in his speech that the nearby former concentration camp Buchenwald is now being used as a detention center for political prisoners. The man is whisked way by officials before he can finish his spiel, though it’s doubtful any of it has swayed Mann at all. In both the East and West, when it’s time to address the audiences who have eagerly gathered to hear his thoughts, he gives heartfelt speeches, anchored in the legacy of Goethe, about the power of art to transform and sustain not just individuals, but whole nations. He seems to truly believe, as he says at one point, that art doesn’t recognize borders—or at least, he truly wants to believe it.

Through all of this, Hüller’s Erika serves her father dutifully. When necessary, she translates his responses to journalists into fluid French; she attends cocktail parties in his honor, even when it means suffering the indignity of running into an oily, traitorous, Nazi-loving ex-husband she’d rather forget forever; she drives her father through the countryside, and though we can’t see her face as she does so, we wonder if this particular task gives her some joy, given her car-racing past. But through it all, because Hüller is such a subtly expressive actor, almost gossamer in the ways she shifts moods, we see the ways in which her resentments toward her father can’t help simmering to the surface. When she learns of her beloved brother’s death, her composure doesn’t crumple visibly—only somehow we know her emotional reserves are folding in and flattening like a house of cards. And when she sees how little this event seems to trouble her father, she can barely go through the motions of helping him on his mission, which, as he seems to envision it, is to save his homeland with his lofty views on art.

How these two find their way back to one another, and to a place of acknowledged and shared grief, is a kind of unspoken miracle that unfolds in last 15 minutes or so of this elegant, remarkably economical movie. Artistically and emotionally, Fatherland accomplishes more in its 82-minute runtime than any two-hour-plus movie released this year. Cinematographer Lukasz Zal, who also shot Ida and Cold War, renders Fatherland in the same satiny black and white, as well as the same serenely squarish, or Academy, aspect ratio. Every frame is spare, elegant, intimate, and the film moves along so gracefully that you’re barely prepared for how exhilarating, and how quietly devastating, its final moment is. At one point, Hüller’s Erika tells her father that Klaus had once said he felt Bach made him feel life was worth living. Her father shrugs that information off, but the final moments of Fatherland—incorporating a serendipitous conjuring, like a specter, of “Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring”—bring it back to him. Can a person have more than one Heimat? Or can that place of spiritual belonging shift over time? Perhaps Bach had the answer. Perhaps he put it in a piece of music, for us to find when we most need it, in a church or even in a movie.

‘Black October’: cybersecurity experts sound the alarm over new era of AI-enabled hacking

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After discovery of severe bug in the PHP programming language, executives warn of ‘vulnerability tsunami’

Leaders in cybersecurity are sounding the alarm after the discovery of a major vulnerability this week. They are predicting that artificial intelligence tools will expose more fundamental weaknesses in global internet infrastructure in the months to come.

“I no longer think we’re going to be able to patch all bugs,” said Sam Altman on Thursday evening, joining the chorus of executives. In the midst of what’s being called “Black October”, for which researchers used AI to help identify critical flaws in a coding language that is used across millions of websites and may have been unpatched for years, tech executives are warning about the ways AI will be used both to identify and exploit security vulnerabilities at an unprecedented scale and pace.

The next few months will see a “vulnerability tsunami”, according to Jason Clinton, Anthropic’s deputy chief information security officer. “There won’t be enough time to patch critical infrastructure,” Clinton said in a post on X on Thursday evening. Marc Benioff, the Salesforce CEO, agreed and called it a “code red”.

AI is uniquely able to find and identify security flaws in a way and at a pace that will feel catastrophic because the security community will not be able to move quickly enough to address them, said Matthew Prince, the Cloudflare CEO. “It’s going to feel like the sky is falling. It’s going to feel like there’s nothing we can do.

“The next two years are going to be incredibly, incredibly, incredibly fraught with new tools that have infinite patience to go look for vulnerabilities across the Internet,” he wrote in a post on X.

While cybersecurity experts have been warning about a “vulnpocalypse”, or the ability for AI to more easily identify security bugs for months, it wasn’t until “Black October” that researchers were able to use AI to find a flaw as fundamental and critical as the one announced on 21 October.

Massive bugs were found in a programming language called PHP, which may have left the millions of websites that run on it vulnerable to hackers for years. Some experts estimated 70% of all the sites on the internet were at risk of hacking.

However, in the same way AI will be used to aid in identifying security vulnerabilities at an unprecedented pace, tech leaders worry it’ll also be used by malicious actors to exploit those bugs at an unprecedented pace.

Attempts to exploit the PHP vulnerability have already begun.

After the vulnerabilities were announced, government and private websites across the US and UK temporarily took down their sites to patch the bugs. The US National Security Agency (NSA) issued an alert recommending any site that runs on PHP implement the patches immediately. Malicious cyberactors may seek to further exploit these bugs to access servers without authorization, the NSA said.

“Such an access vector could help attackers steal sensitive data, disrupt services or move deeper into a network,” the warning read.

“That jumped to tens of thousands in the 45 minutes post announcement, hundreds of thousands within 2 hours, and millions now,” he wrote.

A Tourist Spotted Strange Footprints in Thailand. They Were 120 Million Years Old, Made by a Giant Dinosaur

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Facebook photo led researchers to six ancient footprints left by a giant carnivorous dinosaur.

Pichai Prathetsena was exploring a forest stream with his family in northeastern Thailand when he noticed something peculiar in the rock beneath his feet. Three long toes seemed to emerge from the stone, forming an impression far too large for any known land animal.

Curious, he photographed the strange markings and posted them in a Thai Facebook group called “What is this creature?”

It didn’t take long to get an answer. Pichai had discovered an extremely rare dinosaur footprint.

On October 20, just three days after Prathetsena’s discovery, Thailand’s Department of Mineral Resources confirmed that the markings were 120-million-year-old dinosaur footprints. And there wasn’t just one. Investigators found six impressions forming a continuous trail left by a large, meat-eating dinosaur.

The discovery, made in the Phu Hin Chom That–Phu Phrabat National Park area of Udon Thani province, is now officially the first confirmed carnivorous dinosaur tracksite in the province.

The footprints belong to a theropod, a group of predominantly carnivorous dinosaurs that walked on two legs and included formidable predators such as Tyrannosaurus rex. The exact species, however, cannot be determined from the footprints alone.

Each three-toed impression measures approximately 29 centimeters (11 inches) long and 26 centimeters (10 inches) wide. The tracks are preserved in fine-grained sandstone belonging to the Phu Phan Formation, which dates to the Early Cretaceous period.

Based on their dimensions and spacing, researchers estimate the animal stood approximately 1.42 meters (4.7 feet) tall at the hip and measured 6.3 meters (21 feet) from head to tail — about as long as a large delivery van, with hips nearly as high as the roof of a typical car.

The footprints were separated by about one meter (aprox. three feet), with a stride length of approximately 2.2 meters, according to Thailand’s Department of National Parks.

Local paleontologists compared the animal’s estimated size with Siamotyrannus isanensis, a large carnivorous dinosaur previously discovered in Thailand. However, the exact dinosaur species remains undeterminable.

The ancient landscape also looked rather different when the footprints were made in mud, and then some sudden event preserved them, leaving the impression locked in hard minerals over the course of millions of years. Investigators believe the area was once a waterside environment with relatively gentle flowing water.

Unlike fossilized bones, which tell you what a dinosaur looked like, footprints offer paleontologists evidence of prehistoric animals in motion. Their shape and spacing can help reconstruct how dinosaurs walked, how quickly they moved and, when multiple trackways occur together, potentially whether they traveled in groups.

Thailand has a surprisingly rich history of such discoveries. The country’s first documented dinosaur footprints emerged in the 1980s at Phu Luang Wildlife Sanctuary. That site preserves 15 three-toed impressions attributed to large theropods, according to a 2026 scientific review of Thailand’s prehistoric footprints.

At the moment, authorities are most concerned with protecting the trackway. The Thai government has temporarily closed the discovery area while specialists assess how to prevent damage from visitors and water erosion.

The goal is to ultimately develop the location into an educational and paleontological tourism site.

The Trump administration sued Alaska on Thursday to delay certification of election results if incumbent Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan loses.

Why it matters: The race between incumbent Sullivan, Democrat Mary Peltola, and retired teacher Dan Sullivan has been marked by controversy, with Republicans accusing Democrats of trying to confuse voters by planting a candidate with the same name.

  • Alaska's use of ranked-choice voting, which eliminates candidates in rounds if no one gets 50% of the initial vote, complicates matters.
  • "An election result may be impacted by voter confusion if: (1) Senator Sullivan does not win the election; and (2) had all votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan been cast instead for Senator Sullivan and Alaska's rank-choice process played out accordingly, Senator Sullivan would have won the election," the lawsuit, filed in Alaska District Court, states.

Zoom in: Because of a previous Alaska Supreme Court decision and Sullivan's name already being on the ballot, the administration contended in the lawsuit that the only means to ensure that the winner of the race is voters' choice "is to ensure that the certification is not impacted" by his candidacy.

  • The lawsuit requests a delay in certification until after an investigation determines "whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan."

What they're saying: "Alaska's Division of Elections determined that Mr. Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy was intended to mislead voters and compromise the fairness of the ballot," a DOJ spokesperson said in a statement.

  • "Therefore, the Justice Department, consistent with the National Voter Registration Act enacted by Congress, is taking the necessary steps to ensure that this frivolous candidacy does not undermine the integrity of Alaska's election or the will of its voters."
  • The White House referred Axios to the DOJ.

President Trump told reporters Friday that he told the DOJ to file the suit against Alaska.

  • He then explained the situation to Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, who stood beside him, and then called Daniel J. Sullivan "the fake" candidate.

Catch up quick: The retired teacher Sullivan will appear on the ballot as Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. after the state's Supreme Court ordered the state's Division of Elections to include him in the race in June.

  • Senator Sullivan will appear as "Sullivan, Dan S. (Registered Republican) Incumbent," per the state's sample ballots.
  • Sullivan has argued his candidacy is not a sham, and Peltola's campaign has denied any involvement with his run.

The intrigue: The race is among those being closely watched as Democrats appear to be making inroads in red territory.

  • The administration acknowledged the tight race in its lawsuit, saying "there is a realistic chance" that the non-incumbent Sullivan's presence on the ballot could tip the scales for Peltola.

Flashback: In the primary, Senator Sullivan collected around 41% of the vote, versus Sullivan's 2.5%. Peltola earned 49.5%

  • Cook Political Report currently rates the race as a toss-up.

Reality check: Notre Dame law professor Derek Muller tells AP that the Alaska suit won't survive long, since the National Voter Registration Act — the statute cited by the DOJ in the suit — "simply doesn't let the Department of Justice enjoin certification."

  • "The lawsuit could well be mooted by a wide Democratic victory or a narrow Republican one, and if not, the court will likely dismiss it next month," he said.

NASA’s Artemis III Mission to Test Future Moon Lander Operations

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Before astronauts return to the lunar surface, NASA will first conduct one of the most ambitious and complex test missions in the agency’s history.

Launching in 2027, Artemis III will send NASA astronauts Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio, Andre Douglas, and ESA (European Space Agency) astronaut Luca Parmitano aboard the Orion spacecraft into low Earth orbit to perform a series of demonstrations with test versions, or test articles, of human landing systems being developed by industry partners Blue Origin and SpaceX. During the planned 14-day mission, crews and flight controllers will practice the rendezvous, docking, habitation, and operational procedures that will be essential when Artemis astronauts begin landing near the Moon’s South Pole in 2028. The mission is designed to reduce risk, validate hardware, and gain operational experience before NASA attempts a crewed lunar landing.

“Every piece of this mission in Earth orbit is designed to reduce risk before we put astronauts back on the Moon,” said Jeremy Parsons, NASA’s Artemis program manager. “We’re launching three spacecraft on three different rockets and testing how they operate together — something NASA has never attempted. Every lesson we learn here gets us closer to landing on the Moon in 2028.”

First stop: Blue Origin’s lunar lander test article

Earlier Artemis missions used only NASA’s SLS (Space Launch System) rocket and Orion spacecraft. Artemis III will involve three separate spacecraft launched on three different heavy-lift rockets within a relatively short period of time.

The mission begins with Blue Origin launching a test version of its Blue Moon Mark 2 lander into a circular low Earth orbit approximately 273 miles (237 nautical miles) above Earth. The spacecraft can remain on orbit for up to 30 days providing schedule flexibility. Artemis III is designed as a tightly choreographed campaign, with NASA planning to launch Orion on top of SLS from the agency’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida only days after the Blue Moon test article launch and confirmation the vehicle is healthy and ready for joint operations.

Following launch, Orion will initially enter an elliptical Earth orbit of 18 x 1122 miles (16 x 975 nautical miles). It will use its service module to perform a series of maneuvers to transition to a circular orbit at about 345 miles (300 nautical miles) for thermal stabilization before then gradually setting up its rendezvous with the waiting Blue Moon lander test article. Because each spacecraft operates in slightly different orbital regimes optimized for its mission requirements, Artemis III requires a carefully choreographed sequence of orbital maneuvers and rendezvous operations throughout the flight.

During the first two days of flight, the crew will check out Orion’s systems, prepare for proximity operations, and gradually position themselves for the mission’s first major objective: rendezvous and docking with the Blue Moon test vehicle.

The operation represents NASA’s first opportunity to evaluate how Orion and a commercial lunar lander work together in space. Engineers will assess how software aboard both spacecraft, guidance systems, communications systems, and operational procedures perform in real-world conditions, while flight controllers gain experience coordinating activities across multiple teams and organizations.

Once docked, astronauts will spend approximately two days conducting evaluations aboard the Blue Moon test article. Crew members will open the hatch and enter the spacecraft, allowing them to assess the cabin environment, controls, and life-support systems firsthand. Teams will verify the spacecraft’s ability to maintain the proper atmosphere and pressure.

The Blue Moon test article incorporates many of the avionics, software, flight-control systems, crew-cabin technologies, and life-support functions planned for future lunar missions. Testing those systems in orbit will provide the first opportunity to evaluate how many of the lander’s flight systems perform in space as an integrated vehicle.

By the time Orion undocks, NASA will have completed its first evaluation of a commercial lunar lander with crew, gathering data on spacecraft systems, crew operations, and spacecraft interoperability not possible on the ground.

After completing operations with Blue Origin’s lander, Orion will undock and reposition to support the next phase of the mission: a second rendezvous operation.

SpaceX will launch a Starship Version 3 test article from Florida. Because Starship is designed for a much shorter on-orbit lifetime than Blue Origin’s lander, launch occurs later in the mission, allowing NASA to efficiently sequence the two docking demonstrations. Starship will enter a lower orbit at 206 nautical miles, optimized for communications coverage, and will carry an added docking system mounted at the forward end.

Orion will rendezvous and dock with the spacecraft during a test expected to last up to two hours. Crew will not enter Starship during this test. The demonstration is focused on validating how Orion and Starship interact as a docked system in space. NASA and SpaceX plan to evaluate spacecraft controllability, communications, integrated software performance, and the dynamics associated with docking and undocking operations.

The docking itself also presents a unique test, using different docking geometries. Orion will connect to a port beside Blue Moon’s crew cabin, then later align its forward docking system with the one at Starship’s nose. Testing both approaches during a single mission gives NASA an opportunity to assess different operational concepts while gathering valuable engineering data.

Together, the two docking campaigns will demonstrate how Orion interfaces with two very different commercial lander architectures during a single mission.

Even after docking activities are complete, Artemis III will continue generating data that supports future missions.

Once the Starship demonstration is complete, Orion and its crew will have up to five additional days available in orbit before beginning preparations for the journey home. NASA plans to use this time to complete a variety of test objectives that support future Artemis missions, including additional evaluations of the Orion Crew Survival System suits, docking sensor tests, acoustic and thermal measurements, human-factors assessments, and a range of science and technology demonstrations.

Several investigations led by NASA will build on studies conducted during Artemis II, collecting additional data that will help inform future deep-space missions. The crew also will conduct a proof-of-concept study examining how astronauts could perform health-related assessments when they are unable to communicate directly with mission control, an important capability for future lunar surface expeditions and other missions farther from Earth.

NASA also plans to fly a cellular biology experiment during the mission while researchers use the flight to test concepts for returning lunar samples to Earth. Teams will evaluate how future samples could be transported, received, and moved into curation facilities after landing, while also conducting assessments of Orion’s environment to better understand potential contamination considerations for future lunar missions.

The mission also will include Earth and solar observations that help astronauts continue developing critical observation skills while taking advantage of the spacecraft’s unique orbital vantage point. Plant seeds will fly aboard Orion as well, providing opportunities for scientific study and educational engagement following the mission.

Mission planners have also built flexibility into the flight timeline, providing additional opportunities to complete test objectives depending on how operations unfold in real time.

When ready, Orion will begin phasing for return, and the crew will configure the cabin for reentry and splashdown, which will occur in the Pacific Ocean, off the coast of California. Orion and its crew will be recovered by NASA personnel and the United States Navy.

Every activity performed during Artemis III supports a larger goal: reducing risk before astronauts return to the lunar surface.

The mission will provide NASA with experience operating alongside multiple commercial partners while managing complex rendezvous and docking operations involving three different spacecraft. Engineers will gain insight into spacecraft interoperability, mission-control coordination, communications architecture, crew operations, and hardware performance.

By the time Artemis III concludes with Orion’s splashdown on Earth, NASA will have demonstrated technologies and operational concepts that future lunar crews will rely on as they travel to and from the Moon.

Most importantly, Artemis III will provide the operational experience needed to transform plans for future lunar landings into proven flight procedures. The lessons learned throughout the mission will help refine future exploration plans, strengthen partnerships with industry, and ensure astronauts are prepared for humanity’s next giant leap at the Moon.

As part of a Golden Age of innovation and exploration, NASA will send Artemis astronauts on increasingly difficult missions near the lunar South Pole to explore more of the Moon than ever before. In addition to providing economic benefits and advancing scientific discovery through its Moon missions, NASA will establish an enduring human presence on the lunar surface and build on the agency’s foundation for the first crewed mission to Mars.

NASA explores the unknown in air and space, innovates for the benefit of humanity, and inspires the world through discovery.

Could GTA VI Sick Leave Really Cost the US Economy Over $1 Billion?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Millions are rearranging their work days for one video game.

November 19 sounds like pretty much any other day. But on November 19, something unusual might happen to America’s workforce. Offices could be emptier, sick days could mysteriously multiply, and some employees working from home might find themselves considerably more interested in stealing virtual cars than answering emails.

There’s a new bug going around. The GTA bug.

We’re talking, of course, about Grand Theft Auto VI, a video game so anticipated that some estimates suggest 1.5 million Americans could skip work to play it, potentially costing the US economy $1 billion in a single day.

It sounds ridiculous. But after 13 years of waiting for a new mainline Grand Theft Auto game, fans are making vacation plans around its release.

For anyone who hasn’t played Grand Theft Auto, the premise is straightforward: imagine being dropped into an enormous, detailed version of an American city and given permission to cause almost unlimited chaos. You can steal cars, pull off elaborate heists, flee the police, or simply drive around listening to the radio. The games are part crime drama, part satire of American culture, and part digital playground.

The upcoming GTA VI takes players back to Vice City, Rockstar’s fictional version of Miami, and the surrounding state of Leonida, inspired by Florida. The story follows two criminals, Jason and Lucia, caught up in a conspiracy.

It’s difficult to overstate the anticipation. The previous installment, GTA V, came out in September 2013, when the PlayStation 4 hadn’t even launched. According to publisher Take-Two Interactive, the game generated more than $1 billion in worldwide sales in just three days. Now, after multiple delays, Rockstar has scheduled GTA VI for Thursday, November 19, 2026.

And Thursday, unfortunately for employers, is usually a working day.

Please check your inbox and confirm your subscription.

A September report from Money found that requests for paid time off on November 19 were already running 9% to 17% above historical averages for the equivalent Thursday, according to data provided by human resources platform BambooHR. Of course, it’s hard to prove GTA is to blame, but the timing is suspicious.

One California automotive parts company, Burger Motorsports, even announced a tongue-in-cheek operational pause around the launch, warning customers that employees might be unavailable because they’re busy exploring Vice City. Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick also half jokingly predicted that plenty of people would call in sick on November 19.

The latest survey suggests he might have a point.

On October 20, gaming and media company GameSquare released research involving 1,000 full-time employed American gamers.

The results were striking. Ten percent said they planned to skip work on launch day, while another 30% were considering taking time off. Among those considering it, 36% said they might call in sick. Meanwhile, 42% expected the game’s arrival could make them less productive at work.

Of course, these weren’t percentages of all American workers. They came from a sample of people who already play video games, surveyed by a company with a commercial interest in gaming culture. And saying you’ll take a day off is not the same as actually doing it.

But a lot of things are saying November 19 won’t exactly be the most productive day in American history.

The claim appears in a July 2025 report by Spanish broadcaster Cadena SER, which discussed the game’s potential economic consequences with José García Montalvo, an economics professor at Pompeu Fabra University in Barcelona.

The broadcaster suggested that absenteeism could cost the American economy around $1 billion on the game’s first day. Montalvo discussed why younger workers, particularly men aged 18 to 30, and people working remotely in technology might be especially tempted to stay home or spend less time working.

As it so often happens, the claim got spread massively. But it’s not exactly a careful calculation; it’s more like a specialist’s estimate.

One widely circulated explanation assumes 1.5 million absent workers, each producing about $70,000 of economic output annually on average.

But divide that $70,000 across approximately 250 working days, and each worker produces about $280 a day. Multiply that by 1.5 million people, and you get roughly $420 million, not $1 billion.

So, it’s reasonable to expect significant losses on account of GTA being launched, but it’s hard to come up with an accurate prediction.

But this is where the economics gets even more interesting.

According to the Entertainment Software Association’s 2026 report, approximately 212 million Americans play video games at least an hour a week. The average player is 37 years old. And video games really can change how people decide to spend their time.

In a study published in the Journal of Political Economy, economists Mark Aguiar, Mark Bils, Kerwin Kofi Charles and Erik Hurst examined changes in leisure activities and working hours among young American men.

Using time-use surveys and economic modelling, they investigated a simple question: what happens when entertainment becomes significantly more appealing?

Their findings suggested that improvements in recreational computing, including video gaming, had reduced working hours among men aged 21 to 30 by around 2% over the period examined.

But that figure is often misunderstood or misquoted

The researchers weren’t saying that a new game (say, GTA) would cut national productivity by 2%. They were studying longer-term changes in how a particular group of people divided their time between paid work and leisure.

When entertainment becomes better, free time becomes more valuable. A person might prefer an extra evening gaming with friends over working additional hours, just as someone else might choose more time with family or a shorter working week.

GTA VI is so big and so intensely expected that it adds the sense of a shared cultural moment. GTA is a cultural phenomenon. And there’s something faintly absurd about describing a day spent doing something enjoyable and cultural as a national economic catastrophe.

GDP, the standard measure of economic output, tells us how much an economy produces. It doesn’t tell us whether people enjoyed producing it, or whether they would have preferred to do something else.

The OECD explicitly recognises that GDP doesn’t capture everything that matters to people’s lives, including work-life balance and wellbeing. I’m not saying productivity is irrelevant or that employers shouldn’t care about staff shortages. It’s just that the economic value of work and the personal value of free time are different things.

There are plenty of perfectly ordinary reasons to take a vacation day: attend a wedding, visit family, travel somewhere new, or simply rest. Spending the day playing a video game is just another choice, and one that will likely make many people happy.

Perhaps the most remarkable thing about GTA VI isn’t how much money it might cost employers. It’s that it’s convinced millions of people how they’d want to spend their Thursday.
