Shaun Wright-Phillips has become the fourth celebrity to exit this year's Strictly Come Dancing, leaving the competition with his professional partner Alexis Warr.

The former England footballer and Warr were defeated in the dance-off by actor Jaime Winstone and Carlos Gu.

Winstone and Gu had finished in the bottom two after the public vote, despite scoring 25 for their dance on Saturday's episode of the BBC show.

Sunday's results show also brought a surprise announcement: Strictly's first triple dance-off, which will take place during next week's Halloween episode.

The three couples with the lowest combined judges' scores and public votes will dance again next week, before the judges eliminate one pair.

Strictly will return to a two-couple dance-off for the rest of the series.

In the dance-off, Wright-Phillips and Warr reprised their jive to “Faith” by Stevie Wonder featuring Ariana Grande, while Winstone and Gu performed their foxtrot to “Man I Need” by Olivia Dean again.

Judges Craig Revel Horwood and Motsi Mabuse voted to save Gu and Winstone.

Head judge Shirley Ballas voted for Wright-Phillips and Warr, leaving Du Beke, who had this week's casting vote, to decide who remained on the show.

The pair had ended up in the dance-off despite finishing above former Love Island star Cach Mercer and his professional partner Maddie Ingoldsby, who scored 24.

Zookeeper is fighting for her life after being mauled by a leopard at Netherlands park she had worked at since she was 17

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published: 19:58 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 20:23 GMT, 25 October 2026

A zookeeper is fighting for her life after being mauled by a leopard at a Netherlands wildlife park she has been employed at since she was 17.

The woman - who has not been named but is in her fifties - was attacked after entering the enclosure of two male Persian leopards at Safaripark Beekse Bergen in Hilvarenbeek on Saturday morning.

She was found by colleagues who heard her on their walkie-talkies crying out for help.

The woman, who has worked at the park since she was a teenager, was then helicoptered to hospital with severe head and neck injuries and immediately operated on.

Despite the operation being successful, the woman remains in a coma, with her condition described as 'worrying' by Martin van Hees, head of animal care at Safaripark Beekse Bergen.

Mr van Hees said: 'At this moment, the woman is in the hospital and is undergoing surgery; her condition is worrying. We know there is a risk when working with dangerous animals, but you never want to experience something like this.'

A zookeeper is fighting for her life after being mauled by a leopard at Safaripark Beekse Bergen in the Netherlands (Pictured: paramedics at the scene)

Safaripark Beekse Bergen in Hilvarenbeek houses two Persian leopards. It is not clear which leopard was involved in the attack

Mr van Hees also confirmed the leopard involved in the attack will not be put to sleep.

The zoo houses two Persian leopards, which due to Storm Austen were being kept indoors at the time of the attack. It is not yet clear, however, which attacked the zookeeper.

Mr van Hees said: 'We are not going to do anything with the leopard. It just sat in its enclosure and did what it did. The animal cannot help it.

'In principle, the enclosures are separated for humans and animals. In this case, however, something went differently than it should have.

'She entered the enclosure while the leopards could also get there, and as a result, she was caught. Her immediate colleague heard the cry for help and rushed to her aid.'

A spokesperson for the wildlife park added: 'We are naturally deeply saddened by this news and are doing everything we can to support her family, colleagues, and other loved ones as much as possible and to provide additional guidance where necessary.'

The attack comes just weeks after 24-year-old William Chapman was mauled to death by a tiger at Yorkshire Wildlife Park.

The former child actor was killed after climbing into an enclosure housing a 200kg Amur tiger named Altai at the Doncaster attraction.

The attack comes just weeks after William Chapman (pictured) was mauled to death by a tiger at Yorkshire Wildlife Park near Doncaster

Altai, the 200kg Amur tiger which fatally mauled Mr Chapman, is pictured in his enclosure following the attack

Paramedics and an air ambulance had rushed to the wildlife park shortly after 1.35pm on October 10, but Mr Chapman was pronounced dead at the scene.

Just minutes before the fatal attack, he was seen dancing on a raised walkway above the tiger enclosure while listening to music through his AirPods.

Witnesses said he asked a visitor to take photographs of him with the animal in the background before climbing over two fences to enter the enclosure.

Mr Chapman subsequently posted a final video to Instagram showing himself approaching the tiger.

A post-mortem examination provisionally recorded his cause of death as 'multiple traumatic injuries'.

His family have described him as a 'bright, warm and engaging young man' who had struggled with significant mental illness over several years.

Macron accused of putting France’s right to protest at risk with ‘vandal’s law’

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Making innocent protesters liable for damage would be bad for rule of law and play into far-right hands, say opponents

Emmanuel Macron has been accused by trade unions of trying to curb the fundamental French right to protest as the government fast-tracks a bill to make protesters pay for damage caused by vandalism at street demonstrations even if they did not directly cause it themselves.

The French president, who leaves office next spring, has faced more than a month of protests in which high-school students have blockaded schools demanding a response to teacher absences, repairs to decrepit buildings and changes to the university applications platform.

Trade unions and gilets jaunes (yellow vest) anti-government protesters are now joining sustained street demonstrations in a growing social crisis over schools, as well as the cost of living and public services. Authorities have increased security measures and placed restrictions on demonstrating after France saw one of its worst days of violence in years earlier this month when largely peaceful demonstrations were marred by looting, fires and vandalism of state buildings.

The senate will this week debate the government’s proposed “vandals pay” law. The legislation would mean that a person protesting could be held liable to pay up to €10,000 for damage and vandalism even if they did not directly cause it themselves, if they were continuing to demonstrate nearby or as part of the same group as perpetrators. The bill also provides for greater use of drones over protests.

The prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, said the bill, which was originally prepared in the wake of violent clashes after Paris Saint-Germain’s victory over Arsenal in the football Champions League in May, was “absolutely essential” after looting and burning during recent demonstrations across France.

The bill comes as the government struggles to contain the current crisis. Marine Le Pen, of the far-right, anti-immigration National Rally (RN), has seen her frontrunner status in next spring’s presidential election boosted as she seeks to reinforce her law-and-order credentials and back police.

Le Pen, who has lodged an appeal to France’s highest court over her conviction for embezzlement of European parliament funds, launched her presidential campaign on Sunday at a rally in Orléans, promising to “bring back order to France”.

The RN has claimed the “vandals pay” bill was originally its idea and Le Pen said she would ensure damage to property was paid for by rioters. Le Pen told a cheering crowd of more than 8,000 people at the rally: “Vandals will pay and if it takes their whole life to pay, then they will spend their whole life paying.”

She said: “We are going to restore the rule of law. Who can claim that there is rule of law when hordes come to ransack and burn our buildings?”

Éric Coquerel, from Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s radical left La France Insoumise, said on Sunday that the “vandals pay” law “would mean the end of street demonstrations” in France because people would not want to take the risk of being held responsible and made to pay up to €10,000 for vandalism they had not committed. The Communist senator Fabien Gay told the parliamentary TV channel Public Sénat: “No demonstration could take place again in our country.”

In an open letter to Le Monde, leftwing trade unions and France’s Human Rights League wrote: “This unfair law, which is a serious attack on the freedom to demonstrate, should be abandoned, or risk a major step back for the rule of law.”

At this weekend’s protests there was widespread anger at Macron for banning non-union demonstrations that his government deemed “illegitimate” and for keeping all protesters away from city centres.

Although Macron has not put in place a state of emergency, the government and local authorities have introduced limits on demonstrating, including bans on gatherings in front of schools and demonstrating in city centres, as well as curfews in some places and extended periods of police custody for the record number of people arrested.

At demonstrations this weekend, many said they had come out to defend the French right to protest in the street. Street protests in France, which are seen as a fundamental way for people to make themselves heard by a centralised state, have regularly been used to express criticism of changes to the law, from pension changes to same-sex marriage.

Serge Slama, a professor of public law at Université Grenoble Alpes, told Telerama magazine: “Although a state of emergency has been ruled out [by Emmanuel Macron] at this point, we have seen a number of decrees bringing a wide restriction on public liberties.”

He said this was “unprecedented” and would benefit the far right if they won elections next year, allowing them to put in place further crackdowns without changing the law.

Lecornu will meet representatives of protesting high-schoolers on Monday as the government comes under pressure to respond to their demands with funding and school repairs in order to avoid further school blockades.

Cabinet ministers back Burnham’s call for debate on return to free movement with EU

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

PM’s plea supported by Angela Rayner, Ed Miliband and business leaders, while some ‘red wall’ MPs voice concern

Andy Burnham’s call for the UK to consider a return to free movement with Europe has been backed by senior cabinet ministers but risked a rift with “red wall” Labour MPs, who say it could jeopardise Brexit-voting seats.

The prime minister made his plea for an “honest discussion” about freedom of movement in an interview with the Sunday Times, making clear that staying out of it would no longer be a red line in talks with the EU.

Burnham also revealed his belief that rejoining the single market and accepting free movement could be done without the need for another referendum, if Labour put it in their manifesto. In contrast, he said fully rejoining would need another in-out poll.

The prime minister said: “Freedom of movement had very significant challenges associated with it and I spoke about them in my previous time in parliament. But, at the same time, it was a type of immigration that perhaps wasn’t as difficult [as now].

“Revolving-door migration where people would come and then go back is different. The balance of migration has changed, hasn’t it, since then? I think we’ve just got to have an honest discussion about those things.”

His remarks were supported by Angela Rayner, the housing secretary, as well as Stella Creasy, the chair of the Labour Movement for Europe. However, Shabana Mahmood, the home secretary, is among those Labour MPs who have previously been more cautious about closer ties with Europe, while a number of backbenchers with heavily pro-Brexit seats in the north and Midlands are wary about reopening the arguments for free movement or rejoining the EU.

Rayner told the BBC’s Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg she would “like to have an honest conversation about what the trade-offs could be, and then put that to the people at a general election”, and Creasy, the Labour MP for Walthamstow, said Burnham was right that the UK was “long overdue for a grownup conversation about what EU freedom of movement actually means”.

A number of senior cabinet ministers are keen for the UK to form a much closer relationship with the EU to help boost growth, potentially involving a return to the single market and accepting free movement as a result. Those in favour of reopening the discussion include Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary.

At a New Statesman event during the Labour party conference in September, however, Mahmood said rejoining the EU would not be a “simple, straightforward thing” and there were “quite hard trade-offs”, such as eliminating trade friction without “some of the burdens that also brings”.

One senior minister under Keir Starmer said the reopening of the debate on free movement was “brave” given how divisive it had been in the Brexit years. “It’s a question of timing. Public opinion is of a place where you can start to have this debate,” he said. “Theresa May, Rishi Sunak, Keir Starmer: what they were all trying to do was get as close as possible without freedom of movement. There is now the a possibility of being brave and making the case on single market and free movement without going for rejoin.”

Labour MPs with pro-Brexit seats have expressed scepticism about rejoining the bloc, including Jonathan Hinder, the MP for Pendle, who recently pressed the prime minister in parliament to rule out a return to free movement.

One red wall MP said they thought debating free movement was the right thing to do but many colleagues in the north-east, especially in coastal areas, had begun to raise serious concerns. “These MPs are definitely raising the alarm,” they said. “Andy may have to work out if he can win without them, which is brutal but true.” Another Labour MP from a Brexit-voting area said it would “undoubtedly put seats at risk to Reform” but that Burnham was making an argument from a position of strength, having taken the party to higher poll ratings in recent weeks.

Worries were brought up publicly on Sunday by Richard Walker, the Labour peer and executive chair of Iceland, who told Times Radio that restarting the debate would be a “dangerous precedent” and that opening the borders would not help the “1 million young people that are out of work”.

In his party conference speech last month, Burnham fired the starting gun on the argument about rejoining the EU as one potential route, but his latest comments suggest he appears to be favouring the option that would avoid a referendum.

Although Labour’s manifesto pledges no return to free movement and the single market, Burnham signalled it could still be up for discussion at next month’s EU summit.

Burnham’s comments at the weekend drew an immediate response from the right of politics, with the Conservatives and Reform UK criticising the idea of reopening the debate. Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, said the idea of reopening free movement was “another example of Burnham’s foot-in-mouth syndrome”.

Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, called it a “total betrayal of Brexit Britain and his Makerfield pitch”.

“He is also deluded to say immigration was different when we were members of the EU. We will fight this dishonesty,” he added.

Polling suggests voters in the UK are increasingly open to rejoining the EU or a single market arrangement involving free movement. The concept of free movement was that British and EU citizens could move more freely for jobs within the bloc, which also shared a single market for goods and services.

Luke Tryl, a polling and focus groups expert at the More in Common thinktank, said on Sunday that people tended to be less concerned about free movement given years of high non-EU migration but would be more exercised about EU budget contributions or joining the euro.

During the Brexit referendum, the topic was hotly debated, with the leave campaign arguing that the UK would be able to take control of its borders if it voted to exit the EU. However, in the years that followed, net migration rose to a peak of 944,000 in the year ending March 2023 under a new points-based system, up from 330,000 before the Brexit vote.

Burnham’s decision to open the debate on free movement was welcomed by some businesses, which have grown frustrated at the lack of progress on the issue, and on reducing the extra paperwork required to do business with EU customers since Brexit – both seen as obstacles to faster economic growth.

The British Chambers of Commerce lobby group called for the Treasury to analyse the various economic options for different forms of EU-UK relationships. Most economic studies have concluded that a closer EU relationship would benefit the UK economy.

William Bain, head of trade policy at the BCC, said the common understanding strategy agreed by Keir Starmer in May 2025 was “approaching the ceiling of what can be achieved”.

This week’s budget is expected to underscore the damage to the economy from Brexit, including from a drop in net migration. The Resolution Foundation, a thinktank, said that if migration falls by 300,000 over the next five years it would translate to a hit to government tax revenues of £4.7bn in 2029-30, reducing chancellor John Healey’s room for manoeuvre.

The Institute for Fiscal Studies, another thinktank, estimated that lower net migration estimates would reduce the chancellor’s “headroom” by between £1bn and £5bn by 2031-32, depending on how the government’s Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) chooses to model the expected reductions in population.

The OBR has previously estimated that Brexit barriers to trade and free movement have caused a long-term hit of about 4% of UK output.

After almost a year away, Venezuelan opposition figure Maria Corina Machado has returned to her home country following a series of unsuccessful attempts that strained her relationship with US President Donald Trump's administration.

"I'm here. With you all," the Nobel Peace Prize-winner posted on X on Sunday.

Footage shared by her press team showed her appearing at a church in the western state of Lara, where bystanders gathered to sing the national anthem and shout "liberty!" as she departed in an SUV, according to a Reuters witness.

Her staff stated she will travel overland to Caracas.

Her attempts to re-enter the country have tested relations with the Trump administration, which she sought to cultivate as an ally prior to the removal of President Nicolas Maduro from power in January.

U.S. officials had asked her at least once to postpone her return, Reuters reported in June, following several failed entry efforts. While her team did not reveal the details of her journey, two people with knowledge of the matter said she took a private flight from Panama to Colombia before crossing overland into Venezuela.

Trump has consistently backed interim President Delcy Rodriguez, the former deputy to ousted President Nicolas Maduro, while praising business ventures and oil production agreements made by US firms in the country.

In September, Trump remarked that it was too soon to discuss democratic elections in Venezuela.

At the White House on Sunday, Trump stated he was unaware of Machado's arrival and suggested Washington would maintain its support for Rodriguez.

"She's a very nice woman," he said of Machado. "But we're doing really well in Venezuela."

In a statement regarding her return, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said: "The conditions allowing for the safe return of Ms. Machado to Venezuela are the direct result of the US-supported reconciliation talks which have already seen hundreds of political prisoners freed and hundreds of political opposition figures return from exile."

Rubio added: "We expect all political actors to support these talks which represent the best chance Venezuela has had in thirty years to peacefully achieve not just a free and fair election but an enduring democracy."

A faction of the opposition and the interim administration held their latest negotiation session this month to restructure the supreme court and electoral council ahead of future votes.

Machado's movement is not included in these talks. Venezuela's communications ministry did not immediately reply to requests for comment.

"Maria Corina Machado will achieve liberty and democracy in Venezuela," said 69-year-old Hernan Adames, who celebrated alongside other supporters in Barquisimeto.

Machado's top foreign affairs adviser, David Smolansky, told Reuters that she plans to meet with families of political prisoners, victims of the fatal earthquakes that struck Venezuela in June, international delegates, and leaders of the country's democratic movement.

"She will work proactively for a date for free and fair elections," Smolansky added.

International energy companies committed last month to expanding operations in Venezuela during a ceremony overseen by Rodriguez and US Energy Secretary Chris Wright. Machado has maintained that only a "serious, democratic government" can offer the stability required by investors.

Machado was previously banned from exiting Venezuela by Maduro's government but secretly left in late 2025 to collect the Nobel Peace Prize after over a year in hiding.

On Saturday, her Vente Venezuela movement launched a mobile application to gather voter registration details from Venezuelans living abroad. The team behind the app—built over 18 months by 15 staff members—hopes to share the data with a reformed electoral council to officially register users.

Edmundo Gonzalez, a former presidential candidate from Machado's movement currently living in Spain, stated on X that he would join her in Venezuela soon.
