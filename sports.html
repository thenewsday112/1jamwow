Ireland beat Israel 3-0 in Nations League match overshadowed by political controversy

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ireland easily beat Israel in a Nations League fixture that 24 hours earlier appeared in doubt. One Irish player pulled out of the match to express his opposition to Israel's actions in the Gaza Strip.

The Irish team bowed their heads during playing of the Israeli national anthem on Sunday night and they declined to shake hands with their opponents, a gesture that is customary before international fixtures.

Ireland played the match wearing black armbands in memory of those killed in Israel's military operation in the Gaza Strip, and just 24 hours earlier it was unclear whether the match would go ahead at all.

On Saturday, the players voted in favor of playing what was technically a home match for Israel in Debrecen, Hungary, despite widespread opposition to their participation among the Irish public.

David Courell, the chief executive of the Irish FA (FAI), told a press conference that had been delayed for several hours while players and coaches met to discuss the issue, that a significant majority of the squad had voted to play.

"Based on that, the association took ⁠the decision on behalf of Irish ​football that this fixture would continue, as we have always intended to fulfil the fixtures," he said.

However, the vote was not unanimous and one player, reserve goalkeeper Gavin Bazunu, decided to pull out of the squad for this and a second match against Israel during this international break.

In Sunday's match, Troy Parrott netted the opener for Ireland in the 14th minute, with ​Adam Idah scoring two minutes later. Jack Moylan added a third in the 25th to make the final score 3-0.

The fixture was played in a largely empty stadium in Debrecen, with just a few hundred Israeli fans and a handful of Irish supporters in attendance.

The FAI had not reserved any tickets for their fans to attend the match and public broadcaster RTE showed the match without the usual halftime analysis.

Over the previous few days, the Irish team had been under intense pressure from Irish fans and politicians to boycott the fixture.

Ireland has long been a strong supporter of the Palestinian cause and formally recognized a Palestinian state in 2024 alongside Spain and Norway. Dublin has also repeatedly criticized Israel over the Gaza conflict.

A Hamas raid into Israel on October 7, 2023, killed around 1,200 people, prompting Israel's attack on Gaza which has killed over 73,000.

Ireland's next Nations League match was originally scheduled to be played in Dublin on October 4, but due to security concerns, it has been moved to the Serbian town of Backa Topola, where it is to be played behind closed doors.

After defeat to Greece, Jürgen Klopp faces the first real test of his tenure as coach of Germany's national men's football team. Can his charisma alone push this group forward?

In his first season in charge at Liverpool, Jürgen Klopp lost just one of his first five games.

Germany hopes for a similar run emerges after a shock 1-0 defeat to Greece, a team that hasn't qualified for a major international tournament in 12 years. The defeat left Klopp with just one point after his first two games in charge of the national team.

True to form, the head coach took responsibility for the loss, shielding this new crop of Germany players from criticism.

"We have been together for a week now, all mixed together, and that needs time," Klopp said after the match.

"Let the new players develop. The established players are top players at their clubs, and they have my support. Feel free to criticize me, but not the boys. We don't need to completely tear everything apart. I was surprised that some people booed. The future is as bright as we make it. We'll take it on with everything we have."

He also emphasized development over short-term panic, citing the need for more training sessions to cement the automatic responses needed to break down well-organized defenses.

Herein lies the challenge for one of football's greatest coaches. In likely his final job as a head coach, can the multiple league winner adapt his coaching methods to work in the national team? Klopp, a coach and a person famous for his ability to develop relationships and grow a team at a club, has said he believes he can adapt.

His legacy at Borussia Dortmund and Liverpool leaves no doubt that his communication style is what this team needs. His charismatic focus on developing a culture and growing collectively in connection with the fans is what is required.

After many years of frustration and pain watching Germany's men at major tournaments, the biggest mistake would be to tear down this new group before it even begins.

The team both needs and deserves the time to figure out how far it can go. Bambase Conte and Hennes Behrens made their debuts against Greece after a host of others had done so against the Netherlands.

These are the first steps towards finding out who will drive Germany towards an improved outing at the 2028 European Championship. It is the main reason why Klopp chose so many players for these first four games.

But what if, for all of Klopp's positivity and culture building, this new generation of German players is just not good enough to win a trophy? Clearly, it is too soon to know the answer, as Klopp himself has said.

"We want to help prepare these players to be competing at the very top over the next two or three years," Klopp had said at his unveiling.

"We took a different approach and focused not on what we don't have, but on what we do have."

What Germany has right now is both a new team looking to grow and a team that just suffered their first-ever defeat to Greece.

At Liverpool, it took Jürgen Klopp two years and approximately 500 training sessions to reach the point where the team looked like his. In the 2017-18 season, they reached the Champions League final. Alongside the time it took for the team to learn the new system, new signings, notably Mohamed Salah, Roberto Firmino and Sadio Mane, improved the quality.

Klopp doesn't have that option with the German national team. He has to do more with fewer training sessions — Klopp will have approximately 200 before Euro 2028. The closest he can get to new signings is by calling up uncapped Germany players — there were 11 first-time call-ups in this Germany squad.

The 59-year-old said his whole career brought him to this job. It might well prove to be the greatest challenge of his career, too.

What do Manchester City's charges mean for German football?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Manchester City have reportedly been found guilty of major financial rule breaches. What does this mean for German football?

Manchester City have reportedly been found guilty on 114 of the 115 Premier League charges over alleged breaches of financial regulations. They relate to a period between 2009 to 2018 and include allegations around financial reporting, disclosure of player and manager payments, compliance with UEFA and Premier League financial rules, and cooperation with the investigation. The Premier League has yet to comment, with the news having first been reported by The Athletic.

The process started when the charges were brought in February 2023. The review was started in 2024 before the reported verdict of guilty on almost all charges arrived on September 25, 2026.

The English champions, owned in practice by the United Arab Emirates' ruling family, deny all charges and insist their case is supported by a "comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence."

The Premier League has long had a reputation for rampant capitalism, but it has started to enforce financial regulations that control spending in recent years.

In 2026, Chelsea were fined €11.6 million ($13.8 million) and given a suspended ban from registering new players for two transfer windows for breaching rules relating to payments to agents between 2009 and 2022. The club did avoid a six-point deduction though. In the recent years before, Everton and Nottingham Forest were docked Premier League points for breaking profit and sustainability rules (PSR).

City previously won, for the most part, a similar battle with UEFA. The 2023 UEFA Champions League winners were hit with a huge fine by UEFA in 2014 over a breach of the organization's FFP (Financial Fair Play) rules. Then, in February 2020, UEFA handed City a €30-million fine and banned the club from competing in the Champions League for two years for falsely inflating sponsorship revenues between 2012 and 2016. But in July of the same year, the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) overturned the ban and reduced the fine to €10 million. City denied all charges and did not miss a game in Europe's premier club competition.

The key difference in City's current case to Chelsea's earlier in the year is that Chelsea self-reported and admitted guilt, which allowed them to negotiate with the Premier League over the sanction. City have stressed that they are innocent throughout.

German football is by no means immune to scandals or corruption. The most prominent examples are the 1971 match-fixing scandal in which players took bribes to throw matches, and the 2005 match-fixing scandal involving referee Robert Hoyzer, who confessed to fixing and betting on matches in the second division and German Cup.

The majority of financial breaches have come from clubs not being able to prove they were financially viable. Both 1860 Munich and Uerdingen are examples of this, but major systematic abuses or breaches have not happened because the structure of German football is designed to prevent it.

Mainly because of the 50+1 rule. In place since 1999, the rule stipulates that the parent club, for example Bayern Munich, must hold at least 50% plus one vote in its spun-off professional football company – in this case, FC Bayern München Fußball AG. This ensures that the majority of voting rights always lie with the club and its members.

The rule prevents external investors from taking complete control of a club and managing it solely according to financial criteria. It is also meant to ensure a certain level of equality, as it is not possible for a club to invest disproportionately more money than other clubs thanks to its powerful investor with a controlling stake.

The vast majority of organized fans in Germany oppose the commercialization of football. The 50+1 rule makes many fans in Germany not just paying spectators but active club members who can, for example, have a say inimportant club decisions.

Critics argue that the rule puts German football clubs at a financial disadvantage. Spending in the Premier League proves that to be true, but City's reported offenses are also proof of why the league's sustainable structure matters. The Bundesliga has been approached by DW for comment.

Not until the ruling and punishment are final. However, one of the main talking points is what City's success prevented other teams from claiming, whether it be prize money, Champions League places or transfer opportunities. These are compensatory claims that might be explored should the charges remain after appeals.

Spain grows fruit and vegetables for Europe, but it's running out of water

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Spain's southern coast produces up to 25% of Europe's fruit and vegetables. But amid intensive farming, climate change and illegal wells, the water it takes to grow them is drying up.

Donana National Park in southwestern Spain — one of Europe's most important wetlands, home to millions of migratory birds and threatened species like the Iberian lynx — is running dry. But rising temperatures and drought caused by climate change are only part of the problem.

The UNESCO-protected region on the Atlantic coast relies on an underground aquifer that's been tapped by hundreds of illegal wells. The reason: Europe's fruit and vegetable supply. Spain provides one-quarter of Europe's fresh produce, supplying colder parts of the continent with fruit and vegetables year-round.

The scale of water extraction in Spain is staggering: In 2016, WWF Spain estimated that the Donana wetland was home to over 1,000 illegal wells and more than 1,700 ponds illegally irrigating an area as big as 4,200 football fields.

"It's gotten hotter every year. And the water dries up so quickly," one local resident in the town of El Rocio, just north of the wetlands, told DW.

"For me, day to day, the main issue is my animals," said another, hitching his horse up to a tourist carriage. "I have to draw water for them, but the well doesn't have enough water to provide it all at once."

The Donana area used to be royal hunting grounds, consisting of marshes and ponds. In the early 20th century, parts began to be used for dryland farming, agriculture in arid regions that relies heavily on rainwater.

But in 1971, Spain — still under the dictatorship of General Francisco Franco — declared the region's irrigation to be of national interest. The Almonte-Marismas Plan, named after the aquifer it tapped into, was part of a massive, state-sponsored effort to increase food production.

Authorities decided to bet on strawberries, which they called "red gold." According to calculations based on FAO data, strawberries can yield up to €150,000 (about $170,000) in gross revenue per hectare per year, compared to roughly €20,000 for potatoes or €15,000 for grapes.

But growing strawberries at scale also meant using a lot more water. Authorities handed out generous quotas and allotments, seemingly without considering how much extraction the aquifer could handle. Even those legal quotas weren't enough for the ever-expanding fields.

Farmers turned to illegal wells and reservoirs to make up the difference. They built them to stockpile water, draining supplies from local residents and the UNESCO World Heritage Site next door.

"There was extreme drought and lack of attention from the administration," said Pedro Banez, of the Condado Water Users' Association. "And we found ourselves with a number of farms — or parts of farms — that fell outside the land classified as irrigable agricultural land."

The turning point came in 2019 when, following numerous reports of unlawful wells, a 2-year-old fell into deep, reportedly illegal borehole on farmland near Malaga and died. In the aftermath, Spain's Civil Guard police force created dedicated units focused on water theft.

Two years after that, in 2021, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that Spain had failed to protect the Donana aquifer, finding the country in violation of EU law.

Today, police and NGOs like WWF Spain track illegal extraction — near Donana, and other overtaxed agricultural regions across the country — using a mix of old and new tools. They start with satellite images of the region, comparing them against land rights maps to flag areas that appear greener than their legal status allows. Drones follow, giving investigators a closer look at where the water is actually coming from.

Some wells are completely illegal. Others are legal but extract more than they're allowed, which requires closer, on-site inspection to prove.

Investigators check the flow meters that measure how much water a well is pulling. Tampering is common, often using a powerful magnet which disrupts the internal mechanism of the flow meter, allowing water to pass through unrecorded.

Farmers caught extracting illegally can be charged with crimes against the environment and fraud. Most face fines, but sentences can run up to five years in prison.

In a bid to undo decades of overuse, local and national authorities have negotiated a €1.4 billion joint plan. The government has continued shutting down illegal wells, sealing almost 300 in the Donana region alone.

Officials have also started buying up farmland, rewilding it and turning it into protected areas, while setting up funds to create jobs in other sectors like tourism and renewable energy.

Farmers, meanwhile, have had to change how they water their crops.

Many have switched to drip irrigation systems, where each plant gets its own individual dripper, paired with moisture sensors that report exactly how much water a plant needs. That cuts down on the overuse of both water and fertilizer.

Farmers have also begun planting less water-intensive crops and shifting their growing season to colder months.

Spain isn't alone in confronting this problem. As economies mature, fruit and vegetable production starts being outsourced to places with cheaper labor, fewer restrictions and more sun.

Mexico, Brazil and Colombia grow food for parts of the US, while countries in southeast Asia export a lot of produce to China or Japan. Some countries in northern Africa, including Morocco and Egypt, also supply fresh fruits and vegetables to their European neighbors to the north. But, as in Spain, intensive agriculture has strained local water supplies.

Morocco has tried to turn the tide by using satellite comparisons to monitor irrigation and offering subsidies for farmers who switch to drip irrigation. In India, the government is trying to move farmers toward less water-intensive crops.

And in California, the US state has paid farmers to halt certain types of irrigation and introduced legislation to monitor and allocate water more sustainably.

Securing water, no longer seen as an unlimited resource, will increasingly be a challenge as the effects of climate change intensify. Addressing overextraction will take a coordinated effort from governments, farmers and the industries that depend on their water.

Senegal's Youth Olympics makes Olympics in Africa 'logical'

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Youth Olympics are to be hosted in Africa for the first time ever. The edition in Senegal is also a chance to prove the continent can host a full Olympics in the future.

At the end of October 2026, the first ever Olympic event on African soil will take place as the Youth Olympics comes to Senegal.

Around 2,700 young athletes are expected to compete across a total of 153 events in 25 sports, with many qualifying through the African Youth Games last year.

"We were entrusted with hosting this event and that is why from Johannesburg to Nairobi, from Dakar to Djbouti, it is all of young Africa that is watching you and watching you with pride," Senegalese President Bassirou Diomaye Faye said at the arrival of the flame.

The moment marks yet another step forward for global sport in Africa. In 2025, the first major road cycling event took place on the continent, and in September this year it was announced the first-ever World Athletics Championships would be held in Kenya in 2029. There are also on-going negotiations about Formula 1 returning. It appears that 16 years on since the 2010 World Cup in South Africa, global sport is finally returning to different parts of Africa.

"This is a victory for Africa. It opens the door for Africa to host major events," Malick Diouf, head of a marketing agency and founder of the Dakar Sport Summit, told DW.

"Africa is in the process of opening up to the world, hosting major events and everything. And all of Africa must celebrate this kind of victory."

With more major events in Africa, the question around an Olympic Games being on the continent looms.

Sports scientist Kamilla Swart has said awarding Africa a Games would make the world "take Africa more seriously." Diouf believes it's the logical next step.

"The Youth Olympic Games, it's a major test for Africa. And I think this is where we need to succeed in organizing these Games and also in sending a message," Diouf said.

"We, Africa, are ready to host the Summer Olympics, and I think that would be the logical next step after the Dakar Olympics. We have Olympic champions; we have record holders; we have Olympians, so we deserve to host the Olympic Games."

The move to re-use and refurbish existing facilities rather than build whole new ones is likely why Senegal's bid was so tempting for the International Olympic Committee (IOC).

The fear of the white elephant facility appears to have been considered here. The Iba Mar Diop Stadium, the track and field stadium, is expected to be used for athlete training ahead of the 2028 Olympics.

"It’s by using these facilities, by participating in and hosting international events, and by consistently maintaining them to the required standards, that we’ll be able to keep these stadiums up to the standards they currently meet," Diouf said.

There's also the social and economical impacts of the Youth Olympics. Diouf believes the impact is beyond just infrastructure, but into roads, the development of the hospitality, security and broadcasting sectors and the young athletes themselves.

"There's life after the Games. Now we need to figure out how to use the resources we have, we need to develop and capitalize on what we have showcased during the Games," said Diouf.

The significance of these Games stretch beyond even the history they are making in Africa. In May of this year, IOC President Kirsty Coventry put the brakes on the future of the Youth Olympics, leaving real doubts as to whether there will be any more after the 2028 winter edition.

"We need to pause and have a real reflection on why we're doing the Youth Olympic Games and we realized that across the Movement it's very disjointed. There's not a true North Star of why we're doing the Youth Olympic Games," Coventry told reporters.

"So the EB (IOC Executive Board) decided to pause the 2030 process right now so there will not be an election in June, and rather we need to take a step back and develop a youth strategy."

A lot has changed since the Youth Olympic Games were launched in 2010. Initially it was seen as a way to engage a younger audience (athletes were limited by age to between 15 and 18) by trialing new sports as well as testing host country's on a smaller stage. However, with the age of athletes decreasing and the challenges of engaging a younger audience in the modern sports entertainment landscape even more challenging, it appears clear the IOC are reconsidering their options when it comes to investment.

"My only wish is that Dakar be memorable and that people reconsider their decision not to host the Games in 2030," Diouf said.

"I don’t want these to be the last Youth Olympic Games, but if these are to be the last Youth Olympic Games, we need to go out with a bang. Dakar needs to show the world what Senegal is all about, what Africa is all about."

From October 31 to November 13 this year, Senegal will have the chance to do just that.

Manchester City Premier League charges: What do they mean?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Since being bought by the UAE in 2008, Manchester City has become one of the world's most successful football clubs. A raft of serious financial charges threatens to undermine this and bring chaos to the Premier League.

Manchester City has won the English Premier League eight times over the last 14 years. Whether those titles will remain is one of a plethora of issues at stake after England's top division charged one of its most high-profile clubs with more than 100 breaches of its financial regulations.

On Tuesday, the Premier League announced that an independent commission had found City had drawn up "sham contracts" as well as "relying on sham agreements to artificially inflate the club's revenues and reduce its costs." These contracts, dubious sponsorships and a number of other methods were, according to the commission's report, used to inflate the club's revenue to the tune of about £900 million (€1.05 billion) over the period between the 2009-10 and 2017-18 seasons.

The case has been ongoing for eight years after initial revelations from Der Spiegel, a German magazine, and Rui Pinto of the 'Football Leaks' website. City continues to deny any wrongdoing.

"The Club is innocent of the accusations made by the Premier League and a comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence exists in support of all of its positions, relating to this case," read a club statement. "Manchester City FC will now pursue the appeal avenues open to it, on the basis that the opinion contains clear material errors, of law, principle and fact, and is unsafe."

On Friday, the club issued a further statement saying it had lodged a "comprehensive Appeal."

The 750-page report was damning, accusing City of inflating contracts and sponsorship deals, running secondary contracts off the books, making "concerted efforts to stop and frustrate the Premier League investigation" and signing off inflated image rights deals to artificially boost their earnings. The report says all this enabled them to pass both Premier League and UEFA financial spending limits.

The Premier League's chief ­executive, Richard Masters, described the case "as the most significant in Premier League history," adding that the club "systematically broke Premier League Rules for nearly a decade."

Although the Premier League, the world's most commercially successful football league, has drawn some praise for its actions, others point out that it took Pinto leaking emails and media reports to take action. The report shows that Manchester City, which had won the league once in 71 years before being taken over by the United Arab Emirates (UAE) in 2008, were consistently posting revenues way higher than any of their more established and successful rivals.

In this context, its the owners. The Abu Dhabi United Group, essentially an investment vehicle for the UAE royal family, bought the club in 2008. Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, know simply as Sheikh Mansour, fronted the takeover. He is a member of the ruling Al Nahyan family and the UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister. Khaldoon Al Mubarak, who also holds several government positions in the UAE, was brought in as chairman.

Nation-state ownership of this type is controversial in European football. Manchester City's owners have long been criticized for the country's human rights record and using the club for sportswashing. Similar criticisms have been made of European champions Paris Saint-Germain, which Qatar owned and City's Premier League rivals Newcastle United, which is owned 85% by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.

City's CEO, Ferran Soriano, joined in 2012 as City expanded their ownership into foreign leagues, buying up clubs in the US, Japan and Australia. He replaced former head coach Roberto Mancini with fellow Spaniard Manuel Pellegrini before former Bayern Munich and Barcelona coach Pep Guardiola took over in 2016, ushering in the club's most successful period.

The club immediately started splashing out on big-money transfers after the UAE investment arrived. At the time, neither UEFA nor the Premier League had the tight financial rules they now have. UEFA introduced its Financial Fair Play (FFP) rules for the 2011-12 season, while the Premier League followed with its Profit and Sustainability Rules (PSR) in 2013-14.

City's free spending and relatively low commercial income meant they were likely to fall foul of such rules. The club was handed a conditional €60 million UEFA fine for losses in the 2011-12 and 2012-13 seasons. They would later be banned from the Champions League on similar grounds in 2020 before being reinstated on appeal.

In an attempt to try and comply with these financial rules, City set up a system they called "Project Longbow" in 2012. The commission said much of this was genuine, but one arm of it was an agreement with a third party called Fordham that was, according to the ruling, "little more than a front." Essentially, it reads, the club's UAE owners "would be used (and were used) to enable Fordham to acquire from the club (at a sizeable, artificially inflated price) the club's entitlement to benefit financially from its players' image rights."

In other words, City's owners were pumping money into their own club through vastly inflated sponsorship and image rights deals so their income looked higher and they could spend more, particularly on player transfers and wages. The report also claims that City paid three individuals, whose names are redacted, through a third party to keep them off the books.

That's the question on everyone's lips. So far, no sanctions have been imposed on City, and the appeal will likely drag proceedings out even longer. Meanwhile, the club, which is top of the Premier League, faces Liverpool on October 11, after the international break.

While there is no precedent for such a tranche of charges, clubs have previously fallen foul of the Premier League's PSR regulations. Everton were handed a 10-point penalty for a £19.5 million breach over a period ending with the 2021-22 season. This was based on a Premier League tariff of six points for the initial breach, plus an additional point for every £5 million the club overspent. Given that City's overspending is close to a billion pounds, applying that would certainly relegate them, though the Premier League does not operate England's second-tier league, the Championship.

For now, it looks like City and the league will play on under a cloud. But if the verdict remains after appeal, clubs and individuals whose careers and honors stand to be impacted will likely come forward and litigate. City themselves could be relegated, docked points over successive seasons, face transfer bans or any combination of those things. The titles won in the relevant period could also be stripped.

Ultimately, the faster the game grows, the greater need to close the gaps.

Michael Jordan was violently ill before game five of the 1997 NBA finals, but somehow played through severe discomfort to score 38 points and give the Chicago Bulls a 3-2 lead over the Utah Jazz, setting them up to win the series in their next game.

Nearly 30 years later, it’s still talked about. The ‘Flu Game’ was one of Jordan’s most memorable performances, and a core moment in his own sporting mythology – even though, as it turned out, he actually had food poisoning, not the flu.

Alexander Volkanovski has just produced his own version, one that might soon be looked back upon the same way.

Days out from his UFC featherweight title defence against previously undefeated Russian contender Movsar Evloev, Volkanovski was feeling rough. His breathing was heavy, to the point where he was genuinely worried he might not last five rounds. It was later revealed that he was suffering from a chest infection.

He was offered medication that was perfectly legal to take, but Volkanovski was worried it might somehow be perceived as giving him an unfair advantage.

And he liked the idea of making things harder for himself. So he refused.

“Not many people, I guess, would choose to do that,” he said on Monday, hinting that there was “more to the story” which he’ll tell another day.

“I looked at it as more of a challenge. I was like, ‘No, no, I like the idea of this being more of a challenge.’ I’m just weird like that. Put it this way: my team weren’t happy with me, but I guess they’re not surprised as well.

“Most people would probably panic and freak out. I’ll look at it like, ‘Hey, when this story is told, it’s just going to make that so much better.’ And that’s how it was.”

Indeed, what followed was, by his own assessment, the greatest moment of Volkanovski’s great career to date.

Volkanovski saw off Evloev with a second-round TKO finish at UFC 333 in Abu Dhabi, his record ninth featherweight title-fight win – and he got it done quickly, just as he’d promised his wife, Emma, as a belated anniversary present.

Hours later, he was back in Sydney, his belt proudly draped over his shoulder next to a shimmering diamond necklace, his face looking only slightly worse for wear, still coughing through the remnants of his illness.

“I think it has to be right up there. I really do think it’s right up there,” he said. “Undefeated fighter ... you know what? Yeah, I’ll probably say that tops it, to be quite honest.”

Volkanovski is obviously much closer to the end than the beginning; had he taken much damage against Evloev, he might have been talking about his retirement plans on Monday, rather than celebrating his latest victory.

Such is his exalted status now in the UFC world, Volkanovski is in the envious position of being able to effectively call the shots, and nominate his next challenge.

“I had a good chat with the bosses of UFC, Dana White and Hunter Campbell,” Volkanovski said.

“You’ve got those guys even saying publicly that, ‘What Alex wants, we’ll give him. We’re not really going to say no to Alex.’ They don’t usually say those types of things.”

Top of his list is Justin Gaethje, the reigning UFC lightweight champion.

Volkanovski has never held belts in two weight classes before; his only concern was that he would be seen to be “cutting the line” ahead of other lightweights. But Gaethje is 37, and the idea of both of them retiring afterwards and vacating their belts, so as to not hold up their respective divisions, appeals greatly to ‘Volk’.

And so does the idea of doing it in Sydney, if he can convince White and Campbell.

“Let these two old fellas go have a crack, and then we can just sail off in the sunset. I just feel like that’s something that’s never been done, a cool story, and I think that’ll be great for the UFC,” he said.

The word “story” came up a lot on Monday. Volkanovski is happy to let others worry about titles, numbers, statistics, legacies and the GOAT debate; he is only chasing new chapters that will transcend and resonate with people at a deeper level.

For all his achievements, Volkanovski acknowledges that mixed martial arts still occupies a different, slightly obscured place in the Australian consciousness to more traditional sports. His wildly popular cooking channel on YouTube and other long-form videos have helped him reach a broader audience, one that doesn’t necessarily care about what he does inside the octagon.

As long as people are connecting with him, whichever way they’re doing that, Volkanovski is content.

“This is a different sport, and I get that. I’ve tried my best to change it, and change people’s perceptions of the sport,” he said. “But it’s all good. I like that people know me outside of that as well. I’m not too fussed. Either way, they know who I am as a person.”

Whenever this story is finished, it deserves to be remembered as one of the greatest this country has seen in sport.

Ever since soccer star Mohamed Salah posted a photo of himself last month sitting on a small boulder in a remote highland more than an hour from the city of Trabzon in northeastern Turkey, fans have flocked to the spot to pose in the same position.

The unlikely tourist attraction is not the only sign of the Salah-mania sweeping Turkey since the Egyptian star signed with the Trabzonspor club in August after leaving England’s iconic Liverpool club.

His arrival came as a much-needed boost to Turkish soccer fans’ spirits, which had been dashed months earlier when Turkey’s national team played in its first World Cup in 24 years only to exit the tournament without scoring any goals.

“God willing, we will win the championship title with him this year,” said Sinan Mor, who attended a match hosted by Trabzonspor Monday against Besiktas, one of Turkey’s most popular football clubs, which was in talks to sign Salah before he went to Trabzonspor. Ahead of the match, Associated Press journalists who visited the “Salah rock” counted 46 visitors within two hours.

“If you can bring a global superstar to one of the most remote highland villages in Trabzon, that means something to us,” said Mustafa Bektas, a 44-year-old Trabzon supporter who travelled from Sakarya in western Turkey for the game and made the trek to pose on the boulder.

In the city, Salah-themed merchandise and art have popped up everywhere. Muralist Yildiray Yildirim said he's received multiple requests for Salah-themed murals outside local businesses after he painted an image of him wearing his Trabzonspor jersey on a wall outside a park.

The move of a world record-breaking football star from the English Premier League to a lower tier European league team would usually mark the twilight of their career.

But for Salah, transferring from Liverpool to Turkey’s Trabzonspor at the age of 34 marked a new dawn.

Salah made a name as the third-highest goal scorer in Liverpool’s history and the world record holder for the most left-footed Premier League goals, but his latest campaign was disappointing. He scored only 12 goals, had a public falling out with coach Arne Slot, and in March announced he would leave.

Since his transfer to Trabzonspor, fans and commentators say Salah rediscovered his scoring touch, with teammates who bolster his strengths and an entire city that is turning into a fan club.

Nearly all the seats in Trabzonspor’s stadium, with capacity for more than 41,000 people, were full Monday. The stands roared every time Salah touched the ball. In the 71st minute, Salah scored one of the goals that won his team the derby and made him the leading scorer in the Turkish Super League.

The chants “Allah Allah Allah, Mohamed Salah” and “Trabzonlu (from Trabzon) Mohamed Salah” rose from the stands following the goal.

“He is a top player and a very good guy," Nigerian defender Chibuike Nwaiwu, Trabzonspor’s centre-back, told reporters. "He brings a lot of quality to the team.”

Trabzon, a city of nearly a million people on Turkey’s Black Sea coast, is known for its green valleys, steep hills, a Byzantine-era monastery and head-strong locals with punchy accents.

The local economy runs on agriculture and tourism, and its team, where Istanbul’s jailed Mayor Ekrem İmamoglu was a board member before stepping into politics, is the city’s crown jewel.

Arkansas in play?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Arkansas Democratic Senate nominee Hallie Shoffner speaks with voters at a campaign stop in Hope, Ark. Friday, Oct. 23, 2026. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

HOPE, Ark. — For Senate candidate Hallie Shoffner, the most important political label she wears isn't that she's a Democrat. Or that she's not a Republican. Instead, it's that she is an Arkansan.

As a lunchtime crowd ate at Tailgater's Burger Company Friday, Shoffner decried what she called a historically unproductive Congress driven by extreme voices in each party and pitched herself to receptive — if skeptical — voters as a candidate who would better serve their needs.

Arkansas is one of the most conservative states in the country, with nearly two out of three voters supporting President Trump in 2024 and Republican Sen. Tom Cotton in 2020.

But in the final days of the 2026 midterm election, Shoffner and other Arkansas Democrats are crisscrossing the state with a message that seeks to capitalize on the Trump administration's unpopularity, stubbornly high prices and anti-incumbency vibes.

"We're the sleeper race," Shoffner told NPR. "I mean, we're the race that no one's noticed and that no one is going to see coming."

Republicans have taken some notice, pre-emptively pouring millions of dollars into the Senate race, the Little Rock-based 2nd Congressional District and robust get-out-the vote efforts to try and stem the rising tide of dissatisfaction with the party.

But even in a midterm cycle where Senate races in states like Kansas, Iowa, Ohio and Texas are viewed as tossups, and where the landscape of potentially competitive House races stretches deep into conservative territory, Arkansas seems a bridge too far for election forecasters.

Arkansas' 2nd Congressional District is considered "Likely Republican," according to the Cook Political Report, and its Senate race is "Solid Republican."

Hallie Shoffner, the Democratic Senate nominee in Arkansas, has emphasized her farming background and belief that both parties have ignored the people's needs in the state. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

At an outdoor coffee shop in Arkadelphia, Vanilla Hannah said she was there to support Shoffner and talk about her own reelection bid to be a local justice of the peace. She said it's possible that Arkansas could elect Democrats this year because the crop of candidates seems willing to work with people to meet their needs — and there are a lot of them.

"When it comes down to basic needs or whatever, the 'D' or the 'R' after your name don't mean a thing, because we're all suffering and all having the same issues and everybody's ready for a change," she said. "And running for an election is not just running specifically on the Democratic policies or the Republican or the libertarians or whatever. It's just what you believe in, and it seems like more and more people are having a common belief now."

Later, Shoffner visited a small family farm in nearby Bierne where several members of the Hatley family cooked sorghum to make syrup and talked about why they were supporting her campaign.

Shoffner is a multigenerational farmer who decided to shut down her family's operation in 2025, citing financial headwinds from rising input costs and Trump administration tariff and trade policies. That in turn inspired her Senate run and influenced much of her campaign's focus.

Sliding behind the wheel of a side-by-side utility vehicle, Shoffner takes off across the property and recounts her life story that led her away from the farm, into nonprofit work and back home when her dad got sick.

She doesn't buy the argument that her chances are low to win.

"This race isn't about Democrats or Republicans or independents or any of that," she said. "This isn't some kind of weird political alignment, this is a values alignment. This is reorienting representation in Arkansas based on common sense, based on compassion and based on showing up."

She said national Republicans — and Democrats — have made the mistake of ignoring the needs of Arkansas residents, especially with agriculture and trade policies.

"I come from a place, a community, that died in my lifetime," she said. "I was the last one left and I felt left behind. I felt like no one was really talking to me or talking about me. I felt like folks were making decisions about me, but without me, and ultimately they were rewriting the rules."

Now, with record diesel prices, the war in Iran and tariffs, Shoffner said she believes that Arkansas voters of all stripes want something different. She's also glad that the race hasn't seen an influx of outside air support from national Democratic groups.

"I don't need them, I really don't," she said. "This is an Arkansas First campaign, and I'm an Arkansan, and I want to talk directly to Arkansans, and I want them to know that when I go to D.C., the only letters that will mean anything next to my name are the "A" and the "R."

Chris Jones, the Democratic nominee for Arkansas' 2nd Congressional District, says voters are frustrated with promises made by the Trump administration that haven't been kept. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

On the flip side, Chris Jones has the backing of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee as one of dozens of "Red to Blue" flip targets.

At an outdoor jazz night held at a brewery in Little Rock, Jones worked the crowd between visits to polling sites and a high school football homecoming game on a campaign that has seen millions of dollars in outside spending by Republicans.

"They understand that people are in pain," he said. "Folks are hurting. They can't afford gas. They can't afford groceries. And our campaign has always been about affordability and accountability for all. So prices are way too high."

Jones also ran for governor against Republican Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders in 2022 and lost, so he started off with higher name recognition as a serious challenger to Rep. French Hill.

He knows it's a tall task, even in a national environment more favorable to Democrats. Hill is a longtime incumbent whose district only became more conservative after the last Census-led redistricting in 2021.

But he also knows that Democrats, Republicans and independent voters alike are open to looking for something other than the current leadership.

"Here's what's so fascinating: a lot of folks here voted for the president and this administration because they wanted lower prices, they wanted to end foreign wars, and they wanted to end corruption, particularly with the Epstein files," he said. "None of that has happened, so they're super frustrated."

Like many states that have seen the potential for close races next week, Arkansas has a longer history of single-party control that has led to anger and frustration at the status quo and incumbency in a way that extends beyond typical partisan labels or voting history.

"One of the things that we forget, because there's so much that comes out of this administration: It wasn't but 15 years ago that the entire federal delegation of Arkansas was Democrat," he said. "So it's not that long ago. The reason they got in office was because of the conditions that existed, the economic anxiety and pain that people feel — and folks feeling like what they wanted to happen out of Washington was not happening. We're in that same place now."

In addition to the DCCC support, Jones has had campaign visits from former DNC Chair Jaime Harrison, former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg and Rep. Jasmine Crockett of Texas. Illinois Gov. Après un match de football américain, une fusillade blesse 12 personnes, dont six mineurs, en Floride

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Article rédigé par Anne-Sophie Canton

l'essentiel Plusieurs personnes, dont la plus jeune âgée de onze ans, ont été blessées dans une fusillade survenue près d’un stade universitaire à Tallahassee, dans l’État américain de Floride.

Dans la soirée du samedi 24 octobre 2026, des coups de feu ont été tirés à proximité d'un stade universitaire, en Floride. Parmi les douze victimes figurent six mineurs, dont un de onze ans, a déclaré le chef de la police de Tallahassee, Lawrence Revell, aux médias américains lors d’un point presse. Trois victimes présentent des blessures graves.

Le maire de Tallahassee, John E. Dailey, a qualifié l’incident de "tragédie véritablement atroce et bouleversante", selon la chaîne d'information CNN.

Ce samedi se déroulaient des festivités d'anciens élèves, avec notamment une rencontre de football américain. L'attaque s'est produite quelques heures après le match entre les Golden Lions d’Arkansas-Pine Bluff et les Rattlers de Florida.

Le chef de la police a précisé ce dimanche 25 octobre qu'aucune arrestation n'a encore été effectuée. Un seul tireur serait impliqué.  La police n’a pas révélé l’identité des victimes, elle a toutefois indiqué que l’une d’entre elles serait un étudiant de l’université de Floride, d'après les propos recueillis par la chaîne d'information NBC News. La fusillade serait liée à une dispute entre deux ou trois personnes mais l’enquête est toujours en cours.
