After a long, strange trip, this CEO is close to taking a psilocybin pill to market

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In 2022, Kabir Nath had a cushy executive job at a pharmaceutical firm that developed anti-psychotic medication.

Then he got a call from a scrappy upstart with a patent on a synthetic version of psilocybin — the active ingredient in magic mushrooms. It hoped to use the compound to help those with treatment-resistant depression.

They wanted him to join as CEO. Nath, whose father died by suicide, bit on their second pitch.

"A number of people have said to me in the last couple of years: 'You do realize what a risk that was,'" Nath told Business Insider about eventually jumping ship for what was, and still is, a drug company without FDA approval for its product.

"I was probably the first really senior executive from traditional pharma to make the move to psychedelics," he added. "But, you know, it was the right thing to do."

Nath's personal risk is now magnified as Compass Pathways finishes filing its final forms ahead of what could be FDA approval as soon as next year, and then a public rollout of its psilocybin pill, called COMP360.

Compass has recently added 150 new hires to its payroll, expanded its marketing division, and moved into new office space in Jersey City in the hope it all works out. "The company is growing rapidly," Nath said.

Though a subsidiary of Johnson and Johnson attained FDA approval with a version of ketamine for depression patients in 2019, it is by no means guaranteed that Compass, leveraging a more traditional psychedelic, will get the same approval.

Should the FDA deny Compass' bid, investors would rush to dump their shares, likely sending the company into bankruptcy. The Jersey City office, which just received a new lime green paint job, would be untenable.

If you ask Nath, it would also be bad news for 4 million patients with depression in the US, for whom more traditional antidepressants like SSRIs don't work.

"These are people who have been suffering from depression, and nothing's really working," he said.

Like other drug companies — especially those with a single product — Compass' stock has "been on quite the adventure," Nath said, as the company progressed through various human trials.

When markets closed on Friday, the share price hovered around $11, up $5 from last year. In 2020, when the company went public, shares went for $17.

Psilocybin's journey to the forefront of medical research didn't begin with Compass, and it hasn't exactly gone in a straight line.

Few did more to publicize the drug than Timothy Leary, the Harvard professor turned acid dropout. In a speech at Harvard's Cambridge campus in 1962, he told students that taking psilocybin allows people to experience an "internal rebirth," according to the Harvard Crimson.

In time, Leary would be fired from Harvard as he, along with a measurable portion of the US population, began to experiment more heavily with psychedelic drugs. Come 1966, the Grateful Dead were spiking their Kool-Aid with acid, and a definitive subculture of long-haired, psychedelic-taking hippies had emerged.

Meanwhile, researchers were experimenting too. Studies involving LSD and psilocybin flourished during the 1960s before hitting a brick wall in 1970, when psilocybin was classified as a Schedule I substance, rendering possession illegal, even for research purposes.

Work on the drug languished for years, until 2000, when the FDA gave scientists at Johns Hopkins University permission to conduct research on psilocybin.

And more recently, psychedelics have become trendy with the tech crowd.

From Burning Man to luxury retreats, experimenting with psychedelics is now common among tech founders and executives like Elon Musk and Sam Altman, who credit the drugs with quieting their nerves, boosting productivity, and allowing them to better harness their creativity.

"There was actually a biotech boom from 2018 through to about 2022," Nath said. "In that boom, literally hundreds of psychedelic companies went public."

In 2018, Compass' proprietary psilocybin formulation received breakthrough therapy designation from the FDA. A few years later, it started testing the drug on people.

Nath was an executive in 2018 at Japanese pharmaceutical giant Otsuka, where he said he assembled a team to investigate whether psychedelics could become viable medicines after reading Michael Pollan's book "How to Change Your Mind," which explores the history and therapeutic potential of psychedelics.

Compass isn't selling an experience analogous to a spiritual awakening at Burning Man, though.

The drug-taking process for a patient enrolled in one of Compass' trials is a bit different. First, a patient has to have treatment-resistant depression, which is clinically defined as depression that two or more standard drugs have failed to treat.

Then, a clinician explains the details of the process that is about to unfold. After that, they're given the psilocybin in pill form.

"It's a quiet room with a couch," Nath said. "They're offered a blindfold, which most patients do actually wear."

As the patient begins to trip, a playlist of music, including classical and new age, plays softly in the background. The clinician, who Nath said could be a nurse, doctor, or therapist, sits silently in the room if the patient wants to talk, and for safety reasons.

Most of the time, though, there isn't any talking.

"This is not psychotherapy," Nath said. "We are not directing the patient's experience."

Six hours later, after some crying, laughing, and everything in between, the patient goes home.

In Compass's most recent trial, results for which were published in September, just under half of participants who took the drug experienced at least a 50% reduction in depressive symptoms within six weeks of treatment.

After a decade of slogging through tests, first on animals and then on humans, Compass has completed all the necessary prerequisites and is busily working to get its last papers in order.

In a statement provided to Business Insider, an FDA spokesperson was noncommittal, though.

"The FDA evaluates experimental medications, including psychedelic drugs like psilocybin, through a rigorous, evidence-based process designed to ensure that any approved treatment is safe and effective for the indicated use," the spokesperson said.

Compass isn't the only psychedelic drug company wading through the lengthy process of trialing a drug.

AtaiBeckley, another psychedelic drug company trading on the Nasdaq whose product is not yet approved by the FDA, is developing a version of DMT administered via nasal spray. It received breakthrough status in 2025, years behind Compass.

For Compass, though, it could face the music as soon as the first half of next year, when it expects the FDA to render its decision. The company is already getting ready.

In addition to adding 150 new hires, Nath said the company is preparing its sales force to go online and is in frequent communication with doctors and healthcare providers about operating in clinics where therapists can administer the drug to patients.

"This has the potential to be radically different, and again, the rapidity is something that they're very excited about, and the durability," Nath said, referring to psychiatrists.

Bis zu 12.000 Arbeitsplätze: Schweizer Bankgewerkschaft fürchtet Job-Kahlschlag bei UBS

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Die Schweizer Politik plant härtere Vorgaben für Großbanken. Sollte die UBS das Land deshalb verlassen, könnten bis zu 12.000 Arbeitsplätze verloren gehen, warnt der Schweizer Bankpersonalverband. Laut Medienberichten prüft das Management bereits Notfallpläne für eine Verlagerung der Konzernzentrale.

Bei der Großbank UBS könnten nach Einschätzung des Schweizerischen Bankpersonalverbandes (SBPV) im Falle eines Wegzugs bis zu 12.000 Arbeitsplätze in der Schweiz wegfallen. Sollten strengere Kapitalvorschriften das Institut zu einem Umzug, einem Verkauf oder einer Fusion zwingen, stünden bis zu 12.000 der rund 34.000 Stellen im Heimatmarkt auf dem Spiel, warnte der Verband in einem Brief an Parlamentarier.

Der SBPV bezeichnete dies als "das schlimmstmögliche Szenario", zitierte die "NZZ am Sonntag" aus dem Schreiben. Diese Stellenstreichungen würden noch zu denen dazukommen, die die UBS bereits wegen der Übernahme der Credit Suisse geplant hat.

Hintergrund sind Pläne der Schweizer Politik für schärfere Bankenregeln als Reaktion auf den Zusammenbruch der Credit Suisse und deren Notübernahme durch die UBS im Jahr 2023. In der kommenden Woche stehen Beratungen in einem Ausschuss des Schweizer Nationalrats über die künftigen Kapitalanforderungen für die ausländischen Tochtergesellschaften der UBS an.

Der Ständerat hatte im vergangenen Monat vorgeschlagen, dass Banken diese Beteiligungen zu 90 Prozent mit hartem Kernkapital unterlegen müssen. Die UBS hält diese Vorgaben für zu streng. Der SBPV forderte die Parlamentarier in seinem Brief auf, einen tragfähigen Kompromiss zu finden, der der Bank keine derart schwere Last aufbürde. Zugleich betonte der Verband, die UBS habe die Pflicht, sich zu ihrem Schweizer Standort zu bekennen.

UBS-Verwaltungsratspräsident Colm Kelleher hatte bereits gewarnt, die Bank müsse ihre Zukunft in der Schweiz sorgfältig prüfen, falls die neuen Regeln zu hart ausfielen. Einige Aktionäre haben das Institut Insidern zufolge bereits aufgefordert, das Land zu verlassen. Die UBS arbeite an Notfallplänen, die eine Verlegung des Hauptsitzes oder eine Fusion mit einem anderen Geldhaus umfassten, sagten mit den Vorgängen vertraute Personen der Nachrichtenagentur Reuters.

Die UBS, die wiederholt ihr Bekenntnis zum Standort Schweiz bekräftigt hat, kommentierte die genannten Stellenstreichungen bisher nicht. Ein Sprecher teilte mit, die Bank wolle die durch die Credit-Suisse-Übernahme bedingten Einschnitte "so gering wie möglich halten". Das Institut geht weiterhin von rund 3000 Entlassungen in der Schweiz aus, die über mehrere Jahre hinweg vor allem durch natürliche Fluktuation und Frühpensionierungen erfolgen sollen.

Just Two Days on a Vegan Diet Triggered Unexpected Changes in the Liver

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Switching from a vegetarian to a vegan diet altered liver metabolism in mice within just two days, making glucose production and toxic waste removal less efficient.

Millions of people follow a vegan diet, excluding all animal-derived products. Plant-based eating is recommended in part for its environmental benefits, but the health consequences of getting all dietary protein from plants are not yet fully understood. For researchers, that raises a question about how the body responds when the source of its protein changes.

Scientists at the University of Geneva (UNIGE) investigated that question in mice, focusing on the liver, which helps maintain blood sugar and processes the components of dietary protein. Their study, published in Molecular Metabolism, compared a lacto-vegetarian diet containing both animal and plant proteins with a vegan diet.

“In just two days, the mice fed an exclusively vegan diet showed less efficient glucose production,” explains Pierre Maechler, full professor in the Department of Cell Physiology and Metabolism and at the Diabetes Center of the UNIGE Faculty of Medicine. “Furthermore, their livers were less effective at eliminating ammonia, a toxic substance released during the breakdown of amino acids. A significant reduction in physical activity also suggests effects on the body as a whole.”

Proteins from plants and animals consist of amino acids, small molecules joined together like links in a chain. Their types and proportions differ depending on the food source, so changing protein sources gives the body a different mixture to process. Plant proteins naturally contain fewer branched-chain amino acids than animal proteins. Along with helping build the body’s proteins, these amino acids supply some of the raw material the liver uses to produce glucose and maintain sufficient blood sugar during fasting.

“Overall, a change in diet alters liver metabolism very quickly, but this effect is just as rapidly reversible,” emphasizes Maechler. “The body adapts within one to two days to even subtle changes in dietary composition and returns to normal upon the reintroduction of animal proteins.”

The combination of less efficient glucose production and ammonia removal pointed the team toward a liver enzyme called glutamate dehydrogenase, or GDH. Located in mitochondria, the structures inside cells that help process fuel, GDH connects protein metabolism with sugar metabolism. To investigate its role, the researchers compared two groups of mice lacking the enzyme in their livers. One group ate a lacto-vegetarian diet, while the other ate a strict vegan diet.

Without GDH, the mice eating only plant proteins developed a severe buildup of ammonia and impaired blood glucose regulation compared with mice whose diet also included animal proteins. This confirmed the enzyme’s essential role in the metabolic response to an exclusively plant-based diet.

The experiments were conducted in mice, so they do not establish that people would experience the same changes. They do, however, provide a way to investigate the physiological questions that accompany a change in protein source.

“In addition to the ethical and environmental considerations that often motivate a switch to a plant-based diet, there are also scientific factors relating to the physiological functioning of our metabolism that are worth being aware of,” notes Maechler.

For people following a strict vegan diet, the researchers emphasize choosing protein sources rich in branched-chain amino acids, such as nuts, tofu, and lentils, to support the liver’s metabolic functions. They place that consideration alongside the need to ensure an adequate intake of specific vitamins.

I paid off $11,000 of credit card debt in 18 months while still traveling, shopping and living my life

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Yes, I am a personal finance reporter with over seven years of experience telling readers how to wisely manage their money. Yes, I've also been in credit card debt for basically all of that time.

I have carried a balance on one or more credit cards for nearly all of the 10 years I have had credit cards. Sometimes, especially in the early years when I was still a college student, I did not have the cash on hand to pay it all off. Most of the time, however, I just didn't have the discipline.

I was making more than the minimum payment every month, but I also felt like credit card debt was normal so I didn't make a concerted effort to fully clear my balance. I assumed when my peers were putting things on credit, they too, weren't paying it off right away, though many of them actually were.

I did get my cards paid off for a brief period in 2020 when I put my Covid-19 stimulus checks toward my debt. But then I got a dog, moved into my own apartment and continued traveling and spending at levels I felt were manageable. Soon I was carrying a balance again and that balance gradually grew to a peak of $11,310 in March 2025.

But as of Sept. 18, I am credit card debt-free. Here's how I racked up five figures of debt and paid it all off in under two years.

My debt came from a mix of genuine necessities and trivial pursuits. I charged vet bills and supplies for my dog but also flights and hotels for trips whenever I felt like I could afford them, and treated myself to dinner or a new outfit admittedly too often. Instead of intentionally making payments to bring down the balance, I'd often use so-called "girl math" to justify my continued card usage. I'd make a mental note of whatever I spent when I swiped my card, then pay back what I thought was more than the total I'd spent — without actually keeping track or paying much attention to the interest adding up.

For example, in October 2024, I spent $764 on one of my cards on dog-sitting services, a hotel and dining out, and I made payments totaling $838, which seemed like a net positive. But when you factor in the $60 I was charged in interest during that period, it was as if I'd hardly made a payment at all.

Despite the months I paid the credit card company more than I spent, an interest rate of at least 21% made my balance continue to grow. My strategy was purely vibes-based, just spending when I wanted to then throwing a chunk of money from my paycheck at my balance every other week. I didn't really sit down and look at the numbers to see how much I was moving my balance each month — or in which direction it was headed.

The end of 2024 and beginning of 2025 got particularly spend-heavy as I went on several trips and attended my best friend's wedding. I got a bit carried away, and my balance surpassed $10,000. Though I checked my account regularly, it didn't really occur to me how dangerous it was to carry that much debt. If I'd lost my job for a period or had some expensive emergency at that time, I could have struggled to keep up with even a minimum payment or meet my other financial obligations.

Thankfully, that didn't happen. I could still manage all my regular monthly bills including my rent and student loan payment. I was still contributing to my 401(k) and some emergency savings. Chipping away at my credit card debt felt like just another one of those obligations, not something that was actively holding me back.

I started dating my now-fiancé in early 2025, and he kindly helped me realize how significant this level of debt was, even if it's not all that uncommon. Around 45% of credit cardholders have carried a balance at least one month in the last year, according to LendingTree's analysis of Federal Reserve data. Americans with any kind of debt have an average credit card balance of $7,756 as of the first quarter of 2026, per LendingTree's analysis of over 400,000 of the company's users.

I sat down with my budgeting spreadsheet and crunched the numbers. Pulling up my monthly credit card statements, I looked at the net payments I was making on the debt each month and was fairly surprised to see how many months I was actually adding to the balance instead of bringing it down, even slowly.

There are plenty of tactics people use to address credit card debt, from putting their cards in the freezer to committing to "no-spend" periods. I am fortunate enough that I didn't have to take any kind of drastic measure when I got serious about paying off my debt.

I did cut back on some spending areas, mainly dining out and shopping for clothes. Living in New York, it's easy to drop $100 on dinner or drinks with friends. I said yes to fewer nights out, and when I went out to dinner, I would skip the $15 cocktail, for example. I stopped Ubering home as frequently as I used to, saving another $40 or so when I did go out.

I can be a very impulsive shopper when it comes to clothes, so I got more intentional about pre-selecting items and waiting before buying to make sure I really needed or wanted them. Instead of going to Target and mindlessly adding things to my cart, I'd make online orders so I only got what I needed. I made a great effort to save for trips ahead of time and paid those charges off right away instead of over several months.

And instead of just throwing big chunks of money at my debt hoping it would exceed whatever I spent in a month, I actually started putting my monthly statement balance in a spreadsheet. This way, I couldn't deny the months I added to my balance, despite still making payments. I moved in May 2025, so that month, for example, my balance went up slightly as I paid movers instead of putting extra cash toward my debt.

For the most part, I would use my debit card for purchases, but I didn't cut myself off from my credit card altogether.

I took the classic budgeting advice of "paying myself first" to work down my credit card balance while also saving some for emergencies. On my biweekly pay days, I made a payment on my credit card balance and had an automatic contribution to my emergency savings fund. Then I'd use whatever was left in my checking account for groceries and other necessities. I'd often make an extra payment the weeks I didn't get paid, too, if I had extra cash after those essentials. From October 2025 through September 2026, I've managed to throw nearly $600 a month, on average, at my debt.

That emergency fund proved especially handy in May 2026 when my dog had a very expensive vet visit.

It's something I write about often — how it's smart to keep emergency savings even while you're paying down debt — but actually going through it was a good learning experience. It kind of hurt to have to tap into the cash savings I'd been really proud of building. But in the end, I was glad I didn't just have to add the $6,000 vet bill to my credit card debt. It slowed down my progress a bit that month, but ultimately didn't derail me. And now that I'm not putting $500 toward credit card debt every month, I can focus on rebuilding my savings and other financial goals.

Personal finance columnist Michelle Singletary recently told me "Debt limits your possibilities." I didn't really feel that way when I was on a vacation that I charged to my card. But as I recently turned 30, started planning my wedding and thinking about my future, I started feeling like my debt payment is a bill I don't need or want to have to pay every month. And I feel lighter without it.

Trump admin. progressing toward treating millions of veterans with psychedelics

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Nearly one in three veterans who relies on the government for health care could benefit from taking psychedelic drugs like magic mushrooms or MDMA.

That's not the assessment of some hippie or fading 1960s rock star. It came from Andrew Gradison, a senior adviser at President Donald Trump's Veterans Health Administration. Gradison told a conference for psychedelics enthusiasts this month that he is eager to ramp up the VA's capacity to give the drugs to as many as 2.1 million veterans with mental illnesses like post-traumatic stress disorder or depression. Gradison said 6.4 million veterans regularly rely on the VA for care.

No government regulator has deemed psychedelics safe or effective for mental illness. Under former President Joe Biden, the Food and Drug Administration in 2024 resoundingly rejected a proposal to authorize MDMA — also known as ecstasy — combined with talk therapy, to treat people with PTSD.

But psychedelics are getting a second chance in the Trump administration. Trump in April issued an executive order to accelerate research, clinical trials and patient access to the drugs. Republicans in Congress are among psychedelics' biggest backers. And the constituency they are seeking to please, veterans, are disproportionately Republicans.

Veterans, frustrated with high suicide rates among their fellow ex-service members, are lobbying aggressively to see Trump follow through.

"We were able to roll out a vaccine for Covid because of President Trump's Warp Speed initiative," said Juliana Mercer, a Marine Corps vet who also spoke at the Horizons: Perspectives on Psychedelics conference in New York City. "Why aren't we warp-speeding this treatment to save our veteran lives? What's keeping us from doing that?"

Gradison said practical challenges, and the need to ensure safety, stand in the way. He said that "it'll be a challenge" for the VA "in the first year to treat four digits worth of patients" with an FDA-approved psychedelic drug.

"Our demand will exceed our ability to supply these therapies, no doubt," Gradison said.

Still, psychedelic enthusiasts can also point to some momentum coming out of the Trump administration. A week after Trump's April executive order, the FDA allowed fast-track application reviews for three drugmakers developing psychedelic treatments. One, Compass Pathways,is set to finish submitting an application to the agency this quarter for its synthetic formulation of psilocybin for treatment resistant depression. Psilocybin is better known as magic mushrooms by those who use it for fun, rather than for healing. Compass anticipates an approval during the first half of 2027.

The psychedelic movement's excitement over the federal action dominated discussions at the three-day New York conference, which drew attendance from federal officials, top executives at psychedelic-focused drug companies, biotech investors, clinicians and mental health advocates.

"We stand here on a new day and a 180 degrees different trajectory than what 2024 brought to us," W. Bryan Hubbard, CEO of the psychedelic medicine advocacy group Americans for Ibogaine, said while speaking on Oct. 17, earning applause from the audience. Ibogaine, a psychedelic drug derived from an African shrub widely available in Mexico, has drawn interest from veterans for treating conditions like PTSD.

Trump's psychedelics push has even been embraced by former skeptics of the drugs. Nora Volkow, director of the National Institute on Drug Abuse, told senators in 2024 she thought psychedelic medicine backers were getting ahead of the evidence. "When people get excited, they want to start believing a little bit in fairy tales," she said. However, she stood in the Oval Office with Trump in April as he signed his order, applauding the effort. She's also spoken positively about the drugs in recent interviews.

Though psychedelics have yet to meet government regulatory standards, advocates of their approval often point to anecdotes about veterans' experiences receiving psychedelic therapy in Mexico and promising clinical trial data as reasons to move forward.

Glenn H. Curtis, a retired Army major general, described the impact of taking ibogaine last year in Mexico as having a "miraculous" effect on his PTSD and traumatic brain injuries.

"Those same people that had issues coming home from deployments, even like myself, 20 years later, still have issues today that they have not received any kind of treatment [for]," said Curtis, who is a volunteer for Americans for Ibogaine.

Veterans have multiple champions in Washington for their cause. Sen. Tim Sheehy (R-Mont.), a former Navy SEAL who served in combat in Iraq and Afghanistan, has offered his support,sponsoring legislation to establish an office within the VA dedicated to helping the agency prepare for psychedelic drug approvals and integrate the therapies into patient care. Reps. Lou Correa (D-Calif.) and Jack Bergman (R-Mich.), a former Marine Corps general, co-chair the Psychedelics Advancing Therapies Caucus, which recently released a suggested framework for implementing FDA-approved psychedelic therapies into the U.S. health care system.

At the same time, psychedelic enthusiasts are set to lose two prominent advocates on Capitol Hill. Texas GOP Rep. Dan Crenshaw, a former SEAL ousted by a primary challenger in March, led a successful push in 2023 to pass legislation that funded federal clinical trials on psychedelic therapies for active-duty military members. Another Texas Republican, Rep. Morgan Luttrell, a former SEAL who sought ibogaine treatment in Mexico for a traumatic brain injury he incurred during training, is retiring from Congress in January after deciding not to seek reelection.

"Executive orders have a tendency to go away as the tides ebb and flow, and I would just hate for something like this to be removed," Luttrell told POLITICO, emphasizing his desire for Congress to codify Trump's executive order.

Veterans are a reliable Republican voting bloc, with 61 percent voting for Trump over Kamala Harris in 2024.

The VA has led the push in Washington to advance access to psychedelic therapy. It's involved in 20 psychedelic clinical trials and was one of the first major health care systems to roll out Spravato, an FDA-approved treatment for depression using esketamine, a chemical component found in ketamine. Ketamine — an anesthetic frequently referred to as a psychedelic due to its psychoactive effects — is currently the only psychedelic-like drug approved for medical use.

Trump administration officials say they hope more will be approved soon.

"We want to see the drug approved if it's safe and effective, and the reason why is because there's a mental health crisis," Matt Zorn, deputy general counsel at the Health and Human Services Department, told POLITICO.

Despite the federal government and advocacy groups' ongoing efforts to support the veteran community, mental health issues among the population have long persisted. The VA has cited research finding that 23% of veterans that receive care through the VA had PTSD at some point in their lives, compared to 7% of veterans who don't seek care through the VA. The agency has pointed to war zone deployment, training accidents and military sexual trauma as some of the main situations that could lead to a military service member developing PTSD.

U.S. veterans take their own lives at a rate roughly 57% higher than the general adult population, with an average of nearly 18 veterans dying by suicide every day.

While psychedelic enthusiasts feel closer than ever to scoring an FDA approval, cracks within the coalition have widened over what treatment should look like. Drugmakers and biotech investors have financially supported a drug-first model but some clinicians, mainly psychotherapists, and lawmakers argue that psychedelics are safer and more effective for treating mental health conditions when paired with psychotherapy.

"This is not dropping a pill and saying 'I'm going to be cured tomorrow,'" Correa said during a press briefing in September. "There's pre-preparation, psychiatric, psychological counseling, the actual treatment and then post-treatment psychological, psychiatric treatment in order to be very effective, most effective."

This tension came to a head with the FDA's 2024 rejection of a MDMA-assisted therapy application from Resilient Pharmaceuticals, then known as Lykos Therapeutics. Among the agency's chief concerns was that regulators struggled to determine how much of the clinical improvement came from the effects of MDMA versus the intensive talk therapy component of the treatment. The agency also cited concerns about missing safety data, trial manipulation accusations and the potential risk for misconduct among therapy practitioners.

But Compass' psychedelic therapy doesn't include a talk therapy component, allowing the FDA to more easily evaluate the efficacy of the drug.

"Patients are in need, and this is a class that's been well studied. We're making sure that all standards are met so that patients can receive treatment safely," Lori Englebert, Compass' chief commercial officer, told POLITICO in a recent interview.
